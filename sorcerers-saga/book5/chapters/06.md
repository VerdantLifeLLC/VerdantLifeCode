# Mist on Glass

Wren missed the tree.

Not the mark. The mark was a chalk circle the size of a plate on the trunk of a birch at thirty paces. She'd have been ashamed to miss the mark at thirty paces at nine years old, with her father's bow, which was too big for her.

She missed the *tree*.

The arrow went past it on the left. Into the snow. Skidded. Vanished under the white with a hiss like a snake going into a hole.

She stood in the birch wood behind the cottage at Harrow, in the grey light of a winter afternoon, with her bow in her right hand and her left hand at her cheek, and looked at the place where the arrow had gone.

Then she said a word she hadn't said since she was a tracker at Thistledown with a Ceiling of twenty-four and nothing to lose.

---

She'd been practising for six months.

Every day. Since the summer. Since the Night of Glass, and Orrin's storm coming over the yard wall, and the gold dust coming down on her drawing hand while she loosed and loosed and loosed into the dark.

She'd shot eleven arrows after her fingers stopped. Lex had counted them. In the mud the next morning. She'd heard him counting, under his breath, from the window. *Nine. Ten. Eleven.* She'd pretended to be asleep.

She didn't remember drawing them. She remembered her hand going cold, and the cold going into the bone, and the feeling going out of three fingers like water out of a cup, and thinking, *not now*, *not yet*, and drawing again. With the fingers she had left. Hooking the string with the last two and her thumb—her gold thumb, that wouldn't bend—jammed against the nock like a peg.

Eleven arrows. She didn't know if any of them hit anything.

---

So she'd switched hands.

That was all. That was all it was. You held the bow in your left hand—your gold fingers wrapped round the grip, which they could do, because gripping was the one thing they were still good for, frozen in a curl like the claws of a bird—and you drew with your right.

Simple.

Everyone said so. Kestrel said so. Elowen said so. Tilda, who'd learned to swing a hammer with her left in a month, said so, and showed her how to set her feet.

Simple.

It was like learning to write with your other hand. It was like learning to walk on your other leg. Every muscle in her body had been built for twenty-seven years to draw with the left. Her shoulders. Her back. Her eye. She'd been the best bowman in Thistledown at fifteen. She'd shot a hare through the eye at sixty paces in a snowstorm to feed her mother and her brothers.

She couldn't hit a tree at thirty.

---

She drew again.

Set her feet. Breathed. The way Kestrel had told her. The way her father had told her, when she was nine. *Breathe in. Hold. Let it go on the out.*

Her right arm shook.

She let go.

The arrow hit the tree. A foot below the chalk. A foot to the right. It went in with a *thock* and stood there quivering.

She looked at it.

She didn't feel anything. Not pleased. Not angry. Just tired. Six months of it. Every day. In the snow, in the rain, in the summer heat in the yard. Six months, and she could hit a tree.

She looked at her left hand.

At the three gold fingers. Curled round the grip. Smooth. Bright. Cold. With the nails on them, gold too, perfect, as if somebody had cast them in a mould.

She hadn't cried about it.

She hadn't. Not once. Not that night. Not the next morning, with Lex counting arrows under the window. Not since. Not when she found she couldn't do up the buttons on Lark's smocks. Not when she couldn't plait her own hair. Not when she lay awake at night and felt them on the blanket beside her face, cold, like something that didn't belong to her.

Harrow women don't cry. Seren said that. Seren's mother never cried, not once, not even dying.

Wren wasn't a Harrow woman.

She still hadn't cried.

---

She shot until the light went.

Forty arrows. She counted. Eleven in the tree. Two in the chalk. The rest in the snow.

Then she went in.

---

Lex was at the forge.

He was always at the forge. She could hear him from the yard. *Clang*. *Clang*. *Clang*. The same rhythm he'd hammered since he was eleven. Steady. Heavy. Like a heartbeat. Like a man trying to beat something into a shape it didn't want to take.

He'd come back from Ashbourne three days ago. From the green. From the King. He hadn't said much. He'd said *he won't*, and then he'd told her why, in the dark, in bed, in a flat quiet voice, and then he'd lain awake beside her until four.

She'd lain awake too. She didn't tell him.

They were both very good at not telling each other things. It was the thing about them she liked least.

---

She bathed Lark by the fire.

The tin tub. In front of the hearth. Half full of water heated in the big kettle and cooled with snow from the sill. Lark loved it. Lark loved anything that let her make noise and mess at the same time. She sat in the tub with the water up to her round middle and slapped it with both square fists and shrieked.

"*No*," said Wren. "*Not on the floor.*"

"*No*," agreed Lark. And slapped it on the floor.

Wren sat back on her heels and let her.

It was the only time of day she wasn't tired. Bath. Firelight. The baby in the water, pink and furious and delighted, with her hair plastered flat for once and her green-grey eyes enormous. Steam going up. The fire ticking. The HERE lantern on the east sill, small and gold.

She soaped Lark's back with her right hand. Rinsed it. Lark grabbed the soap. Tried to eat it. Spat it out. Looked outraged.

"*That's what you get*," said Wren.

Lark slapped the water.

The water went still.

---

Not still. Lark was still slapping. Wren could see her fists going up and down. Could hear the splashes. Could feel the drops on her face.

But the water in the tub—the surface of it, between the baby and the side—

*Still*.

Like glass. Like a pond on a windless morning. Flat. Black. Shining.

And in it—where Wren's own face should have been, leaning over the tub in the firelight, with her braid coming apart and soap on her cheek—

Another face.

---

Narrow. Pale. Clear. Not pale like skin. Pale like ice. Like frost on a window. You could see through it, faintly, to the bottom of the tub. White hair, very fine, pulled back off a high brow. Ice-grey eyes. And on the brow, very thin, very bright, a crown of glass, like a line of frost along the edge of a leaf.

Smiling.

"*Hello, Mistwalker*," said the Glass Queen.

---

Wren didn't scream.

She'd thought about it. A great deal. Six months of thinking about it. Lying awake. Every window horn. Every bucket lidded. Every sheet of glass at Harrow broken out of its frame and replaced, by Lex, in the week after the Night of Glass, with horn shaved so thin you could read through it.

She'd known it would happen again. Somewhere. Somehow. She'd thought about what she'd do.

She'd thought she'd scream.

She didn't. She put her right hand into the tub, flat, between the baby and the face, and said:

"*Get out of my daughter's bath*."

---

The Glass Queen laughed.

It came up out of the water like a bell under ice. Clear. Cold. Delighted.

"*Oh*," she said. "*Oh, that's good. Nobody's ever said that to me. Nine hundred years.*" The ice-grey eyes moved. To Lark. Who had stopped slapping, and was sitting very still in the tub, looking down at the water between her fists. At the face. With her mouth open. "*Hello, little one.*"

"*Don't*," said Wren. "*Don't talk to her.*"

"*Why not*? *She's looking at me.*" The face tilted. "*She's the only one who ever does. Did you know that? She looked at me in the stream. On the night the edge fell. Through the ice. Straight at me.*" A pause. "*As if there were something there.*"

Lark reached down.

Wren caught her wrist.

---

"*I'm not going to hurt her*," said the Glass Queen.

"*You reached for her*. *At Midsummer. Out of the water barrel.*" Wren's voice didn't shake. She was pleased about that. "*You'd have had her if your brother's old man hadn't broken the glass with his cane.*"

"*Ah*. *Arion.*" Something moved across the clear face. Quickly. Like the shadow of a bird across a pond. "*I liked him. He knew my sisters. He told me I had Mae's chin.*" The eyes came back to Wren. "*I'm sorry he's dead. I am. I don't say things I don't mean.*"

"*I know you don't*."

"*Then you know I'm not going to hurt her*."

"*You don't have to mean to*," said Wren.

---

The Glass Queen was quiet for a moment.

Wren watched her. In the still black surface of the bathwater. Holding Lark's wrist. With her own heart going like a hare's.

She'd met her once. Properly. At Midsummer. In the yard. A tall clear figure stepping out of the water barrel in a long glass gown, glittering, with her hand out. Cold. Curious. Vain. Funny—that was the thing that frightened Wren most, afterward, lying awake. She'd been *funny*. She'd said something about Wren's boots that had nearly made Wren laugh, in the middle of all of it, with gold coming down.

She looked different now.

Wren couldn't have said how. The same face. The same crown. The same cold clear eyes.

But at Midsummer she'd looked like a queen come to collect something.

Now she looked like a woman who hadn't slept.

---

"*You're angry*," said the Glass Queen.

"*Yes*."

"*Not at me*. *Not mostly.*" The ice-grey eyes moved over her. Slowly. The way you'd look at a page. "*At your hand. And at him, a little, for looking at it. And at yourself for minding that he looks.*"

Wren didn't move.

"*And at the tree*," said Ysenne. "*In the wood. You missed it. Twenty-nine times out of forty.*" A pause. "*I was in the snow. On the ice on the stream. Watching. You didn't know.*" The clear face tilted. "*You wanted to cry. On the twenty-third arrow. You didn't. You put your teeth together and drew again.*"

"*Get out*."

"*Why didn't you cry*?"

"*Get out of my daughter's bath*."

"*It's a true question*," said the Glass Queen. "*I only ask true questions. I want to know.*" The eyes were very steady. "*Your mother-in-law says Harrow women don't cry. You're not a Harrow woman. You're an Ashdown. Ashdowns cry. Your mother cried for a week when your father died. I've seen it. In her window. In Highcrest. She keeps his bow on the wall.*"

Wren's breath caught.

"*So why don't you*?" said Ysenne.

---

She didn't answer.

She couldn't. Her throat had closed. She knelt by the tin tub in front of the fire with her daughter's wrist in her right hand and the Glass Queen's face in the water and her own three gold fingers lying on her knee, cold, and couldn't say anything at all.

"*Because if you start*," said the Glass Queen softly, "*you think you won't stop.*"

---

Wren breathed out.

Not a sigh. Not a sob.

*Mist*.

---

It came up out of her the way it had come at Midsummer. From the bottom of her lungs. From the place under her breastbone where the wind lived. Grey. Soft. Wet. Cold. A breath like the breath of a valley at dawn, like the fog that comes up off a river in October. Gale Commanding. Mistwalker. She'd been born in a storm. She'd carried the First Breath in her lungs for three days once, at the eye of a storm above the Teeth, and come down with something in her she still didn't have a name for.

It rolled out of her mouth and down over the tub.

Over the water. Over the still black surface. Over the face.

*Fog on glass*.

---

The face went.

Not all at once. Blurred. Softened. The way your reflection goes in a window when you breathe on it. The clear lines of the brow and the crown and the cold eyes going grey, going soft, going—

"*Oh*, *that's clever*," said the Glass Queen. Faintly. From somewhere under the mist. "*That's very—*"

Gone.

---

The water rippled.

Lark slapped it.

*Splash*.

Wren knelt by the tub with mist curling off her lips and her heart going and her daughter's wrist still in her hand, and looked at the water.

Just water. Grey with soap. Rippling. Showing the firelight, broken, in a hundred little pieces.

And her own face. Tired. Freckled. Wet.

Hers.

---

Lark looked up at her.

"*No*," said Lark. Thoughtfully. Not to Wren. To the water. Where the face had been.

Then she slapped it again.

---

Wren got her out of the tub.

She did it very fast. She wrapped her in the towel warming on the fender and held her against her chest, wet and squirming and indignant, and stood up, and stood there in front of the fire with the baby in her arms and the tub at her feet.

Then she kicked the tub over.

Water across the flags. Across the hearthrug. Into the ashes. Hissing.

Lark stopped squirming and stared at her.

"*Sorry*," said Wren. "*Sorry, chick. Sorry.*"

And then she was crying.

---

Not quietly. Not the way she'd thought she'd do it, if she ever did it, alone in the wood, a few tears, wiped away.

Great ugly heaving gulps. With her face in the baby's wet hair. Standing in a puddle of bathwater in front of the fire. Her whole body shaking. Lark clutched in her arms so hard the baby squeaked.

She couldn't stop.

That was the thing. The Glass Queen was right. She'd been right about everything. *If you start, you think you won't stop.*

She didn't stop.

---

Lex found her like that.

She didn't hear him come in. The forge had stopped—she hadn't noticed it stop—and the door had opened, and he was there. In the doorway. In his apron. With soot on his face and the little hammer still in his hand.

He took one look.

He dropped the hammer.

He crossed the room in three strides, through the puddle, and put his arms round her. Round both of them. Her and the baby. And held on.

He didn't say anything.

He didn't say *what happened*. He didn't say *are you hurt*. He didn't say *it'll be all right*. He didn't say anything at all.

He just held on.

---

She told him. After.

Sitting on the floor in front of the fire, on the wet hearthrug, with Lark asleep at last between them in a nest of towels and blankets. In the firelight. With the HERE lantern on the sill.

All of it. The bath. The face. *Hello, Mistwalker*. The stream on the night the edge fell. *She looked at me as if there were something there.*

And the rest. The tree. The twenty-third arrow. *Why don't you cry?*

"*She was in the stream*," said Wren. "*The whole time I was shooting. Under the ice. Watching me miss.*"

Lex was very quiet.

"*She knew about my mother*," said Wren. "*About Da's bow. On the wall. In Highcrest. She's been in my mother's window.*"

"*She's been in everyone's window*," said Lex.

"*I know*." Wren wiped her face on her sleeve. "*I know. But she was right. That's the worst of it. She was right about all of it.*" She looked at her gold fingers. "*She doesn't lie. Seren says. Harrow lore. The Glass Queen never lies.*"

"*No*," said Lex. "*She just tells you the true things you were trying not to know.*"

---

He looked at her hand.

He did it every day. She saw him do it. At breakfast. At supper. In bed. His grey eyes going to the gold fingers and staying there. Not for long. A breath. Then away. As if he'd touched something hot.

He did it now. And didn't look away.

"*I hate it*," he said.

She looked at him.

"*Your hand*," he said. "*I hate it. Every time I see it. I can't fix it. I've tried. Every way I know. Fire. Room. Hammer. Nothing.*" His voice was very low. "*I'm a smith. You bring me a thing that's wrong, and I fix it. That's what I'm for. That's all I've ever been for.*" He swallowed. "*And I can't fix this. I can't fix your hand or Brannoc's or Tilda's. I can't fix the boy in the field. I can't make the bread rise. I can't make the King give it back.*"

He looked at the fire.

"*I can't make anything*," said Lex Vey.

---

She took his hand.

His burned one. His left. In her right. And held it.

"*You made her*," said Wren.

He looked down. At the nest of towels between them. At the round sleeping face. At the fluff of dark hair, drying, standing up already like a bird's crest. At the square fist, closed round a horseshoe nail on a ribbon.

"*We made her*," he said.

"*We made her*," agreed Wren. "*So stop it.*" She squeezed his hand. Hard. "*And stop looking at my fingers like they're your fault. They're not your fault. They're Orrin's. They're the King's. They're mine, now.*" She lifted the gold hand. Turned it in the firelight. "*I don't want you to fix them. I want you to stop being sorry every time you see them.*"

He looked at her.

"*I don't know if I can*," he said.

"*Try*," said Wren.

---

They didn't sleep much that night either.

But they lay awake together. For once. Talking. In the dark. In the loft. With Lark in the ash-and-iron cradle with KNIT on the rockers, breathing.

About the Glass Queen. About what she wanted. *She looked at me as if there were something there.* About the stream, which couldn't be lidded, and the ice on it, and the pond in the birch wood, and the puddles that would come with the thaw.

"*We could go*," said Lex. In the dark. "*Somewhere with no water.*"

"*Where*?"

"*The Sunreach*. *The Glass Desert.*" He paused. "*No. Not the Glass Desert.*"

She laughed. Despite everything. Into his shoulder.

"*Harrow women move on*," she said. "*When the moths come. That's the rule. Thirty houses in twenty generations. Seren's told me all of it. Never stay. Never leave a light on. Never let a moth in.*"

"*You're not a Harrow woman*."

"*No*." She was quiet for a while. "*I'm an Ashdown. Ashdowns stay.*" She found his hand in the dark. "*I ran in the autumn. With her. Round the high country. With the King behind me. For eighty-six days.*" Her voice went very quiet. "*I'm not running again. Not from a face in a bath.*"

"*Then what*?"

"*Then I breathe on it*," said Wren Ashdown. "*Every time.*"

---

She woke before dawn.

She didn't know why. The loft was dark. Lex was asleep at last, on his back, with his mouth open and one arm over his eyes. Lark was asleep in the cradle.

Something had woken her.

She lay still and listened.

The stream. Loud. Under its ice. The wind in the birches, west. The fire downstairs, ticking.

And something else.

Faint. Very faint. Like a breath. Like the sound a seed makes in the ground in spring, if you could hear it. A small dry slow *unfolding*.

---

She went down the ladder in her nightshirt. Barefoot. With her bow, because she'd taken it up the ladder with her the night before and laid it beside the bed, and her hand found it in the dark before she'd thought about it.

The fire was low. She didn't wake it.

She opened the door.

---

Grey. The very first grey of dawn. Snow on everything. The birches white and black. The stream black and loud. The stepping stones. The blue-door cottage across the water, dark, with one small gold light in its west window. Horn. Blurred. *TOO LATE*.

And the grave.

The grey stone by the stream. The twenty lanterns round it, small and gold in the snow. Pim's turnip, black and soft.

And beside the turnip—growing up out of the snow, out of the frozen ground, between the lanterns, at the foot of the stone—

A rose.

---

Red.

Dark red. Nearly black at the heart. One flower. Open. On a long thin stem, green, with thorns. Standing up out of the white snow in the grey dawn by the grave as if it had every right to be there.

It hadn't been there last night. Wren had walked past the grave at dusk. She'd have seen it.

She went across the yard. Barefoot. In the snow. She didn't feel the cold.

She knelt down by the stone.

---

There was something tied to the stem.

Under the flower. A scrap of cloth. Grey. Silk. Old. Rotten at the edges. Tied round the stem with a thread pulled from its own weave.

She'd seen one like it before. In the spring. Lex had brought it home from the edge of the world, from a gold field, from the place where the first rose came through the crack in the Veil. A scrap of grey silk with one word on it.

*HES?*

This one had more.

---

Not ink. Not paint. *Holes*. Tiny ones. Pricked through the silk in rows, in a careful, shaking hand, with something very sharp. A thorn. Spelling out letters in pinpricks, the way a child would prick out a pattern on a sampler.

She held it up to the grey light.

She read it.

---

*YSY IN STONES*

*WANTS CROWN IN ROAD*

*NEEDS VEY HAND*

*SMALL ONE*

*HELP ME*

---

Wren knelt by the grave with the silk in her hand and the rose in the snow and the dawn coming up grey over the birch wood.

She didn't breathe.

Behind her, across the yard, in the loft of the cottage, through the shaved-horn window, she heard her daughter wake. And say the one word she always said first, every morning, to whoever was there.

"*No*."
