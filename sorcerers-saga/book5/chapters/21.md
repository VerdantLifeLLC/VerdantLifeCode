@@PART Part Two: The Sorcerer's Dawn
# Still

Somebody had chalked it on the wall of the Crooked Kettle.

In the night. In big white letters, a foot high, on the old grey stone by the door where Benno hung the sign for the cider.

*BRING THE CHILD*.

---

Lex saw it on the eighth morning.

He'd come down the lane from the Forge at dawn with Lark on his back to fetch a sack of charcoal from the cart at the inn. The way he did most mornings. And there it was. On the wall. In the grey light.

He stopped in the lane.

Lark, on his back, in the sling, with her chin on his shoulder, looked at it too. She couldn't read. She looked at it anyway. The way she looked at everything. Very seriously.

"*No*," she said.

"*No*," agreed Lex.

---

Benno came out while he was still standing there.

The landlord of the Crooked Kettle. Fat and bald and soft-spoken, with a leather apron and a face like a kind potato. He'd poured Lex his first ale at fourteen and his last one before the Blightmarch and every one between. He saw Lex. He saw the wall. He went white.

"*Smith*—"

"*It's all right*, *Benno.*"

"*It's not all right*." Benno was already rubbing at the chalk with his apron. Hard. Smearing it. *BRING* went grey. *CHILD* went into a white streak. "*It's not all right. I didn't—it wasn't me, smith, I swear on my mother's grave, I didn't see who—*"

"*I know it wasn't you*."

Benno stopped rubbing.

He stood there with chalk on his apron and the smeared letters on the wall behind him and looked at Lex. At the baby on his back. His soft face working.

"*It's the second one*," he said. Very low. "*There was one on the chapel door. Two days ago. Agathe washed it off before anyone saw. And one on the mill.*" He swallowed. "*People are frightened, smith.*"

"*I know*."

"*They've all got it*. *In their panels. Every night. That voice.*" Benno's hands were shaking. "*She doesn't say much. She just—shows them. What they are. Still. And says midsummer.*"

"*I know, Benno*."

"*And the grey's coming*," said the landlord of the Crooked Kettle. "*Everyone knows the grey's coming.*" He looked at Lark. Then away. Fast. As if he'd been caught. "*And they look at your little one, smith. In the lane. At the Forge gate. They look at her and they think—*"

He didn't finish.

He didn't need to.

---

Lex carried the charcoal back up the lane with his daughter on his back and the sack on his shoulder and didn't look at anyone.

He felt them look at him.

From doorways. From windows. From the well. People he'd known all his life. Hollis Penn with flour on his elbows. The widow Tull. Old Jory the cooper. The Arley boys from the mill. He felt them look up when he passed. Look at him. Look at the baby on his back.

Look away.

---

It had been eight days.

Eight days since the panels opened in the dark in every house in Caldor, and a woman of glass sat in an empty chair at the bottom of the world and said *hello, everyone*.

Eight days of the net being still.

---

It was hard to explain what that meant. To anyone who hadn't grown up with the net in the back of their head, the way Lex had, the way everyone in Caldor had. A thing you didn't think about. Like your own heartbeat. Like the floor.

Nothing *happened*. That was the strange thing. Nobody fell over. Nobody went blind. The sun came up and went down. People worked and ate and slept.

But nobody *grew*.

---

Dunny Tobbin had been Level 41, a Berserker, sixteen and fierce and grinding every Delve he could find before the shafts closed. He'd been at Level 41 with three-quarters of the way to 42, and he'd gone out with the Ashbourne Keepers to clear a wolf-pack in the hills above Thistledown, and he'd killed eleven wolves.

Experience: STILL.

He'd come back to the Forge white-faced and thrown his axe in the quench-trough and sat on the floor and cried.

---

Tilda had been working on a new skill. *Runecraft — Apprentice*. She'd been working on it for a year. Every night after the forge was banked. She'd been at Apprentice 9. One more rank to Journeyman.

She'd cut a hundred runes since the net went still.

*Runecraft — Apprentice 9*.

She'd stopped cutting them on the fourth day. She hadn't said why. She didn't need to.

---

And the Kindlings.

There weren't any. Not anymore. The Queen had forbidden them. But there were children who'd turned sixteen in the last eight days, and would turn sixteen in the next eight, and the eight after that. Across Caldor. Hundreds of them.

Children who'd never been told what they were.

Children who'd go to sleep at night with a voice in their heads saying *you are what you are*, and nothing after it.

---

"*It's not the levels*," said Seraphine.

She was at the long table in the Forge. Without spectacles. With her nose an inch off the drawing of the door, and Antho beside her—back from Mal's pool, three days now, with a face like a man who hadn't slept since he arrived—reading the figures off her notebook.

"*It's not the levels*," she said again. "*Levels don't matter. Not really. They're just numbers. Notches on a stick.*" She sat back. Rubbed her eyes. "*It's what they mean.*"

"*What do they mean*?" said Brannoc. From his anvil.

"*That you can change*," said Seraphine. "*That's all. That's what the net's for, now. Since Lex reforged it. Since the Ceiling came off. It tells you what you are, and then it tells you you can be more.*" She looked at the drawing. "*Every level's a little promise. You're not finished. You can grow.*"

She was quiet for a moment.

"*And she's taken that away*," she said. "*Not the levels. The promise.*"

---

Nobody said anything.

The Forge was full. It was always full now. Every anvil busy. Every hearth lit—Kestrel had relit them all, on the first morning, reaching round the net with her eyes shut and her jaw set and sweat running down her face. Brannoc and Tilda and Hedda and half of Ashbourne. Grandmother Asha on the edge of the quench-trough with her feet in the water. Teo, dripping, in the corner, with Barnaby in his jar.

All of them working on the door.

---

The frame was done.

It stood at the far end of the hall. By the great hearth. Upright. Seven feet high. Three and a half wide. Apple wood. Pale gold at the heart. Darker at the edges. Fitted. Pegged. No nails. Lex had cut every joint himself.

It was blooming.

It never stopped. Petals came off it all day. Small. White. Pink at the edges. Drifting down onto the flags round its foot. Tilda swept them up every evening and every morning there were more.

People came to look at it. From the village. In the evenings. They stood at the door of the Forge and looked at the frame at the end of the hall, blooming, and didn't say anything, and went away.

Lex didn't know if they understood what it was.

He didn't know if he did.

---

"*The Wells still work*," said Antho. Flatly. Reading. "*Round the net. Untracked. Kestrel's fire. Lex's. Wren's mist. Teo's tide.*" He looked up. "*Mine. Shade's still there. I can open a door.*" A pause. "*I tried it. At the Keep. The black door. It opened.*"

"*So the door can still be made*," said Brannoc.

"*The door can still be made*," said Seraphine. "*It's not of the net. It's of the Wells. She can't hold it still.*" She put her finger on the drawing. On the bottom of the list. On the empty line. "*If we can finish it.*"

*Latch:*

Nothing after it. Underlined twice.

---

"*And the grey*?" said Kestrel.

Seraphine didn't answer at once.

She looked at her notebook. Antho looked at it with her. Lex saw his brother's face change as he read.

"*Two miles*," said Seraphine. "*A day. Since the net went still.*" She swallowed. "*Some days three. Elowen's stakes. It's past the lantern line in four places. It's past Hobb's Cross.*"

"*And here*?"

"*Six weeks*," said Seraphine. "*Maybe seven. At this pace.*" She looked up. "*Before midsummer.*"

Nobody said anything.

"*She knows*," said Seraphine. Very quietly. "*She knew when she said it. Midsummer. She knew the grey would be in the Vale by then. In Ashbourne. At the Forge.*" She looked down the hall at the frame. At the petals. "*She wants us to be standing in the grey when we decide.*"

---

Lex didn't say anything.

He was at the long anvil. With Lark asleep in the sling on his back for once, her cheek against his neck, her breath warm. He had a bar of iron in the tongs and the little hammer in his hand. He wasn't doing anything with either of them.

He was looking at the empty line.

*Latch:*

---

Wren came in at noon.

From the yard. With her bow over her shoulder and her face set. She'd been to the village. To the market. For bread and cheese and oil. Lex hadn't wanted her to go. She'd gone anyway. *I'm not hiding in a forge for twelve weeks*.

She came straight down the hall between the anvils. To him.

"*Hollis wouldn't sell me bread*," she said.

Lex looked at her.

"*He wouldn't look at me*," said Wren. "*He had the loaves on the counter. He looked at the counter. He said, sorry, Mistress Vey, I've none to spare today.*" Her voice was very flat. "*There were twenty loaves on the counter.*"

Nobody in the Forge said anything.

"*The widow Tull crossed the road*," said Wren. "*When she saw me. Posy didn't. Posy stood in the door of the cottage and looked at me and I thought she was going to say something and then her mother called her in.*"

"*Wren*—"

"*There's chalk on the chapel*," said Wren. "*Again. Agathe was washing it. She looked at me. She said, they're frightened, child. She said, fear makes people stupid.*" A breath. "*Then she said, I'm frightened too.*"

---

Lex put the tongs down.

He put the hammer down.

He reached back over his shoulder and put his hand on his daughter's head. On her fluff of hair. Asleep. Warm.

"*They think it's true*," said Wren. "*What she says. And it is true. Everything she said.*" She looked at him. "*The grey's coming. The net's still. And she can stop the grey. If someone brings her the child.*"

"*She didn't say there was another way*," said Brannoc. Gruffly. From his anvil.

"*No*," said Wren. "*She didn't. And nobody out there knows there is.*"

---

That was when Lex understood.

He'd been thinking about it for eight days. In the Forge. At night. Kneeling at the hearth with his daughter asleep in the loft. *She didn't mention it*. Brannoc had said it, the first night. *That's not lying, lad. That's leaving something out.*

He'd heard it. He hadn't *understood* it.

He understood it now.

---

*She's not talking to us*, he thought.

*She never was. She's talking to them*.

*To every frightened person in Caldor. In every house. Every night. In their panels. Telling them the truth. The grey's coming. You're still. I can save you.*

*And leaving out the door*.

*Because she can't lie about it. So she doesn't say it at all. And nobody knows*.

*And they look at my daughter in the lane and think, it's her or us*.

---

"*I've been hiding*," said Lex.

Everyone looked at him.

"*In here*," said Lex. "*In the Forge. Eight days. With the door. With her.*" He put his hand on Lark's back. "*Working. Thinking if I can just finish it, if I can just find the latch, if I can just get it to the edge by midsummer, it'll be all right. Nobody needs to know. I'll just do it.*"

Nobody said anything.

"*That's what I always do*," said Lex Vey. "*Wren says. Antho says. Father said. Malachi said it the first day in the Stillwater. You think you have to carry it alone.*" He looked at the frame at the end of the hall. Blooming. "*I've been carrying it alone. In here. And out there, they think there's no door. They think there's her or the grey. Because I didn't tell them.*"

---

He took the sling off.

Carefully. With Lark still asleep in it. And gave her to Wren. Wren took her. Without a word. Looking at him.

He walked down the hall.

Past the anvils. Past Brannoc and Tilda and Hedda and Kestrel and Asha and Teo. Past Seraphine at the long table with her nose on the drawing. Past Antho.

To the frame.

He put his hand on it. On the upright. Apple wood. Warm. Blooming. A petal came down and landed on his wrist.

Then he lifted it.

---

It was heavy. Seven feet of seasoned—not seasoned, *growing*—apple wood, pegged and fitted. He got his shoulder under the head of it and lifted, and it came up off the flags, and he felt his back take it, his legs, the way he'd taken weight his whole life.

Brannoc was beside him.

He hadn't heard the old smith get up. He was just there. On the other side of the frame. With his gold fingertips round the upright and his shoulder under the head.

"*Where*?" said Brannoc.

"*The green*," said Lex.

---

They carried it down the lane.

The two of them. Lex and Brannoc. With the frame between them on their shoulders, upright, like a doorway walking. Blooming. Petals coming off it all the way down. Drifting behind them in the lane like snow.

And behind them—

Lex didn't look round. He heard it.

Tilda. Hedda. Kestrel. Teo, dripping. Asha, on Teo's arm, in her three blankets. Seraphine, squinting, with her notebook. Antho, with his hands in his pockets. Wren, with Lark on her hip, awake now, looking at everything.

And Seren. Who had come down from Harrow that morning with Rosie to bring the paint. Harrow blue. In a pot. Seren carrying it. And Rosie beside her, a woman of forty with dirt on her hands and green eyes, looking at the frame going down the lane in front of her with her mouth a little open.

All of them. Down the lane. Behind the frame. Toward the green.

---

The village saw them coming.

Lex felt it. The doors opening. The faces at the windows. People coming out. Into the lane. Onto the green. Hollis Penn in his apron. Benno with chalk still on his. The widow Tull. Posy, behind her. Old Jory the cooper. The Arley boys. Agathe, bent like a crook, at the chapel door, with a bucket and a brush and chalk-water dripping off it.

And others. Strangers. From the Westmarch. From the farms past Hobb's Cross that had gone grey. Families. With carts. With everything they owned. Who'd come east ahead of the grey and stopped in Ashbourne because it was the next village and there was nowhere else to go.

Lex saw a man among them.

Thin. Red-haired. With a hard set mouth and a boy beside him. Nine. Red-haired. Gap-toothed. With a bandage on his hand.

Davy Pruett. And his father.

---

They set the frame down on the green.

In the middle. Beside the Stone.

The Stone was still wrapped. Four layers of sacking. Canvas. Chains. Padlocked. With a hush-rune cut at its foot. And through the canvas, nothing. No glow. Dark. Since noon on the day the Stones went dark.

The frame stood beside it. Upright. Seven feet. Apple wood. Blooming.

A doorway with nothing in it.

---

Lex stood in front of it.

He didn't know what he was going to say. He'd never said anything to a crowd in his life. That was Antho. That was Seraphine. That was his father. Lex was a smith. He stood in a forge and hit things.

He stood on the green at Ashbourne in front of an empty doorway with petals coming down on him and two hundred people looking at him, and didn't know what to say.

So he said the true thing.

---

"*She's not lying*," he said.

---

His voice was too quiet. He heard it go out across the green and not reach the edges. He said it again. Louder.

"*She's not lying*. *The Glass Queen. Everything she's told you is true.*"

The green was very quiet.

"*The grey's coming*," said Lex. "*It's past Hobb's Cross. It'll be here before midsummer. The net's still. Nobody's growing. Nobody's Kindling.*" He looked round. At the faces. "*And she can stop it. If you bring her my daughter. If you put my daughter's hand on a Stone, she'll take the net, and she'll keep you. All of you. In glass. Forever. Safe from the grey.*"

Nobody moved.

"*That's all true*," said Lex. "*Every word.*"

He put his hand on the frame. On the apple wood. Warm.

"*But she left something out*," he said.

---

He told them.

All of it. He didn't make it good. He didn't make it grand. He told it the way he'd tell someone how to shoe a horse. Plainly. Step by step.

The grey. What it was. A country drunk dry, nine hundred years empty, and no edge between it and the world anymore. Drinking.

The Veil. A wall. Aurelian's. And when the wall came down, the grey came through.

And the door.

Not a wall. A door. At the edge. Where the Veil had been. Out of all seven Wells. Not of the net. Not woven. *Forged*. A door that asks. That lets room through when someone knocks. A little. On purpose. And closes again. Like a tide. Like a breath.

"*This is it*," he said. With his hand on the frame. "*Or it will be. When it's done. This is the door.*"

He looked at it. The empty doorway. Blooming.

"*It's Old Mag's apple tree*," he said. "*She gave it to me. On her deathbed. She said, make something with it. Something that matters.*"

Somebody at the edge of the crowd made a sound. Lex didn't see who.

"*If we get it to the edge by midsummer*," he said, "*the grey stops. And the Glass Queen has nothing to offer anyone. Because nobody needs keeping.*" He looked round. "*She didn't tell you that. She can't lie. So she didn't say it at all.*"

---

The green was silent.

Then a voice.

"*And if you don't*?"

---

Lex looked.

The red-haired man. Davy Pruett's father. Thin. Hard-mouthed. Standing at the front of the crowd with his hand on his son's shoulder.

"*If you don't get it there*," said Tom Pruett. "*By midsummer. If you can't finish it. If it doesn't work.*" His voice was rough. "*What then?*"

Lex didn't answer at once.

"*Then the grey comes*," he said.

"*And my boy's hand never closes*," said Tom Pruett. "*And my farm's gone. It went grey in the winter. Three generations. My grandfather cut that field out of the forest with an axe.*" His jaw was working. "*And my wife's carrying. Seven months. And the midwife at Hobb's Cross says the babies near the grey are coming late. And small. And quiet.*" His voice cracked. "*Too quiet.*"

Nobody said anything.

"*She says she'll keep us*," said Tom Pruett. "*The glass one. In my panel. Every night. She says Davy's hand won't ever get worse. She says my wife and the baby will be safe. Forever.*" He looked at Lark. On Wren's hip. Then away. "*And all she wants is one hand. On a Stone.*"

---

Lex looked at him.

At the thin red-haired man. At the boy beside him. At the bandage on the boy's hand, over a cut that had been open since the winter. Not festered. Not healed. Just staying.

"*She'd keep it*," said Lex. "*Davy's hand. You're right. It wouldn't get worse.*"

Tom Pruett looked at him.

"*It wouldn't get better either*," said Lex. "*Not ever. It'd be open forever. That cut. In glass. He'd be nine forever, with his hand open, the way he was on the day he grabbed the scythe.*"

Davy Pruett looked up at his father.

"*Your wife*," said Lex. Quietly. "*The baby. She'd keep them. Seven months. Forever. The baby would never be born.*"

---

Tom Pruett's face went white.

"*That's what keeping is*," said Lex. "*I've seen it. In a field at Westerling. A boy with his crook out. Mid-step. Fourteen. Kept since a Tuesday in the spring. His mother sits beside him every day and talks to him and his eyes move.*" He swallowed. "*He's safe. He'll never get worse. He'll never get anything.*"

He looked round the green.

"*That's what she's offering*," he said. "*Not safe. Still. Like the bread at Westerling. Like the net. Like her.*"

---

Nobody said anything.

Tom Pruett stood at the front of the crowd with his hand on his son's shoulder and his face white and his mouth working, and didn't say anything at all.

Then Davy Pruett said something.

Small. Clear. In the silence. Nine years old.

"*I don't want to be nine forever*," said Davy Pruett. "*I want to be ten. I want to have the cat in the bed.*" He looked up at his father. "*Mag said I could have him.*"

---

Somebody laughed.

It wasn't a happy laugh. It was the kind of laugh that comes out of a crowd when something's been wound too tight for too long and something small and true breaks it. A cracked laugh. Then another. Then someone crying. Then more laughing. The widow Tull with her hand over her mouth. Benno. Hollis Penn, red to the ears.

Tom Pruett didn't laugh.

He looked down at his son for a long time.

Then he knelt. In the slush. On the green. And put his arms round the boy. And held him. And his shoulders shook.

---

Lex stood by the frame.

He didn't know what to do. He stood there with his hand on the apple wood and the petals coming down and two hundred people on the green at Ashbourne, laughing and crying and looking at him.

Then he did the only thing he knew how to do.

He asked.

---

"*I need help*," said Lex Vey.

---

The green went quiet.

"*I can't do it alone*," he said. "*I've been trying. In the Forge. For eight days. I can't.*" He looked at the frame. "*It needs seven Wells. I've got them. All but one. I'll have that one soon.*" He looked back at them. "*But it needs more than that. It needs hands. It needs lanterns. Every lantern you've got. Given. Along the grey. To slow it. To hold it back. Long enough.*"

"*We gave lanterns*," said someone. At the back. "*In the winter. For the line. It's past them.*"

"*Give more*," said Lex. "*Give them all. Every one. Every window. Every house.*" He looked at his mother. At Seren. Standing at the edge of the green with the pot of Harrow blue in her hands. "*My mother's family never left a light on for themselves. Not in twenty generations. But they never went past a dark window without leaving one there.*"

Seren looked at him.

"*The grey's a dark window*," said Lex. "*The biggest there's ever been.*"

---

The green was very quiet.

Then Hollis Penn stepped forward.

Red to the ears. With flour on his elbows. Not looking at Lex. Looking at the frame. At the petals.

He went back into his bakehouse.

He came out with a loaf.

He walked across the green with it. To Wren. Who was standing by the frame with Lark on her hip. And held it out.

Then didn't.

He bent down. And set it on the ground. On the slush. At Wren's feet. And took his hand away. And straightened up.

And looked away. At the chapel. At the sky. At anything.

"*Sorry*, *Mistress Vey*," said Hollis Penn. To the chapel roof. "*Found I had one to spare after all.*"

---

Wren looked at the loaf.

Then at Hollis.

Then she laughed. Short and cracked and wet.

"*Thank you*, *Hollis*," she said.

"*It's only bread*," said Hollis Penn. And fled.

---

Then the others.

Not all at once. One at a time. Benno, with a lantern from the Kettle's sign-post. The widow Tull, with an old brass one, green with age, that had been her husband's. Old Jory the cooper, with a barrel-hoop he'd bent into a frame and hung a candle in. Hedda, with a rune-carved one from the Forge. Agathe, from the chapel, with the altar lamp. Bent like a crook. Walking across the green with it in both hands. Setting it down by the frame. Straightening up. Slowly.

"*The Lady won't mind*," said Sister Agathe. "*She's never once complained about anything I've given away.*"

---

They set them round the frame.

On the green. In the slush. In a ring. Lit. Given. Nobody looking back.

Twenty. Fifty. A hundred.

And round each one, a small circle. Not white—there was no snow now. *Green*. The grass under the slush. Greener. As if it had heard something.

---

Tom Pruett was the last.

He stood at the edge of the ring with his son. With nothing in his hands. He'd brought nothing. He'd come from the Westmarch with a cart and everything he owned in it, and he'd come to the green to see the man who wouldn't give up his daughter.

He looked at the frame for a long time.

Then he looked at Lex.

"*I haven't got a lantern*," he said. "*They're all in the cart. Grey. From the farm. They don't light anymore.*"

"*That's all right*."

"*I've got hands*," said Tom Pruett. Roughly. "*I was a carpenter. Before the farm. My father was a carpenter.*" He looked at the frame. At the pegged joints. "*That's apple. It'll want a planked leaf, and ledges, and a brace.*"

Lex looked at him.

"*Yes*," he said.

"*I can do that*," said Tom Pruett. He looked down at his son. At the bandaged hand. "*If you'll have me.*"

---

Lex didn't answer.

He held out his hand.

Not to shake. He held it out. Open. Over the apple wood. Over the frame. The way his mother had taught him to put a lamp down. The way his father had put down a cup of tea, every morning, for a year and a half.

Tom Pruett looked at it.

Then he put his own hand beside it. On the frame. On the upright. Apple wood. Warm.

A petal came down. And landed on both of them.

---

Seraphine fainted that night.

---

At the long table in the Forge. Over the drawing. With her nose an inch off the paper and Antho beside her and the hall full of people—Tom Pruett and two of the Arley boys and Hollis Penn's eldest, at the saw-bench, cutting the ledges for the leaf; Hedda, cutting runes; Brannoc and Tilda and Kestrel at the anvils.

She was saying something about the brace. The angle. Squinting.

And then she wasn't saying anything.

And then she was sliding sideways off the bench, very slowly, the way a candle goes over when the tallow's melted at the base.

Antho caught her.

He was very fast. Lex had forgotten how fast. He had her before she touched the floor, in his arms, her head on his shoulder, her notebook falling out of her hand and the pages going everywhere.

"*Sera*—"

"*I'm all right*," said Seraphine. Faintly. With her eyes shut. "*I'm all right. I just stood up too fast.*"

"*You were sitting down*," said Antho.

"*Then I sat down too fast*."

---

Lex was beside them. Kneeling. On the flags. With the drawing on the floor and petals everywhere.

Seraphine's face was white. Not the white of fright. A different white. A grey-white. Clammy. Her lips pale.

"*She hasn't eaten*," said Antho. Hoarse. "*She hasn't eaten properly in a week. Two weeks. Since before the net. She keeps saying she's not hungry.*" He looked at Lex. Wild-eyed. "*Lex, is it the grey, is it the still, is it—*"

"*It's not the grey*," said a voice.

---

Rhosyn was standing over them.

Lex hadn't seen her come. She'd been at the back of the hall all evening, by the great hearth, with his mother. Watching the work. With her brown gardener's hands in her lap and her green eyes on the apple wood.

She was looking at Seraphine now.

She crouched down. Beside Antho. Slowly. Her knees cracking—she was forty, and she'd only been forty for a month, and her knees were still surprised by it.

She looked at Seraphine's face for a long time.

Then she put her brown hand flat on Seraphine's stomach.

Through her coat. Gently. The way you'd put your hand on a seed-bed in spring to feel if it was warm.

And smiled.

---

"*Oh*," said Rhosyn. "*Oh, it's not the grey, love.*"

Seraphine opened her eyes.

"*You're carrying a light*," said the woman who had been the Thorn-Crowned.
