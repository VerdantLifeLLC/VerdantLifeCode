# The Heart

Lex had been here before.

He'd sat in that chair.

Not for long. A day. Less. On the night of the Heart, at the bottom of the world, with his father beside him and his brother below and the eaten worlds rising round them in gold. He'd sat in the white chair with the net in his hands, ten thousand threads, every Kindling, every panel, every hand in the world, and felt all of it. The weight. The warmth. The pull.

*Hold it*, the net had said. Not in words. In the hum. *Hold it. Keep it. It's yours.*

And he'd let go.

He'd thought, at the time, that it was the hardest thing he would ever do.

He'd been wrong about that too.

---

He got out of the boat.

His ribs screamed. He let them. He stepped off the black wood onto the white stone of the Heart, and it was cold under his boots, and smooth, and old, and it hummed. Faintly. The way it had hummed the first time. The way a bell hums for a long time after it's been struck.

Except it didn't. That was the thing. It should have hummed. It had hummed the first time like a hive. It was silent now.

Still.

---

He walked across the floor.

Through the threads. Ten thousand of them. Rising out of the stone round him like the bars of a cage made of light. Silver. Gold. Blue. Every colour there was. Taut. Unmoving. He walked between them and they didn't bend toward him the way they'd bent when he was Root. They didn't do anything. They hung there, held, like the strings of a harp with a hand flat across them.

He didn't look at the chair.

He didn't look at the anvil, or the crown on it, or the clear glass woman sitting beside it with the whole net bent round her like a bower.

He looked at the third step.

---

Wren stood up.

Slowly. With Lark on her hip. Asleep. Heavy. With one fist wound in Wren's shirt and the other in her mouth.

She came down the steps. Two of them. And stood on the white floor at the bottom. And waited for him.

He stopped in front of her.

He didn't know what to say. He'd thought about it all the way down the river. All the way past the Drowned Country. He'd thought about what he'd say to her, and every word had been wrong, and he'd thrown them all away.

He stood there. Broken. Burned. With his hands in wet cloths and his ribs strapped and his face—

"*You took your time*," said Wren.

Her voice cracked right down the middle.

He put his arms round both of them.

---

Lark woke up.

Squashed between them. Crushed. Outraged. She took her fist out of her mouth and pushed at his chest with both hands, the way she always did when she was held too tight, and opened her mouth to complain—

And saw who it was.

"*DA*," shrieked Lark.

The whole of the Heart rang with it.

---

He didn't know how long they stood there.

The three of them. At the bottom of the seven steps. In the middle of the net. With Lark's fists in his hair and her face in his neck, talking—*Da, Da, Mam, Gan, no, no, Da, wa'er, Gan*—a long furious babble about everything that had happened to her, none of which he could understand and all of which he understood perfectly.

Wren had her forehead against his shoulder. Shaking.

He held them.

Behind him, he heard Antho get out of the boat. And Rhosyn. He heard the King's heavy tread on the white stone, stopping at the bottom of the steps. He heard the long oar of the Ferryman go in the water and hold.

He didn't let go.

---

"*She didn't hurt us*," said Wren. Into his shirt. Low. Fast. "*She didn't. She carried her all the way down. She sang to her. She let me hold her.*" A breath. "*Lex, she's going to do it anyway. At dawn. She's going to put Lark's hand on the anvil. She says it doesn't matter what Lark says. She says it's the hand the net wants and not the answer.*"

"*I know*," said Lex.

"*How do you know*?"

"*Because I sat there*," said Lex. "*Once.*"

---

"*Smith*."

---

He looked up.

The Glass Queen was watching him from the chair.

Not moving. Not standing. Sitting as she'd sat for four months. Clear. Still. With her long glass hands on the white arms of the chair and the net bent round her and her face like a frost on a window.

She looked down at him across seven steps. At the three of them. At the child in his arms.

"*You're hurt*," said Ysenne.

"*Yes*."

"*Your reflection did that*."

"*Yes*."

"*I didn't tell it to*," said the Glass Queen. "*I want you to know that. I only showed it what you are. What you looked like, in the spring, for one heartbeat, when I showed you the crown.*" Her clear eyes didn't blink. "*It did the rest itself. It was you.*"

"*It was the bit of me that wanted it*," said Lex.

"*Yes*."

"*Then it wasn't me*," said Lex. "*Not all of me. Not even most.*"

The Glass Queen looked at him for a long time.

"*That's what everyone says*," she said.

---

"*Ysy*."

The King. At the bottom of the steps. Huge. Gold. With a white petal in one hand and a thin silver-grey crown in the other. Standing on the white floor of the Heart, and the white floor staying white under him.

"*Ysy*," he said again. Gently. "*Come down.*"

The Glass Queen turned her head.

Slowly. The way glass turns. And looked at her brother.

For the first time since the boat, Lex saw something move in the clear face. Not a crack. A shift. Like light going across ice when a cloud moves off the sun.

"*Oz*," she said.

"*Come down*," said the King. "*Please. It's nearly dawn. You've been sitting there four months. You must be tired.*"

"*I'm always tired*," said Ysenne. "*I've been tired for nine hundred years.*"

"*I know*."

"*You don't*." Her voice was very quiet. Very clear. Like a bell under ice. "*You don't know, Oz. You had them. Your household. Nine thousand of them. Walking behind you down the road. Looking at you. All day. Every day. For nine hundred years. Every face turned toward you.*" Her glass hands tightened on the arms of the chair. "*Caddoc had his thread. His mountain. Rosie had her roses, her tower, her little grey boy. You all had something that looked back.*"

The King didn't say anything.

"*I had glass*," said Ysenne. "*Every pane in the world. Every face. Every one of them looking into me. And none of them seeing me. Not one.*" A pause. "*I walked through every mirror in the world for nine hundred years, Oz, and I never saw my own face. Not once. Not since the pool.*"

---

"*I know*," said Rhosyn.

Beside the King. At the bottom of the steps. With her bound hands held in front of her, the linen dark with blood. Forty years old. Barefoot. With thorns in her hair.

"*I know, Ysy*," said Rhosyn. "*Oz told me. In the winter. About the mirror.*"

The Glass Queen didn't look at her.

Lex saw it. Saw the glass face turn—not toward Rhosyn, but away. Very slightly. A quarter-inch. The way you turn away from a light that's too bright. The way a child turns her face away when you try to make her look at you after she's done something wrong.

"*Ysy*," said Rhosyn. "*Look at me.*"

"*No*," said the Glass Queen.

---

"*It's nearly dawn*," said Ysenne.

She stood up.

The net moved with her. All of it. Ten thousand threads, rising, bending, following her as she rose out of the white chair, like a gown with a train a thousand miles long. The whole Heart shifted. The light changed. Lex felt it in his feet. In his teeth. In the strapped ribs. A pressure. A cold.

She came down the steps.

One. Two. Three.

Lex didn't move. Wren didn't move. Lark, between them, went quiet. Stopped babbling. Turned her head on his shoulder and looked at the woman coming down the steps toward them.

Her eyebrows came down.

---

The Glass Queen stopped on the bottom step.

A yard away. Close enough to touch. She was taller than Lex had thought. Taller than him. Clear and cold and beautiful in the way a frost on a window is beautiful. And up close, he could see—

Faces.

In her. Under the surface of the glass. Faint. Moving. Thousands. Millions. Every face that had ever looked into anything. Drifting under her skin like fish under ice. A woman brushing her hair. A boy in a puddle. A soldier. A girl in a spoon.

All of them. In her. Looking out.

None of them her own.

---

"*Give her to me, smith*," said Ysenne.

"*No*."

"*She'll be safe*," said the Glass Queen. "*I promise you. I don't lie. I've never lied. Not once in nine hundred years. Ask Rosie. Ask Oz.*" Her clear eyes were on Lark. "*She'll put her hand on the anvil and the net will take her. And she'll be Root. And I'll hold the crown for her. I'll sit in the chair. I'll be regent. Until she's grown.*" A pause. "*She'll never be hurt. Never be ill. Never be old. She'll never die. Nobody will. Nobody in the whole world. The grey will stop. The edge will close. And everyone will be kept. Safe. Still.*" Her voice didn't shake. "*Forever.*"

"*And everyone will look at you*," said Lex.

The Glass Queen went very still.

"*That's it*," said Lex. "*Isn't it? That's the bit you left out.*" He held his daughter tighter. "*You'll be in every pane. Every panel. Every Stone. The net will show everyone what they are, and keep them that, and they'll stand in front of their panels forever. Looking. At you.*"

"*Yes*," said Ysenne.

She didn't deny it. She never lied.

"*And it won't help*," said Lex. "*They'll look at you. Every face in the world. And they'll see themselves. Not you. Never you. That's what glass is.*"

---

Something went across the glass face.

Fast. Gone. Lex saw it. He didn't know what it was.

"*Give her to me*," said Ysenne again. Quieter.

"*No*."

The Glass Queen looked at him. For a long moment.

"*Then I'm sorry*," she said. And meant it.

And lifted her hand.

It came up out of the floor.

Out of the white stone. Out of the threads. Out of the net, all of it, every strand, ten thousand of them, all at once. It came into his feet and up his legs and into his chest and his arms and his face. Cold. Clear. The cold of the long mirror. The cold that remembers.

And a panel opened in front of him.

Silver-blue. Hanging in the white air of the Heart.

And he saw himself.

Not in the panel. Around it. In the air. In the light. In every thread of the net, as if every one of them had become a pane of glass. A man. Broken. Burned. Holding a child. Standing at the foot of seven steps with his arms round his wife and his daughter.

Stopped.

Exactly as he was. This moment. This breath. Every hair. Every burn. Every crack in his ribs. Kept. The way a beetle is kept in amber. The way the gold kept Ilda.

He couldn't move.

---

He tried.

He reached. Down. Round the net. Past it. To the Wells. To the heat. To the slow patient deep of the earth. The way he'd learned to do since Midwinter. Hand over hand. Climbing.

Nothing came.

Not slowly. Not at all. There was no *down* any more. No *round*. There was only the cold, and the panel, and the reflection of himself in every thread of light, held. He was a man in a picture of a man. He was what he was.

He couldn't even close his eyes.

---

Beside him, Wren was still.

He could see her out of the side of his eye. Frozen. With her arms round Lark. With her face turned toward the Glass Queen and her mouth open on a word she'd never finish. Her gold fingers. Her red braid. A panel in front of her, silver-blue.

*WREN ASHDOWN. MISTWALKER. Lv 73. YOU ARE WHAT YOU ARE.*

Behind him—he couldn't turn, but he knew—Antho. Rhosyn. The King. The Ferryman in his boat, perhaps. Perhaps not. Perhaps the Ferryman was the only thing in the Heart the net didn't reach.

Everyone else. Still.

---

Except Lark.

---

She was squirming.

Between them. In the circle of his frozen arm and Wren's. Pushing at his chest. Wriggling. Kicking. The way she always did when she was held too tight. As if nothing were wrong. As if her mam and da had just gone a bit stiff and stupid and she wanted to get down.

There was no panel in front of her.

Of course there wasn't. She was twenty months old. She'd never Kindled. She'd never stood in front of a Stone and had the net look at her. She had no class. No level. No experience. Nothing for the net to hold.

She wasn't anything it knew how to keep.

---

The Glass Queen bent down.

Slowly. Carefully. With the net bending round her. And put her long glass hands round the small squirming body.

And lifted.

Out of Lex's arms. Out of Wren's. They didn't stop her. They couldn't. They stood there, still, held, a man and a woman in a picture, and their arms stayed in the shape of the child they'd been holding, round nothing.

Lark screamed.

---

It was the loudest sound Lex had ever heard.

He'd heard a lot of loud things. A Well screaming. The lid lifting off the world. The Veil falling like a cloth. A dragon's thunder. Nothing like this. This was a small girl who'd been taken away from her mam and da twice in two days by the same cold lady, and had had enough. It went up through the Heart, through the still threads, up through the grey water and the drowned city, and it didn't stop. It went on and on and on.

The Glass Queen held her. Against her glass shoulder. Gently. Firmly.

"*I know*," said Ysenne. "*I know. I'm sorry. I know.*"

She turned.

And walked back up the steps.

---

Lex watched her go.

He couldn't do anything else. He couldn't move his eyes. He could only see what was in front of him. The bottom step. The second. The third. The glass woman going up them with his daughter screaming over her shoulder, small fists beating on clear glass, face red and wet and furious, reaching back. Over the shoulder. Down the steps. To him.

*Da*.

*DA*.

He couldn't even blink.

---

*[...brother...]*

---

Very faint. Very far. Under the floor. Under the white stone of the Heart. Under the net. Under everything.

*[...brother...you're held...]*

Mal.

Three hundred miles off. And further. And down. A pool in a green field with five hundred and fifty-six lanterns round it. A voice like the sound in a well when you drop a stone. Reaching up through the ground. Through the roots of the world. To here.

*[...I can't take what's held...]*

Lex knew. He couldn't answer.

*[...but I can make room...]*

---

He felt it.

Not in his feet. Not in his chest. In front of him. Right in front of his face. In the quarter-inch of air between his eyes and the reflection of himself in the threads.

Room.

Soft. Dark. Patient. The space in front of things. The way Pim cupped his hands in front of a gold face. The way Lex had held room between the King's hand and Old Mag's. A little pocket of nothing, right in front of him. Not freeing him. Not unkeeping him. Just—there. A breath of space between him and what he was.

And in it, faint, a long way down—

Not fire. He couldn't reach fire. Not stone. Not tide or gale or shade or radiance. They were all round the net, and there was no round any more.

Something else.

The thing he'd felt in the Forge, in the spring, on the floor, with the Key in three pieces on the flags in front of him.

The space where something *was*.

---

He couldn't move his arm. He couldn't move his mouth.

But he didn't need to. The seventh Well wasn't something you reached for. It was something you made room for.

He made room.

---

Up on the dais, the Glass Queen had reached the anvil.

She stood in front of it. Gold. Huge. Old. Plain. Worn smooth by blows struck nine hundred years ago. With the crown on its face.

She held the screaming child against her shoulder with one arm. And with the other, very gently, she took the small right hand. The square one. The hand with the wide palm and the short strong fingers that Lex's mother said was Wenna's and Brannoc said was a smith's and Wren said was Lark's and nobody else's.

And held it out over the anvil.

Lark screamed.

---

Lex felt the room in front of him open.

Not much. A crack. The width of a hair. A thread. Between him and his reflection. Between him and *YOU ARE WHAT YOU ARE*. The panel in front of him flickered. Once.

He could move his eyes.

He moved them. Up. To the dais. To the anvil. To the glass hand round the small one, a finger's breadth over the gold.

He could move his mouth.

"*Lark*," said Lex.

It wasn't loud. He didn't have loud in him. It came out of the crack of room in front of his face like a breath under a door.

But she heard him.

She always heard him. She'd heard him through the wall of the tool-room at night, and from across the green, and from the bottom of the yard. She heard him now. Over her own screaming. Over the whole of the Heart.

She stopped screaming.

She turned her head on the glass shoulder. And looked down the seven steps. At him.

---

He didn't tell her what to do.

He didn't say *say no*. He didn't say *don't*. He didn't say anything else at all. He had one breath of room and he'd spent it on her name.

He just looked at her.

The way you look at someone across a room to say: *I'm here*.

He understood, then.

Standing at the foot of the steps with the crack of room in front of his face and his panel flickering. He understood something he'd never understood, in all the years since the night of the Heart.

He'd sat in that chair. With the whole net in his hands. And he'd let go.

And the net had *learned*.

It had been made by a frightened young man to take. To drink. To hold. For nine hundred years it had taken. Every Kindling, every class, every ceiling, every level—taken, held, kept. And then a smith had sat in the Root's chair and held all of it, and been asked to keep it, and opened his hands.
