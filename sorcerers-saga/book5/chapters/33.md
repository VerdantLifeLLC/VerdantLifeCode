# The Forging of the Door

They set the Stone down at dusk.

Twelve oxen. Forty men on the ropes. Brannoc shouting. Tom Pruett shouting louder. And Lex, at the end of the old road, with his hands flat on the grey grass and his eyes shut, asking.

*Here*, he said. *Please. Across. At the edge.*

The Stone came down off the cart the way it had come up out of the green. Slowly. As if it had been asked. Sixteen feet of grey rough rock, cracked down the middle, earth still on its foot from four hundred years in the ground at Ashbourne. It slid off the back of the cart onto the rollers, and off the rollers onto the grass, and turned, of its own accord, the way a dog turns before it lies down.

And lay across the end of the road.

North and south. Along the seam. A yard from the crease in the air where the world stopped. With the wind going over it into the nothing, cool and steady, pulling at the grass.

The panel opened over it.

*THRESHOLD*.

Lex took his hands off the ground.

"*That'll do*," he said.

---

Nobody slept much.

They made camp on the long rise above the end of the road. Four hundred people. More, by now. With their lanterns set out in a ring round the camp, and a second ring along the top of the rise, and a third, a long line, running north and south a hundred yards back from the seam, as far as the light would reach. Holding the ground. Holding the green. Keeping the grey off while they worked.

The wind blew through all of it. West. Into the nothing. Every flame in every lantern leaning toward the seam like grass in a current.

Lex sat by the cart where the door stood lashed upright on its frame and looked at it.

Seven feet. Three and a half. Apple wood, blooming. Petals coming off it in the wind and blowing west over the Stone and through the seam and away into the white, where they went small and then went nowhere. Hinges of black iron that breathed. A bell on a cord round his neck. A latch in his pocket, gold-grey, plain. A pot of blue paint in the basket at his feet.

Seven Wells.

He'd done the sum a hundred times since the spring. In the Forge. On the floor. On the road. *A wall needs only one Well. A door needs all seven*.

He had all seven. COMMANDING. Every one.

He'd never used all seven at once.

Nobody had. Not since a frightened young man with a little hammer had stood in front of a starving Well with his brother watching and cut his own shadow off at the ankles.

---

"*You're thinking too loud*," said Wren.

She sat down beside him. On the grass. With her bow across her knees. She'd been carrying it for nineteen days and hadn't strung it once. Lark was asleep in the cart behind them, in a nest of blankets on the end of the Stone's empty rollers, with Brannoc snoring on one side of her and Seren dozing on the other.

"*I don't know how to do it*," said Lex.

"*You never do*."

"*This is different*."

"*It's always different*." Wren leaned her head on his shoulder. Carefully. On the side without the ribs. "*You didn't know how to make the hinges. You didn't know how to make the latch. You didn't know how to say no at a hundred.*" A pause. "*You just did it. And then you knew.*"

Lex didn't say anything.

"*You know what Brannoc says*," said Wren.

"*Brannoc says a lot of things*."

"*He says you never make anything on your own*." She looked at the door. "*He says even a horseshoe takes the horse.*"

---

He thought about that for a long time.

Long after Wren had fallen asleep against his shoulder. Long after the camp had gone quiet. He sat by the door-cart in the windy dark with four hundred lanterns leaning toward the edge of the world and thought about it.

*A door needs all seven*.

Not *a smith with all seven*.

*All seven*.

---

At dawn he went to find them.

---

Teo first. Teo Brask, eighteen, who'd been a wet, silent fifteen-year-old at the Keep with a fish called Barnaby in a jar. The Tide Speaker on the coast. Who'd walked two hundred miles from the sea with the jar under his arm because he wouldn't leave the fish with anyone.

He was sitting at the edge of the camp on an upturned bucket, feeding Barnaby crumbs.

"*Tide*," said Lex.

Teo looked up. Wet, as always. Water running off his sleeves for no reason, the way it had since he Kindled. He looked at Lex for a long moment.

"*You said you'd ask*," said Teo. "*In the winter. At the council. You said, when it's time, I'll ask.*" He set the jar down very carefully on the grass. "*It's time, then.*"

"*It's time*."

Teo stood up.

"*Can Barnaby watch*?" he said.

---

Wren. She was awake by then. She looked at him over the cart-rail and didn't say anything. She just got down and picked up her bow, unstrung, and came.

Antho. He was with Seraphine, by the lantern line, holding her hair while she was sick into the grass, which she'd been doing every morning for a week and claimed was the baby's opinion of the grey. He looked up at Lex. And stood. And kissed his wife on the top of her head. And came.

His mother. At the fire. With the clay lamp in her lap. She'd been awake all night. She always was.

"*Radiance*," said Lex.

Seren looked at him. With her brown eyes. Hes's eyes.

"*I've never done it*," she said. "*Not properly. I've only ever carried it.*"

"*That's all it is*," said Lex. "*You taught me.*"

She got up. With the lamp.

---

Brannoc.

He found him at the back of the door-cart, checking the lashings for the fortieth time. Brannoc looked round at him with his gold fingertips on a knot.

"*Stone*," said Lex.

"*You've got Stone*," said Brannoc. "*You've had it since you were twenty.*"

"*So have you*," said Lex. "*You've had it since before I was born. You just never asked it anything.*" He held out his hand. "*It's yours too. You've been standing on it for sixty-five years.*"

Brannoc looked at him for a long time.

Then he wiped his hands on his apron. And came.

---

And Mal.

He didn't go to find Mal. Mal was five hundred miles off, a pool in a green field in the east with five hundred and fifty-six lanterns round it and a boy who didn't talk sitting on the rim.

He knelt on the grass at the end of the road instead. And put his hand flat on it.

*Brother*, he said. Down. Through the ground. *I need you. It's time.*

For a long time, nothing.

Then, very faint, very far, up through the roots of the world, like the sound in a well when you drop a stone—

*[...I know...]* A pause. Soft. *[...I've been waiting...I'm room...I'm what a door's for...]*

---

Six.

And him.

He stood at the end of the road in the dawn wind with the six of them round him—Teo with his wet sleeves, Wren with her unstrung bow, Antho, his mother with the lamp, Brannoc in his apron, and Mal in the ground under all of them—and looked at the door on the cart.

"*Flame*," said Lex. "*That's me.*"

---

They hung it at noon.

Everyone helped.

---

Forty men on the ropes again. Tom Pruett at the top of a ladder. The door, on its frame, coming down off the cart. Slowly. Upright. Swinging a little on its breathing hinges, out and back, petals coming off it in the wind. Across the grass. To the Stone.

And onto it.

The foot of the frame on the grey stone. In the middle. Over the crack. Where Lark had stood on the green at Ashbourne with her arms out and said *mine*. Lex had cut two sockets in the stone for the frame's feet the night before with a cold chisel and a lump hammer, the old way, with no fire. The frame went into them. Snug. Like a foot into a boot that's been worn for years.

The door stood on the threshold.

On the end of the road. At the edge of the world. With the seam a yard behind it, and the nothing beyond, and the wind going past on either side.

Free-standing. A door in a frame on a stone in the middle of nowhere. Like a door in a dream. Like the ruins of a house where everything has fallen down but the door.

Nothing on either side of it.

The wind went round.

---

"*It's not doing anything*," said Davy Pruett. From the crowd. Loudly. Nine years old. "*It's just a door.*"

His father cuffed him. Gently. Not very hard.

"*It's not finished*," said Tom Pruett.

---

Lex took the latch out of his pocket.

He held it. Small. Plain. Gold-grey. Warm. A bar and a catch and a thumb-plate the size of a child's palm.

He didn't fit it.

Not yet.

He put it back in his pocket and turned round. To the six. To the crowd on the rise behind them, four hundred people with lanterns in their hands, standing in a long curve on the green grass with the grey beyond them and the wind at their backs. All looking at him.

"*It needs all seven*," said Lex. Loud. So the crowd could hear. His voice went away on the wind. He made it louder. "*All seven Wells. That's what Malachi wrote. A wall needs one. A door needs all seven.*"

The crowd waited.

"*I thought that meant me*," said Lex. "*Because I've got all seven.*" He looked down at his hands. Burned. Healing. Common. "*I was wrong. I thought it about a lot of things. I thought the net had to be held. I thought somebody had to wear the crown. I thought a hundred was the top.*"

Somebody in the crowd laughed. Shakily.

"*It needs all seven*," said Lex. "*Not one man with seven. Seven. And everyone they love.*" He looked at the crowd. "*Everyone who ever lit a lantern for anyone. Everyone who ever kept a window.*"

He held out his hand.

"*Please*," said Lex Vey. "*Help me make a door.*"

---

Teo went first.

He came forward to the threshold. With Barnaby in his jar in the crook of his arm. Dripping. And knelt on the grey stone at the foot of the door. And put his free hand flat on the bottom of the frame.

And opened his mouth. And the sea came out.

Not water. The *sound* of it. The long slow breath of the tide going out over shingle at Gull's Reach. Out. And back. The turn. The thing Lex had heard at the bottom of the Undertow, in the winter, holding his grief like a stone in his chest. *Out. And back.*

The hinges heard it.

They'd been quenched in it. In the Undertow's turning water. They knew it. They took a breath—a real one, Lex saw the black iron swell and settle—and the door swung. On its own. Out. And back. And out. And back. Like the tide. Like breathing.

And stayed. Breathing. Gently. On its own.

Teo took his hand away. He was crying. Water ran off him in sheets.

"*That's Tide*," said Teo. "*It goes out and it comes back. That's all it is.*"

---

Wren went second.

She didn't kneel. She stood in front of the door with her bow in her hand, unstrung, and her red braid blowing west in the wind. And looked at it for a long time.

Then she breathed in. Deep. From the bottom of her lungs. From the place under her breastbone where the wind lived.

And breathed out.

Not mist. Not gale. Not the First Breath that had lifted Lark into the sky. Just breath. Warm. Soft. The breath you breathe on a cold window, so you can draw on it with your finger.

It went over the door. Over the apple wood. Over the blooming. And every petal on the door lifted at once—Lex saw them—hundreds of them, white and pink, lifting off the wood and hanging in the air in front of it in a cloud, as if they'd been asked to wait.

And settled back.

All of them. Every one. Back onto the wood where they'd been. And stayed. Not blowing off. Not blowing west into the nothing. Staying.

The wind went round the door. Round the frame. Round Wren.

But not through.

"*Gale*," said Wren. Hoarse. "*It carries. It doesn't have to carry away.*"

---

Antho went third.

He came forward slowly. With his hands open at his sides. The way he walked toward a frightened animal. The way he'd walked toward a little grey girl in a drowned street.

He stood in front of the door.

And put both his hands flat on it. On the apple wood. On the blooming.

And the dark came out of him.

Soft. Starlit. Deep. The way it had come down over Ashbourne like a lid, the night of the reflections, cupping every lantern in the ring. It went into the door. Into the wood. Into the grain of the apple. Into the eleven planks Tom Pruett had cut and planed and fitted with his own hands in the spring.

And Lex saw it.

Not dark. A *shape*. In the wood. In the grain. The way the gaps in the stars on the hill above the Keep had been shapes. Faint. Moving.

People.

The shapes of the dead. Dorran. Garrick. Malachi. Kell, with her stick. Elric. Arion. Old Mag, small, with her stick and her cat. Ada Fell. A bread-woman with flour on her hands. Thousands. Moving in the grain of the apple wood the way fish move in a pond. Going in. Going through. Going home.

"*It's a mould*," said Antho. Very quietly. With his hands on the wood and tears on his face. "*Every door's a mould. It's the shape of everyone who's going to go through it.*"

He took his hands away. The shapes stayed. In the grain. Faint. You had to look.

"*Shade*," said Antho.

---

His mother went fourth.

She didn't come to the door. She stood back. A yard off. On the grass in front of the threshold, with the clay lamp in her hands. Wenna's lamp. Cracked across the lip.

"*I don't know how*," she said. To Lex. Quietly.

"*Yes, you do*," said Lex.

Seren looked at him.

Then she looked at the door. Breathing. Blooming. With the shapes of the dead moving in its grain. And held out the lamp.

Not toward it. Not at it. Just out. The way you'd hold a lamp up at a window. So someone coming home in the dark would see it.

And let go.

---

The lamp didn't fall.

It stayed. In the air. In front of the door. Where she'd held it. A small brown clay lamp, cracked across the lip, hanging in the windy air at the height of a woman's hands, burning.

And the door caught the light.

Not reflected. *Took* it. The way a window takes the light of a lamp inside and holds it, so that from outside, in the dark, the whole window glows. The apple wood went gold. All of it. Every plank. Every petal. Soft. Warm. Like a window at night with a lamp behind it.

Lent.

The way Seren had taught him. *Lending with your eyes*. Not giving it away. Not keeping it. Lending. So it would always come back.

The lamp came down. Gently. Into Seren's hands. Still burning.

The door stayed gold.

"*Radiance*," said his mother. She was shaking. "*Oh. Oh, Wenna. That's what you meant.*"

---

Brannoc went fifth.

He came to the threshold with his knees cracking. Sixty-five. Gold to the first knuckle on every finger of his left hand. In his apron. He stood on the grey stone at the foot of the door and looked down at it for a long time.

"*I don't know how to ask*," said Brannoc. Gruff. "*I've never asked it anything. I've just stood on it.*"

"*Then just stand on it*," said Lex.

Brannoc looked at him.

Then he planted his feet. On the threshold. Either side of the frame. The way he'd planted them every morning for forty-five years at the great anvil at Ashbourne. Square. Heavy. Rooted. A man who'd stood in one place and swept the floor and banked the fire and been there, every day, when a boy of eleven came in from the snow with nobody.

And the Stone knew him.

Lex felt it. Through his own feet. Through the grass. A hum. Deep. Slow. Like a bell a long way off across fields. The Ashbourne Stone, that had looked at every child in the village for four hundred years and said what they were, looking up at Brannoc Holt standing on it and saying—

*There you are*.

The crack down the middle of the threshold closed.

Not all the way. Not healed. Just—closed. Like a mouth closing round a word. The two halves of the grey stone came together under the frame and held it. Gripped it. The sockets Lex had cut with a cold chisel tightened round the feet of the frame like hands.

The door was set.

Brannoc stepped off. He didn't say anything. He went and stood by the cart with his back to everyone and his gold fingers over his eyes.

"*Stone*," said Lex. For him.

---

And Mal.

---

Lex knelt on the grass in front of the threshold. And put his hand flat on it.

*Brother*.

*[...I'm here...]*

It came up through the ground. Soft. Dark. Patient. Enormous. Up through the roots of the world, five hundred miles, from a pool in a green field. Through the grass. Through the Stone. Into the frame. Into the door.

Into the space *behind* the door.

Lex felt it. Everyone did. The crowd on the rise, four hundred of them, made a sound like wind. Because the door—the free-standing door in its frame on the end of the road, with nothing on either side, with the seam a yard behind it and the wind going round—

The door had a *back*.

Not wood. Not the other side of the planks. A back. A behind. A *room*. As if the door were standing in the wall of a house, and on the other side of it, where there had been a yard of grass and then the seam and then the nothing, there was now—

Space.

Soft. Dark. Waiting. The space in front of things. Room for a baby. A bud. A loaf. A door.

*[...that's me...]* said the seventh Well. Shyly. *[...I'm what's behind it...I'm where it goes...]*

"*Hollow*," whispered Lex.

---

Six.

---

He stood up.

He turned round. To the door. Breathing on its hinges, out and back. Blooming, every petal staying. With the shapes of the dead moving in its grain. Glowing gold as a window at night. Set in a threshold that knew what people were. With room behind it.

And not finished.

The crowd was waiting. The six of them were waiting. He could feel them. Behind him. Waiting for the seventh.

Flame.

He'd had it since he was nineteen. Since he Kindled. Since Ember woke in his chest and said *good morning*. It had been his first. It had been the only one, for a long time. Fire. Heat. The forge.

He reached for it.

---

It came.

Through. Not round. The way it had come at the Heart. Straight up out of the earth, out of the Stone, out of the edge of the world, white-gold, roaring, enormous. More than he'd ever felt. More than at the Heart. More than at the lid. More than when he'd sat in the Root's chair with the whole net in his hands. It came up into him like a river into a mill-race and filled him and kept coming.

And a panel opened.

---

FLAME — COMMANDING

YOU HAVE ALL SEVEN.
YOU STAND AT THE EDGE.

THE FIRE OFFERS: BECOMING.
:::

---

He stared at it.

*Becoming*.

He knew what it meant. He didn't know how. He felt it. In the fire. In the roar. In the heat filling him and filling him. The fire was offering to take him. All of him. Not to drink him, the way the Wells had been drunk. Not to keep him, the way the gold kept. To *become* him. Or him it.

To make him the door's fire.

To set him in the frame the way Ember had been set in his chest. A flame in the door. Forever. A light in the window at the edge of the world. Keeping it. Warming it. Opening it for the dead and closing it against the grey. Never tired. Never old. Never gone.

He could feel how it would be. Warm. Gold. Enormous. A smith become a forge. A man become a lantern.

*Good morning*, he'd say. To everyone who came through. Forever.

---

"*Lex*," said Wren. Behind him. Very quietly.

He didn't turn round.

"*Lex*," said Wren again. "*What does it say?*"

He looked at the panel.

He thought about Ember. Who'd been a flame in a man's chest. Who'd read every book in the Spire and said good morning every day for seven years and gone into the deep gold and come back and paid a price at the Veil and was a flame in a lantern now, on a boat, on a river, courteous, curious, with no love in it for anyone.

He thought about Lark.

Asleep in the cart. Twenty months old. Who'd want to show him her first horseshoe. Her first nail. Her first lantern. Who'd want him to hold the hammer with her and bring it down.

He thought about a forge at five in the morning, with the fire banked, and Brannoc snoring through the wall.

---

"*No*," said Lex.

To the fire. Gently. The way you'd say it to a friend who's offered you something too big to take.

"*No. Thank you. I'm a smith.*" He looked at the door. "*I make things. I don't become them.*"

---

:::root
BECOMING
DECLINED.
:::

---

The fire didn't go away.

He'd half thought it would. He'd half thought that saying no would be like the Sovereign, like the crown—that the gold would go out of the panel and the fire would go down and leave him cold.

It didn't.

It stayed. White-gold. Roaring. Filling him. And under the roar, faint, warm, in a voice he'd never heard it use before—not Ember's, not anyone's, just *fire*—

*[Good*.]

A pause.

*[Then make it*.]

---

He took the latch out of his pocket.

He held it out. In his left hand. In front of the door. And with his right, his burned right, he took the bell off its cord round his neck. Malachi's bell. Small. Bronze. Dented. The bell an old man had rung to call his goats home across the Pale Wastes, and Hesper had rung, before that, a long time before that, at a garden door, all night, in case anybody wanted to come out.

And he put the fire into both of them.

---

He didn't need an anvil.

The door was the anvil. The threshold was the anvil. The edge of the world was the anvil. He stood in front of it with the fire pouring through him and the latch and the bell in his hands, and he *made*.

The bracket for the bell. Out of nothing. Out of the heat. A curl of black iron growing out of the top of the frame like a fern uncurling, a hook at the end, and the bell on the hook. Swinging. Not ringing. Waiting.

The keeper for the latch. Out of the frame. A notch of iron, for the catch to drop into. And the pin, through the thumb-plate. And the bar, across the door. And the hook, at the end of the bar, falling into the keeper.

*Click*.

---

The latch dropped.

The door shut.

---

The wind stopped.

---

All at once. Everywhere. The wind that had been blowing past them for three days, west, out of the world, into the nothing—pulling at their clothes and their hair and leaning every lantern flame toward the edge—stopped.

Not slowed. Stopped. As if someone had closed a window.

Every lantern flame on the rise straightened up. Four hundred of them. More. All at once. Standing straight. Still. Burning upright in the windless air like candles in a church.

The silence was enormous.

---

Lex stood in front of the door.

With the fire going out of him. Slowly. Gently. Not draining. Settling. Like a forge banked for the night. With his burned hand still held out where the latch had been.

The door was shut.

Seven feet. Three and a half. Apple wood, blooming, every petal staying. Gold as a window at night. Breathing on its hinges. With the shapes of the dead in its grain and a bell on a bracket over its head and a plain gold-grey latch across it. Set in a threshold that knew what people were. With room behind it.

And on either side of it—

---

The seam was gone.

---

He looked. North. South. As far as he could see. The crease in the air, the line where the world stopped, the place where the Veil had hung for nine hundred years—

Gone.

Not closed. Not walled. Not sealed. *Pulled in*. Toward the door. All of it. The whole length of the edge of the world, hundreds of miles, drawn in toward the frame on the threshold at the end of the road the way a cloth draws tight round a buttonhole when you pull the thread.

There was no edge any more.

There was a door.

---

"*Paint*," said Lex.

His voice came out hoarse. He didn't know he'd said it until he heard it.

His mother was beside him. With the pot. Harrow blue. She'd mixed it in the spring at the Forge, from woad and lime and something she wouldn't tell anybody, the way Wenna had, the way every Harrow woman had, for every door at Harrow for four hundred years. She held it out with a brush in it.

He didn't take the brush.

He turned round.

To the crowd. Four hundred people on the rise, standing in the still air with their lanterns burning straight up.

"*Everyone*," said Lex.

---

They came down.

All of them. One at a time. In a long line, the way they'd walked the green road. Each one taking the brush from the one before. And painting.

Not much. A stroke. A dab. Some of them just put a finger in the pot and touched it to the wood. Hollis Penn painted a loaf. Badly. Tilda painted a hammer. Davy Pruett painted his own name, very large, before his father could stop him, and then looked appalled at himself, and then painted *sorry* underneath, smaller. Teo painted a fish. Kestrel painted a flame and then, after a moment, a small sleeping girl beside it—Tamsin, Lex thought. Jem Tolley painted a bee. Old Mag's neighbour painted an apple.

Rhosyn painted a rose. With her bound hand. Crooked.

The King painted nothing. He came to the door last of the grown folk and stood in front of it, gold, enormous, with the brush in his huge hand, for a long time. Then he gave the brush back to Seren without using it, and put his gold palm flat on the wood. Lightly. Over the place where the handle would be, if it had a handle.

The wood didn't go gold.

He took his hand away. And there was a print there. In the wet blue. A big hand. Five fingers.

Blue.

---

And Lark.

---

Wren brought her down from the cart. Half asleep. Furious about it. With her black hair flat on one side and her fists in her eyes. She set her down on the threshold in front of the door, on the grey stone that had closed its crack.

Lark looked at the door.

At the blue. At the loaves and the hammers and the fish and the bee and the rose and the name and the *sorry*. At the big blue handprint.

At the pot.

She put her whole hand in it.

Before anyone could stop her. Right in. Up to the wrist. And took it out, dripping, blue to the elbow, and looked at it with enormous satisfaction.

And slapped it on the door.

Low. At her own height. A small square handprint. Short strong fingers. Wide palm. Blue.

Right under the latch.

---

"*Mine*," said Lark.

---

Nobody laughed this time.

The whole of the rise was very quiet. Four hundred people in the still air with their lanterns burning straight up, looking at a small blue handprint on a door at the end of the world.

Lex knelt down beside his daughter on the threshold. With his ribs. And looked at it with her.

*Her hands are very square*, the Glass Queen had said. In the spring. In his panel. With a crown on his head.

They were.

---

He was still kneeling there when it happened.

The door was shut. The latch was down. The wind had stopped. The paint was wet. The lanterns burned straight up.

And from the other side—from the room behind the door, from the space in front of things, from where the edge of the world had been—

Somebody knocked.

---

Three times.

Slow. Soft. Patient. Polite.

The way you knock on a door you've been standing outside of for a very long time, in case somebody wants to let you in.
