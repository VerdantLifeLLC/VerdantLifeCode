# The Apple Tree

The tree was still blooming when they came to cut it down.

Lex stood at the end of Old Mag's garden path with an axe over his shoulder and looked at it.

March. A cold grey morning with a wind from the east and the last of the snow gone to slush in the lanes. The cottage shut up, its door closed, its windows dark. The step worn hollow in the middle. The path still gold, from the gate to the door, where a king had walked it every day for two weeks.

And beside the door, the apple tree.

Old. Black-barked. Bent like the woman who'd owned it. Thick in the trunk as a man's body, splitting low into three great limbs that went up and out and over the thatch like hands held out to a fire.

And on every branch, on every twig, thick as snow—

Blossom.

White. Pink at the edges. Open. In March. As if it were May. As if it had always been May and always would be.

Petals were coming down in the wind. Steadily. A slow white drift across the garden, across the gold path, across the step.

Lex watched them fall.

*Make something with it*, she'd said. *Something that matters.*

---

"*It's a shame*," said Tilda.

She was standing beside him. With a saw over her shoulder and a coil of rope and her gold hand tucked into her apron. Her eyes on the tree.

"*I know*."

"*Thirty years it was dead*. *Then it woke up.*" She sniffed. "*She used to sit under it. In the summer. With the cat. With her pipe. Said it was the only thing in Ashbourne that never got any older.*"

"*I know*," said Lex again.

"*It doesn't give apples*," said Tilda. "*She told me. It just flowers. All year. Never sets fruit.*" She looked at him. "*What do you want it for? It's not good timber. It's apple. It's crooked. It'll warp.*"

Lex looked at the tree.

"*A door*," he said.

---

Seraphine had drawn it.

Four days ago. On the long table in the Forge. With no spectacles.

That had been the hardest part. Not the drawing. The spectacles. She'd come back from Mal's pool with her face cut in six small places where the lenses had shattered, and her eyes red, and no way to see anything closer than the end of her arm.

*I can't wear glass*, she'd said. *Not now. Not ever. She came through it, Lex. Through my face. Through my eyes. I felt her looking out of them.* She'd shuddered. *I'm never wearing glass again.*

So she'd drawn the door with her nose an inch off the paper. Squinting. With Antho beside her reading the measurements aloud from her notes, in a patient flat voice, because she couldn't read her own hand.

*Height. Seven feet. Width, three and a half. Thickness—*

*Two inches*, said Seraphine. Into the paper.

*It says three*.

*Then I was wrong*. *Two.*

*Sera*—

*I can't see, Antho, I'm not deaf*.

Lex had stood at the end of the table and watched them. His brother reading. His brother's wife with her nose on the page. And something in his chest that had been tight since Midwinter had gone, a little, loose.

---

It was a plain door.

That was the thing he kept coming back to. He'd thought it would be—he didn't know what. Something enormous. Something with runes on every inch. Something like the Lockward, or the Key, or the great black door at the bottom of the Hollowmaw.

Seraphine had drawn a door you'd find on a cottage.

Seven feet high. Three and a half wide. Planked, upright, with three ledges across the back and a brace between them. A threshold-stone at the foot. A frame. Two hinges. A latch. A bell.

That was all.

*It has to be*, Seraphine had said. When he asked. Squinting up at him. *It's not a wall. It's not a gate. It's a door. The kind of door people knock on. The kind they come home through.* She'd tapped the drawing with her quill. *If it's big and grand and terrible, nobody will knock. They'll stand outside it and be frightened. Like the black door.*

*The black door had Mam behind it*.

*And nobody knocked for thirteen years*, said Seraphine. *Until your brother did.*

---

She'd written the parts down the side.

In her tiny neat hand. With Antho reading them out.

*Leaf: wood. Something that grows. Something new every morning.*

*Threshold: stone. Something that knows what people are.*

*Hinges: iron. Something that has turned before.*

*Bell: something that rings when someone's sad.*

*Paint: blue.*

And at the bottom, underlined twice:

*Latch:*

And nothing after it.

---

*The latch is the problem*, Seraphine had said.

*Why*?

*Because a door is only as good as its latch*. She'd sat back. Rubbed her eyes. *A wall says no. A hole says yes. A door says both. And the thing that decides is the latch. It's the latch that knows when to hold and when to let go.* She'd looked at the empty line. *It has to say yes and no. Both. And know which. And when.*

*Iron*? said Brannoc. From his anvil.

*Iron only says no*. *Iron holds.*

*Gold*?

*Gold keeps*. *That's worse.* She shook her head. *I've been through every metal in every book in the Forge. Every one of them says one thing. Hold or give. Close or open. None of them says both.*

*Then what*?

Seraphine had looked at the empty line for a long time.

*I don't know*, she said. *I don't know yet.*

---

Lex swung the axe.

It went into the trunk a foot above the ground with a sound like a door slamming. *Chock*. Deep. Into the black bark. Into the pale wood under it.

The tree shivered.

All of it. Every branch. Every twig. A great soft shudder going up through it from the root, the way a horse shudders when a fly lands on it. And the blossom came down.

All at once. Like snow off a roof. A great white drift of petals pouring down off every branch, over Lex, over Tilda, over the garden and the path and the step, so thick he couldn't see the cottage. White. Pink at the edges. Falling and falling.

He stood in it with the axe in his hands.

When it cleared, the branches were bare.

---

For a breath.

Then the buds came.

He saw them. On every twig. Small. Green. Swelling. Splitting. *Opening*. White. Pink at the edges. In a breath. In two. As fast as the old blossom had fallen, the new came. Every branch. Every twig. Until the tree stood over him exactly as it had before, blooming, white, with the wind from the east moving through it and the petals coming down in a slow steady drift.

New.

---

"*Oh*," said Tilda.

Lex stared at it.

He looked at the axe in his hands. At the cut in the trunk. A hand deep. Pale wood. Fresh. Wet.

He put his hand on it.

Warm.

Not warm like a tree in the sun. Warm like *skin*. Like the side of a horse. Like his daughter's cheek when she'd been asleep. Warm and wet and alive, under his palm, with something moving in it. Slowly. Steadily. Like a pulse.

He felt for room.

He didn't decide to. He did it the way he did it every time now. Reaching. With the seventh Well. Three hundred miles off. A pool in a green field, bigger than it had been. *Hollow*.

And the tree was *full* of it.

---

Not full like the bread at Westerling. Not empty and drunk away. Full like a mould just before you pour. Like a womb. Like a cupped hand. Every fibre of it. Every ring of the wood, from the bark to the heart. Room. Waiting. Making room. Over and over. Every moment. For the next thing. The next leaf. The next bud. The next flower.

New every morning, she'd said.

He'd thought she meant the flowers.

She meant all of it.

---

He took his hand away.

"*It's the Hollow*," he said. Slowly. "*It's been the Hollow all along. Two winters ago. When Mal started coming up. Wild Delves on the green. The spring in the forge yard. The mill's second chimney.*" He looked up at the blossom. "*Possibilities. That's what we called them.*"

"*I know*," said Tilda. "*She said.*"

"*It didn't stop*," said Lex. "*The others did. When the Delves closed. When Mal came home. The spring dried up. The chimney fell down.*" He put his hand back on the trunk. On the warm wet wood. "*This didn't.*"

"*Why*?"

"*Because she loved it*," said Lex Vey.

---

He didn't know where it came from. It came out of him the way things did now. From under the breastbone. From the place where the Wells were.

*Because she loved it*.

An old woman, every summer, sitting under a dead tree with a cat and a pipe. Talking to it. Thirty years. *My husband planted it the year we married*. Thirty years of an old woman sitting beside a dead thing and not giving up on it. Making room for it. In her life. In her garden. In her talking.

And when the Hollow came up through the ground two winters ago, looking for somewhere to be, looking for room—

It found a tree that had been waiting.

---

"*Something new every morning*," he said.

Tilda looked at him.

"*Seraphine wrote it*," said Lex. "*For the leaf. Something that grows. Something new every morning.*" He looked up at the tree. At the blossom. At the petals coming down. "*She didn't know about this. She was guessing.*"

"*She guessed right*," said Tilda.

"*Mag knew*," said Lex. "*She said it. On the step. With the King. They're new every morning. That's the thing about flowers.*"

He hefted the axe.

"*Make something with it*," he said. "*Something that matters.*"

---

It took all day.

He didn't use fire. He'd thought about it. He could have burned through that trunk in a minute. He didn't want to. He used the axe. And Tilda used the saw. And Brannoc came down the lane at noon and stood at the gate and watched them for a while with his arms folded, and then took off his apron and took the axe out of Lex's hands and said *you're doing it wrong, lad, you've been doing it wrong since you were eleven*, and swung.

And every stroke, the blossom came down.

And every stroke, it came back.

---

The whole village came.

Not all at once. In ones and twos. Standing at the garden wall. Watching. The Pruett boy with his bandaged hand and Biscuit the cat in his arms, enormous, furious. Hollis Penn with flour on his elbows. Sister Agathe, bent like a crook, leaning on the wall. Benno from the Crooked Kettle with a jug of ale he passed over the wall without a word. The widow Tull. Posy, in a grey dress, not the blue one, with her face pale and her eyes on the tree.

Nobody said anything.

They just stood at the wall and watched Old Mag's apple tree come down. In a storm of blossom that never ended.

---

It fell at dusk.

Slowly. With a long creaking groan. The three great limbs swinging over. The trunk splitting at the cut. Brannoc shouting *away*, and everyone stepping back, and the whole tree going down across the garden, across the gold path, onto the slush and the petals.

It lay there.

On its side. With its branches spread across the garden like hands. Blooming.

Still blooming.

Every branch. Every twig. On the ground. In the slush. White. Pink at the edges. Opening. Falling. Opening again.

Lex stood over it with his chest heaving and sweat running into his eyes.

"*It's not dying*," said Tilda. Very quietly.

"*No*."

"*It's cut*. *It's down. It should be dying.*" She was staring at it. "*Lex, it's still growing.*"

He knelt by the stump.

He put his hand on the cut. The pale wet wood. Warm.

He felt it. The room. The waiting. Not less. Not going out. *Exactly as before*. Every ring of the wood making room, over and over, for the next thing.

"*It's not a tree anymore*," he said. "*Not really. It's the Hollow. Wearing a tree.*" He looked at the fallen trunk. At the blossom. "*It doesn't know how to die. It only knows how to make room.*"

---

Somebody laughed.

At the wall. A woman's laugh. Rough. Cracked. Astonished. Like water coming out of a spring.

He looked up.

---

She was standing at the gate.

A woman of forty. Thin. Weathered. In one of his mother's old dresses, grey, too big, with the sleeves rolled up. With dark brown hair shot through with grey, curling at the ends, tied back with a strip of cloth. With freckles across her nose. With dirt in the creases of her hands.

And green eyes.

The greenest thing in the garden.

She was looking at the fallen tree. Laughing. With her hand over her mouth and tears on her face.

---

He'd met her four days ago.

At Harrow. On the ramp of the blue-door cottage. His mother had brought her home on the dragon from Mal's pool, wrapped in Kit's blankets. And Lex had come across the stepping stones in the dark and found a stranger sitting at his mother's table by the west window, under the horn lantern, eating bread.

Eating it the way Lark ate it. With both hands. With her eyes closed. With crumbs everywhere.

She'd looked up. Green eyes. And he'd known her.

*You held the thorns*, he'd said.

*You're the one with the handprint*, said Rhosyn. Through a mouthful of bread. *Mae's hand.* She'd swallowed. *She always did have a heavy hand.*

---

She was living at Harrow now.

In the blue-door cottage. With his mother. On a cot in the corner where his father's bed had been, under the window. She slept twelve hours a night. She ate everything. She went out into the birch wood in the mornings, in the cold, and came back with her hands full of things. Moss. Snowdrops. A bird's nest, empty, from last spring. A stone with a hole in it.

She'd planted a rose on the grave.

The morning after she came. By the stone. Beside the one she'd sent through the snow, the one with the silk. She'd knelt in the slush by the twenty lanterns with her bare hands in the frozen earth and planted it—a cutting, from her own crown, the crown she'd left on the rim of Mal's pool—and it had rooted. In a night. And put out leaves.

*For Hes's girl's husband*, she'd said. *He sounds like he was a good man.*

*He was*, his mother had said.

*Hes would have liked him*, said Rhosyn. *She liked men who cried.*

---

She came in through the gate now.

Across the garden. Through the slush. Through the petals. To the fallen tree. And knelt by it. On the other side of the stump from Lex.

She put her hand on the trunk.

Brown. Callused. A gardener's hand.

She closed her eyes.

"*Oh*," she said. Very softly. "*Oh, it's so happy.*"

---

Lex looked at her.

"*Happy*?"

"*It's listening*," said Rhosyn. Without opening her eyes. "*It's been listening for thirty years. To her. Every day. On the step. In the summer. With her pipe.*" Her hand moved on the bark. Slowly. Stroking it. "*She talked to it. Did you know? Every morning. About nothing. The weather. The cat. Her husband. Her boy at sea.*"

"*I know*."

"*It loved her*," said the Thorn-Crowned. Who wasn't, anymore. Who had dirt on her hands. "*Trees do. If you talk to them long enough. They lean toward your voice. Like flowers to the sun.*" She opened her eyes. "*It's been leaning toward her for thirty years.*"

She looked at the cottage. Dark. Shut up. The door closed.

"*And she's gone*," said Rhosyn. "*And it doesn't know where.*"

---

Nobody said anything.

The blossom fell. And opened. And fell.

"*It's going to be a door*," said Lex. At last. Quietly. "*At the edge. Where the Veil was. A door for people to come home through.*"

Rhosyn looked at him.

"*She made me promise*," said Lex. "*Make something with it. Something that matters.*" He looked at the tree. "*I'm going to make it the door.*"

The woman who had been the Thorn-Crowned looked at the fallen apple tree for a long time.

Then she bent over it. Over the trunk. Close. Her lips an inch from the bark.

And whispered something.

Lex didn't hear what.

But the tree heard. He felt it. Under his own hand, on the stump, on the pale wet wood. A shiver. A lift. Like a dog's ears coming up at a voice it knows.

And the blossom—all of it, every flower on every branch on the fallen tree—turned.

Very slightly. All together.

East.

---

"*What did you tell it*?" said Lex.

Rhosyn sat back on her heels.

"*That she's gone home*," she said. "*And that the door it's going to be is the door she went through.*" She wiped her face with the back of her dirty hand. "*So it'll know which way to lean.*"

---

They cut it into planks over the next three days.

In the yard at the Forge. On the great saw-bench. With Brannoc on one end of the two-man saw and Lex on the other and Tilda feeding the timber and half the Forge standing round watching.

It didn't warp.

That was the first thing. Apple always warps. Brannoc said so. Every carpenter in the Vale said so. Crooked wood, twisting grain, you can't make a straight plank of it to save your life.

They made eleven.

Straight. True. Two inches thick. Pale gold at the heart, darker at the edges, with a grain that ran through them like water. Warm to the touch. And every one of them—every plank, sawn and planed and stacked in the yard in the cold March wind—

Blooming.

Not much. A twig left on here. A knot there. And from every one, flowers. Small. White. Opening. Falling. Opening again.

The yard at the Forge was ankle-deep in petals by the third day.

---

"*It'll never season*," said Brannoc. Standing over the stack. With his gold fingertips in his armpits and petals on his bald head. "*You can't season wood that won't stop growing. It'll move. Every day. A little. Swell. Shrink. You'll never get a door to hang true.*"

"*It doesn't need to season*," said Lex.

The old smith looked at him.

"*A door that grows*," said Lex. "*A door that's new every morning. That makes room. Every day.*" He put his hand on the top plank. Warm. "*It's not supposed to be finished, Brannoc. That's the point. It's supposed to keep making room.*"

Brannoc looked at the planks for a long time.

"*Your father'd have liked that*," he said. Gruffly. "*He never finished anything either.*"

---

That night, Lex sat at the long table in the Forge with Seraphine's drawing in front of him.

Alone. Late. With one hearth lit and the rest banked and the rain on the roof and the smell of apple wood and coal smoke.

*Leaf: wood. Something that grows. Something new every morning.*

He drew a line through it. *Done*.

*Threshold: stone. Something that knows what people are.*

He looked at it for a long time.

He knew what it was. He'd known since the day of the council, when Seraphine read it out. He'd known before that. He'd known since he was sixteen.

The Ashbourne Stone. Cracked down the middle. Clouded like old ice. Where every child of the village had put their hand at sixteen for three hundred years and been told what they were.

*SMITH*. *Common*. *Ceiling 24*.

Something that knows what people are.

It was wrapped in four layers of sacking and canvas and chains on the green, with a hush-rune cut at its foot. With the Glass Queen behind it. Looking out.

He didn't draw a line through that one.

---

*Hinges: iron. Something that has turned before.*

He looked under the bench.

The basket. Wicker. Lined with straw. He'd brought it from Harrow. In the cart. With the HERE lantern gone from the east window and Wren watching him go.

Three pieces of black iron. Bow. Bit. Blade.

*Something that has turned before*.

He didn't draw a line through that one either. Not yet.

---

*Bell: something that rings when someone's sad.*

He put his hand on his chest. On the cord. On the small silver weight under his shirt, against his breastbone. With its crooked clapper.

It was quiet.

---

*Paint: blue.*

His mother would mix it. She'd said so. *Harrow blue*. The blue of the door on her cottage. The blue every Harrow woman had painted every door of every cottage in thirty villages in twenty generations. *So you'll know it's ours. So you'll know where to come home to.*

*Even though we never stayed*, she'd said. *We always painted the door.*

---

*Latch:*

Nothing after it. Underlined twice.

He looked at it for a long time.

*It has to say yes and no*. *Both. And know which. And when.*

He thought about everything he'd ever made. Hammers. Swords. Horseshoes. Nails. Lanterns. Hinges. Latches—hundreds of latches, for half the doors in the Vale. Iron. Simple. A bar and a catch and a thumb-lift. Every one of them said the same thing. *Shut*. Until someone lifted it. Then *open*. Until someone dropped it.

They didn't *know*. They didn't decide. They just did what the hand told them.

*It has to know which. And when.*

He didn't know anything that knew.

---

The door of the Forge opened.

Rain blew in. Cold. And Wren. In her wet cloak with her hood up and her bow over her shoulder. Without Lark. She'd left her with his mother and Rhosyn and Kestrel and every bucket lidded.

She came down the long hall between the anvils and stopped at the end of the table.

Her face was white.

"*Lex*," she said.

He was on his feet.

"*It's not her*," said Wren. Fast. "*She's fine. She's asleep. Your mother's got her. It's not her.*" She took a breath. "*It's him.*"

---

"*Who*?"

"*The King*."

Lex went cold.

"*He's at Harrow*," said Wren. "*At the edge of the birch wood. At dusk. He came up the gold path. He's standing there. At the bottom of the yard. By the stream.*" She swallowed. "*He won't come any closer. He just stands there. With his cup.*"

"*Did he say anything*?"

Wren looked at him.

"*He said, tell the smith*," she said. "*He said it to me. Across the yard. In the rain. He said, tell the smith she came again. Last night. In the cup.*"

Lex didn't move.

"*He said*," said Wren slowly, "*tell him Ysy says, if I bring her the child, the net will keep everyone. Every thread. Forever.*" Her voice shook. "*No doors at all. Not for anyone. Not ever.*"

She looked at him.

"*He said, tell him I'm thinking about it*."
