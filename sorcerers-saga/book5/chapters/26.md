# The Grey at Harrow

The birds stopped on a Tuesday.

Seren noticed because she was listening for them. She'd been listening for them for a week. Every morning, at the west window, with the kettle on and the pot warming and the horn lantern on the sill burning on nothing. She'd stand there and open the shutter and listen to the birch wood.

Blackbird. Wren. The chiffchaff that said its own name over and over, as if it were afraid of forgetting it. The wood-pigeon, who never finished a sentence. A thrush, somewhere up the slope, practising.

On the Monday, the thrush was gone.

On the Tuesday, they were all gone.

She stood at the window with the shutter open and the kettle starting to sing behind her and listened to the birch wood, and the birch wood was silent. Not quiet. Quiet was a thing with edges. Quiet had a wind in it, and a stream, and leaves. This was silent the way a painting is silent.

The kettle screamed.

Seren didn't move.

"*Are you going to get that*?" said Rhosyn. From the cot. Muffled. Under the blanket. "*Or are we just letting it die*?"

---

"*It's at the top of the wood*," said Seren.

She made the tea anyway. Warmed the pot. One spoon each and one for the pot. Water just off the boil. Her hands did it without her. They'd been doing it for thirty years, and thirteen of those in the dark, with nothing to do it with, and they weren't going to stop now just because the world was ending.

Rhosyn sat up. Slowly. Her knees cracked. Her back cracked. Her neck. She made a face at all of it.

"*How far*?" she said.

"*Half a mile*," said Seren. "*Maybe less. Elowen's stakes.*" She put a cup down in front of her. "*The top one went last night. I saw it from the window. Before dark. A white stake with a red rag on it. And then just a white stake.*"

Rhosyn looked at the tea.

"*How long*?" she said.

"*Today*," said Seren. "*Tomorrow, maybe. If it's slow.*"

She sat down in her chair by the window. With her own cup. Under the lantern. And looked out at the birch wood. White trunks. Black marks. Green leaves, new, the bright soft green of June.

She looked at them very hard. As if she could keep them by looking.

---

Elowen came at nine.

Up the lane from Ashbourne on her big grey mare, in her mail, with her oath-sword on her back and her face set like a closed door. She tied the mare at the gate and came up the path and stopped at the foot of the ramp and looked up at Seren in the doorway.

"*Lady Seren*," said Elowen.

"*Don't*," said Seren.

"*It's in the wood*."

"*I know*."

"*It'll be at the stream by tonight*."

"*I know*."

Elowen took a breath. She was a tall woman, Elowen. Fair. Grave. With a scar through one eyebrow and a way of standing as if somebody had once told her the whole world was leaning on her and she'd believed them. She'd been a knight for twenty years. She'd held a ford against a winged man with a pick. She looked at Seren in the doorway of a small blue-door cottage, with a teacup in her hand, and looked frightened.

"*Lex sent me*," said Elowen. "*He asked. He said, please. He said to say please twice.*" She swallowed. "*Please. Please. Come to the Forge.*"

---

Seren had known he would.

She'd known since the spring. Since Seraphine's figures. *Two miles a day. Some days three.* She'd done the sum herself, on the back of a page of the grey book, in pencil. Two hundred miles from the old edge to Harrow. Harrow first. Then the Vale. Then Ashbourne.

Harrow first.

"*Tell him thank you*," said Seren. "*Tell him I love him. Tell him no.*"

Elowen closed her eyes.

"*He thought you'd say that*," she said.

"*He's a clever boy*."

"*He said, if she says no, tell her the lantern might not be enough. He said, it's one lantern. It's a small one. It's horn, not glass. He made it in an afternoon.*" Elowen opened her eyes. "*He said, I don't know how big the circle will be. I don't know if it'll hold the house.*"

Seren looked past her. At the lane. At the gate. At the stream at the bottom of the garden, running loud and brown and quick over the stepping stones. At the far bank. At the other cottage. Her son's. The one he'd rebuilt with his own hands the year he came home, on the black scorch-mark of the one that had burned. With its empty east window. With the swing he'd hung from the apple bough for Lark, swaying a little in the wind.

At the stone by the stream. Under the roses.

"*Tell him*," said Seren. Quietly. "*Somebody has to keep the window.*"

---

Elowen didn't go.

She sat on the bench by the door and took her sword off her back and laid it across her knees and didn't go. Seren brought her tea. She drank it. She didn't say anything.

Rhosyn came out. In Seren's old blue dress, which was too short for her, and a shawl, and bare feet, because she'd been barefoot for nine hundred years and shoes still felt to her like being held by the ankles. She looked at Elowen. At the sword. At the birch wood.

"*Are you staying*?" said Rhosyn.

"*Yes*," said Elowen.

"*Why*?"

Elowen looked at her. At the thorn-woman. At the Crowned. At the woman who'd grown a wall of brambles round the tenth tower, a mile thick, and made it into a lock, and kept a small grey hollow of a boy in it for nine hundred years.

"*Because she's staying*," said Elowen. "*And I owe her. And I'm not leaving a woman alone in a house with a—*" She stopped.

"*With a monster*," said Rhosyn.

"*With the grey coming*," said Elowen.

Rhosyn smiled. A tired small smile. And sat down on the step.

"*That's kind*," she said. "*You're wrong. But it's kind.*"

---

They watched it come.

The three of them. On the bench and the step. All morning. Looking up the slope at the birch wood.

You couldn't see it come. That was the thing. Seren had thought you'd see it. Like weather. Like a storm coming up a valley, a dark wall, a line. You'd see it coming and you'd have time to be afraid.

You didn't.

You just looked at a tree. A birch. A white trunk, a black mark, green leaves. And you looked away. At the sky, at a cloud, at your teacup. And you looked back.

And it was the same tree. Same trunk. Same mark. Same leaves.

But you couldn't remember what colour the leaves were.

---

"*There*," said Rhosyn.

Very quietly. At noon. Pointing.

Seren looked.

At the top of the garden. Where the birch wood came down to the hedge. Where the hedge had a gap in it that Lark went through on her hands and knees, every time, no matter how many times she was told, to look for the hedgehog that lived under the woodpile.

The hedge.

It was there. All of it. Hawthorn. Elder. A bramble. A dog-rose, pink, open.

It wasn't green any more.

It wasn't grey either. Not exactly. It was just—not. As if someone had painted it very carefully, every leaf, every thorn, and forgotten the paint. As if it were a drawing of a hedge on a page, and the page was the colour of nothing.

The dog-rose was still open. Seren could see every petal.

She couldn't have told you they were pink.

---

It came down the garden.

Slowly. You could see it now, a little. If you didn't look straight at it. Out of the side of your eye. A line in the grass. Not a line of anything. Just the place where the grass on one side was grass and the grass on the other side was a picture of grass.

It came past the woodpile.

The hedgehog's woodpile. Under it, somewhere, a hedgehog. Asleep in the day the way hedgehogs are. Seren thought about it. A small warm thing, curled up, with its nose in its belly. And the grey going over it. And it not waking up. And it not being dead. And it never, ever waking up.

She found she'd stood up.

"*Seren*," said Rhosyn.

She didn't sit down.

It came past the cabbages. Past the beans on their poles. Past the stone trough where Lark sailed walnut shells. The water in the trough went flat. Not still. Flat. Like a pane of slate. The walnut shell on it stopped. Mid-turn.

It came down the path.

Toward the ramp. Toward the door. Toward the cottage, small and blue-doored and whitewashed, with smoke going up from the chimney and a horn lantern on the west windowsill burning on nothing, with *TOO LATE* cut into its base.

Seren stood on the ramp and watched it come.

---

It stopped.

Two yards from the bottom of the ramp. In the middle of the path. Between one flagstone and the next.

It didn't hit anything. There was no sound. No flare. No light. It didn't push against anything and get pushed back. It just—didn't come any further. As if the next flagstone weren't there. As if it had come to the edge of the map.

It went round.

Seren watched it go round. Left and right. Along the side of the house. Past the water-butt on one side. Past the bench where Elowen sat with her sword across her knees, white-faced. Not touching the bench. Not touching the butt. Two yards off. Round. In a curve.

Like water round a stone.

---

She went inside.

To the west window. To the sill. To the lantern.

It was very small. Smaller than she remembered, somehow. She'd looked at it every morning for a year and a half and never really seen it. Horn panes, scraped thin, yellow-brown. A tin frame, soldered, with one hinge that had always stuck until Lex fixed it in the winter. A little handle. And round the base, cut into the tin with the point of a nail, in her son's square careful hand—

*TOO LATE*.

It was burning. It was always burning. It had been burning since the night he'd given it to her, the first night she came back to Harrow, and she'd never once filled it. Never once trimmed it. It burned on nothing, the way all his lanterns did. A small gold flame. Steady. Soft.

She looked out of the window past it.

The grey had gone round the house. She could see it. Out on the slope, to the west, it had gone round and was coming on, past the cottage, down toward the stream. And here, close, in a ring round the house—

Green.

The grass by the wall. The thyme in the cracks of the path. The ivy on the water-butt. The rosemary bush by the door that Arion had planted the first summer, crooked, and that she'd never pulled up because he'd planted it.

Green. Real green. In a ring. Round the house. Round her.

Two yards wide.

---

Seren sat down in her chair.

She didn't mean to. Her legs did it. She sat down in her chair by the west window, under the horn lantern, with the sun coming in on the sill, and put her hands over her face.

---

"*It's holding*," said Rhosyn.

In the doorway. Behind her. Quiet.

"*Yes*," said Seren. Into her hands.

"*Then why are you crying*?"

"*Because it's two yards*," said Seren.

She took her hands away from her face. And looked out of the window. At the slope going down to the stream. At the stream. At the far bank.

The grey was nearly at the stream.

"*Two yards*," said Seren. "*That's the house. That's the house and the path and the water-butt and Arion's rosemary.*" Her voice broke. "*That's not the stream. That's not the stones. That's not—*"

She stopped.

Rhosyn came and stood beside her chair. And looked where she was looking.

Down the slope. At the bottom of the garden. At the bank of the stream. At a grey stone among the long grass, with roses climbing over it. Red roses. Dark red. Nearly black at the heart. Two dozen. Open. As if it were midsummer.

*ARION VEY*.

*He turned back twice*.

The grey was twenty yards from it.

---

Seren was out of the door before Rhosyn could stop her.

Down the ramp. Off the path. Off the green. Two yards. And—

She didn't feel it. That was the worst thing. She'd braced for cold. For a wall. For something. She stepped off the green onto the grass beyond it, and there was nothing. It was grass. It was just grass.

It just wasn't any colour.

She looked down at her feet. At her own feet, in her old brown boots, on the grass. And her boots were—she couldn't have said. She knew they were brown. She'd bought them in Highcrest. She'd worn them for six years. They were brown.

She couldn't see brown.

She ran.

---

She'd never been a runner. Not as a girl, not as a woman. Wenna used to say she ran like a hen in a gale. Thirteen years in the dark hadn't helped, and a year and a half at an old man's bedside hadn't helped, and she was fifty-one and her knees had opinions.

She ran anyway. Down the slope. Through grass that made no sound. Under a sky that was the colour of a page. Toward the stream.

The stream was loud. She could hear it. Brown and quick over the stepping stones, the way it had run every day of her life at Harrow, every day of Lex's life, every day of Antho's. She ran toward the sound of it like someone running toward a voice.

She could see the grey coming down the far side of the garden. On her left. Slow. Out of the side of her eye. A line in the grass. Twenty yards from the stone. Fifteen.

She got to the stone first.

She fell on her knees in front of it. In the long grass. And put her hands on it. On the grey granite. On the letters. On the roses, the thorns, she didn't care, they went into her palms and she didn't care.

And the grey came on.

---

She didn't have a lantern.

She knew that. Kneeling there. With her hands on the stone and the thorns in her palms. She'd run out of the green with nothing. No light. No lamp. No Harrow lamp to give. She was Seren Harrow, the Lady of the Lamp, who'd held a clay lamp in the dark for thirteen years and given it away at the end, and she'd run out of the house without one.

She didn't have anything.

She knelt there with her hands on her husband's grave and watched the grey come down the bank toward her, ten yards, eight, like a tide coming in over sand. Slow. Patient. Not cruel. Not anything. Just *still*.

And she thought: *too late*.

Of course. Too late. Always too late. She'd come out of the dark too late to know him. She'd remembered too late to say it. She'd read the grey book every morning, every single morning, and stood at the window and thought, too late, too late, too late. And now she was going to kneel here and go still and never get up, a woman in a painting, holding a stone, and the roses would be the colour of nothing, and it would be too late for that as well.

Five yards.

She closed her eyes.

---

*Morning, love*.

---

She opened them.

It wasn't a voice. Not really. It was the stream. Loud and brown and quick. Running over the stepping stones. The way it ran every morning. The way it had run on the first morning he'd come back from the dark, an old man in a chair at the window, and opened the shutter and listened to it and said—

*Good morning, Seren*.

And she'd said good morning back. Because it was polite. Because that was what you said.

Every morning. For a year and a half.

She'd never once thought of it as too late. Not then. Not while he was alive. She'd only thought it after. Every morning. At the window. With the grey book.

*He didn't need me to know him*, she'd said to Rosie. In the spring. *He needed me to have a good morning.*

---

The grey stopped.

Two yards from the stone. In the grass. Between one blade and the next.

It went round.

---

Seren knelt there with her hands on the stone and stared.

At the grey going round. Left and right. Along the bank. Curving. A ring. Round the stone. Round her. Round the roses, which were red. Dark red. Nearly black at the heart. She could see it. She could see red.

She looked down at her own hands.

There was blood on them. From the thorns. Bright red blood on her square freckled palms. And light.

Faint. Gold. Coming up out of her palms. Out of the cuts. Out of her. Soft. Steady. Like a lamp behind a horn pane.

She didn't have a lantern.

She'd never needed one. She'd been carrying it for thirty years.

---

"*Well*," said Rhosyn.

On the bank. Above her. Out of breath. With Elowen beside her, sword drawn, both of them standing on the grass beyond the green ring, both of them the colour of nothing. Rhosyn's feet were bare. Rhosyn's blue dress wasn't blue.

"*Well*," said Rhosyn again. Breathing hard. Her knees had opinions too. "*That's one way to do it.*"

Seren looked up at her.

"*Get in*," said Seren. Her voice was shaking. "*Get in the ring. Both of you. Quickly.*"

They got in.

It wasn't a big ring. Two yards. Round the stone. The three of them knelt in it, in the long green grass, by the grave, with the roses over them and the stream loud beside them, and the grey all round. Close. Silent. A picture of a garden. A picture of a world.

Elowen was shaking.

Rhosyn put her arm round her.

---

"*What did you do*?" said Rhosyn.

"*I don't know*," said Seren.

"*You did something*."

"*I remembered*," said Seren. "*Something he used to say.*" She looked at her palms. At the faint gold light coming up out of them. Already fading. Already going back in. "*I've been remembering it wrong. All this time. I've been remembering it as too late.*"

Rhosyn looked at her for a long time.

"*Hes did that*," said Rhosyn. "*With her hands. In the garden. The night she sat in the door.*" She swallowed. "*I never knew how.*"

"*Neither do I*," said Seren.

---

They sat in the ring by the grave for an hour.

Then Seren stood up. Slowly. Her knees had opinions. And looked up the slope. At the cottage. At the green ring round it, two yards wide. At the horn lantern in the west window, a speck of gold. At the grey between here and there. Forty yards of it. A picture of a garden.

"*We have to go back*," she said.

Elowen looked at the grey. And swallowed.

"*Will it hold*?" she said. "*Your—that. If you walk.*"

"*I don't know*," said Seren.

She held out her hands. One to each of them.

They took them.

And they walked up the slope together. Three women. Holding hands. Through the grey. And the grey went round them. Not two yards. Less. A foot. Six inches. A thin green ring round three pairs of feet, moving up the slope, leaving nothing behind it. Like a lantern carried through a dark room.

They got to the house.

Seren let go of their hands on the step and sat down on the ramp and was sick in the rosemary.

---

The grey reached the stream at three o'clock.

Seren watched it from the window.

It came down the bank on both sides of the grave. Past the ring. Round it. Round the stone and the roses, two yards, green, small, like a coin dropped in the grass. And down to the water.

And into the stream.

---

She'd wondered about that.

All spring. In the night. Lying awake. What the grey did to water. The letters said the sea at Morrow had stopped. The tide had stopped. Teo had gone down to Gull's Reach and found the waves standing on the shingle like glass. Like ice. Like a painting of waves.

But a stream wasn't the sea. A stream came from somewhere. A stream came down off the hills, from the springs at Long Ridge, from the snow on the Teeth. A stream had somewhere to go. It came from real ground, still. From the east. From upstream.

She'd thought—hoped—that it would keep running.

---

It came down the bank and into the water.

The water stopped.

Not all of it. Not at once. Just the part the grey touched. On the near side. The far side. It came in from both banks, slowly, like ice forming, like a skin on milk. And the water under it stopped. Mid-ripple. Mid-splash. The little white curl of water over the first stepping stone. Stopped. Still. Hanging. Like a carving of water.

And the water upstream kept coming.

Brown and quick. From the east. From the hills. From real ground. It came down to where the grey was and—

Didn't stop. Couldn't go on. Rose.

---

"*Oh*," said Rhosyn.

She was at the window beside Seren. With her hand on the sill.

"*Oh, no*," said Rhosyn.

---

The stream was filling.

Upstream of the stopped water. Above the stepping stones. Behind the grey. The water was coming down from the hills the way it always had, and meeting the stillness, and not being able to go through it, and backing up.

Spreading.

Out over the banks. Into the long grass. Into the bottom of the garden. A sheet of brown water. Rising. Slow. Filling the hollow between the stone and the far cottage. Pooling.

Real water. Moving water. Live.

Held in by a dam of stillness on one side, and coming in from the hills on the other.

---

"*It's a pond*," said Elowen. In the doorway. Blankly. "*It's making a pond.*"

"*It's making a mirror*," said Rhosyn.

---

Seren looked at her.

Rhosyn was white. Whiter than the birches had been. She was holding the sill so hard her knuckles stood out.

"*The grey doesn't reflect*," said Rhosyn. "*Nothing does, out there. Have you noticed? I noticed in the Court. Nine hundred years. I used to kneel by the grey river and look for my face and there wasn't one. The grey drinks the light. Glass is just glass.*" She swallowed. "*Ysy couldn't come into the grey. Not into it. Not out there. There's nothing for her to come through.*"

"*Then*—" said Seren.

"*But that's not grey*," said Rhosyn.

She pointed. Out of the window. Down the slope.

At the water. Spreading. Brown. Live. Real. Rising in the bottom of the garden, in the hollow behind the dam of stillness. Settling. Going quiet. Going smooth. The way a pond goes smooth at evening when the wind drops.

Going flat.

Going bright.

---

The sun was going down behind the birch wood. Behind the grey. A picture of a sunset. No colour in it.

But in the pond—

In the pond, the sky was blue.

---

Seren stared at it.

At the water in the bottom of her garden. A sheet of it. Forty yards across. Round the grave. Round the two-yard ring of green, which stood up out of the middle of it now like a little island with a stone and some roses on it. Up to the edge of the stillness, where the stepping stones stood in their carved water.

Smooth. Bright. Real.

And in it, upside down, perfect, every leaf—the cottage. The blue door. The ramp. The green ring round it. The horn lantern in the west window, a speck of gold.

The only colour for a mile in any direction.

Reflected.

---

"*How far does it go*?" said Rhosyn. Very quietly. "*The stream. Upstream. Where does it come from?*"

"*Long Ridge*," said Seren. "*The springs. Then the Teeth.*"

"*Through Ashbourne*?"

Seren's hands were cold.

"*Past the Forge*," she said. "*It turns the mill. It runs under the bridge on the green. It goes past the Forge yard. Brannoc takes his quench-water from it. Lex—*" She stopped. "*Lex washed Lark's feet in it. On Sunday. In the yard. She likes to splash.*"

Rhosyn didn't say anything.

She didn't need to.

Real water. Live water. From the east, from the hills, from real ground, running down through the Vale, through Ashbourne, past the Forge, under the bridge, into the bottom of a garden at Harrow, and meeting the grey, and stopping, and going smooth.

One long thread of it. From the Forge to here. A road. A glass road. With a mirror at the end of it.

---

The light went.

The sun went down behind the grey birch wood. The sky went from a picture of evening to a picture of night. No stars. Grey country doesn't have stars. Just a flat dark like the inside of a closed book.

The pond went dark.

Except for one gold speck. Reflected. From the window.

Seren stood at the window with the lantern beside her on the sill and looked down at the gold speck in the black water. At her own lantern. Upside down. Very small.

She thought about Lex. Three miles away. At the Forge. With the door hung and unfinished and the King in the yard and the whole of Ashbourne coming to give lanterns. With Wren. With Lark.

With the stream running past the yard.

---

"*We have to tell him*," said Elowen.

"*Yes*," said Seren.

"*I'll ride*." Elowen was already reaching for her sword. "*Tonight. Now. Through the grey. If I'm fast—*"

"*You won't be*," said Rhosyn. "*Nothing's fast in the grey. You'll walk your horse into it and it'll stop and you'll stop and they'll find you in a hundred years, a knight on a horse in the middle of a field, like a statue in a park.*"

Elowen stopped.

"*Then how*?" she said.

Seren looked at the lantern.

Then she picked it up.

---

It was warm. It was always warm. Small. Light. Horn panes. Tin frame. A little handle. She turned it over in her hands and looked at the letters cut into the base.

*TOO LATE*.

She'd said it every morning. For a year and a half. At this window. Holding this lantern. *Too late, too late, too late*. She'd made it into a prayer. A lament. A thing to hold.

He'd cut it for her. Lex. The first night. Because she'd asked him to. Because she'd said, *cut what I'm feeling, love, I don't know how else to say it*, and he'd looked at her for a long time and then cut it, slowly, with the point of a nail, and given it to her.

And it had held the house anyway.

Not because of the words. Because he'd given it. Because somebody who loved her had made her a light, and put it in her hands, and said, *here*.

---

She found a hairpin.

In her hair. Where they always were. She took it out and straightened it between her finger and thumb and sat down in her chair with the lantern in her lap.

Rhosyn watched her. Elowen watched her.

Seren turned the lantern over. And under the letters Lex had cut, in the soft tin of the base, very slowly, with the point of the pin, in her own crooked hand that had never been any good at letters, she cut two more words.

It took a long time. The tin was harder than it looked. Her hands shook. The pin slipped twice and she cut her thumb and said a word that Wenna would have washed her mouth out for.

When she was done, she turned it the right way up and set it back on the sill.

*TOO LATE*.

*STILL HERE*.

---

The flame didn't change. It wasn't that kind of story.

But when Seren looked out of the window—

The green ring round the house was wider.

Not much. A yard. Maybe two. But wider. Out past the water-butt. Out past the bench. Down the path to the first flagstone of the slope.

And in the pond, the gold speck of her lantern's reflection was a little brighter.

---

"*You still can't get through to him*," said Rhosyn. Quietly.

"*No*," said Seren.

She stood up.

"*But I can keep the window*," said Seren Harrow. "*Somebody has to.*"

---

They sat up all night.

The three of them. Taking turns. At the window. Watching the pond.

It was very still. Very black. Very smooth. The water from the hills came down in the dark and spread and filled, slowly, silently, and the pond got a little bigger every hour. Lapping at the edge of the grey on one side. At the edge of the green on the other. At the foot of the ramp, by midnight. Black and flat and bright.

Nothing moved in it.

No face. No cold white light. No hand.

Just the gold speck. And the dark.

---

At dawn, Seren was at the window.

She'd made tea. Warmed the pot. One spoon each and one for the pot. Water just off the boil.

The sky went from a picture of night to a picture of morning. Grey. Flat. No birds.

The pond was still black.

Seren looked at it. And at the stepping stones beyond, in their carved water. And at her son's cottage on the far bank, grey, the colour of nothing, with the swing on the apple bough not swaying any more. Stopped. Mid-swing. Leaning a little, toward the east, toward the house, the way a swing leans when a child has just jumped off it.

She looked at it for a long time.

Then she looked back at the pond.

And there was a face in it.

---

Not white. Not cold.

Small. Round. Pink. With a crest of black hair sticking up at the back like a jackdaw's, and square determined eyebrows, and a mouth wide open with delight.

Upside down. Reflected. In the black water. As if somebody were standing on the bank at the foot of the ramp, at the very edge of the green, leaning over, looking in.

There was nobody on the bank.

---

Seren dropped the cup.

It didn't break. It fell on the rag rug and rolled.

She was at the door. On the ramp. On the path. At the edge of the green. At the edge of the water. On her knees. Looking down.

At the pond. Black. Smooth. Bright.

At her own face in it. Grey-haired. Freckled. White. Fifty-one. Upside down.

And beside it, in the water, where nobody was standing—

Her granddaughter. Twenty months old. In her nightshirt. With her fists clenched. Looking up out of the pond at Seren with an expression of enormous satisfaction.

As if she'd found a new way into Gan's house. And couldn't wait to show everyone.

---

"*Mmo-ning*," said the face in the water.

Seren heard it. Clearly. Not through the water. Through the air. Through the morning. From very far away. From three miles off, upstream. From a forge yard, where a small girl in a nightshirt had got out of the door before anyone was up, and gone down to the stream to splash, the way she did every morning, and leaned over the water, and seen—

Seen her Gan. In it.

And laughed.

And behind the small round face in the water—

Behind it. Below it. Far down. Where the black water went deeper than a garden pond had any right to go. Deeper than the stream. Deeper than the ground.

A light.

White. Cold. Clear.

Coming up.
