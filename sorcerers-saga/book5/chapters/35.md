# The Last No

He had held the tongs.

That was the first thing Oszric remembered. Before the pool. Before the drinking. Before the grey and the white road and the nine hundred years. He'd been nine, and his big brother had been nineteen, and their father had been dying in the room upstairs, slowly, the wasting kind, the kind where you watch someone get thinner every morning like a candle burning down. And Aurelian had gone down to the forge in the night because he couldn't sleep, and Oszric had followed him because he couldn't either.

*Hold these*, Aurelian had said. And put the tongs in his hands. *Don't let go. Whatever happens. Hold on.*

He'd held on.

He'd been holding on ever since.

---

The crown was too small for him.

He could feel it. Sitting on the top of his head like a ring on a thumb. Silver-grey. Cold. Thin. It didn't go gold. Nothing had gone gold since the Heart—not the white stone, not the grass of the green road, not the petal Rosie was holding for him now in her bound hand—but he'd thought, perhaps, the crown might. It had been his little sister's. It had been in the family.

It sat there. Grey. Itself.

*It's got one more no in it*, the Harrow woman had said.

He hoped so. He didn't have any of his own.

---

"*Walk me to the door*," he said.

Rosie took his arm.

She didn't hold it. She couldn't. Her hands were still bound up in linen from the glass, and anyway it was a big arm, a gold arm, the size of a small tree. She just put her bandaged hand on it. Lightly. Above the elbow. The way you'd lay your hand on the arm of someone very old who might need it on the stairs.

They walked.

---

It was a few yards. From the bottom of the slope to the threshold. Ten steps. Twelve. He'd walked two hundred and forty miles from the Veil to the Harrow woman's door in the winter, and two hundred more on the green road, and nine hundred years before that up and down the white road with nine thousand people behind him.

These were the longest steps he had ever taken.

---

The door was open.

The Doorkeeper stood in it. The tall one. The dark one. The one who'd been a monster once, they said, and had a hungry Well in his chest, and talked to the dead. He stood in the open door with one hand on the frame and one on the blue wood, and behind him, the room.

Soft. Dark. Deep. Full of stars. With shapes moving in the gaps between them, going in, going on. And far down, at the bottom, a long way off, a pool. Green grass round it. Gold lanterns in a ring.

And people standing in the grass.

Gold.

His.

---

He stopped on the threshold.

Not on it. In front of it. On the grass. With the grey stone a foot in front of his toes. And the Ferryman sitting on it, with his back against the frame, and his long oar across his knees, and his eyes closed in the last of the evening sun.

Oszric looked down at him.

The Ferryman didn't open his eyes.

"*You're not on my list*," said the Ferryman. Quietly. Without moving. "*You've never been on my list. I told you that. On the shingle. Nine hundred years ago. When I rowed you out.*"

"*I remember*," said Oszric.

"*That was your punishment*," said the Ferryman. "*Not mine. You drank the Well of endings. You can't have one.*"

"*I know*," said Oszric. "*I've had a long time to think about it.*"

The Ferryman was silent.

Then he opened his eyes. Grey. The colour of water over stones in the dark. And looked up. At the gold king standing over him. At the little silver-grey crown on his head.

He looked at the crown for a long time.

"*Ah*," said the Ferryman.

---

Oszric looked past him.

Through the door. Down through the soft dark. To the pool. To the green grass. To the gold people standing in it, three hundred and some, each with a column of grey dust in front of them, bending.

He could feel them.

He'd always been able to feel them. Every one he'd ever kept. It was the hold. The one hand. Nine thousand threads, gold, running out of his chest to every person he'd ever touched or breathed on or looked at with his gold eyes and thought *no, not you, I can't bear it, stay*. He felt them the way you feel your own fingers. The way you feel your own heart.

Three hundred and some at the pool.

And the rest. All over the world.

---

He felt them now. All of them. He let himself.

On the roads. In the fields. In the long grass at the edges of villages where they'd stopped walking, because they'd forgotten where home was. Standing. Humming. *Home*.

At a gate in the east wall of the Court, far out in the white, a man in a Keeper's coat with his staff held crosswise. Gold. Kept in the winter when he wouldn't let a king's household through. *Roole*. Oszric had never learned his first name.

In a barley field on a hill above a village called Westerling, a young man with his hand up. Gold. Kept in the spring when he stood between Orrin's dust and his mother. And beside him, on the wall, an old woman with a lantern. Sitting. Waiting.

At the edge of the camp on the rise behind him. A big grey-haired man in a leather apron, with gold to the first knuckle on every finger of his left hand. A young woman beside him, broad-shouldered, with a smith's hammer on her back and her right hand gold to the wrist. The smith's wife. Two gold fingers and a gold thumb.

The Doorkeeper. Standing in the door. With a gold coin on the back of his left hand, where a pinch of dust had landed in the winter on the green at Ashbourne.

At the pool, five hundred miles off—a big man with a staff and his right arm gold to the elbow.

In a little room off the east stair of a keep in the mountains, an old woman in a chair under a window. Gold. With her eyes moving. Who'd said *no, thank you*, very faintly, *I don't want to be kept*, *I want to go and see my boy*. And her husband had said yes for her. Because he couldn't bear it.

*Bet*. Oszric remembered her name. Orrin had told him.

He'd never met her. He'd kept her anyway.

---

And at the pool. In the grass. Beside a stone house with a lantern in the window. A little gold girl. Eight. Barefoot. In a kitchen smock with a burn-mark on the hem.

Holding the hand of a boy who didn't talk.

With a grey column of dust beside her. Bending over her. The way a woman bends over a cot.

---

Oszric shut his eyes.

---

"*I can't*," he said.

Very quietly. To Rosie. To nobody.

"*I've tried*," he said. "*Every night. Since the smith's old man. Since Brannoc. I sat in the dark with the petal in my hand and I tried. One finger. Just one. Just the little one. Just to see.*" His voice was shaking. "*And it wasn't one finger. It was all of them. All at once. I felt them start to go. Like sand. Like Maudie on the floor of the hall. Nine thousand of them. Frightened. Cold.*"

"*I know*," said Rosie.

"*And I grabbed it back*," said Oszric. "*Every time. I couldn't help it. It's not a thing I do. It's a thing I am.*" He opened his eyes. "*I've been holding on since I was nine years old, Rosie. Since Aurelian put the tongs in my hands. I don't know how to let go. I've never known.*"

Rosie didn't say anything.

She just stood beside him. With her bandaged hand on his gold arm. Lightly. Above the elbow.

---

"*Oz*," she said, after a while.

"*Yes*."

"*Do you remember Hes in the garden*?"

He didn't answer. He didn't need to.

"*The night after the pool*," said Rosie. "*After we drank. All of us. Mae and Ysy and Cad and you and me. And Hes didn't.*" Her voice was very soft. "*We were all in the hall. Shaking. Burning. Full of it. And Hes went out into the garden. And sat in the door. All night.*"

"*In case one of us wanted to come out*," said Oszric.

"*In case one of us wanted to come out*," said Rosie.

She looked up at the crown. On his head. Small. Silver-grey. Too small.

"*She's still sitting there*," said Rhosyn. "*Oz. In the door. That's what that is. That's all it is. It's Hes, in the garden door, all night, saying no.*" She swallowed. "*Not no to you. No to the hall. No to the drinking. No to the keeping.*" Her bound hand tightened on his arm. "*You don't have to say it yourself. You never could. She'll say it for you. That's what she was waiting for.*"

---

He looked at the door.

The open door. Blue. Blooming. Gold as a lit window. With the Doorkeeper standing in it, and the Ferryman sitting on its step, and the soft dark behind it full of stars, and his household at the bottom, in the grass, waiting.

The door at the edge of the world.

Not the garden door. But a door.

And somebody in it. All night. In case.

---

"*All right*," said Oszric.

He took Rosie's bandaged hand off his arm. Gently. And held it. In his huge gold one. Carefully. So as not to hurt the cuts.

"*Don't let go*," he said.

"*I won't*," said Rosie. "*Silly. I never have.*"

---

He stepped onto the threshold.

---

The Ferryman moved his oar.

Just a little. Out of the way. So the King's foot could come down on the grey stone beside him without touching it. The way you'd move your stick out of the way for someone coming up the stairs.

The Stone hummed.

It knew him. Oszric felt it. The Ashbourne Stone, that had looked at every child for four hundred years and said what they were. It looked at him now. Up. Through the soles of his gold feet. And said what he was.

He didn't need a panel. He'd never had one. The Crowned were older than the net.

But he knew what it said.

---

*KEEPER*.

---

The crown on his head went cold.

---

Not cold like the glass. Not cold like the grey. Cold like a garden at night in early spring, with dew on the grass and the stars out and the door of the hall open behind you, spilling gold light onto the path. Cold like a girl of seventeen sitting on a doorstep in her nightdress with her arms round her knees, shivering, not going in.

Cold like *no*.

---

*No*, said the crown.

---

It didn't say it to him. It didn't say it out loud. It said it the way the net said things. In the hum. In the threshold. In the gold threads running out of Oszric's chest to nine thousand people all over the world.

It said it down every one of them.

*No*.

*Not you*.

*Not ever*.

---

He felt the hold break.

---

It wasn't a letting go. He'd been right about that. He'd never have been able to let go. It wasn't in him.

It was the hand being opened *for* him.

Gently. Finger by finger. All at once. The way someone opens the fist of a child who's holding something he shouldn't—a coal, a knife, a bee—and won't let go, and can't, because he's frightened. You don't pull. You don't pry. You just put your hand over his and say *no, love*, and hold it, and wait, until the fingers open on their own.

His opened.

All nine thousand.

---

He felt them go.

---

Roole, at the east gate, in the white. The gold going off him like frost off a window in the sun. Not coming apart. He was young. He'd only been kept since the winter. The gold went, and underneath it was a man. Grey-haired. Grim-mouthed. In a Keeper's coat. Blinking. With his staff in his hands held crosswise, still, across a gate that nobody was trying to come through any more.

He lowered it.

Oszric felt him lower it.

---

Col Ashby, in the barley, on the hill above Westerling. The gold going. A young man with his hand up. Standing in the green barley—green, it was green, the grey had gone off that hillside in the spring when his mother sat down beside him with a lantern—and lowering his hand. Slowly. And turning round. And seeing an old woman on the wall of the field with a lantern in her lap.

*Mam*, said Col Ashby.

*There you are*, said Nan.

---

Brannoc Holt, on the rise. The gold going off his fingertips. Five of them. Like five little candle flames going out. Leaving fingers. Old. Scarred. Burned at the tips from forty-five years at the forge.

He looked down at them. Then he put them in his mouth, the way a boy does when he's caught them in a door, and turned away so nobody would see his face.

---

Tilda. Her hand.

The smith's wife. Two fingers and a thumb.

Oszric felt her go still. Felt her look down. Felt her flex them. Pink. Stiff. Cold. Alive. Felt her make a fist. Open it. Make a fist.

Felt her start to cry, very quietly, with her face in the smith's shoulder.

---

The Doorkeeper.

He was standing right there. In the door. A yard away. Oszric felt the gold coin on the back of his left hand go—just go, like a breath off glass—and the Doorkeeper look down at the place where it had been, and then up. At Oszric. With his dark eyes.

He didn't say anything.

He nodded. Very slightly. The way you nod to a man across a room who has done something hard.

---

And in a little room off the east stair of a keep in the mountains—

Bet Dale.

The gold went off her like a breath off a window. And under it, an old woman. Thin. Grey. In a chair under a window. With her eyes moving, and then not just her eyes. Her hands. Her mouth. Her chest. In. Catch. Silence. In again.

An old man on a stool beside the chair, with his shepherd's crook against the wall, lifted his head.

*Fen*, said Bet Dale. Very faintly.

*I'm sorry*, said the old shepherd. *I'm sorry, love. I'm sorry. I couldn't bear it.*

*I know*. A breath. *I know you couldn't*. Her thin hand found his hair. *It's all right. I'm going to see our boy now.*

In. Catch. Silence.

Not again.

Oszric felt her go. Not like sand. Not frightened. The way a candle goes out at the end of a long evening when it's burned all the way down.

He had kept her for a year and a half. He felt her go, and was glad.

---

And the others.

The long ones. The old ones.

---

They went like sand.

He'd known they would. He'd felt it every night in the dark, practising. Nine hundred years is a long time. Longer than a body is built for. Longer than bones. They'd been dead—they should have been dead—since before the Lattice. Since before the net. Since the first year in the grey. And the only thing holding them together was him. His hand. His gold. His *no, not you, I can't bear it, stay*.

And now there was another *no*. Older. Kinder. Saying *yes, you, you can go, it's all right*.

They went.

---

All over the world.

On roads. In fields. In the long grass at the edges of villages where they'd stopped walking. Nine thousand gold people. The gold going off them like frost. And under it—grey. Dust. Fine. Pale. Like flour. Like ash. Like sand.

Coming apart.

He felt the first of them start to be frightened.

A laundry maid, on a road in Vhessa, alone. Looking down at her hands. Watching them go grey. Watching the fingers of them drift away on the wind like smoke. Opening her mouth to scream—

And something came out of the door.

---

He felt it go past him.

Out of the room. Out of the soft dark behind the Doorkeeper. Past the Ferryman on his step. Past Oszric and Rosie on the threshold. Out into the world. Fast. Silent. A wind that wasn't a wind.

The dead.

Thousands. Grey. The ones who'd been walking upriver toward Mal for months. The ones in the stars in the soft dark of his room. The ones who'd stood round six lanterns in a grey square in the Drowned Country for years because it was warm. The bread-woman's kind. The ones who'd come apart in the first year in the grey and been walked past by a crying gold king and never kept.

They came out of the door like a breath out of a chest.

And went to their own.

---

The laundry maid on the road in Vhessa didn't scream.

A grey column came down out of the sky in front of her. Thin. Small. An old woman's height. And bent. Over the maid. Over her greying hands.

*Gran*, said the laundry maid.

And stopped being frightened.

---

Everywhere.

A soldier on one knee in a field with his hands going to dust, and a small grey column no taller than his shoulder coming down to meet him. A steward in a coat with a chain on it, and a woman's dust, fifty years married. A gardener. A footman. A harper—Leolin, his harper, who'd played for him at dinner every night for a hundred years before the pool—sitting on a stone by a road in the Sunreach with his gold harp in his lap going grey, and a column of dust coming down beside him that had been a girl he'd loved once and never told.

*Nobody should come apart on their own*, the boy had written. *Not again*.

Nobody did.

---

And at the pool.

---

He felt it best at the pool. Because it was closest. Because it was right there, at the bottom of the soft dark, through the door. He could see it.

Three hundred and some gold people in the grass. Going grey. All at once. And three hundred and some grey columns of dust in front of them, bending, closer, closer, until the dust of the living and the dust of the dead were touching. Mixing. A quarter of an inch, and then nothing. Holding.

At last.

Holding.

---

And in the grass by the stone house, a little gold girl. With her hand in a boy's.

The gold going off her.

Not like frost. Like the last of the snow off a roof in March. Slowly. Patches. Her hair first. Then her face. Then her hands. Pink, for a moment, as the gold went—pink, a child's skin, with a white line across one fingertip where she'd burned it on a spit-iron nine hundred and twelve years ago—and then, under the pink, grey.

She was looking at her mother.

Oszric felt her look. He felt her, for the last moment, before she stopped being kept and stopped being his. A small bright fierce thing. Eight years old.

Not frightened.

*Mam*, said Ilda. *I can feel it. Mam. I can feel it.*

*What is it, love*?

*I'm happy*, said Ilda. *That's happy. Isn't it?*

---

And then she wasn't his.

---

He was standing on the threshold.

He didn't know how long it had been. A moment. An hour. He was standing on the grey stone with Rosie's bandaged hand in his and Hes's crown on his head, and he was empty.

The threads were gone.

All of them. Nine thousand gold threads running out of his chest to every person he'd ever kept. Gone. Cut. Opened. He reached for them, out of nine hundred years of habit, the way you reach for a stair in the dark that isn't there.

Nothing.

His hands were empty.

---

No.

Not empty.

He looked down.

At his free hand. The left. Huge. Gold. Open. Palm up. Where the threads had been.

There was still something in it.

---

It was in him. Under the gold. Under his skin. In his chest, where the threads had run from. A warmth. A weight. A light.

The thing he'd drunk.

The first. Before Mae. Before Ysy and Cad and Rosie. He'd been first to the pool. The eldest. The one who'd held the tongs. He'd knelt on the edge of a pool in a garden at the bottom of the world with his father dead upstairs and put his face in the water and *drunk*.

Endings. That was what the seventh Well was. Room. The space where something *was*. The hole a thing leaves when it's gone. He'd drunk the endings out of the world so nothing he loved would ever end.

And it was still in him.

It had been in him for nine hundred years. Every ending he'd ever stopped. Every death he'd ever kept. Swallowed. Held. Gold.

It was the last thing he was holding.

---

"*Oz*," said Rosie.

He looked at her.

"*It's his*," said Rosie. Very gently. "*It was always his. Like mine was. Like Ysy's.*" She lifted the small linen packet in her other hand. The glass dust. "*Pour it back.*"

---

He'd watched her do it.

In the winter. At the pool. A woman with thorns in her hair and blood on her hands, kneeling on the rim, holding the Harrow woman's hand. Pouring back nine hundred years of growing things. Going from Crowned to mortal in a breath. Forty. Tired. Alive.

He hadn't understood how.

He understood now. Standing on the threshold. With his hands open.

You didn't pour it. You couldn't pour it, any more than you could let go. It wasn't in you to do.

You just stopped holding it.

---

He stopped holding it.

---

It went out of him like a breath.

Down. Through the soles of his feet. Into the threshold. Through the grey stone that knew what he was. Through the frame and the hinges and the blue door. Into the room behind it. Into the soft dark. Into the space in front of things.

Into Mal.

---

*[...oh...]* said the seventh Well.

All round them. Soft. Deep. Suddenly huge. Like the sound in a well when you drop a stone and listen and it doesn't land, it goes on falling, down and down, and then you realise there's no bottom, there was never a bottom, the well goes all the way down through the world and out the other side into the stars.

*[...oh...that's...]*

The room behind the door got bigger.

Oszric saw it. Everyone did. The soft dark stretching. Deepening. The stars in it going further and further apart. The shapes between them moving faster. Going in. Going on. And at the bottom, a long way down, the pool—

The pool *opened*.

Not wider. Deeper. The water in it going dark. Clear. Bottomless. Like looking down a well into the sky. Like looking into an eye.

*[...that's the last of me...]* said Mal. Wonderingly. *[...that's the first bit...the oldest...the bit he took first...]* A pause. *[...I remember...I remember the garden...]*

---

The gold went off him.

---

He didn't feel it go. He saw it. In Rosie's face. Looking up at him. Her eyes going wide. Her mouth opening.

He looked down at his hands.

They were grey.

---

Not dust. Not yet. Skin. Old skin. Grey and thin and spotted, the way his father's hands had gone at the end. Blue veins. Knuckles like knots in a rope. A man's hands. A very old man's hands. Shaking.

He was small.

He'd been the size of a small cart for nine hundred years. Gold. Enormous. Taking up the whole of a doorway. He wasn't now. He was a man. An old man. Bent. Thin. In a gold robe that had gone grey and hung off him like a sail with no wind. With a little silver-grey crown on his head that fitted.

It fitted.

---

He looked at Rosie.

She was crying.

"*You look like Da*," she said.

He tried to say something. His voice came out cracked. Thin. An old man's voice.

"*I always did*," said Oszric. "*Round the eyes.*"

---

The Ferryman stood up.

Slowly. With his oar. Off the threshold step. He stood up beside them, very tall, grey, hooded, with the last of the evening sun behind him, and looked down at the old man in the grey robe with the little crown on his head.

He looked at him for a long time.

Then he reached into his robe. And took out something small. A scrap. Grey. Folded. Like a page torn out of a very old book.

He unfolded it.

Oszric couldn't see what was written on it. It was too small and his eyes were old now, eyes that had never had to be old before, and the light was going.

The Ferryman took a stub of something from his robe. Grey. Like a pencil. Like a burnt stick.

And wrote.

One word. Small. At the bottom of the list.

---

"*Now you're on my list*," said the Ferryman.

---

Oszric looked up at him.

He'd thought he'd be frightened. He'd been frightened of this for nine hundred years. More than of anything. More than of the grey or the glass or the smith's fire or the little one saying no. *An ending*. The one thing he'd drunk the whole world dry to stop.

He wasn't frightened.

He was tired. And old. And empty. And light. Lighter than he'd ever been. As if he'd put something down that he'd been carrying since he was nine.

"*Good*," said Oszric.

---

The Ferryman looked at him a moment longer.

Then—so slightly that Oszric nearly missed it—he inclined his hooded head. The way you'd nod to a man across a room.

And sat back down on the step.

"*In your own time*," said the Ferryman. And closed his eyes. "*I'm not going anywhere.*"

---

"*Rosie*," said Oszric.

"*Yes, Oz*."

"*Walk me in*?"

She didn't answer. She didn't need to. She took his arm. His thin grey old arm. Properly, this time. Her bandaged hand round it. Tight.

He looked back.

Once. Over his shoulder. The way the smith's father had, twice, on a stair. At the slope. At the four hundred on the rise with their lanterns burning straight up in the dusk. At the smith. At the smith's wife, with her pink fingers round her child. At the Harrow woman with her clay lamp. At the old blacksmith with his fingertips in his mouth.

At the little one. Asleep on her father's shoulder. Blue to the elbow.

She'd said no to him. Twice. In a yard and in a forge. Square eyebrows. Fists.

She was the only one in nine hundred years who'd ever said it to his face.

"*Thank you*," said Oszric. Very quietly. To her. To all of them.

And turned. And went through the door.

---

They walked down through the room.

He and Rosie. Arm in arm. Through the soft dark. Between the stars. With the shapes of the dead going past them on either side, slow, unhurried, the way people walk through a house at night from room to room.

It wasn't far. Or it was very far. He couldn't tell. It didn't matter.

Behind them, he heard something. A soft dry sound. Like dust blowing.

He looked back.

---

Orrin.

His steward. Grey. Dust. A man made of dust, the way he'd always been, since the first year in the grey when Oszric had held his hand on the floor of the hall and not been able to keep him, quite—only half—only enough for him to stay as dust and serve. Courteous. Faithful. Nine hundred years.

He'd been standing at the back of the crowd on the rise. Oszric hadn't seen him. He hadn't looked.

He was coming down through the room after them. A few steps behind. With his dusty hands folded in front of him. The way a steward walks behind his master, close enough to be called, far enough not to intrude.

"*Orrin*," said Oszric. "*You don't have to.*"

"*I know, Majesty*," said Orrin.

He kept walking.

"*I'm not your Majesty*," said Oszric. "*Not now. Look at me. I'm an old man in a dressing gown.*"

Orrin looked at him. With his dust face. With the faint courteous smile that had never once changed in nine hundred years.

"*Yes, sir*," said Orrin. And kept walking.

---

The pool was there.

At the bottom of the dark. Green grass. Gold lanterns, five hundred and fifty-six, in a ring. A stone house on the slope with a lantern in the window. And all round the rim, in the grass, grey dust. Hundreds of little heaps of it. Pale. Fine. Lying in pairs. Each pair touching. Where three hundred gold people and three hundred grey ones had held each other at last, and come apart together, and lay in the grass side by side like flour spilled on a table.

Waiting.

And a boy on the rim. With his knees drawn up. Fifteen. Thin. With a stub of pencil behind his ear and his face wet.

He looked up as they came down out of the dark. At the old man in the grey robe. At the thorn-woman. At the dust-steward behind.

He didn't say anything.

He moved over. On the rim. To make room.

---

Oszric sat down.

His knees cracked. Both of them. His back. His neck. Nine hundred years of not bending, and they'd found out about it all at once.

He sat on the rim of the pool beside the boy who didn't talk and looked down into the water.

It was dark. Clear. Bottomless. Like looking into an eye.

No reflection. There never had been, in Mal's pool. It was a hollow. It showed you nothing.

He looked into it anyway.

---

Rosie knelt beside him. On the grass. With the linen packet.

She unfolded it. Slowly. Carefully. On her knees. The glass dust inside, pale, glittering, fine as frost. She held it over the water.

"*Ysy*," she said. "*Here. Go on. Cad's in there. And Mae. And Hes, a bit, I think. And all the cousins.*" Her voice was steady. "*Go on, love. Go and see everyone.*"

She tipped the linen.

The dust went down into the pool like frost going into a river. Glittering. Then gone.

---

*[...hello, Ysy...]* said the water. Very softly. *[...I can see you...]*

---

Rosie sat back on her heels. And let the linen fall in her lap. And didn't say anything.

Oszric reached into his robe.

Into the pocket, over his heart, where he'd carried it since the night he woke. Thin. Silver. Wire. Twisted. A little crown a girl had made herself, nine hundred years ago, out of wire from the palace gardens, because she was the second daughter and there wasn't one for her.

*Mae's*.

He held it over the water.

"*There*," he said. To the pool. To his sister. "*You were always cross nobody gave you one.*"

He let it go.

---

Then he took off Hes's crown.

---

It came off easily. It fitted now. It came off his old grey head the way a hat comes off at the end of a long day.

He held it in his lap. Small. Silver-grey. Seven points. Thin. Plain.

"*Thank you*," he said. To it. "*For sitting in the door.*"

He gave it to Rosie.

She looked at it. Then at him.

"*Give it back to the Harrow woman*," said Oszric. "*Or the little one. It's theirs. It was always theirs.*" He looked at it in her bandaged hands. "*I don't think it's got any more no in it. I think that was the last.*"

"*It'll have a yes, then*," said Rosie. "*Next.*"

He smiled. It hurt his face. He'd forgotten how.

"*Probably*," said Oszric.

---

He looked down into the pool for a long time.

Then he looked at the boy beside him. The boy who didn't talk. Who'd written down three hundred and eight names on scraps of paper and kept them in his pocket. Who'd remembered Maudie.

"*Will you hold my hand*?" said the Gilded King. "*Just for a bit. I don't want to do it on my own.*"

The boy looked at him.

Then he held out his hand.

---

It was small and thin and warm. Ink on the fingers. Callused from the pencil.

Oszric took it in his old grey one.

On his other side, Rosie took the other.

And behind him, on the grass, very quietly, Orrin sat down. Folded his dusty hands. And waited.

---

He went the way the dead go.

Not like sand. Not frightened. Not alone. Gently. The way the snow comes off a branch when the sun gets to it. The way a fire goes down in a hearth when the night's over and nobody's going to bank it.

He sat on the rim of the pool with a boy holding one hand and his sister holding the other, and the lanterns burning gold round him in the long grass, and his household lying in pairs on the grass behind him, all of them, waiting—

And came apart.

Grey. Fine. Like flour.

And the last thing he felt, before there wasn't anything to feel it with, was the boy's hand. Not letting go.

Holding on.

He'd been holding on since he was nine years old.

It was nice, at the end, to have someone hold on for him.
