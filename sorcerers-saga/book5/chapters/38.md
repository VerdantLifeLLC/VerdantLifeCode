# Lantern Night

Wren missed the tree.

Not the chalk. The *tree*. The whole birch, white and black, as wide as her two hands spread. Twenty paces. She drew, with her left hand on the grip—her own left hand, pink, stiff, five fingers that could feel the wood again and had forgotten what to do about it—and her right hand at her cheek, and held, and loosed.

The arrow went past the birch on the left by a yard and vanished into the bracken.

Wren lowered the bow.

She looked at the place where the arrow had gone. For a long time.

Then she laughed.

---

It was the morning of Lantern Night. Cold. Clear. The birch wood behind the cottage at Harrow gone gold and brown and red, leaves coming down in the wind like sparks, the ground thick with them. Her breath smoking. The chalk circle on the birch, the size of a plate, freshly drawn, untouched.

Forty arrows in the quiver.

She'd shot nine. None in the tree.

Last winter, with the wrong hand, shaking, she'd put eleven in the tree out of forty and two in the chalk, and gone in and cried in the woodshed where nobody could see.

She wasn't crying now.

She nocked the tenth.

*It carries*, she thought. *It doesn't have to carry away.*

She drew. Held. Loosed.

The arrow hit the birch. A hand's width outside the chalk. *Thock*. And stood there. Quivering.

Wren looked at it.

"*Good*," she said. Out loud. To nobody. To the wood.

And nocked the eleventh.

---

Lark found her at the twentieth.

Came through the gap in the hedge at the bottom of the wood on her hands and knees, the way she always did, no matter how many times she'd been told. Stood up on the other side. Two years old tonight. Nearly. In a red wool coat that Seren had knitted, too big, and boots, and Hes's crown round the neck of her rag rabbit, which she carried everywhere by one ear.

She looked at the birch. At the arrows in it. Four. And the one in the chalk.

Then at the bracken behind it. Where the other fifteen had gone.

"*Mam*," said Lark. Very seriously. "*Missed.*"

"*Yes*," said Wren. "*Lots.*"

Lark considered this.

"*Again*," she said. Encouragingly.

---

They came in the afternoon.

Wren saw them from the top of the wood. A shape in the sky over the west. Blue. Big. Getting bigger. Wings beating so slow and so strong she felt the air shift in the birch leaves before she heard it.

Ashka.

The storm-mother's daughter. Grown again since the spring, since the First Breath. She came down out of the cold blue sky over Harrow in a long slow spiral, and the leaves went up off the ground in a whirl round her, and she landed in the meadow on the far side of the stream with a thump that shook the stepping stones.

And on her back—

Antho. In his old coat. With the bone flute stuck through his belt. Climbing down stiffly, the way you climb down after six hours in the cold.

And Seraphine. Wrapped in three cloaks and a blanket. With her horn spectacles fogged up. And a bundle in her arms. Small. Wrapped. Wailing.

---

"*He hates flying*," said Seraphine. At the stream. Handing the bundle across the stepping stones to Seren, who'd come out of her cottage at a run with her apron still on. "*He hated it for six hours. He has very strong opinions. He gets them from his father.*"

"*I don't have strong opinions*," said Antho.

"*You have strong opinions about moths*."

"*Everyone has strong opinions about moths*."

Seren wasn't listening.

She was standing on the bank of the stream with the bundle in her arms. Unwrapping it. Very gently. A fold of blanket. Another. Until she could see.

A face. Red. Furious. Crumpled. Two months old. With a shock of black hair and a mouth wide open, yelling at the whole of the world as if it had a complaint to make.

And ears.

Wren saw Seren see the ears.

They stuck out. A little. At the top. The way an old man's had, in a chair by a window, who'd said good morning every day for a year and a half to a woman who didn't know him.

"*Oh*," said Seren.

She sat down on the bank of the stream. Right there. In the leaves. With her grandson in her lap. And put her face down close to his.

"*Hello, Arion*," said Seren Harrow.

The baby stopped yelling.

He looked up at her. With his dark furious eyes. For a long time.

Then he sneezed.

---

The Doorkeeper had left the door with Roole.

"*He offered*," said Antho. At the kitchen table. With a mug of tea in his hands and his boots off and his feet on the fender. "*He said he'd had practice. Standing at gates. He said one night wouldn't kill him.*" He drank. "*He said, if anybody knocks, I'll ask them their business. I said, they're dead, Roole. He said, that's no excuse for bad manners.*"

Lex laughed. At the hearth. With the poker.

Wren watched him laugh.

He'd been laughing more. Since the summer. Since the green road. Not all the time—he'd always been a quiet man, a man who said three words where other people said thirty—but more. At things. At Lark. At Brannoc's ale, which was terrible. At Ember.

She watched him stop laughing and look at the fire.

He did that too. Every evening. Every morning. A look. Quick. Not long. The way you look at the door of a room where someone's sleeping, to see if they're awake yet.

The fire crackled. Gold. Comfortable.

*[Is that the Doorkeeper*?] said Ember. Interested. *[The one with the strong opinions about moths?]*

Antho looked at the hearth.

Then he looked at Lex.

Lex didn't look back. He was looking at the fire.

"*Yes*," said Antho. Carefully. "*That's me.*"

*[I've read a great deal about you*,] said Ember. *[In the smith's mother's notes. You were a monster once.]*

"*Once*."

*[And then you weren't*.] A pause. *[I'd like to hear about that. For the book. If you have time.]*

Antho put his mug down.

"*I've got all night*," he said. Gently. "*It's Lantern Night.*"

---

They lit the lanterns at dusk.

---

Every window at Harrow. Both cottages. The way they had on the night Lark was born, two years ago, when the whole of the Vale had come up the river path with lanterns and set them in every window and on every post and along the garden wall, and Wren had lain in Seren's bed with a small furious red thing on her chest yelling at the world, and looked out of the window at all the light.

They did it again.

Lex had made new ones. He'd been making them all autumn. In the evenings. At the Forge. Tin and horn. One for every window. And he'd cut a word in the base of every one, the way he always did, with the point of a nail.

Wren went round with Lark and read them. Lark couldn't read. She liked the shapes.

*HERE*. That was the east window. His. The one that had been empty since he gave the last one to Nan Ashby.

*HOME*. The kitchen.

*STAY*. Over the door.

*MORE*. That one was Lark's. In the little window of the loft where she slept now, in a real bed, with a rail. Lex had said it was the only word he could think of that she'd say yes to.

And on the west sill of Seren's cottage, across the stream, the old horn lantern. With two lines cut in the base. One in a smith's square careful hand. One in a woman's crooked one.

*TOO LATE*.

*STILL HERE*.

---

They came up the river path at dark.

The Vale. The way they'd come two years ago. But more. Brannoc, with Tilda, with a basket of something under a cloth that smelled of burned sugar. Kestrel, with Tamsin. Teo, with Barnaby, who had a new jar. Hollis Penn, with a loaf the size of a cartwheel. Elowen, in her mail, because she said it was a feast day and she'd be properly dressed. Davy Pruett and his father and his mother and the new baby. Jem Tolley, with a jar of honey. Old Mag's neighbour, with her great-grandmother's iron lantern with the cracked pane, which she'd carried two hundred miles to the end of the world and two hundred back, and wasn't going to leave at home for this.

Nan Ashby, with Col. Col was carrying the HERE lantern. Nan said he carried it everywhere.

And Pim.

He'd been at Harrow since August. He slept in Seren's loft and helped in the Forge and said perhaps eleven words a week. He came up the path with the others with a lantern in each hand. Plain. Tin. One of them was the one from the Coldwater house window. *HOME*. Hask had sent it. With a note. *For the little one. From the pool. Rosie says happy birthday. Ivy says don't eat coal.*

The other one, he gave to Lark.

It was very small. Clumsy. The tin bent in places. The soldering lumpy. Somebody had made it who had never made a lantern before and had only seen it done.

There was a word cut in the base. Very carefully. In a small neat hand.

*ILDA*.

---

Lark held it.

She didn't know what it said. She held it in both hands, with the rag rabbit tucked under her arm and Hes's crown sliding down its neck, and looked at the light in it. Small. Gold. Burning on nothing.

Then she looked up at Pim.

"*Yes*," said Lark.

Pim went red to the ears.

He didn't say anything. He never did, if he could help it.

---

They ate in the garden.

Tables from both cottages carried out onto the grass by the stream. Benches. Barrels. Lanterns on everything. The roses on the grave still flowering, dark red, nearly black, in October, as if it were midsummer. Rosie's roses. Wren thought they always would.

Hollis cut the cartwheel loaf. Brannoc uncovered the basket, which turned out to be a cake, of a kind, which had been on fire at least once. Tilda said it was meant to look like that. Nobody believed her. Everybody ate it.

And at the end, when the cake was gone and the honey was gone and the cartwheel was mostly gone, and it was full dark, and cold, and the lanterns were the only light for miles—

Antho took the bone flute out of his belt.

---

He didn't say anything. He just stood up. At the end of the table. By the stream. And put the flute to his lips.

And played the Lantern Song.

---

Wren knew it. Everyone knew it. Every child in Caldor had had it sung over their cot. It was the first song anyone ever learned and the last one anyone ever forgot. Old Mag had sung it on her last night with a gold king's hand in hers. Seren had sung it into the dark for thirteen years.

*If the dark comes calling, love, don't you fear*—

Seren started singing.

Flat. On the high note. The way she always was. The way Antho said she always had been, the way Arion had said, the way Old Mag had said. Seren Harrow couldn't hold the high note in the Lantern Song to save her life, and had never once in fifty-one years let it stop her.

*There's a light in the window, and I'm right here*—

Brannoc joined in. Gruffly. Off the note entirely. Then Tilda. Then Kestrel. Then the whole garden. Fifty voices. More. In the cold. In the dark. By the stream. With the lanterns burning in every window and the roses on the grave.

*And if you go walking where I can't see*—

Wren sang it too.

She'd never had much of a voice. She sang anyway. With Lark on her knee, and Lex's arm round her, and her pink fingers in her daughter's hair.

*Leave a light in the window, love, and come home to me*.

---

The fire was listening.

Wren saw it.

Through the kitchen window. Through the open door. In the hearth. Where Ember was. The flame had gone very still. Not crackling. Not commenting. Not taking notes for the book. Just—still. Listening. Leaning a little toward the door. Toward the garden. Toward the singing.

The way the flames had leaned toward Lark, the night the Veil fell.

Lex hadn't seen. Lex was singing, badly, with his eyes shut, with his arm round her.

Wren didn't tell him.

---

Lark fell asleep at midnight.

On Wren's knee. In the garden. With the ILDA lantern in her lap and her rabbit under her arm and her cheek on Wren's chest. Two years old. Tonight. Exactly. She'd been born at midnight on Lantern Night, with the whole of the Vale lit round her, yelling.

She didn't yell now. She just slept. Heavy. Warm. Breathing.

Wren sat there with her for a long time.

---

The guests went home at one. Down the river path with their lanterns. A long line of gold going away under the alders. Singing, some of them. Hollis Penn was singing something that wasn't the Lantern Song and wasn't fit for a feast day.

Antho and Seraphine went to bed in Seren's loft, with the baby between them. Pim slept on the settle. Seren didn't sleep. Seren never did. Seren sat in her chair by the west window under the horn lantern with a cup of tea going cold, looking out at the grave.

Wren carried Lark up to the loft. And put her in her bed with the rail. And set the ILDA lantern on the sill beside the MORE one, so there were two.

She stood there for a long time. Looking at her.

Then she went to bed.

Lex was already asleep. Sideways. With his feet hanging off the end. The way he always slept after a feast, like a man who'd been felled.

Wren lay down beside him.

She didn't sleep.

---

She heard him get up.

In the grey before dawn. The way he always did. A smith gets up before the sun. She felt the bed shift. Heard his feet find the floor. Heard him go down the stairs, quietly, skipping the fourth one, which creaked.

She lay there.

Then she got up too.

---

She didn't go down.

She sat on the top stair. In the dark. In her nightshirt. With her knees drawn up and her arms round them. The way she'd sat on the top stair at Thistledown when she was small, listening to her father bank the fire.

She could see the kitchen from there. Through the banisters. A slice of it. The hearth. The banked coals, red under the ash. And Lex.

Kneeling on the hearthstone. In the dark. With the poker.

---

He raked the ash off the coals.

Slowly. Carefully. The way he did every morning. The way Brannoc had taught him when he was eleven. Not too much. Not too fast. You don't wake a fire by shouting at it.

Then he leaned down. And blew on them. Gently.

The way you'd wake someone who'd been up all night.

---

The fire woke.

A flicker. A lift. A small gold flame coming up out of the red, catching on the kindling he'd laid, climbing. Warm. Gold.

Lex sat back on his heels.

Wren saw his face in the light. Tired. Patient. Twenty-seven years old and looking, in the firelight, about twelve. The crooked mouth. The square hands on his knees.

"*Good morning*," said Lex.

---

The fire didn't answer.

---

Wren waited. On the top stair. With her arms round her knees.

Lex waited. On the hearthstone.

The fire burned. Small. Gold. Quiet. Not crackling. Not commenting. Not saying *good morning, smith* the way it had every morning since the summer. Politely. Pleasantly. The way you'd say it to the man who keeps the inn.

It didn't say anything.

It just burned. And looked at him. Wren could feel it looking. The way you can feel someone looking at you across a room in the dark.

A long time.

---

Then—

---

*[You've got ash on your nose*,] said Ember.

---

Lex went very still.

---

*[You've always got ash on your nose*,] said the fire. Slowly. Wonderingly. Like someone reading a page they've read before and only just understood. *[Every morning. Every single morning. You blow on the coals and the ash goes up and you get it on your nose and you never notice. I've told you. A hundred times. A thousand. You never listen.]*

Lex didn't move.

*[You were nineteen*,] said Ember. *[The first time. In the Forge at Ashbourne. On the floor. After the Kindling. You'd fallen asleep by the hearth because you were frightened to go to bed. And I woke up in your chest and you didn't know what I was. And you got up in the morning and blew on the coals and got ash on your nose and said—]*

The fire stopped.

Wren, on the top stair, had her hand over her mouth.

*[You said good morning*,] said Ember. Very quietly. *[To the fire. Not to me. You didn't know I was there yet. You just said it. To the fire. Because you always did. Because your father did. Because it seemed rude not to.]*

A pause.

*[And I thought—that's the best thing I've ever heard. I thought, I'm going to like this boy*.]

---

Lex's shoulders were shaking.

He didn't make a sound. He knelt there on the hearthstone in the grey light with his square hands on his knees and his head bowed and his shoulders shaking, and didn't make a sound at all.

*[I've been sitting in this hearth since the summer*,] said Ember, *[trying to work out why I'm happy in the mornings. I've been writing it down. I've got forty pages of notes. Your mother's got them. I thought it was the draw on the chimney. I thought it was the oak. I thought it was the little one and her worms.]* A crackle. Small. Dry. *[It's you. It's been you the whole time. You come down the stairs in the dark and skip the fourth one because it creaks, and you rake the ash, and you blow, and you say it. Every morning. You said it when I didn't know you. You said it for three months to a fire that called you smith.]*

The flame leaned. Toward him. Out of the grate. Toward the bowed head and the shaking shoulders.

*[I don't remember loving you*,] said Ember. *[That's gone. The old man was right. It went where prices go.]*

A pause.

*[But I've been watching you do it*,] said Ember. *[Every morning. Since the summer. And I think I've worked out how it goes.]*

---

Wren couldn't see Lex's face.

She didn't need to.

---

*[Good morning, Lex*,] said Ember.

---

Lex lifted his head.

He looked at the fire. In the grey light. On his knees on the hearthstone in the kitchen of the cottage he'd built with his own hands on the black scorch-mark of the one that burned. With ash on his nose. With his face wet.

He looked at it for a long time.

Then he smiled. The crooked one. The one that made him look about twelve.

"*Good morning, Ember*," said Lex.

---

Wren went back to bed.

She didn't go down. It wasn't hers. She got up off the top stair very quietly and went back along the landing and got into the warm bed and lay there in the grey with her pink hand on her own chest and her eyes wet and listened.

To the kettle going on.

To the cups coming down off the hooks. Two. Out of habit. Then a third, and a laugh, and the third going back up.

To the fire, crackling, talking, fast, the way it used to, dry and fond and irritable, telling him he was holding the kettle wrong.

To her husband laughing.

---

Above her, in the loft, Lark woke up.

Wren heard her. The rail creaking. The bare feet on the boards. A thump, which was the rabbit being thrown out of the bed ahead of her, as a scout. The small fierce sounds of a person who has just turned two and has a great deal to do today and doesn't intend to wait for anyone.

The loft hatch opened.

A head came down. Upside down. Black hair sticking up at the back like a jackdaw's. Square eyebrows. Looking at her.

"*Mam*," said Lark. "*Mmo-ning.*"

"*Good morning, love*," said Wren.

And outside the window, over the birch wood, over the stream and the stepping stones and the roses on the grave and the blue door of the cottage across the water with the horn lantern in its window—

The sun came up.
