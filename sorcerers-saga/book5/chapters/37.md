# The Book of What Is

They walked home in sixteen days.

It should have been longer. It was two hundred miles, and there were four hundred of them, and some were old and some were small and one was a fish in a jar. But the road was green now, all of it, not just the strip their lanterns had held, and the grass was soft, and the fords were running, and nobody was afraid.

And the world kept waking up round them.

---

Lex had thought it would be like a thaw. Like spring. The grey going off the land the way snow goes, slowly, from the south slopes first, leaving mud.

It wasn't. It was like a house in the morning.

You came down the stairs in the dark and the house was quiet and cold and still, every chair where you'd left it. And then someone opened the shutters. And the light came in. And it was the same house—the same chairs, the same table, the same cup on the drainer—and it was completely different, because it was morning, and things could happen in it again.

That was what the world was like. On the walk home. Every village. Every field. Every farm.

Morning.

---

They passed the woman with the washing on the fourth day.

She was taking it in. She'd pegged it out in the spring, she said, leaning on her gate as four hundred people went past, and she'd only just noticed it was still there. Must have been a dry spell. Funny. She couldn't remember the summer at all. Had it been a good one?

"*Not very*," said Hollis Penn. And gave her a loaf.

---

They passed a farm where a man was standing in the middle of his yard with a bucket, staring at his hens. The hens were laying. All of them. Furiously. As if trying to make up for lost time. There were eggs in the nesting boxes and eggs in the straw and eggs under the cart and one, inexplicably, on the roof of the privy.

"*I don't know what to do with them all*," said the farmer. Helplessly.

Brannoc bought forty.

---

They passed a mill where the wheel had started turning in the night, on its own, when the stream came back, and the miller had woken to the sound and run down in his nightshirt and stood there crying in the race until his wife came and fetched him.

They passed a churchyard where a new grave had been dug in the spring and never filled, because the old man who was meant to go in it had been kept in his bed, gold, with his eyes moving, for three months. He'd gone in the night. When the King let go. His daughter was sitting by the grave in the morning sun with her knees drawn up.

"*He was ready*," she said, when Seren stopped. "*He'd been ready for a year. He said so. He said he'd had a good long go and he wanted his tea and his bed and then whatever came after.*" She wiped her face. "*And then the dust-man came and asked me if I wanted to keep him. And I said yes. Because I couldn't bear it.*"

Seren sat down beside her on the grass.

"*I know, love*," said Seren. "*Nobody can. That's why it's a door. So you don't have to.*"

---

They passed Westerling on the eighth day.

Nan Ashby was at the gate of her croft with her son. Col. Twenty-two. Thin. Pale. With a farmer's hands and a farmer's shoulders and a look on his face like a man who's been asleep for a very long time and isn't sure yet that he's woken up.

He was holding the HERE lantern.

Nan didn't say anything as they went past. She just lifted her hand. And Col, after a moment, lifted his. The same way. As if he'd learned it from her just then.

---

They came over the last rise above the Vale on the sixteenth day.

And there was Ashbourne.

---

It was green.

All of it. The village and the fields round it and the hills behind. The lantern ring was still there—seven hundred and twelve and more, in the grass, on the gateposts, in the hedges—but you couldn't tell any more where the ring stopped and the rest began. It was green inside and green outside. The mill-pond at the bottom of the village was water again, brown and quick, and the wheel was turning. The church tower had a jackdaw on it. The Forge chimney was smoking.

Tilda's. She'd gone on ahead with Kestrel and Teo on the twelfth day, to light the hearths.

Lex stood at the top of the rise with Lark on his shoulders and looked at it for a long time.

"*Home*," said Lark. Very clearly. Pointing.

Lex looked up at her.

She'd never said it before.

She'd heard it, though. He knew that. From the bottom of the soft dark behind a door. From three hundred gold people standing in the grass round a pool, humming one note for nine hundred years, until they didn't have to any more.

"*Yes*," said Lex. "*Home.*"

---

They went to Harrow first.

He and Wren and Lark and his mother. Down the river path. Three miles. Under the alders. With the stream beside them, running brown and quick and loud over the stones, the way it always had.

The birch wood was green. Every leaf. The hedge at the top of the garden was hawthorn and elder and bramble and dog-rose, and the dog-rose was pink.

The pond was gone.

There was a muddy hollow in the bottom of the garden where it had been, with the grass already coming up through the mud, and a tidemark round the stones of the grave. The roses were still there. Red. Dark red. Nearly black at the heart. Two dozen. More. Climbing over the stone.

*ARION VEY*.

*He turned back twice*.

---

His mother went to the grave first.

She didn't say anything. She knelt down in the grass beside it with the clay lamp in her lap, and put her hand on the stone, on the letters, and stayed there.

Lex left her.

He went across the stepping stones, with Lark on his shoulders and Wren behind, to the other cottage. His. The one he'd built on the black scorch-mark of the one that burned. With its empty east window, where the HERE lantern had hung before he gave it away.

The swing was moving.

On the apple bough. Gently. In the breeze. Out. And back.

Lark saw it and shrieked and drummed her heels on his chest until he put her down, and she ran across the grass to it, and he lifted her into it, and pushed. And she went up, and back, and up, shrieking, with her hair flying and her blue-stained arm round the rope, and the swing creaked on the bough the way it always had.

Wren went round to the woodpile.

She knelt down at the bottom of it in the long grass. And looked under. And was quiet for a moment.

Then she sat back on her heels and laughed.

"*It's there*," said Wren. "*The hedgehog. It's asleep.*"

"*Is it*—"

"*It's snoring*," said Wren. "*Lex, it's snoring.*"

---

He set the lantern down in his own hearth that evening.

---

It was the first thing he did. Before supper. Before Lark's bath. Before anything. He came in out of the garden with the swing still creaking behind him and knelt on the hearthstone in front of the cold black grate in his own kitchen, where he'd knelt ten thousand mornings, and opened the little tin door of the Ferryman's lantern.

The flame looked out at him.

*[Is this it*?] said Ember. *[Is this the hearth?]*

"*Yes*."

*[It's very small*.]

"*It's a cottage*."

*[I see*.] A pause. Considering. *[I was expecting something grander, from the way you talked about it. In the boat. On the road.]* Another pause. *[But it has a good draw. I can feel it. That's a well-built chimney.]*

"*I built it*," said Lex.

*[Did you*?] Interested. *[You must be a very good smith.]*

"*I'm a common one*."

*[I don't think that's the same thing at all*,] said Ember. Primly.

And went out of the lantern into the grate.

---

It caught.

Not slowly. Not the way a fire catches from a spark, a thread of smoke and then a lick and then a flame. It went into the cold black grate like a cat going into its own basket, and turned round once, and settled, and the whole hearth lit up gold.

Warm.

The kitchen filled with it. The light. The heat. The small dry crackle. It went up the chimney and out into the evening, and Lex heard Wren in the garden say *oh* and stop pushing the swing.

He knelt on the hearthstone and looked at it.

The fire looked back.

*[Oh*,] said Ember. Softly. From the middle of the gold. *[Oh, I like this. This is a very good hearth. I think I've been here before.]*

"*You have*," said Lex.

*[Have I*?] A pause. *[Was I happy?]*

Lex didn't answer for a long time.

"*I think so*," he said. "*You said you were. Every morning.*"

*[Then I expect I'll be happy again*,] said the fire. Comfortably. *[Good night, smith.]*

"*Good night*," said Lex.

---

He said good morning to it the next day.

He came down the stairs in the dark, the way he had every morning of his life, and raked the ash off the banked coals and blew on them. Gently. The way you'd wake someone who'd been up all night.

The fire woke.

"*Good morning*," said Lex.

*[Good morning*,] said Ember. Politely. *[Did you sleep well?]*

"*Yes*."

*[I didn't sleep at all*,] said Ember. *[I don't, I think. I spent the night reading the books on your shelf. There are only eleven. One is about sheep.]* A pause. *[I found it very moving.]*

---

He said it the next day too.

And the next. And the next.

Every morning. In the dark. On his knees on the hearthstone. Blowing on the coals.

*Good morning*.

And every morning, Ember said it back. Politely. Pleasantly. The way you'd say it to the man who keeps the inn where you're staying.

*[Good morning, smith*.]

Never *Lex*. Never *boy*. Never *idiot*. Never the dry fond irritable voice that had lived in his chest for seven years and told him he was holding the tongs wrong.

*Smith*.

He said it anyway.

---

"*How long are you going to do that*?" said Wren.

In the second week. In bed. In the grey before dawn. With Lark asleep between them, sideways, with her feet in Lex's ribs.

"*Do what*?"

"*Go down and say good morning to it*."

Lex lay there and thought about it.

"*Arion did it for a year and a half*," he said. "*To Mam. Every morning. Across the table. And she didn't know him. She said good morning back because it was polite.*"

Wren didn't say anything.

"*He didn't need her to know him*," said Lex. "*Mam said. He needed her to have a good morning.*" He looked at the ceiling. "*I can do a year and a half.*"

"*And if it's longer*?"

"*Then it's longer*."

Wren was quiet for a long time.

Then she put her hand—her left, the pink one, stiff and cold and learning—on his chest. Over his heart. Where Ember used to live.

"*You're very like your father*," said Wren.

---

Ember asked questions.

That was new. Or not new—it had always asked questions, it had asked a thousand a day, it had asked them at two in the morning when he was trying to sleep—but they were different questions now. Not about the Wells, or the net, or the history of tempering in the Saltreach foundries. It knew all that. It remembered all that.

It asked about him.

*[Why do you say good morning to me*?] it said. In the third week.

Lex was kneeling at the hearth. With the poker. He stopped.

"*Because it's morning*," he said.

*[That's not a reason*,] said Ember. *[That's a description. Everyone knows it's morning. You don't say it to the chair.]*

Lex didn't say anything.

*[Do you say it to the chair*?] said Ember. Suspiciously.

"*No*."

*[Then why me*?]

Lex put the poker down.

He knelt there on the hearthstone. In the dark. With the fire looking at him. Small. Gold. Curious. Without the faintest idea why a smith it had met three weeks ago came down every morning and said good morning to it like a prayer.

"*Because I love you*," said Lex.

---

The fire went very still.

For a long time.

*[Oh*,] said Ember.

A pause.

*[I see*,] said Ember.

Another pause. Longer.

*[I'll write that down*,] said Ember. *[If you don't mind. For the book.]*

"*I don't mind*," said Lex.

---

The book was his mother's idea.

*The Book of What Is*. Volume Three. Ember had been writing it, it said, in its head, since the far shore. Since it crossed with Arion in the winter and found itself in a lantern on a boat with no love left and nothing to do but look at the world and describe it.

*[It's very hard*,] Ember said. *[Describing what is. I find I keep wanting to explain why it matters. And I can't, because I don't know.]*

So Seren wrote it down.

She came across the stepping stones every afternoon. With a stack of paper and a pen and Wenna's clay lamp, and sat at Lex's kitchen table by the hearth, and took dictation. Two hours a day. Sometimes three. In her bad crooked hand that had never been any good at letters, with her tongue between her teeth.

*[Chapter Nine*,] said Ember. *[A door at the edge of the world, apple wood, in flower.]*

*Chapter Nine*, wrote Seren. *A door*.

*[No—strike that. A door at the end of a road. It wasn't the edge. It became the edge. There's a difference.]*

Seren struck it out. Wrote it again.

*[Paragraph. The door is painted Harrow blue. The paint was mixed by a woman named Seren Harrow from woad and lime and an ingredient she declines to name.]*

Seren's pen stopped.

*[Is that right*?] said Ember. *[The smith told me. He said you wouldn't say what was in it. I wondered why. I think it's rather wonderful that there's something in the world you won't tell anyone.]*

Seren put the pen down.

She looked at the fire for a long time.

"*It's tears*," she said. "*A Harrow woman's tears. A drop. In every pot. For every door.*" She picked up the pen. "*Wenna told me. When I was nine. I've never told anyone.*"

The fire was silent.

*[I won't write that down*,] said Ember. Very quietly. *[That's not mine to keep.]*

---

Antho and Seraphine didn't come home.

---

They stayed at the door.

Lex had known they would. He'd known it the moment his brother stood on the threshold with his back to the room and his face to the sunrise, in both at once. Antho wrote in the second week. A long letter. Seraphine's hand, Antho dictating, with Seraphine's notes in the margins disagreeing with him.

They were building a house. At the end of the road. Beside the door. Tom Pruett had stayed on with a dozen men from the Vale and they'd cut the stone from the hill behind and had the walls up in a week. Two rooms and a loft. A fat chimney. A window facing east, toward the world. And another, facing the door.

*There'll be a lantern in both*, Antho wrote. *One for the people coming. One for the people going.*

*Somebody has to keep the door*, Antho wrote. *I'm the one who opens it. It turns out that means you have to live next to it. Who knew.*

*Seraphine says I knew*, he added. *Seraphine says I've known since I was nineteen.*

*She's right*, wrote Seraphine, in the margin. *He's not.*

---

They came, he wrote. The dead.

Not in crowds. Not like the winter. One at a time. Two. Walking. Up the green road from the east, with the lanterns going past them every mile. Grey. Dust. Columns of it. The ones who'd died in the spring and the summer, while the world was still. The ones who'd died in the night when the King let go. The ones who'd been drifting in the Drowned Country and the grey river for longer than anyone remembered and had finally heard there was a door.

They came up the green road and over the rise and down the slope to the threshold. And stopped. And waited.

And Antho opened it.

*Every one*, he wrote. *Every single one. I lift the latch. And they go through. And I say goodbye. And sometimes they bend a little on the way past, the way they do, and I know they're saying thank you.*

*I don't know what I did for nine years*, he wrote, *before I was a doorkeeper*.

*Shouted at moths*, wrote Seraphine.

---

And the living came too.

That was the part Lex hadn't expected. People. Walking up the green road. With lanterns. To see. Because there was a door at the end of the world now, apple wood, painted blue, in flower, and the whole of Caldor had seen the WORLD NOTICE, and people wanted to see it for themselves.

They came and stood on the slope and looked at it.

Some of them painted something on it. Antho let them. He kept a pot of blue on the step.

Some of them knocked. Just to see. And Antho would come out and say *yes*? And they'd go red and say *sorry, just wanted to see what it sounded like*. And Antho would say *it sounds like that*, and go back in.

*It's going to be a village*, Antho wrote. *I can see it coming. A hundred years from now there'll be an inn here and a market and a man selling little wooden doors on sticks.*

*I hope there's an inn*, wrote Seraphine. *I'd kill for an inn.*

---

The baby came in August.

---

The letter came by raven. Quill, ragged and furious, three days on the wing from the end of the road. Lex untied it in the Forge yard with his hands shaking so badly he tore the paper.

One line. Antho's hand. Very large. Very bad.

*IT'S A BOY. HE'S LOUD. SERAPHINE SAYS HE'S THE 413TH. HIS NAME IS ARION.*

And under it, in Seraphine's hand, very small, very neat—

*He has his grandfather's ears. God help him.*

---

Lex sat down on the upturned quench-trough in the yard.

Brannoc came out of the Forge. Looked at him. Looked at the letter. Took it out of his hand and read it, holding it at arm's length, with his lips moving.

Then he handed it back. And sat down beside him on the trough. With his old scarred fingertips on his knees.

Neither of them said anything for a long time.

"*Arion*," said Brannoc. At last. Gruffly.

"*Yes*."

"*Good name*."

"*Yes*."

Brannoc looked at the sky.

"*I'll make him a nail*," he said.

---

At the end of the summer, Pim came through the door.

---

Lex heard about it from Antho, first. Then from Pim himself, which was rarer, and shorter.

He'd been at the pool. All summer. With Ivy, and Hask—whose arm was flesh to the shoulder now, and who'd taken up a pick again, left-handed, and was digging a proper drain for the spring so the house wouldn't flood—and Kit, and the thorn-woman. Who'd stayed.

*Rosie's planting roses*, wrote Pim. In his small careful hand. *Round the pool. Round the lanterns. She says Mal likes them. She says he told her.*

*She sits on the rim every morning and says good morning to him. Like Ivy. She says she's got a lot of good mornings to catch up on.*

And then one morning at the end of August, he'd walked down the slope to the pool, and round it, and up through the soft dark to the back of the door. And knocked.

And Antho had let him in.

---

He came to Harrow on a carter's wagon a week later. Thin. Brown. Silent. With a pack on his back and a pencil behind his ear.

He walked up the path to Seren's door. And stood on the ramp. And didn't knock, because he'd never liked knocking, it made a noise.

Seren opened it anyway. She'd seen him from the window.

Pim took something out of his pack. Wrapped in linen. Small. And held it out.

---

Seren unwrapped it.

It was a crown.

Small. Silver-grey. Seven points. Thin. Plain.

*Hes's*.

---

"*Rosie said*," said Pim. Slowly. Getting each word out like a stone out of a field. "*Give it back. To the Harrow woman. Or the little one.*" He looked at it in her hands. "*She said it's said its last no.*"

Seren looked at it for a long time.

"*Did she say anything else*?"

Pim thought about it.

"*She said it'll have a yes next*," he said.

---

Seren didn't put it under a flagstone.

She'd thought about it. Lex saw her think about it. She stood in her doorway with the crown in her hands and looked at the floor of her kitchen, where every Harrow woman had kept it for nine hundred years, hidden, in a box, under a stone.

Then she turned round. And went across the stepping stones to Lex's cottage. And in through the open door. To where Lark was sitting on the hearthrug in front of the fire, explaining something very important to Ember about a worm.

And knelt down. And held it out.

Lark looked at it.

Then she took it. With both hands. Turned it over. Examined it with her eyebrows. Bit it, experimentally.

Then she put it on her rag rabbit.

It slid down over the rabbit's ears and round its neck like a collar. Lark considered the effect. Seriously. For a long time.

"*Yes*," said Lark.

---

The fire laughed.

Lex heard it. From the doorway. Where he'd come in from the Forge with soot to his elbows and stopped.

A small dry crackle. Startled. As if it had got out without asking. Like water out of a crack in ice.

*[Oh*,] said Ember. *[Oh, that's—I'm sorry. I don't know why that was funny. It was very funny.]*

Lark beamed at it.

*[It was the rabbit*,] said Ember. Helplessly. *[The rabbit's face. Did you see the rabbit's face?]*

"*I saw*," said Lex.

*[It looked so—*] The fire crackled again. *[It looked so pleased with itself*.]

Lex leaned in the doorway with soot to his elbows and watched his daughter and his mother and his old friend laughing together on the hearthrug in front of a fire he'd built with his own hands, and didn't say anything at all.

---

He went down the next morning in the dark.

The way he always did. Raked the ash off the coals. Blew on them. Gently.

The fire woke.

"*Good morning*," said Lex.

*[Good morning*,] said Ember.

And then a pause. A short one. As if it had been about to say something else, and had stopped, and wasn't sure what it had been about to say.

*[Good morning*,] said Ember again. Slowly. Puzzled. As if trying the words out to see how they fitted.

Then, very quietly—

*[...smith*.]

Lex knelt on the hearthstone and waited.

It didn't say anything else.

He got up and put the kettle on.

That was all right. It was only September.

He could do a year and a half.
