# The Dead at the Rim

Antho Vey counted the lanterns every morning.

He didn't need to. He knew how many there were. He'd hung most of them himself, over seven years, one by one, on hooks and nails and iron pins driven into the stone of the cave at the Hollowmaw, from the mouth all the way back to the black door at the end. Lanterns for the lost. Lanterns for the ones who hadn't come home. Iron and brass and tin and clay and glass and horn. Every shape. Every size.

One thousand, one hundred and thirteen.

He counted them anyway. Every morning. Walking slowly down the long cave in the grey light with his hands behind his back, looking up. The way his father used to walk the ledgers in the Spire. The way you'd walk a line of sleeping children to make sure they were all still breathing.

*One. Two. Three.*

It was a habit. It was a prayer. He'd started it in the mad years, when he was alone up here with a voice in his head that wanted to eat the world, and counting was the only thing that held. He'd never stopped.

*Four hundred and twelve. Four hundred and thirteen.*

They were all lit.

They'd been lit since Lantern Night. Every one. Not one had guttered in seven weeks. He hadn't filled them. Nobody had. They didn't need it. They burned on nothing now, on air, on whatever it was that a given light burns on when the giving is finished and the light stays anyway.

*Nine hundred and ninety-nine. One thousand.*

At the end of the cave, at the black door, the last three. Hung together. On one hook. A clay lamp, small and lopsided, with a thumb-print in the rim. An iron one, with *ARDENT* on the base. And a third, plain tin, that his father had hung there the night before the wedding, two winters ago.

*One thousand one hundred and eleven. One thousand one hundred and twelve.*

He stopped in front of the tin one.

*One thousand one hundred and thirteen*.

He stood there for a while.

Then he went back up the cave to put the kettle on.

---

It was Midwinter. The shortest day. Snow at the mouth of the cave, deep, blue in the dawn. The Greyspine white and sharp against a sky like a knife. Cold enough to crack stone.

Seraphine was asleep.

She slept in the alcove off the cave mouth, on the low bed they'd built into the rock, under three blankets and a bearskin and a quilt Odile had made, with her spectacles folded on the shelf beside her and her ink-stained fingers curled under her chin. She slept like a scholar. Badly. With books. There were four in the bed with her this morning. One of them was open on her chest, rising and falling.

He stood in the doorway of the alcove and looked at her.

He did that a lot. More, lately. Since the autumn. Since a bed by a west window and a hand going slack in his, and his mother's voice, very quiet, saying *there*, the way you'd say it when the last stitch goes in.

He'd come home from the bottom of the world on Lantern Night and walked up the mountain in the dawn and found her here. Standing at the mouth of the cave. Alone. Looking up at the sky. With every lantern behind her lit.

She hadn't said anything. She'd just put her arms round him.

He hadn't let go for a long time.

---

He made the tea.

He made it the way his father had made it, in the Spire, two hundred years of practice: warm the pot, one spoon each and one for the pot, water just off the boil, not on it, never on it, *you'll scald the leaf, Anthoren, it's not a horseshoe*. He'd learned it in the last year. In the mornings at Harrow, in the blue-door cottage. His father in the wheeled chair by the window, too weak to lift the kettle, telling him how. Patiently. Every morning. As if it mattered.

It did matter. Antho knew that now.

It was the only thing his father had ever taught him with his hands.

---

He took a cup to the cave mouth and stood in the snow and drank it.

Below him the mountain fell away into cloud. Somewhere under the cloud was Ashbourne. And beyond Ashbourne, south, the Vale. And somewhere at the end of the Vale, in a birch wood, by a stream, a grey stone with twenty lanterns round it, and a cottage with a blue door, and a horn lantern in the west window.

*TOO LATE*.

He'd been at the window when Lex set it there. In the grey dawn after Lantern Night. He'd watched his little brother carry it up the ramp and through the door and set it on the sill. And stand there. And not move. For a long time.

He hadn't said anything. There wasn't anything to say.

He drank his tea.

He looked at his left hand.

---

On the back of it, between the knuckles and the wrist, a coin.

Not a coin. A *place*. A round of gold the size of a coin. Smooth. Bright. Cold. Where a single grain of the Gilded King's dust had landed on him in the grey country, in the crack, running, with the Glass Queen's servants behind them and Hask going gold.

He could feel it. Not with the hand. With everything else. A small cold stillness in the middle of him. Like a held breath. Like a door that won't open, in a house where all the other doors do.

He'd tried to open it.

Every night. In the dark. With Shade, with Hollow, with everything he had. He was the Threshold Warden. He was the man who kept the edge between the world and the dark. He'd opened the black door at the bottom of the Hollowmaw with a knock. He'd opened a breach in the world a hundred miles across by asking.

He couldn't open a coin of gold on the back of his own hand.

*It's not locked*, Lex had said. In the autumn. Holding the hand. *It's not a door. It's kept. There's nothing in it to open.*

*Then what do I do*?

*I don't know*, his brother had said. *I don't know. I'm sorry.*

He'd never heard Lex say that. Not about iron. Not about anything you could put your hands on.

He put the hand in his pocket. And drank his tea.

---

The voice came at noon.

He was in the cave, halfway down, on a ladder, taking down a lantern from a high hook to look at its chain, which had gone thin at one link. Not because it needed doing. Because his hands needed doing.

And the lantern spoke.

No.

The *hollow* in the lantern. The round empty space inside it, under the flame, where the light stood. Every lantern had one. Every lamp, every cup, every bowl.

*[...brother...]*

He nearly fell off the ladder.

*[...brother...it's me...I'm in your lantern...I mean...not IN it...in the room in it...I didn't know I could do that...Ivy says I can do it anywhere there's a little room...I'm sorry...did I frighten you...]*

He held onto the ladder.

He knew the voice. He'd known it for eight years, when it was the only voice in the world and it wanted to eat him. And he'd known it for two years since, when it came back, small and sorry and frightened, asking for the lamp-lady and for someone called *Mal*.

*Mal*.

*[...brother...]*

*I'm here*. He came down the ladder. Slowly. With the lantern in his hand. *I'm here, Mal. What is it?*

*[...they're here...]* said the seventh Well. *[...the grey ones...]*

---

It told him all of it.

In pieces. In the small frightened voice of a child in a very large dark house. The grey ones. The first night. The wind, coming from the west, and on the wind, thousands of them. Drifting. Grey. Columns of dust standing up off the snow and swaying, the way smoke sways. Coming across the world from the edge. Toward the green circle. Toward the pool. Toward *him*.

*[...because I'm the room...that's what they want...they want somewhere to GO...and I'm the only room there is...]*

*Then let them in*.

*[...I CAN'T...]*

The lantern shook in his hand. The flame flinched.

*[...there are so many...brother...thousands...and I'm still so little...if I let them all in I'll be full...and if I'm full I'll stop...and then there won't be any room...not for anything...not for babies or seeds or bread or new things or ANYTHING...]*

Silence. The flame steadied.

*[...I told the smith...]* said Mal. Smaller. *[...just now...I found his fire...he's coming...Ivy says bring you too...she says...]* A pause. *[...she says please...]*

---

He went to the alcove.

Seraphine was sitting up in the bed with her spectacles on and a book in her lap and another one under her elbow and a quill behind her ear. She looked up when he came in. Saw his face.

She put the book down.

"*Mal*," she said.

"*Mal*."

"*In the hearth*. *Just now.*" She pointed. At the little fire in the alcove grate. "*It talked to me. Out of the ash pan. I thought I was dreaming. I've been sitting here for ten minutes writing down what it said in case I was.*" She held up the paper. Her hand was shaking. "*Antho, it said there are thousands of them.*"

"*It said that to me too*."

"*It's frightened*."

"*It's frightened*." He sat down on the edge of the bed. "*It's been frightened for seven weeks. Seven weeks, Sera. With thousands of the dead standing round it. And it didn't say anything. To anyone. Because it didn't know it could.*"

She looked at him.

"*Like someone else I know*," said Seraphine Ilvane. Gently.

He didn't answer that.

He got up and went to saddle the dragon.

---

Lex came up the mountain at dusk.

On foot. In the snow. Fast. The way he always came up mountains, as if they'd insulted him. With a pack on his back and a coat over his forge clothes and his face red with cold and the handprint on his cheek standing out grey-gold against it. And the bell. Antho could hear the bell, under his shirt, faint, as he came up the last switchback. Not ringing. Just moving. The way a bell does when the man wearing it is walking hard.

Ashka heard him first. She lifted her great head off the snow at the mouth of the cave and made a sound low in her chest. Not a growl. A rumble. The sound she made for people she'd decided were hers.

Lex stopped at the top of the path. Looked at the dragon. At his brother. At the saddle on the dragon. At the packs.

"*You heard it too*," he said.

"*Out of a lantern*."

"*Out of my hearth*."

"*Out of the ash pan*," said Seraphine. Coming out of the cave with her arms full of books. "*Hello, Lex. Have you eaten? You haven't eaten. You never eat when you're frightened. Antho, give him the bread.*"

Antho gave him the bread.

---

They flew east in the dark.

Ashka was the size of a barn now. Grey as a storm, with silver along the edges of her scales like frost along the edges of slate, and wings that cracked when she opened them like a ship's sail filling. She'd been the size of a cat once. Antho had found her in a snare with a torn wing in the Greyspine, ten years ago, in the mad years, and carried her home inside his coat.

She carried him now.

Him and his brother and his wife and a pack of books, east across the sleeping world at Midwinter, under a sky so full of stars it looked like frost on black glass.

---

Nobody talked much.

You couldn't, really. Not on a dragon. The wind took the words. But it wasn't only that.

Lex sat behind him. Not touching. Antho could feel him there. A warmth at his back. Steady. The way you feel a banked fire in a room behind you.

He thought about the last time they'd flown together. East. Home. From the edge. In the autumn. With the grey country behind them and a gold road running across the world below them, a step wide, wandering, the King's footprints. And their father dying at the end of it.

They hadn't talked then either.

*Lex*, he thought. Into the wind. Into the dark. *Lex. Do you still say good morning to it? The fire?*

He didn't say it out loud. He knew the answer.

---

Somewhere over the Vhessan hills, past midnight, Lex spoke.

Close. At his ear. Over the wind.

"*Elowen wrote*."

"*What did she say*?"

"*The bread won't rise*."

Antho turned his head. In the dark. Lex's face was a pale shape behind him, and the handprint a darker shape on it, and his eyes—grey, their father's—catching the starlight.

"*What*?"

"*At Westerling*. *Near the old edge.*" Lex's voice was flat. Careful. The voice he used for things he didn't understand yet and didn't want to frighten anyone with. "*Good yeast. Warm kitchen. Sat all night. Didn't notice the night go by.*" A pause. "*Eggs won't hatch. A boy's cut won't close. Not healing. Not festering. Just staying.*"

Antho was quiet for a long time.

Then he looked down at his left hand. On the dragon's neck. In the dark. At the place on the back of it where there was a round of gold the size of a coin.

"*Kept*," he said.

"*Not gold*," said Lex. "*Not like that. The bread's just bread. The boy's just a boy. Nothing's gold.*" The wind took some of it. "*But—*"

"*But stopped*."

"*Yes*."

Neither of them said anything else.

They didn't need to.

---

They came down over the green circle at dawn.

---

Antho had seen it before. Every month since the Blightmarch. A hundred miles across. A ring of green cut into the dead grey of the old march, where for sixteen years there had been nothing but Gloam and ash and silent birds, and now there was grass. Real grass. And clover. And thorn and hazel and young birch coming up in clumps along the old trench where the brothers had cut the glyph. And a river, new, that hadn't been there two years ago, finding its way across the green from somewhere in the hills.

It was under snow now. White. A white circle a hundred miles across, glittering, with the sun coming up over the eastern rim of it red as iron.

And in the middle—

A pool.

Small. Round. Dark. Not frozen. Never frozen. A disc of black water, still as glass, that didn't reflect anything. Not the sky. Not the snow. Not the sun. You could look straight down into it and see nothing at all, not even your own face. Just dark. Soft. Deep. Like looking into a cupped hand at night.

And at the bottom of it, very far down, three small gold lights. Close together. Like candles in a window a long way off.

Malachi. Aurelian. Isaura Kell.

The three who'd stayed.

---

And round the pool—

---

He'd known. Mal had told him. *Thousands*. He'd heard the word. He'd thought he understood it.

He hadn't.

---

They stood round the rim.

Grey. Columns of dust. Tall as a man, some of them. Taller. Some no higher than a child. Standing up out of the snow in their thousands, on every side of the pool, close together, ten and twenty and fifty deep, so many that from the air the dark disc of the water looked like the pupil of an eye and they were the iris round it. Swaying. All of them. Very slightly. In a wind that wasn't blowing.

The way smoke sways.

The way the tall grass sways at dusk when there's no one there to see it.

---

Ashka wouldn't land among them.

She circled. Twice. Low. Antho felt her trembling under him. Not afraid. Dragons aren't afraid. *Unwilling*. The way a horse is unwilling to go into a burned stable. She came down at last a quarter mile off, on the snow, outside the ring, and folded her wings, and put her head down, and would go no further.

They got off.

They walked in.

---

It was quiet among the dead.

That was the first thing. Not silent. Antho knew silence. He'd lived in it. This was quiet the way a church is quiet, or a sickroom, or a house at night with a baby asleep upstairs. Quiet with something in it. Something holding its breath.

The snow was grey.

That was the second thing. Outside the ring it was white. Blue-white, sparkling, new, the way snow should be at dawn. Inside the ring, among the dead, it was grey. Not dirty. Not ash. Just *grey*. The colour gone out of it. As if somebody had laid a fine grey veil over it, the way you'd lay a cloth over a mirror in a house where someone has died.

He knelt and touched it.

It was cold. It was snow. It was just snow.

It didn't melt under his fingers.

---

The dead didn't move as they passed.

They swayed. That was all. Tall grey columns, fine as flour, standing in the grey snow. You could see through them. Not clearly. The way you see through mist. A shape here and there, maybe. A shoulder. The line of a head. A hand. Then gone again, into the grey.

And as he passed each one, close, Antho heard them.

Not voices. Not words.

Names.

---

He was the Threshold Warden. He kept the edge between the world and the dark. He'd made pacts with spirits in the mad years—terrible pacts, pacts he still dreamed about—and he'd walked the grey river and talked to the Ferryman and held the hand of a grey girl in a dead world while she went up into the light.

He could hear the dead. He always could. It was the oldest thing about him.

And these dead were saying their names.

Over and over. Very quietly. Each one. The way you'd say your name in the dark, to yourself, if you'd been lying on the floor of an empty hall for nine hundred years and were frightened you might forget it.

*Wenlock. Wenlock. Wenlock.*

*Bess, I'm Bess, I'm Bess.*

*Hollin of the stables. Hollin. Hollin.*

*Tamsy. Tamsy. My name is Tamsy.*

---

He stopped walking.

Seraphine stopped beside him. She couldn't hear them. Not like he could. But she could hear something. He could see it in her face. The Scribe of the Seventh, who heard the spaces between things. She could hear the gaps where the names were.

"*Antho*," she said. Very quietly.

"*They're saying their names*."

"*I know*." She'd gone pale. "*I can hear the holes in the air where they are.*"

Lex had stopped too. A little ahead. He couldn't hear any of it. He was looking at the grey snow. At the dead. At the dark pool beyond them, with its three small lights.

He looked very young suddenly. Antho hadn't seen him look that young in years.

---

They found Ivy on the rim.

She was sitting on the very edge of the pool, on a folded blanket on the stone, with her crutch beside her and her bad foot tucked under her and her good one hanging over the water. Small and thin and sixteen, with her hair in two plaits and a shawl round her shoulders and her nose red with cold. Talking.

To the pool.

Quietly. Steadily. The way you'd talk to a frightened animal in a stall. About nothing. About sheep. About the snow. About a cat at the Coldwater house that had had kittens in a boot. About anything.

She'd been talking to it for two years. Since the Blightmarch. Since a Well the size of a country came up through a breach a hundred miles wide and folded itself down into a pool the size of a pond, small and dark and frightened, and a girl on a crutch who'd been Kindled with a class nobody could name sat down on the rim beside it and started talking about sheep.

HOLLOWKEEPER. Unique. The net had never seen one before.

She looked up when they came through the dead. And her face—

Antho had seen that face before. On children in the mad years. On villagers who'd seen him come down the mountain with black eyes.

On himself. In still water. Once.

The face of someone who has been holding something very heavy for a very long time, all alone, and has just seen someone else come into the room.

"*Oh*," said Ivy Moss. "*Oh, you came.*"

And burst into tears.

---

Seraphine got to her first.

She sat down on the stone and put her arms round her and held her, and Ivy cried into her shoulder in great ragged gulps, the way children cry when they've been brave for too long. And Seraphine held her and rocked her and said *I know, I know, I know*, over and over, the way you do.

Lex stood over them. Helpless. With his big burned hands hanging at his sides.

Antho looked past them.

At the boy.

---

Pim was sitting a little way along the rim. On his own. On the stone. With his knees drawn up and his arms round them. Very still. Watching the dead.

Fourteen. Nearly fifteen. Small for it. Thin. Dark hair sticking up. A face that had learned, in twelve years of silence, not to show you anything, and was only now, slowly, learning how.

He didn't look round when Antho came and sat down beside him.

They sat together. Not talking. Pim had never talked much. Two words in a row was a speech. And Antho had learned, in seven years, that the best thing to do with Pim was sit.

So he sat.

---

"*Tamsy*," said Pim. After a long time.

Antho looked at him.

"*That one*." Pim lifted his chin. At a grey column a few yards off. Small. No higher than a child of six. Swaying. "*She says it. Over and over. Tamsy.*" He swallowed. "*I can't hear it. Not like you. But I can feel the shape of it. In the room.*"

The room. Pim had a room. A little piece of the seventh, inside him. A hollow he'd made in himself out of being hurt, in the years before the Keep, before anyone was kind. Moths couldn't drink from him. The Gilded King's gold couldn't set in front of him. He could cup his hands and make a little space in the air where something could breathe.

"*There are so many*," said Pim.

"*I know*."

"*And Mal can't*." Pim's jaw was tight. "*Mal wants to. Mal cries. At night. Ivy hears it.*" He looked at the pool. "*I hear it too.*"

---

Hask came down from the Coldwater house at mid-morning.

It stood a half mile off. A long low timber house with a turf roof and a smoking chimney and a forge-shed and a pen with three goats and a horn lantern in every window. Where Kit and the Coldwater crew lived, and kept the pool, and fished the new river, and sat on the rim in shifts at night so the Hollowkeeper could sleep.

He came through the dead without looking at them. A big man. Bigger than Lex. Grey-bearded. Grey-lunged—Antho could hear the catch in his breath from twenty yards, the faint rattle at the bottom of it. With Isaura Kell's old staff in his left hand. Cut down. Shod with iron.

His left hand.

His right arm hung at his side. Gold. From the fingertips to the elbow. Smooth and bright and cold. With a black seam down the forearm, where an old iron scar had been, gold now too.

His pick-arm.

He stopped at the rim. Looked at the brothers. Grunted.

"*Took you long enough*," said Silas Hask.

---

They talked in the Coldwater house.

Round the long table, by the fire. With bread and cheese and Kit's fish stew, and the goats complaining in the pen outside, and the dead standing grey in the grey snow beyond the windows, a half mile off, swaying.

Ivy had stopped crying. She sat with Seraphine's arm round her and a bowl of stew in her lap she wasn't eating.

"*Seven weeks*," she said. "*Since the first night. The night the edge came down.*" She looked at her hands. "*They came on the wind. From the west. I saw them coming. Like smoke. Like fog. Over the rim of the circle. And then down. Toward the pool.*" She swallowed. "*And Mal said—Mal said—*"

"*Mal said come in*," said Hask. Heavily. "*First thing. Before anyone could stop it. Come in. Come home.*"

"*It's what Mal's for*," said Ivy. "*It's a room. It can't help it.*"

"*And some of them did*." Hask turned his cup in his good hand. "*Went in. A few hundred. That first night. Drifted down onto the water and went under like smoke going down a chimney.*" He looked at the window. "*And the pool went grey.*"

---

"*Grey*?" said Seraphine.

"*At the edges*," said Ivy. "*Just at the edges. A ring of grey round the black. Like frost. And Mal went quiet. Very quiet. For a whole day.*" Her voice shook. "*And when it talked again it was—slow. Like someone talking through a mouthful of something. Like someone very tired.*"

"*Full*," said Lex.

Ivy looked at him.

"*Fuller*," she said. "*Not full. Not yet. But fuller. And it got frightened. Because it could feel it. The room getting smaller. And there were thousands more outside, all waiting, all wanting to come in, and if they all came in—*"

She stopped.

"*Then there'd be no room left*," said Seraphine. Quietly. "*Not for anything.*"

"*Mal said that*," said Ivy. "*Mal said, if I fill up I'll stop. Like the grey country stopped.*" She looked at the window. At the dead. "*So it asked them to wait. It said please. It said, I'm sorry, please wait, I'm too little, I'll grow, I promise I'll grow.*"

"*And they're waiting*," said Kit. Quietly. From the end of the table. He was nineteen now, Kit. Thin and solemn, with Coldwater coal-dust still in the creases of his knuckles though he hadn't been down a mine in two years. "*They've been waiting seven weeks. They don't do anything. They don't go anywhere. They just stand there. Saying their names.*"

"*And where they stand, the snow goes grey*," said Hask.

"*And the grass under the snow*," said Ivy. "*I dug down. Under one. It's grey too. Not dead. Just—grey. Stopped. Like it's waiting too.*"

---

Antho sat very still.

*Like the bread*, he thought. *At Westerling. That didn't notice the night go by.*

He looked at Lex. Lex was looking at him. The same thought. The same face.

*It's the same thing*, thought Antho. *Here and there. The dead here, with no room to go. The edge there, with no edge at all. Grey coming in. Stopping things.*

*It's the same thing*.

---

"*Let me talk to it*," said Lex.

---

They went back out to the rim.

Through the dead. Through the grey snow. Ivy on her crutch, Seraphine beside her. Hask behind. Kit. Pim, who'd stayed on the rim all the while and hadn't moved.

Lex knelt on the stone at the edge of the pool.

He put his hands flat on the cold rim. And leaned over. And looked down into the dark. Into the soft black water that didn't show you anything, not even your face.

Antho saw the grey. At the edges. A ring of it, a hand wide, round the black. Like frost on a window. Like the beginning of a cataract in an old man's eye.

*Mal*, said Lex. Out loud. Gently. *I'm here.*

---

*[...smith...]*

Not from the hollow of a lantern. Not from the ash pan of a hearth. From the pool. From the dark. From everywhere. Soft and enormous and very young, like the whole night sky trying to whisper.

*[...you came...you came...]*

*I came*. Lex's hands were white on the stone. *Ivy told me. About the grey ones. About the room.*

*[...I'm sorry...]*

*Don't be sorry*.

*[...I AM sorry...I should be bigger...I should be big enough...I'm the room...it's what I'm FOR...and they've been waiting nine hundred years...nine hundred years with nowhere to go...and I'm too little...]*

Something moved in the dark. Far down. The three gold lights flickered. As if the water over them had shivered.

*[...I was bigger once...]* said the seventh Well. *[...I remember...before...I was a pool in a garden...a little one...but I went DOWN...all the way down...I went down to the bottom of everything...there was room in me for the whole world...]*

*I know*, said Lex.

*[...and then they drank me...]*

---

Silence. On the rim. Among the dead.

Antho felt it go through all of them. The Coldwater crew. Kit. Hask. The word. *Drank*.

He'd seen it. In the Well's own memory, two winters ago, in a cold-room at the Keep with a Delve folding shut under Pim's hands. Nine crowned figures kneeling at the edge of a black pool in a hidden garden. Cupping their hands. Drinking. Laughing. Standing up.

And a tenth. A girl. Standing apart. With her crown in her hands.

Not drinking.

---

*[...they drank me dry...]* said Mal. *[...nine of them...and I was empty...and empty is hungry...and I was hungry for nine hundred years...I don't remember all of it...I don't want to...]*

*I know*.

*[...and then the old man sang to me...and the other old man held my hand...and the lamp-lady brought me home...and I wasn't hungry anymore...]* A pause. *[...but I was still little...]*

*Because they've still got it*, said Antho.

---

He hadn't meant to say it. It came out of him the way things did. Too fast. Too loud. Before he'd thought.

Everyone turned.

He was standing at the rim behind his brother. With his hands in his pockets. With the coin of gold on the back of his left hand cold against his thigh.

"*Because they've still got it*," he said again. Slower. "*What they drank. They drank it nine hundred years ago and they never gave it back. It's still in them. That's what they are. That's why they don't die. That's why they can't make anything new.*" He looked down at the dark. "*They're full of you, Mal. And you're empty.*"

---

The pool was very still.

Then—

*[...yes...]* said the seventh Well. Slowly. As if remembering something. *[...yes...I felt it...]*

*Felt what*? said Lex.

*[...on the Night...the lantern night...when the old man turned the Key...and the grey curtain fell...]* The dark stirred. *[...something came HOME...]*

*What came home*?

*[...a piece of me...a big piece...heavy...cold...from very far down...from the very bottom of everything...where the Key was...]* A pause. *[...it was cold like iron...it came up through the dark and into me...and it said one thing...as it came in...]*

*What*?

*[...it said...Hesper's...]*

---

Antho closed his eyes.

He saw it. He'd been there. At the bottom of the world, in the Knot, on Lantern Night. A square dark man with an iron crown, standing on the last thread as it went slack. Letting the brothers go. Looking at the old man in Hesper's crown. Nodding. The way he'd nodded at her on the shingle, nine hundred years ago, in chains.

*Hesper's*.

And stepping off. Into the dark. With the thread.

*Caddoc*.

---

"*Caddoc*," said Seraphine. Out loud. She'd gone white. She had her notebook out, the way she always did when she was frightened, and her quill, and she wasn't writing anything. "*Caddoc fell. With the thread. And what he drank came home.*"

*[...and I got bigger...]* said Mal. *[...a little...not enough...but I felt it...I felt the room get bigger...like a breath...]*

"*Because he died*," said Lex. Quietly.

"*Because he let go*," said Antho.

They looked at each other.

---

"*Then that's it*," said Hask.

Everyone turned. He was standing at the back. Leaning on Kell's staff with his good hand. His gold arm hanging. His breath catching at the bottom.

"*That's the answer*," said Silas Hask. "*Three of them left. The King. The glass one. The thorny one.*" He shrugged. A heavy movement. "*They're full of what the pool needs. So we take it back.*"

"*They're deathless*," said Seraphine.

"*Caddoc wasn't*." Hask looked at the pool. "*Not in the end.*"

"*Caddoc chose*," said Antho. "*He stepped off. Nobody pushed him.*"

"*Then we push*."

---

Nobody said anything.

The dead swayed round them in the grey snow. Saying their names. *Wenlock. Bess. Hollin. Tamsy.*

Antho looked at Hask. At the big grey grim man, Unbound by Lex at a Coldwater Stone four summers ago, who'd become the most powerful Delve-breaker in Caldor, and drunk a Moth Queen's light, and broken a hundred shafts down into the young Well before anyone understood what they were. Who'd stood in the crack in the grey country, in the autumn, with gold dust coming down, and curled himself round a boy so it wouldn't touch him.

Who kept the pool now. With a dead woman's staff. And a gold arm.

He wasn't cruel. Antho knew that. He'd never been cruel.

He was only very tired. And very sure.

---

"*No*," said Lex.

He hadn't got up. He was still kneeling on the stone at the rim, with his hands flat on it, looking down into the dark.

"*We don't push*."

Hask looked at him.

"*Smith*," he said. "*You've seen what the King does. You've seen my arm. Your wife's hand. The boy in the field.*" His voice didn't rise. It never rose. "*You want to ask him nicely?*"

"*Yes*," said Lex.

He stood up.

He turned round. To Hask. To all of them. Grey-eyed. Burned. With the handprint dark on his cheek.

"*She asked*," said Lex Vey. "*Hesper. At the pool. Nine hundred years ago. She didn't push anyone in. She didn't make anyone drink or not drink. She stood there with her crown in her hands and asked them to stop. And when they wouldn't, she poured hers back and walked away.*"

He looked at the dead. At the grey snow.

"*That's what we do*," he said. "*We don't take it. We ask for it.*"

---

The pool was quiet.

Then, from the dark, very small—

*[...yes...]* said Mal. *[...please...ask...]*

---

Hask didn't argue.

He looked at Lex for a long moment. Then he grunted. And shifted his weight on Kell's staff. And looked away. At the pool. At the three small gold lights at the bottom.

"*She'd have said that*," he said. Gruffly. "*Kell. She'd have said exactly that.*"

"*I know*," said Lex.

---

It was Pim who moved.

He'd been sitting on the rim all the while. Silent. Watching. With his knees drawn up and his arms round them.

He got up now. And walked. Away from the pool. Into the dead.

"*Pim*—" said Seraphine.

He didn't stop. He walked out into the grey snow, among the tall swaying columns, small and thin and dark-haired, until he was twenty yards off, in the middle of them. And stopped. And stood there.

And cupped his hands.

---

Antho went after him.

Not close. He stopped a few yards off. And watched.

The boy was standing among the dead with his hands cupped in front of him. The way he'd cupped them on the white road, in the summer, in front of a gold kitchen girl. Making a room. A little space in the air. Where something could breathe.

He was saying something.

Antho couldn't hear it. He moved closer.

---

"*The bread-woman*," Pim was saying. Quietly. To the dead. To the grey columns on every side. "*Is she here? The bread-woman. From the King's kitchen. She had a daughter. Ilda. Eight. A spit-girl.*" His voice cracked. He steadied it. "*She was coming apart. The first year. In the grey. With her daughter in her arms. And the King kept the girl. But not her.*"

The dead swayed. Saying their names.

"*I promised*," said Pim. To the cupped space in his hands. "*I promised Ilda. I'd come back. When it's big. When there's room. I'd find her mam.*" He swallowed. "*I'm finding her. I'm finding her now. Is she here?*"

---

Silence.

The names. All round. Soft. Over and over. *Wenlock. Bess. Hollin. Tamsy.* Thousands of them. The whole grey field, murmuring.

And then—

---

One of the columns bent.

Antho saw it. A tall one. A little way off. Grey. Fine as flour. Standing in the grey snow with the others. Swaying.

And then not swaying. *Bending*. Toward the boy. Slowly. The way a woman bends over a cot. The way a mother leans down to a child who's said her name in the dark.

And as it bent, Antho heard it. Under all the others. Not a name.

A word.

*...Ilda...?*

---

Pim didn't move.

He stood in the grey snow among the dead, with his hands cupped in front of him and the tears running down his face, and didn't make a sound.

"*She's here*," he said. Barely a whisper. To Antho. Without turning round. "*She's here. She's been waiting.*"

The grey column leaned over him. Toward the room in his hands.

*...Ilda...?*

"*She's coming*," said Pim. To the bread-woman. To the dust. To nine hundred years of a mother on the floor of an empty hall. "*She's walking. She's on her way. She's coming home.*"

He looked down at his cupped hands.

"*I promise*," he said.

---

Behind them, on the rim, in the dark of the pool, the seventh Well was crying.

Antho could hear it. Soft. Enormous. Like rain on a very large roof at night.

*[...I'm sorry...]* it was saying. *[...I'm sorry...I'm sorry...I'll grow...I promise I'll grow...]*

And on the stone, kneeling, with his hands flat on the cold rim and his head bowed over the dark water, his little brother was saying something back.

Antho couldn't hear what.

He thought it might have been *good morning*.
