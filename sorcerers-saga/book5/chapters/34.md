# The Doorkeeper

Nobody moved.

Antho heard the knock the way he'd heard the dead say their names on the hill above the Keep. Not with his ears. Lower. In the place under his ribs where the Murmur had lived for eight years, eating him, and where there was room now. Quiet. A threshold.

Three knocks.

Slow. Soft. Patient.

Polite.

---

Four hundred people stood on the rise in the windless air with their lanterns burning straight up and looked at the door.

Seven feet. Three and a half. Apple wood, blooming, gold as a lit window. Painted blue in four hundred strokes. Loaves and hammers and fish and bees and a rose and a big gold king's handprint and a small square child's. Breathing on its hinges. Latched.

Standing at the end of the road. On a threshold of grey stone. With nothing on either side of it.

And somebody on the other side of it, knocking.

---

Lex was still on his knees on the threshold. With Lark beside him, blue to the elbow, looking at the door with her head on one side, the way a dog does at a strange noise.

"*Somebody's there*," said Lark. Very clearly.

It was the longest sentence she'd ever said.

Nobody noticed.

---

Lex stood up.

Slowly. With his ribs. He put his thumb on the latch. On the plate. The small round plate the size of a child's palm, gold-grey, that had been a crown three weeks ago, at the bottom of the world.

And pressed.

The latch didn't lift.

---

He pressed again. Harder. Antho saw his brother's thumb go white on the plate. Saw the muscles in his forearm stand out. A smith's forearm. Twenty years of hammers.

The latch didn't move.

Not stuck. Not locked. Antho could see that from where he stood. It wasn't the kind of not moving that iron does when it's rusted, or jammed, or frozen. It was the kind of not moving a dog does when the wrong person calls it.

Lex took his thumb away.

He looked at the latch for a long time.

"*It's not mine*," he said. Quietly. Wonderingly. "*I made it. And it's not mine.*"

---

Wren tried.

Then Seren. Then Brannoc, with his gold fingertips, swearing under his breath. Then Teo. Then the King, who put one huge gold finger under the plate, very gently, as if afraid he'd snap it, and lifted, and the latch sat there and didn't lift, and the King took his hand away and looked at it and didn't seem surprised.

The knock came again.

Three times. Slow. Soft. Patient.

---

"*Antho*," said Seraphine.

Behind him. On the grass. With her horn spectacles and her quill and her hand on her belly.

He turned round.

She was looking at him. Not at the door. At him. The way she'd looked at him the first time, years ago, when he'd been a monster with a thing in his chest and she'd been a scribe with a notebook. As if she could see the shape of the gap round him. As if she'd heard something in the quiet.

"*It's a door*," said Seraphine Ilvane. Very softly. "*And you're the Threshold Warden.*"

---

He'd been called a lot of things.

*Error*. That was the first. At his Kindling. Sixteen. In front of the Stone on the green at Ashbourne. *ANTHOREN VEY. CLASS: ERROR.* And then the black flash, and the scream, and the fire, and his mother walking into the dark, and eight years of not being anything anyone had a name for.

*Hollow-Touched Warlock*. *Corrupted Unique*. The net hadn't known what to call him. Neither had he.

*Monster*. People had called him that. In villages. On roads. Before Seraphine. Before Lex came and found him.

*Brother*. The thing in his chest had called him that. In the dark. In the mad years. *Brother. Mal.* And then, later, when he'd understood, when the thing in his chest had turned out not to be a monster at all but a hungry Well, a little one, a hollow, a room that had been waiting nine hundred years for somebody to ask it in—

*Brother*. It still called him that.

*Threshold Warden*. That was the last. After the Hollowmaw. After the Heart. The one who stood at the edge. Who kept the dark from the light and the light from the dark and walked between them and spoke to both.

He'd never thought about what it meant.

A threshold was the bit under a door.

---

He walked down the slope.

The grass was soft under his boots. Real grass. Green. The lanterns behind him burned straight up. The four hundred watched him. He didn't look at them. He looked at the door.

His brother stepped aside.

Lex didn't say anything. He just stepped off the threshold onto the grass, with Lark on his hip, blue to the elbow. And looked at Antho. Crooked smile. The one that made him look about twelve.

Antho stepped up onto the grey stone.

---

It hummed.

Under his feet. The Ashbourne Stone. That had looked at every child in Ashbourne for four hundred years, and at Lex, and split. That had stood on the green while the whole village walked past it into their lives. That had looked at a boy of sixteen, once, and screamed, and flashed black, and sent him home to a cottage that burned that night.

It knew him.

It hummed anyway. Low. Deep. Slow. Like a bell a long way off.

And a panel opened in front of his face.

He looked at it.

He hadn't looked at his panel since the freeze broke. He hadn't had time. Three weeks on the road. A boat on a river. The Heart. He'd glanced at it once, on the white floor, when the WORLD NOTICE came, and seen the numbers moving, and looked away.

They were still moving.

Faster than he'd ever seen them move.

He watched the last number tick. And tick. And tick. Not a hair at a time. A trickle. A stream. Pouring in from somewhere. From the door. From the threshold under his feet. From the four hundred people on the rise behind him, and the lanterns, and the green road going back east two hundred miles with a light every mile.

From everyone who'd ever kept a window.

---

He didn't feel it. Not the way he'd felt levels when he was young, when every one had been a jolt, a fever, a rush of heat. He just stood on the threshold and watched the panel change. *98*. *99*. Like watching a candle burn down.

The knock came again. From the other side.

Three times.

---

The numbers went on.

He watched them. Lex had told him, on the river, in the boat, in a low voice in the dark, what had happened at a hundred. The gold panel. The question. *TAKE UP THE CROWN*. *HOLD THE NET*. *KEEP WHAT IS YOURS*.

He'd laughed, when Lex told him. He'd laughed so hard in the boat that the Ferryman had turned his hooded head to look.

He wasn't laughing now.

---

---

And under it, slowly, the way frost spreads—

---

:::root
TIER V — SOVEREIGN

KEEP THE DOOR.
HOLD THE EDGE.
NONE PASS BUT BY YOUR LEAVE.
:::

---

Antho stared at it.

*None pass but by your leave*.

He knew what it was offering. He understood it at once, the way he'd understood the Murmur in the end, the way he understood the dead. It wasn't a crown. There was no crown any more. His brother had beaten it into a latch.

It was the door.

The door at the edge of the world. The only door. Every dead soul in Caldor would come to it. Every grey column of dust. Every lantern-bearer, at the end. Every old woman under every apple tree. They'd all come to the door at the end of the road, and stand on the threshold, and wait. And he'd decide.

*In*. Or *not yet*.

*Through*. Or *stay*.

Forever. Never tired. Never old. Never gone. Like the Ferryman, but more. Not rowing. *Ruling*. Sovereign of the Threshold. King of the edge of the world.

Keeper.

---

He thought about the King.

Standing on the white road for nine hundred years with nine thousand people behind him, all of them kept, all of them gold, because he couldn't stand to watch them go.

He thought about the bread-woman. On the floor of the empty hall. Coming apart with her daughter in her arms. And a gold king walking past.

He thought about the six lanterns in a grey square in the Drowned Country. And the dead standing round them for years, in the cold, because it was warm there. And then turning, all of them, when there was somewhere better to go. Not because anyone told them. Because they could.

He thought about Seraphine, on the grass behind him, with her hand on her belly.

He thought: *if I take this, I'll never die*.

He thought: *I'll never see it grow up, either. Not properly. Not the way you see someone grow up when you're growing old beside them. I'll stand at this door forever and it'll come to me at the end, an old woman, a stranger, and I'll decide whether to let my own child through.*

---

"*No*," said Antho Vey.

The gold went out of it.

And the question came. The way Lex had said it would. In the hum. In the threshold under his feet. In the space where the panel had been.

WHAT ARE YOU?

He didn't have to think about it.

He'd been thinking about it all his life. In the ditch on the Blightmarch road. On the hill above the Keep, counting eleven hundred lanterns every morning. At the rim. At the pool. In a grey square, crying.

*What are you*.

He looked at the door in front of him. Blue. Blooming. Gold. Latched. With somebody on the other side, knocking.

"*I'm the one who opens it*," said Antho.

Behind him, he heard his brother make a sound.

He didn't turn round. He knew what it was. Lex, on the grass, with Lark on his hip, laughing. Helplessly. Painfully. Holding his ribs. The way Antho had laughed on the white floor of the Heart.

"*Common*," said Lex. Wheezing. "*Antho. You absolute—*"

"*Shut up*," said Antho.

He was laughing too. He couldn't help it. His eyes were running.

*Doorkeeper*. *Common*.

Like a man at the gate of a field. Like a woman at the door of a bakery at five in the morning, turning the sign. Like a boy at the Keep, at dawn, letting the cat out.

Like everyone.

---

He put his thumb on the latch.

---

It lifted.

---

Not easily. Not by itself. He had to press. The plate was cold under his thumb and then warm, and the bar rose out of the keeper, slowly, the way a latch rises on any cottage door, with a small dry scrape of metal on metal. He felt it come up. Felt the catch clear the notch.

And the door swung.

On its breathing hinges. Out. Toward him. The way a door swings when you've lifted the latch and somebody on the other side has been leaning on it.

And the bell rang.

Malachi's bell. Hesper's bell. On its bracket over the frame. Once. Small. Clear.

The way a bell rings when somebody opens the door.

---

He saw the room.

---

It was dark.

Not grey. Not the grey of the grey country, that was still and flat and dead. Not the nothing beyond the seam, that had never been anything. Dark. Soft. Deep. The dark of a room at night with the lamps out and everyone asleep and the fire banked in the hearth. The dark of a cellar where things are kept to grow. The dark of the inside of a seed.

Room.

And stars.

Not in the sky. In the dark. All round. Near and far. Small lights. Thousands. Millions. Hanging in the soft dark like the stars on the hill above the Keep. And between them, in the gaps—Antho saw it, and couldn't breathe—shapes. The shapes of the dead. Drifting. Going in. Going on. Not lost. Not grey. Just going. The way people go through a house at night, from room to room, with a candle.

And far off. Down. At the bottom of the dark. Small.

A pool.

---

He knew it.

He'd have known it anywhere. He'd sat on its rim every morning for a year. A pool in a green circle. In a field. In the east. With a ring of lanterns round it in the long grass, five hundred and fifty-six, burning gold. With three lights in its middle, under the water. And a stone house on the slope above it with a fat chimney and a lantern in the window.

And three hundred and something gold people standing in the grass round the rim, each with a grey column of dust in front of them, bending.

And a boy.

Sitting on the rim. With his knees drawn up. Fifteen. Thin. With a stub of pencil behind his ear.

Looking up.

---

Pim saw him.

Across the whole of the dark. Five hundred miles. Through a door that hadn't been there an hour ago. A boy on a pool's rim, looking up at the sky over the green circle, and seeing—Antho didn't know what he saw. A door in the sky. A gold window, open, high up, with a man standing in it.

Pim stood up.

He didn't say anything. He never did, if he could help it.

He just lifted his hand. Slowly. And waved.

---

Antho waved back.

He couldn't speak. He stood on the threshold of the door at the edge of the world with his hand up, waving at a boy five hundred miles away across the soft dark of his brother's room, and couldn't say a word.

*[...brother...]* said the room.

All round him. Soft. Deep. Glad. Like the sound in a well when you drop a stone and listen and hear it land, at last, in water.

*[...brother...you opened it...]*

"*Yes*," said Antho. Hoarse. "*Hello, Mal.*"

*[...hello...]* A pause. Shy. *[...there's someone here...he's been waiting...he knocked...]*

---

The Ferryman stood on the other side of the door.

Antho hadn't seen him. He'd been looking past. At the stars. At the pool. At Pim. He looked now.

Tall. Grey. Hooded. A yard away. Standing in the soft dark on the far side of the threshold, with his long oar in one hand like a staff, and his lantern in the other. With the small gold flame on its post.

He'd been there all along. On the other side. Knocking.

---

"*Warden*," said the Ferryman.

"*Doorkeeper*," said Antho. "*Now.*"

The hood tilted. Very slightly.

"*So I see*," said the Ferryman.

He didn't move. He stood on his side of the threshold, in the dark, in the room. Not coming through. Antho looked at him and understood that he wouldn't. Not without being asked. He'd stood on the edge of every room in the world for longer than there'd been rooms, and he'd never once come in.

"*You knocked*," said Antho.

"*I did*."

"*Why*?"

The Ferryman was silent for a long time.

"*Because there's a door*," he said. "*There's never been a door before. Only edges. Only the river. Only the shore. I've stood on a lot of edges.*" The hooded head turned. Slowly. Looking at the frame. At the blue paint. At the blooming wood. At the bell. "*I've never knocked on anything. I wanted to see what it was like.*"

"*What was it like*?"

"*Strange*," said the Ferryman. "*Waiting to be let in.*" A pause. "*I don't know how they bear it.*"

---

*[He's been rowing for a very long time*,] said the lantern. On its post. Helpfully. Courteously. *[He told me. On the river. He said he'd quite like to sit down.]*

The Ferryman looked down at the flame.

Antho had never seen a hooded face look embarrassed. He saw it now.

---

"*May I come in*?" said the Ferryman.

---

Antho thought about it.

He thought about a moth at a window at Harrow, in the autumn, years ago. *May I come in*? And someone at the window, with a lamp. *No*.

He thought about a gold king at the doors of the Forge at midnight. *May I come in*? And Brannoc. *Mind the floor. It's been swept.*

He thought about a door. That was all a door was. A place where somebody asked, and somebody answered.

Up was yes.

Down was no.

---

"*Yes*," said Antho. "*Come in. Mind the step.*"

---

The Ferryman came through the door.

Slowly. One long grey foot over the threshold. Onto the grey stone. Then the other. Ducking his hooded head under the lintel, because he was very tall, and the door was a cottage door, made by a smith for people.

And stood on the threshold of the world.

In the light.

---

It was evening. Antho hadn't noticed. The sun was going down. Low. Gold. And it came across the rise and the lanterns and the grass and the grey stone and fell on the Ferryman's hood.

He stood there.

He lifted his long grey face. Out of the shadow of the hood. Just a little. Just enough. Antho saw it, for the first time, in all the times he'd called him. Old. Very old. Lined like a riverbank. Grey as river stones. With eyes that were the colour of water over stones in the dark.

He looked at the sun.

"*Oh*," said the Ferryman.

---

He sat down.

On the threshold. On the grey stone. Just like that. As if his legs had decided for him. He sat down with his back against the door frame and his long oar across his knees and his lantern on the stone beside him, and the evening sun on his face, and closed his eyes.

"*I'd like to sit down*," said the Ferryman. A little late. To nobody. "*Just for a bit.*"

Nobody said anything.

Four hundred people on the rise, with their lanterns burning straight up in the windless air, watched the oldest thing in the world sit on a doorstep in the sun with his eyes shut.

---

*[Is this the end*?] said the lantern. On the stone beside him. Interested. *[Of the story? I'd like to write it down properly.]*

The Ferryman didn't open his eyes.

"*Not yet*," he said.

---

Antho stood in the doorway.

Between the room and the world. With one hand on the frame and one on the open door. With the soft dark behind him, full of stars and shapes and a pool five hundred miles away with a boy on its rim. With the evening in front of him, and the rise, and the four hundred, and the lanterns.

With the Ferryman sitting at his feet.

He looked up the slope.

At Seraphine. On the grass. With her hand on her belly and her quill stopped on the page and her face wet behind the horn. At Lex, with Lark asleep on his shoulder, blue to the elbow. At Wren. At his mother, with the clay lamp. At Brannoc.

At the King.

---

The King was coming down the slope.

Slowly. Gold. Enormous. The grass staying green under his feet. With a white petal in one hand. And a thin silver-grey crown in the other.

Rosie was beside him.

Barefoot. Forty years old. With thorns in her hair and her hands still bound in linen. With a small folded packet held against her chest. Walking beside her brother the way you'd walk beside someone very old, very tired, going somewhere they've put off going for a long time. Close. Not holding his arm. Ready to.

They stopped at the bottom of the slope. A few yards from the threshold.

The King looked at the door. At the open door. At the soft dark beyond it, full of stars. At the pool, far off, at the bottom. At the three hundred gold people in the grass round its rim.

His household.

He looked at them for a long time.

Then he looked down at the crown in his hand. Hes's crown. Small. Silver-grey. Seven points. Thin. Plain. The crown that had said no to the Key and broken it in three. The crown Harrow women had carried in a box under a flagstone for nine hundred years.

*It's got one more no in it*, Seren had said.

---

"*Rosie*," said Oszric.

"*Yes, Oz*."

"*Will you hold this a moment*?"

He gave her the petal. White. Pink at the edges. She took it in her bound hand. Carefully. The way you'd hold a moth.

And the King lifted Hesper's crown in both his huge gold hands.

And put it on.

---

It was far too small for him. It sat on top of his great gold head like a ring on a thumb. Silver-grey on gold. Ridiculous. Nobody laughed.

The King stood at the bottom of the slope at the edge of the world with his little sister's crown on his head, and looked at the open door.

"*Walk me to the door*," he said.
