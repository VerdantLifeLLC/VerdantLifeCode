@@PART Part One: The Open Edge
# Good Morning

"*Good morning*," said Lex to the fire.

It was banked down to a heap of grey ash with a red eye in the middle. He knelt on the hearthstone and blew on it, gently, the way you'd wake someone who'd been up all night.

It woke. Flickered. Caught the kindling. Climbed.

*Good morning*, said the fire.

Not in words. A warmth. A lift. A small bright gladness. The way a dog is glad when you come down the stairs.

He waited.

Nothing.

He'd known there'd be nothing. Seven weeks. Forty-nine mornings. He'd counted. He didn't want to count, but he was a smith, and smiths count. Strokes. Heats. Days.

Forty-nine mornings of kneeling on this stone and saying good morning and waiting, for a breath, for half a breath, for a dry small voice in the back of his head to say *[Good morning. You're up early.]*

Forty-nine mornings of nothing.

He fed the fire a stick of birch. It took it gladly.

He said it anyway. Every morning. Out loud.

*It would be rude not to*.

---

It was Midwinter.

The shortest day. The longest night. Outside the east window the sky was still black, with a thin grey line along the top of the birch wood where dawn was thinking about it. Snow on the ground. Snow on the sill. Snow on the roof of the lean-to, and on the stepping stones across the stream, and on the grey stone by the water with the lanterns round it.

He could see them from the window. Small gold lights. Twenty of them. In a ring. In the snow.

They'd never gone out.

Hollis Penn came up from Ashbourne every second day with a can of oil and filled them. Nobody had asked him to. He just came. Trudged up the track through the birch wood in his baker's apron with flour on his elbows and filled the lanterns round the grave and trudged back down again. Lex had tried to thank him, once. Hollis had gone red to the ears and said *it's only oil* and fled.

Twenty lanterns. Iron and tin and clay. And a turnip, hollowed out, with a candle in it, that Pim had made, which had gone soft and black in the frost and which nobody had the heart to take away.

Round a grey stone. By a stream.

With two words cut into it, in letters Lex had cut himself, on the morning after Lantern Night, with a cold chisel and a hammer and his hands shaking so badly he'd had to stop three times.

*ARION VEY*.

And under that, smaller, because his mother had asked for it:

*He turned back twice*.

---

He stood at the window and looked at the lanterns until the grey line over the birches had gone pale.

Then he went to make the porridge.

---

The cottage at Harrow was four rooms and a loft. Grey Vale stone and new oak beams and a door that stuck in wet weather. A table by the east window. A small iron lantern on the sill, with one word cut into the base where only someone who picked it up would see.

*HERE*.

Still lit. Always lit. For whoever was in the room.

This morning, that was Lex, and the porridge pot, and—arriving at speed down the loft ladder, backwards, on her stomach, with a sound like a sack of potatoes being thrown down a well—his daughter.

"*Lark*," said Lex.

She landed at the bottom. On her bottom. Sat there for a moment, considering whether this was the sort of thing she cried about.

Decided it wasn't.

Got up.

---

She'd been walking for three weeks.

If you could call it walking. It was more like falling forward and catching herself, over and over, very fast, with her arms out and her square fists clenched and a look of fierce concentration on her round face, like someone carrying a full bucket across an icy yard. She went everywhere at that speed. She didn't seem to have another one.

She came across the floor now. *Thump*. *Thump*. *Thump*. Lurching. Wobbling. Her fluff of dark hair standing straight up on top of her head like the crest of an outraged bird. In a nightshirt Wren had cut down from one of Lex's, so long it trailed behind her like a train.

She arrived at his legs. Grabbed his breeches in both fists. Hung there. Looked up at him.

Grey eyes. Going green. More green than grey now. From the edges in, like a field in spring.

"*Morning*," said Lex.

"*Mmo-ning*," said Lark. Approximately.

---

She'd said it first on the morning after Lantern Night. To the fire.

He'd been kneeling on the hearthstone. And she'd come across the floor on her hands and knees—she hadn't been walking then—and sat down beside him and looked at the flames. Very seriously. The way she looked at everything. And said it.

*Mo. Nn. Mmo-ning*.

And the fire had leaned toward her.

He'd seen it. All the flames. Bending. Together. Toward the small square figure on the hearthstone, the way grass leans in the wind, the way a dog leans against your legs. Glad.

And for a moment—

He didn't think about that moment. He'd decided not to. He'd decided it was a fire being a fire, and a baby being a baby, and a man who'd lost a voice in his head hearing it where it wasn't.

Probably.

He'd decided that forty-nine times.

---

"*Porridge*," he said.

"*No*," said Lark.

"*Yes*."

"*No*."

"*You haven't even seen it yet*."

Lark considered this. Then let go of his breeches, sat down hard on the floor, and said, with enormous satisfaction, as if settling the matter for all time:

"*No*."

---

It was her word.

She had others now. *Mmo-ning*. *Mam*, for Wren. *Da*, for him, sometimes, when she wanted something. *Ash*, for the dragon, and for anything else large and frightening and wonderful. *Gog*, for the goat. *Hot*, for the fire and the stove and the forge and, for some reason, the moon.

But *no* was her word. Her first. The one she'd said to a king.

She'd been sitting on the hearthrug in his mother's cottage, three weeks before her first birthday. And the biggest man in the world had been kneeling in front of her. Gold. Glowing like metal in a crucible. With his great gold hand held out. Reaching for her. Very slowly. Very gently. The way you'd reach for a bird.

*Come here, little one*.

And she'd looked at him. At the hand. At the furnace eyes.

And taken her fist out of her mouth.

*No*.

---

He'd laughed.

That was the thing Lex remembered. The Gilded King, kneeling on the floor, with his hand out, and a baby telling him no. And he'd thrown back his great gold head and laughed. Not cruelly. Not angrily. As if it were the best thing he'd heard in nine hundred years.

Then he'd taken his hand back.

And he hadn't reached for her again.

---

"*Please*," said Lex.

"*No*."

"*There's honey*."

A pause. A long one. The round face working.

"*Hunny*," said Lark. Thoughtfully. Not committing to anything.

"*Lots of honey*."

She got up. *Thump*. *Thump*. Arrived at the bench. Raised both arms. Waited to be lifted, with the air of a queen waiting for her carriage.

He lifted her.

She grabbed his nose.

---

Wren came down while the porridge was still too hot. Barefoot. Yawning. In his old shirt and her own breeches, with her fox-red hair in a braid down her back that she'd slept on and was coming apart.

She stopped at the bottom of the ladder.

Looked at the table. At her husband, with his nose in their daughter's fist. At their daughter, with honey on both cheeks and in her hair and, somehow, on her eyebrow.

"*I was gone ten minutes*," said Wren.

"*Fifteen*."

"*How is it on her eyebrow*?"

"*She's very thorough*."

Wren came and sat down. Kissed Lark on the one bit of forehead that didn't have honey on it. Kissed Lex on the cheek. On the left side. On the handprint.

She always kissed that side. He'd noticed. Since the Blightmarch. Since a Moth Queen slapped him in the deep and left her hand there, grey-gold, five fingers, a palm, permanent. Wren always kissed it. As if to say *I see it*. As if to say *it's mine too*.

"*You were up early*," she said.

"*Midwinter*. *It's dark till eight.*"

"*You were up at four*."

He didn't answer.

She didn't push. She took the spoon off him and started feeding Lark herself, with the brisk efficiency of a woman who had been feeding babies for fourteen months and had stopped expecting it to be clean.

---

He watched her do it.

Her right hand on the spoon. Her left hand on the bowl.

Her left hand.

The first two fingers. And the thumb. Gold.

Not gilt. Not paint. *Gold*. From the tips to the second knuckle. Stopped. Kept. Smooth and bright and cold, like the fingers of a statue, with the nails still on them, gold too. She could move the hand. The wrist. The other two fingers. But the gold ones didn't bend.

The Night of Glass. Midsummer. The yard at Harrow full of gold dust and glass and screaming, and Orrin's storm coming over the wall, and Wren with her bow up and her mist pouring out of her, fogging every pane, every barrel, every bucket, so the Glass Queen couldn't find a way through.

And the dust had come down on her drawing hand.

She'd kept shooting. Lex had seen it. Afterward. Seen the arrows in the yard, in the mud, in the gold, a dozen of them, every one of them loosed after her fingers stopped.

She hadn't said anything about it. Not that night. Not since. She'd learned to draw with her right. She'd learned to tie her boots with three fingers. She'd learned to braid her hair one-handed, badly. She'd learned to hold their daughter in the crook of her left arm with the gold fingers spread so they wouldn't press.

She never complained.

He sometimes wished she would. So he'd have something to *do*.

---

"*You're staring*," said Wren. Without looking up.

"*Sorry*."

"*It's fine*." She wiped Lark's eyebrow with her thumb. Her right thumb. "*Is it the fingers or the porridge?*"

"*Both*."

"*The porridge is your fault*," said Wren. "*The fingers are his.*" She spooned another mouthful in. Lark took it. Under protest. "*Eat yours. You're going over the stream.*"

"*How do you know*?"

"*Because it's Midwinter*," said his wife. "*And she'll have been up since four too.*"

---

He went over the stream.

Across the stepping stones, in the snow, with his breath smoking. Five stones. Flat grey ones, slick with ice. He'd laid them himself, two springs ago, so his father's chair could cross. Then he'd built the ramp on the other side, when the stones got too much. Then he'd stopped needing to.

He didn't look at the grave as he passed it.

He always looked at the grave as he passed it.

He looked at it now. The grey stone. The twenty lanterns. The soft black turnip. The snow on top, a little drift, like a cap. *ARION VEY*.

*Morning*, he thought.

He didn't wait for an answer.

---

The blue-door cottage was small and stone and one storey, with an oak ramp up to the door and the boards down off every window now. Brannoc had come up the week after and taken them down, one by one, and stacked them in the lean-to without a word. And polished the glass with a rag. And gone away again.

The door was open a crack.

Smoke from the chimney. A smell of tea.

He knocked anyway.

"*It's open*," said his mother. "*It's always open. You know that. Stop knocking.*"

---

She was at the table under the west window.

In a grey shawl. With a cup of tea going cold in front of her, and a book open beside it. Grey cloth binding. Rubbed pale at the corners. Thick. Full of loose pages tucked in at the back, and a ribbon, and a pressed flower.

The grey book.

She looked up when he came in. Brown eyes. Not grey. Never grey. That was his father. Lex had his father's eyes, and Antho had his mother's, and when she looked up at him in the morning light he always had a moment of—

Nothing. A moment of nothing. Of reaching for something that wasn't there.

"*Sit*," said Seren. "*There's tea in the pot.*"

He sat. He poured.

The cup was blue. Chipped at the rim. Not lopsided. Not too thick. Not the one with the thumb-print. That one had gone gold, and walked away east in a gold hand, and was somewhere in the world now, with a gold disc of tea in it nobody could ever drink.

He drank.

It was the good kind. In a tin.

---

On the sill behind her, in the west window, a lantern.

Horn. Four panes. A crooked hinge—the left one. He'd meant to fix it. He'd been meaning to fix it for a year. He'd made the whole thing in the spring, at the forge, at night, when nobody was watching. Shaped the iron frame. Shaved the horn panes so thin you could read through them. Cut two words into the base, where only someone who picked it up would see.

And wrapped it in sacking. And hidden it. And carried it across the grey country and back. For her birthday. For Lantern Night.

It was lit.

He'd lit it himself, on the morning after Lantern Night, in the grey dawn, with his own fire. He'd carried it in and set it on this sill, beside the candle Wren had left there. Facing out. Facing west. Facing the road and the birch wood and the grave by the stream and the edge of the world.

*TOO LATE*.

It hadn't gone out either.

---

"*It's Midwinter*," said his mother.

"*I know*."

"*The shortest day*." She turned a page. Not reading it. Just turning it. "*He used to say it was the best one. Because after it, they all get longer.*" A small dry sound. Not quite a laugh. "*He said that every year. I remember. I remember every single time he said it.*"

Lex said nothing.

"*Eleven times*," said Seren Harrow. "*Eleven Midwinters. Ten in the old cottage, before he went away. And one here, last year, that I didn't remember at the time and remember now.*" She shook her head. "*Your brother at four. You in a basket. The year it snowed so hard we couldn't open the door and he climbed out of the window to dig us out, in his nightshirt, and fell in a drift.*" She put her hand flat on the open page. "*I remember all of them now. Every one. And there won't be any more.*"

---

That was the thing.

He'd thought about it a lot. In the forge. At night. Kneeling at the hearth.

For a year and a half, his mother hadn't remembered his father at all. Not one day. Not one word. She'd given every memory of him to the Ferryman to come home. She'd come out of the dark at the Hollowmaw past eleven hundred lanterns to an old man in a wheeled chair she'd never seen before, and said, politely, *you have a nice voice*.

And fallen in love with him. All over again. From nothing. In a year and a half. An old man she didn't know, getting older every week, dying in front of her.

And then, at the very end—two nights before Lantern Night, in a bed by this window—she'd got it all back.

Ember had paid for it.

A love for a love. That was the Ferryman's price. Ember had given its love for Lex, the whole of it, everything it had felt for him in seven years of living in the back of his head. And the Ferryman had given Seren back thirty years.

Every Midwinter. Every word. Every snowdrift.

And then his father had died. That same night. With her hand in his.

She'd had him for an hour. All of him. The whole thirty years, every memory, all at once, back where it belonged. For an hour.

And now she had to lose him twice.

---

"*Mam*," said Lex.

"*Don't*," said his mother. "*Don't be kind. I can't stand it this morning. Your brother was kind all of last week and I nearly hit him with the kettle.*"

"*I wasn't going to be kind*."

"*What were you going to be*?"

He thought about it.

"*Hungry*," he said. "*Wren made porridge. There's honey in Lark's eyebrows.*"

His mother looked at him for a long moment.

Then she laughed. A real one. Short and surprised and cracked down the middle, like ice on a pond. And put her hand over her face. And sat there with her shoulders shaking.

He didn't know if she was laughing or crying.

He didn't think she knew either.

He reached across the table and put his hand on her arm. And left it there. And drank his tea.

---

After a while she took her hand away from her face.

"*There*," she said. "*That's done.*" She wiped her eyes on the corner of the shawl. "*Every morning. I sit down and I open his book and I think, this morning I won't. And then I do.*" She sniffed. "*Harrow women don't cry, you know. My mother never cried. Not once. Not even when she was dying.*"

"*Did it help*?"

"*No*," said Seren. "*I don't think so. I think it just made her very good at not crying.*" She looked at the open book. "*He cried all the time. Did you know? At weddings. At songs. At the first lambs. He cried when you were born. Both of you. He cried at the Spire, the first day, on the steps, in the rain, when I told him my name.*" A pause. "*I thought he was mad. I was nineteen. I thought, who is this man, crying on the steps because a girl told him her name.*"

"*Was he*?"

"*Mad*?" She smiled. "*Completely. He was two hundred years old and he'd never been in love before. It went to his head.*" She turned another page. "*It never came out again.*"

---

He looked at the page she'd turned to.

His father's handwriting. Small. Neat. Slanting a little to the right. The hand of a man who'd kept ledgers for two hundred years.

It was the last page.

He knew it was. He'd seen it. On the morning after. His father had written it himself, in the spring, after the long list of names—the Vhessan dead, eleven thousand of them, every one he could find, every one his breach had killed—and after Isaura Kell, and after Elric Thorne. At the very bottom. Last. Where he'd asked to be.

One line.

*Arion Vey. Who had a very long life and a very good wife. Twice.*

And under it, in another hand—Lex's hand, cramped and heavy, a smith's hand, pressing too hard—the date.

That was all.

That was the whole of it.

---

"*I read it every morning*," said his mother. "*Every morning. Like a madwoman.*" She closed the book. Gently. "*Then I go and stand at the window and look at the lantern and think, too late. Every single morning.*" She looked at him. "*Do you know what I think, Lex?*"

"*What*?"

"*I think he knew*," said Seren Harrow. "*When he wrote it. Page nine. Don't ever leave a light on for me.*" She put her hand flat on the grey cover. "*I think he knew I'd do it anyway. I think he wanted me to have something to say back.*"

---

He stayed an hour.

He mended the hinge.

The left one, on the horn lantern. He took it off the sill and carried it to the table and opened the little iron door and looked at the flame inside—small, gold, steady, on its twist of tallowed cotton—and then shut it again, carefully, and fixed the hinge with a pin and a tap of the little hammer from his belt while it was still lit.

It took four minutes.

He'd been meaning to do it for a year.

"*There*," he said. And set it back on the sill.

His mother looked at it. At the hinge. Straight now. At the two words in the base.

"*Thank you*," she said.

"*It's only a pin*."

"*I know what it is*," said Seren.

---

He went back across the stream in the full grey light of the shortest day.

Past the grave. Past the twenty lanterns. Past the black turnip.

*Morning*, he thought.

He went into the forge.

---

The forge at Harrow was small. A lean-to against the east wall of the cottage, stone on three sides and open on the fourth, with a slate roof and a chimney he'd built himself. One anvil. One hearth. One bellows. A rack of tools. A water barrel with a lid on it now—a wooden lid, heavy, that he'd made in the summer and nailed a strap to, so that nothing could look out of it at night.

Every pane at Harrow was horn now. Every window. Every lantern.

Every bucket had a lid.

He lit the hearth.

*Good morning*, said the fire.

*Morning*, said Lex. Out loud.

He waited. Half a breath.

Nothing.

He picked up the little hammer.

---

The basket was under the bench.

Wicker. Old. Lined with straw. The kind of basket you'd carry eggs in, or apples, or a cat to the farrier. It had been his father's. Then it had come down to the bottom of the world with him, on Lantern Night, through Mal's pool and down the long dark to the Knot.

He pulled it out. And knelt. And lifted the lid.

---

Three pieces of black iron.

Not black like soot. Black like the space between stars. Lying in the straw. A ring—the bow. A short square bar notched like a ward—the bit. And a long slim shaft—the blade—that had once held them together.

The Key of Shadows.

Nine hundred years ago a frightened young man with a little hammer had forged it out of his own shadow. It had locked the starving Well. It had unwritten names. It had been held by a traitor and barred by a forge-thing called the Lockward and kept in the Knot at the bottom of the world by an old man's thread for four years.

And on Lantern Night that old man had put on a dead girl's crown and turned it, one last time, against the Veil.

And it had come apart in his hand. Into three. The way it had been made.

Lex had picked up the pieces. He didn't know why. He'd knelt on the black glass at the bottom of the world with his brother beside him and the Veil falling somewhere far off overhead, and picked them up, one by one, and put them in the basket.

He hadn't touched them since.

---

And beside them, in the straw—

A crown.

Small. Plain. Grey. Not silver. Not gold. Not set with anything. Seven points, short and blunt, like the teeth of a comb. The kind of crown you'd make for a child out of whatever metal was lying around.

Hesper's.

The tenth heir. The girl who wouldn't drink. Who stood at the edge of a black pool in a hidden garden nine hundred years ago with her brothers and sisters kneeling round it, and took off her crown, and set it on the grass, and said *no*.

It had said no ever since.

For nine hundred years. In a box. On a chair in an empty tower. On an old man's head, at the bottom of the world, with the whole net offering him everything.

*No*. *Not me. Not ever.*

---

He didn't pick it up.

He'd tried, once. In the first week. And it had been cold in his hand, very cold, colder than iron in snow, and very still. Not saying anything. Not to him. Just—resting. The way a dog rests by a door.

*I think it might have one more no in it*, his brother had said.

Antho had carried it out of the dark. Up through the pool. Home. He'd held it in his scarred hands the whole way, very gently, the way you'd carry a bird with a broken wing.

And put it in the basket. Beside the three pieces of the Key.

And they'd sat there. In the straw. Under the bench. For seven weeks. While Lex lit the forge every morning and said good morning to the fire and made nails and hinges and horseshoes and pot-hooks for half of Ashbourne, because his hands needed something to do.

He looked at them.

*What am I supposed to do with you*?

They didn't answer either.

---

He put the lid back on.

And stood up.

And because it was Midwinter, and because he was a smith, and smiths count—

*Status*, thought Lex.

---

He looked at it.

Silver-blue. Hanging in the air in front of him, over the anvil, where only he could see it. The way it had hung every day of his life since a stranger walked into the Ashbourne forge and the seal on his blood cracked open in a storm of gold fire.

Ninety-nine.

The top of Tier IV. As high as you could go. As high as anyone had gone since Aurelian.

There was nothing after ninety-nine. Nothing anyone knew of. Not in the net. Not for anyone with a thread. Except, maybe, the thing nobody wanted. The thing his brother had felt under his hands at the bottom of the world, on Lantern Night, when the net turned toward an old man in a borrowed crown and offered him *everything*.

The crown in the road.

Still lying there.

---

He didn't look at the level.

He looked at the bottom of the panel.

At the empty space under the last line. Where, for seven years, there'd been something else. Not on the panel. Not in silver. In *gold*. Bleeding through. A line of warm ember-coloured text, small, at the very bottom, in the corner, the way you'd sign a letter.

*[Good morning, Lex*.]

Or *[You're doing it wrong]*. Or *[Footnote six hundred and eleven: the smith has once again failed to eat breakfast]*.

Seven years.

He looked at the empty space under the panel for a long time.

Then he closed it. And picked up the hammer. And drew a bar of iron out of the fire, glowing, and laid it on the anvil, and struck.

*Clang*.

The forge rang.

It was the only voice there was.

---

He made eleven nails before noon.

Hinges for Hollis Penn's bakehouse door. A pot-hook for the widow Tull. A new tyre for a cartwheel off one of the Coldwater wagons, which he did in the yard in the snow, with the wheel on the stone and the tyre heated in a ring of fire he called up out of the ground, because it was quicker than the hearth and he wanted to feel it. The heat. The roar. The weight of it.

*[Show-off*,] said nobody.

He shrank the tyre on with a bucket of snow. It hissed. Steam went up in a white cloud into the grey Midwinter sky.

He stood in the yard in the steam and breathed.

---

Brannoc came up the track at noon.

He came most days. On the pretence of bringing something—a sack of charcoal, a bundle of bar-iron, a pie from Hollis that Hollis had sent up and Brannoc had eaten half of on the way. He never stayed long. He'd stand in the doorway of the lean-to with his great arms folded and look at whatever Lex was making, and grunt, and say something about the quench. And go away again.

He was sixty-five. Huge. Bald. Soot in the creases of his face that never washed out, and a beard like a burnt hedge. He'd taken Lex in at eleven, after the fire. He'd swung him up onto the anvil on the first day and said *this is yours now, lad*.

He was the father Lex had actually had.

He stood in the doorway now. With a sack over his shoulder and half a pie in his other hand.

Not his other hand. His *left* hand.

The fingertips. All four. And the thumb. Gold. To the first knuckle. Smooth and bright and cold. Where Orrin's dust had come down on them on the green at Ashbourne in the spring, when the gilt-storm came and Brannoc stood between it and a cart full of children with nothing but a hammer and a hymn.

He'd laughed about it. Afterward. Held them up in the firelight in the Crooked Kettle and said *look at that, lads, I'm worth something at last*.

He'd never complained either.

Lex sometimes thought that was the worst thing about the people he loved. None of them ever complained.

---

"*Pie*," said Brannoc.

"*Half a pie*."

"*It was a long walk*." He held it out. Lex took it. Mutton and onion. Still warm. Brannoc set the sack down. Charcoal. Then looked at the cartwheel on the stone in the yard. At the tyre. At the faint ring of scorched earth round it in the snow.

"*Hm*," said Brannoc.

"*It's tight*."

"*I can see it's tight*." He walked over. Bent. Looked at the join. Ran his good thumb along the rim. Then his gold one. *Tick*. Gold on iron. "*Called it up out of the ground again, did you?*"

"*It was quicker*."

"*Quicker*." Brannoc straightened up. Slowly. His back cracked. "*You've got a hearth, lad. Right there. Bellows and all. Took you a week to build.*"

"*I know*."

"*So use it*." The old smith looked at him. Small black eyes in the soot. Steady. "*Or don't. Your forge. But I've known you since you were eleven and you've never once done a thing the quick way unless you were running from something.*"

Lex didn't answer.

He ate the pie.

---

Brannoc didn't push either. Nobody ever pushed. They all just stood there and looked at him, and said one thing, and went quiet, and waited. As if he were iron in the fire and they were waiting for the colour to come up.

He wished one of them would just *hit* him.

---

"*Raven came*," said Brannoc. After a while. "*At the Forge. For you. From the west.*" He reached into his apron and drew out a folded paper, small, tied with black thread, and a little crushed from having been in a smith's apron with a pie. "*Elowen's hand.*"

Lex wiped his fingers and took it.

---

*Lex,*

*Westerling. Nine miles in from the old edge. Snow. Bad.*

*They're coming in. The gold ones. The nine thousand. Spreading out across the Westmarch, a few hundred yards a day, some faster. They don't do anything. They don't touch anything. They just walk. East. Through fields, through hedges, through the middle of villages. People are frightened. A carter at Hobb's Cross took an axe to one. Axe went in an inch and stuck. The gold man didn't stop. Walked on with the axe in his shoulder. The carter's not right since.*

*They hum. You know what they hum.*

*Roole's still in the gate at Greyfall. Still gold. Still standing. I go and see him every few days. I talk to him. I don't know if he hears. His eyes move.*

*Something else.*

*At Westerling the bread won't rise.*

*I know how that sounds. I didn't believe it either. The baker there is a sensible woman, and she sent for me, and I went, and I watched her make a batch. Good flour. Good yeast, fresh from the brewer. Warm kitchen. It sat in the bowl all night under a cloth and in the morning it was exactly the same. Not risen. Not soured. Not anything. As if it hadn't noticed the night go by.*

*Her hens haven't laid in a fortnight. The ones on the next farm have laid and the eggs don't hatch. A boy cut his hand on a scythe a week ago and it hasn't closed. Not festered. Not healed. Just stayed. As if it were still the moment he did it.*

*It's only at Westerling and the farms west of it. Nearest the old edge. For now.*

*Seraphine thinks it's moving.*

*Come and look. Bring Wren. Bring your eyes. I don't know what I'm seeing.*

*E.*

*P.S. The King's footprints go east from the old edge across the snow for sixty miles and then turn north toward Thistledown. Gold. A step wide. You can see them from the hills. Nobody will walk on them.*

---

Lex read it twice.

Then he folded it again. Carefully. And held it.

"*Bad*?" said Brannoc.

"*I don't know*." He looked west. Over the birch wood. Over the stream and the grave and the blue-door cottage with its horn lantern in the west window. At the grey Midwinter sky. "*The bread won't rise.*"

Brannoc frowned.

"*Bread always rises*," he said. "*If you leave it. That's what bread does. That's the whole point of bread.*" He paused. "*Bad yeast?*"

"*Good yeast*. *Warm kitchen.*" Lex looked at the paper in his hand. "*It sat all night and didn't notice the night go by.*"

The old smith was quiet.

He looked down at his left hand. At the gold fingertips. Smooth. Bright. Cold. Stopped.

"*Ah*," said Brannoc.

---

He went back in.

Lex finished the pie. Then he stood in the yard for a while, in the snow, by the cartwheel, with Elowen's letter in his hand, looking west.

*It hasn't closed*. *Not festered. Not healed. Just stayed.*

*As if it were still the moment he did it*.

He thought of a gold field. Forty sheep. A boy among them with his crook out. Mid-step. His eyes moving.

He thought of a hawk. Gold. Stopped in the air, mid-stoop, its wings half-folded, hanging over a field at the edge of the world as if somebody had pinned it to the sky.

He thought of the grey country. The dust. The white road. The towers. A country with no sun and no wind and no rain and nothing growing, where nothing had been new for nine hundred years.

And no edge between it and the world anymore. Nothing at all.

*Seraphine thinks it's moving*.

---

The fire was low in the forge when he went back in. He knelt and fed it.

*Good afternoon*, it said. Glad.

He didn't say anything back. He was thinking.

---

That was when he heard it.

---

Not a voice in his head. Not anymore. Nothing lived there now.

A voice in the *fire*.

No.

Under it.

In the hollow of the hearth. In the space under the coals, where the ash falls through the grate into the dark. The small round emptiness every hearth has at the bottom of it, that nobody ever looks at. The room a fire stands in.

*[...smith...]*

He went very still.

He knew the voice. He'd have known it anywhere. Small. Soft. Frightened. Too big for itself and too young for its size. Like a child talking in a very large dark house.

It had spoken to him on Lantern Night. At the bottom of the world. *[...smith, come UP...something's happening...]*

It hadn't spoken to him since.

*Mal*?

*[...smith...can you hear...I'm in your fire...I mean...not IN it...under...in the little room under it...every hearth has one...I didn't know I could...I'm sorry...]*

*I can hear you*. He knelt on the hearthstone with his hands on his knees. *Mal. What's wrong?*

A pause. A long one. Somewhere very far away, three hundred miles off, in a green circle in the east with a pool in the middle, something enormous and young was trying to find words.

*[...they're HERE...]*

*Who*?

*[...the grey ones...the ones from over the edge...they came on the wind...the first night...I felt them coming...thousands...and they're here now...all round me...round the rim...standing...waiting...]*

The voice shook.

*[...they want to come IN...they want to come home...and I want to let them...I do...I really do...I'm the room...that's what I'm FOR...]*

*Then let them*—

*[...I CAN'T...]*

It came up out of the hollow under the coals so hard the fire flinched. The flames bent away. Ash puffed out over the hearthstone.

*[...I'm too LITTLE...smith...I'm still too little...there are so many of them...if I let them all in I'll...I'll be FULL...and then I'll stop...and then there won't be any room...not for them...not for the new things...not for ANYTHING...]*

Silence.

The fire flickered. Steadied.

*[...Ivy's here...]* said the seventh Well. Smaller. *[...she says to tell you...she says come...she says bring your brother...she says...]*

A pause.

*[...she says she's frightened too...]*

---

Lex knelt on the hearthstone in the forge at Harrow, on the shortest day of the year, with a letter from the west in his apron and his hands on his knees and the fire leaning toward him, glad, the way it always was.

He looked at the basket under the bench.

He looked west.

He looked at his hands.

*[...smith*?]

"*I'm coming*," said Lex.

Out loud. To the fire. To the little room under it.

"*I'm coming*."
