# The River

Antho had called the Ferryman three times in his life.

The first time he'd been nineteen, and mad, and dying, and hadn't known what he was doing. He'd been lying in a ditch on the Blightmarch road with the thing in his chest eating him from the inside, and he'd said *please* into the dark, not to anyone, and a lantern had come up the road with a hooded man under it, and looked down at him, and said, in a voice like water over stones, *not you*. *Not yet.* And gone away.

The second time he'd been twenty-four, and sane, mostly, and had known exactly what he was doing. He'd stood on the threshold of the Hollowmaw with a stone in his hand, a flat grey stone from a lake beyond the Teeth, and paid it. The memory of it. Five skips. His father counting.

The third time he'd been twenty-six, and it had been the night of the Heart, and the old man had come without being asked.

He'd never called him for somebody else.

---

"*You'll need a threshold*," said Seraphine.

In the Forge. At two in the morning. The night after the mill-pond. With her horn spectacles on her nose and her hand flat on her belly and her face so white the freckles stood out like spatters of ink.

"*I know*," said Antho.

"*A real one. Not a doorway. A threshold. Something that's an edge.*" She was talking fast. She talked fast when she was frightened. "*He comes to the edge. He's always at the edge. That's what he is. The edge between—*"

"*I know*," said Antho.

He took her face in his hands.

"*I know, love*," he said. Gently. "*I'm the Threshold Warden. I've been standing on edges all my life.*"

She looked at him. Her eyes were wet behind the horn.

"*Come back*," said Seraphine Ilvane. "*I've got four hundred and twelve reasons written down. I'll start a new list. I'll have four hundred and thirteen by the time you're home.*"

"*What's the four hundred and thirteenth*?"

"*It's going to be born in August*," said Seraphine. "*And it'll want to meet you.*"

---

They used the Forge doors.

The great doors at the end of the long hall. Oak, iron-bound, twelve feet high, onto the yard. They weren't a real threshold. Not the kind the Ferryman liked. But they were the oldest doors in Ashbourne—older than the Forge, older than the twenty anvils, older than Brannoc, who said they'd been hung by his grandfather's grandfather and had been opened every morning since at dawn—and they'd seen a great deal go in and out.

Antho stood in them.

In the middle. With one foot on the flags of the hall and one on the cinders of the yard. With the lantern in his hand.

---

It wasn't his lantern. His had gone east, with the five hundred and fifty-six, to ring the pool. This one was Seren's. The clay lamp. Wenna's lamp. The little brown lamp, cracked across the lip, that she'd carried through thirteen years in the dark and given away at the end, and that had somehow come back to her, the way lamps did with Harrow women.

She'd put it in his hands an hour ago without a word.

He held it out. Into the dark of the yard. Into the night.

And said the name.

---

He'd never told anyone the name. Not Seraphine. Not Lex. Not Pim. It wasn't a secret, exactly. It was just that it wasn't the kind of thing you said in daylight. It was an old name. Older than the net. Older than the Wells. Older than the drowned city. The name the first people had called him by, when they first stood on a riverbank at night and watched someone they loved go out across the water and not come back.

It meant *the one who rows*.

Antho said it into the dark.

---

Nothing happened.

For a long time.

Behind him, in the long hall, he could hear them waiting. Lex, on a bench by the great hearth, with his ribs strapped and his burned hands in wet cloths and his face grey. Rhosyn beside him, with her own hands bound up in white linen to the wrists, both palms cut to the bone, blood coming through. His mother. Brannoc. Kestrel. Tilda. Seraphine.

And in the corner by the apple wood door, enormous, gold, sitting on the flags with his back to the wall and a petal in his hand, the King.

Nobody said anything.

Antho held the lamp out into the dark.

---

Then the cold came.

---

It came the way it always came. Up from the ground. Through the soles of his feet. Into his ankles. The cold of wet stone. The cold of a cellar where something has been kept too long. And the smell. River-weed. Mud. The flat grey smell of water that has never seen the sun.

The yard went grey.

Not the grey of the grey country. Not *still*. A different grey. A moving grey. Like mist coming up off a river at dusk. It came up out of the cinders and rolled across the yard, low, knee-high, and the stars over Ashbourne went dim, and the lantern ring round the village went faint and far away, as if seen through water.

And out of the mist, at the bottom of the yard, where the stream had run before Lex turned it—

A light.

Low. Gold. Swinging. As if somebody were carrying it up from the water.

---

The Ferryman stopped at the edge of the cinders.

Tall. Grey. Hooded. Robed. His face in shadow under the hood, always in shadow, so that you saw only the line of a jaw, old, and the glint of something that might have been an eye. He held his lantern in one long grey hand.

He looked at Antho in the doorway.

"*Warden*," said the Ferryman.

"*Ferryman*."

"*You called*."

"*Yes*."

The hood tilted. Very slightly.

"*Nobody's dying*," said the Ferryman. "*Not here. Not tonight.*" A pause. "*I checked.*"

---

"*I need to cross*," said Antho.

The Ferryman was silent.

"*Not to the far shore*," said Antho. "*Down. Down the river. To the Heart.*" He swallowed. "*Like before. Like the night of the Heart. You took us down then.*"

"*I did*."

"*She's got my brother's daughter*," said Antho. "*The Glass Queen. She took her through the mill-pond. She's at the Heart, in the chair, and at dawn on Midsummer she'll put the child's hand on the anvil and the net will take her for a Root.*" His voice cracked. He let it. "*And my brother's wife went after her. Alone. Through the glass.*"

The Ferryman didn't move.

"*Please*," said Antho.

---

The lantern in the Ferryman's hand flickered.

Not the way a flame flickers in a draught. The way a person shifts their weight when they've been listening to a conversation and have something to say.

*[Excuse me*,] said the flame. Small. Dry. Courteous. *[I don't mean to interrupt.]*

Behind Antho, in the hall, something went over with a crash. A bench. Antho didn't turn round. He knew what it was. He knew who'd stood up so fast he'd knocked it over, with his ribs strapped and his hands burned.

*[But did you say the Heart*?] said the flame in the Ferryman's lantern. *[The Heart of the net? Where the old anvil is?]* A pause. Interested. *[I've read about it. I'd very much like to see it. For my book.]*

---

The Ferryman looked down at his lantern.

For a long time.

Then he looked up. Past Antho. Into the hall. At the man standing by the great hearth with the bench on its side behind him and his face—

Antho didn't look. He didn't need to.

"*It doesn't know him*," said the Ferryman. Quietly. To Antho. Not unkindly. "*You understand that. The price was its love. All of it. It remembers everything it ever learned. Every word it read. Every Well. Every forge. It remembers your father and your mother and the net and the nine hundred years.*" A pause. "*It doesn't remember why any of it mattered. It doesn't remember him.*"

"*I know*," said Antho.

"*It'll be polite to him*," said the Ferryman. "*It's always polite. It'll ask him questions.*" The hood turned. Toward the hall. "*It'll break his heart.*"

"*It's already broken*," said Antho.

The Ferryman was silent.

Then he did something Antho had never seen him do. In three callings. In a ditch, on a threshold, at the Heart.

He sighed.

---

"*Four*," said the Ferryman.

"*Four*?"

"*Four can cross*." The hood turned. Looking. Into the hall. At each of them. Slowly. As if he could see through them. As if he were reading them like a list. "*The boat holds four and me.*"

His gaze stopped on the King.

Sitting on the flags by the apple wood door. Gold. Enormous. With his back to the wall and his knees drawn up and a white petal in his gold hand.

"*Not you*," said the Ferryman. "*Still not you.*"

"*No*," agreed the King. Quietly. He got up. Slowly. The flags under him going gold in a circle. "*But I'm coming anyway.*" He looked at the Ferryman across the length of the hall. "*She's my sister. And she's got a little girl who told me no. And I owe them both.*"

The Ferryman looked at him for a long time.

"*You're not on my list*," said the Ferryman.

"*I know*," said the King. "*That's why there's room in the boat.*"

---

So it was four.

Antho. Because he'd called. Because he was the Warden. Because he'd been down the river before, and knew the way, and had promised a girl a light once and kept it.

Lex. Because nobody could have stopped him. Brannoc tried. Brannoc stood in front of him by the great hearth, a foot shorter and thirty years older, with his gold fingertips on Lex's chest, and said *you're broken, lad, you can't swing a hammer with your ribs like that*, and Lex looked down at him and said *I'm not going to swing a hammer*, and Brannoc looked at his face for a long time and then stepped aside.

Rhosyn. Because Ysy was her sister. Because she'd seen her face under the water, for a heartbeat, on the bank of the mill-pond, with her hands round the glass wrist—and something had gone across the cut-jewel face that wasn't cold. Because Rhosyn was the only one who knew what it was.

*Someone looking back*, she'd said to Seren. In the spring. *Who says yes*.

And the King. Because he wasn't on the list.

---

Seren gave Lex the basket.

At the door. In the dark. With the mist rolling round her ankles. The old willow basket, the one that had sat under the hearth at Harrow all winter with the pieces of the Key in it, and now held only one thing.

A crown.

Small. Silver-grey. Seven points. Thin. Plain. Hesper's.

"*It's got one more no in it*," said his mother. "*Wenna said so. Oz said so.*" She put it in his hands. "*Take it. In case. I don't know what for. Take it anyway.*"

Lex looked at it.

Then he gave it to the King.

The King stood very still. Holding it. A thin silver-grey crown in his huge gold hands. It didn't go gold. It sat on his palm beside the white petal, grey and plain and itself.

"*It's hers*," said Lex. "*She'd want you to have it.*"

The King's gold face did something complicated.

He didn't say anything. He just closed his hand round it. Gently. The way you'd close your hand round a moth.

---

The boat was at the bottom of the yard.

Where the stream had been. Where Brannoc took his quench-water. Where Lark had leaned over the black still water and laughed at her grandmother's face. It was all mist now. Grey. Moving. Knee-deep. And in it, a boat. Long. Narrow. Black. Old. Low in water that wasn't there.

They got in.

Antho first. Then Rhosyn, with her bound hands held up in front of her, and Antho handing her down. Then Lex, slowly, with his teeth together and his hand on his side. Then the King.

The boat should have sunk. A gold man the size of a small cart. It didn't even rock.

The Ferryman stepped into the stern. Set his lantern on the post. Took up the long oar.

And pushed off.

---

The yard went away.

Not behind them. Around them. The cinders, the Forge, the great doors with his mother standing in them holding the clay lamp—Seraphine beside her—Brannoc behind—all of it went grey, and faint, and far, like a picture seen through water, and then wasn't there.

There was only the mist. And the boat. And the long oar going in and out. And the lantern on its post.

And the river.

---

Antho had been down it before.

He knew what it looked like. He'd been ready. Grey water, flat, slow, under a grey sky that wasn't a sky. Grey banks, far off, with grey shapes on them. Dead. Drifting. Waiting. The river of the dead, going down from the world to the far shore and the dark, slow, forever.

It didn't look like that now.

---

It was full.

---

The dead were everywhere.

On the banks. In the mist. In the water. Not drifting, not waiting. Moving. All of them. In one direction. Upriver. Against the current. Grey columns of dust, thousands, tens of thousands, walking on the water and along the banks and through the mist, slowly, patiently, the way they'd drifted east across the snow toward Mal in the winter.

Going home.

"*Where are they going*?" said Rhosyn. Very quietly. Looking at them.

"*East*," said Antho. "*To Mal. To the pool.*" He watched them go past. A woman with a child. An old man. Two that might have been brothers. "*He's big enough now. Since you. Since the cousins. They can feel it. All the way down here.*" His throat was tight. "*They've been waiting nine hundred years for somewhere to go.*"

*[How fascinating*,] said the lantern on the post.

---

Lex hadn't said anything.

He sat in the bow of the boat. As far from the stern as it was possible to be. With his back to the lantern. With his strapped ribs and his wrapped hands and his face turned to the mist.

Antho sat beside him.

He didn't say anything either. He'd learned that much. From Pim. From Seren. From the dead. Sometimes you didn't say anything. Sometimes you just sat beside someone in a boat.

*[I wonder*,] said the lantern. Behind them. Politely. To the boat in general. *[If someone could tell me about the anvil. At the Heart. I've read that it was the first anvil. The one the net was forged on. Is that right?]*

Nobody answered.

*[Only I'm writing a book*,] said the lantern. Apologetically. *[Volume Three. The Book of What Is. I've been trying to describe the world as it actually is, rather than as it was, and I find there's a great deal I don't know.]* A pause. *[I knew it all once, I think. I remember knowing it. I just don't remember why I wanted to.]*

Lex's hands closed. In the wet cloths. Slowly.

"*It was the first anvil*," said Lex.

His voice was very rough. He didn't turn round.

"*Aurelian forged the net on it*," he said. To the mist. "*Nine hundred years ago. With a hammer his father gave him. His little brother held the tongs. He made the threads one at a time. Ten thousand of them. He was trying to save everyone.*"

*[Ah*,] said the lantern. Pleased. *[Thank you. That's very helpful.]* A pause. *[How do you know?]*

"*I've got his hammer*," said Lex.

*[Have you really*?] Delighted. *[How marvellous. Are you a smith?]*

Lex didn't answer.

Antho looked at his brother's face. And looked away.

---

*[I'm sorry*,] said the lantern. After a while. Quieter. *[I think I've said something wrong.]*

"*No*," said Lex.

*[Only you sound sad. And I don't know you, so I can't think what I've done. But you sound as though I've done something.]*

"*You haven't*," said Lex.

*[Are you sure*?]

Lex turned round.

Slowly. On the bench in the bow. With his ribs grating. And looked down the length of the boat, past Antho, past Rhosyn, past the King's broad gold back, to the stern. To the post. To the lantern. To the small gold flame inside it, dry and courteous and curious, flickering.

"*You used to say good morning to me*," said Lex.

The flame went still.

"*Every morning*," said Lex. "*For seven years. Since I was nineteen. Since the day I Kindled and you woke up in my chest. You said good morning. And I said it back.*" His voice was very steady. Antho had never heard it so steady. "*You called me boy. You called me idiot. You read every book in the Spire and told me about them while I was trying to sleep. You went away twice and came back twice. You were with me at the Heart.*" A breath. "*And you paid a price for me at the Veil, in the winter, and now you don't know me. And that's all right. It was your price. You chose it.*"

The flame didn't move.

"*So you haven't done anything wrong*," said Lex. "*I just miss you. That's all.*"

He turned back round. To the mist.

---

The boat went on.

Nobody said anything for a long time.

The dead went past them, upriver, in their thousands. The long oar went in and out. The lantern on the post burned, small and gold.

Then, very quietly, so quietly that Antho almost didn't hear it over the water—

*[Good morning*,] said the lantern.

Lex didn't turn round.

His shoulders shook.

*[I don't know why I said that*,] said the lantern. Puzzled. Almost to itself. *[It isn't morning. Is it? I don't think there are mornings here.]* A pause. *[It just seemed like the right thing to say.]*

---

The Ferryman didn't say anything.

But Antho, looking back past the King at the stern of the boat, saw him. Standing at the oar. With his hooded face turned not to the river, the way it always was, but down. To the lantern on the post. To the flame.

Looking at it.

For a long time.

---

The Drowned Country came at what might have been midnight.

---

Antho saw it first. He'd seen it before. Towers in the mist. Roofs. Domes. Grey. Enormous. Old. A city, under water that wasn't water, along both banks of the river, going away into the grey as far as you could see.

The city before the Lattice. Before the Wells were tapped. Drowned in the Hollow when the first Hollow was made, before Mal was Mal, when the seventh Well was a hunger at the bottom of the world.

Its streets were full of dead.

Thousands. Grey. Drifting. They'd always drifted. That was what the Drowned Country was. A city full of people who'd been drowned so long ago they'd forgotten what drowning was, drifting in their streets like smoke, forever.

They weren't drifting now.

They were walking.

East. Upriver. Out of their streets. Down to the banks. Onto the water. In their thousands. Joining the others. Going home.

---

"*Stop*," said Antho.

The Ferryman looked at him.

"*Please*," said Antho. "*Just for a moment. Like before.*"

The Ferryman didn't say anything. But the long oar went in, and held, and the boat slowed, and turned, and slid in to the bank under the shadow of a grey tower. Against a grey stair. Going up.

Antho got out.

---

He knew the way.

Up the stair. Along a grey street. Under an arch. Through a market where grey stalls stood under grey awnings that had been rotting for longer than the world had had a name. Past a fountain, dry, grey, with a grey stone fish on top that had once spouted water for children who'd been dead for ten thousand years.

To a square.

Small. Grey. Paved. With grey houses round it, and a grey tree in the middle, dead, leafless, its branches like cracks in the sky.

And round the tree, in a circle, on the paving stones—

Six lanterns.

---

They were still burning.

---

Antho stopped at the edge of the square.

He'd brought them here two years ago. Three. He'd lost count. On the night of the Heart. Six lanterns from the Keep, tin and horn, plain. He'd set them down round the dead tree where he'd found her. A little grey girl. Drifting in the street. Who'd held his sleeve. He'd lit them, and she'd come, and stood in the middle of them, and held his sleeve again. And at the Heart, when the eaten worlds rose, she'd gone up. Gold. Into the light.

He'd never come back.

He'd thought about it. Every year. On the night of the Heart. He'd sat on the wall at the Keep and thought about six lanterns in a grey square in a drowned city, going out one by one, with nobody to see.

They hadn't gone out.

They were burning. All six. Small. Gold. Steady. In the grey. The only colour in the Drowned Country.

And round them—

---

The square was full.

Dead. Grey. Hundreds. Standing round the six lanterns. Close. Bending. Not drifting. Not walking. Just standing, the way people stand round a fire on a cold night. Holding out hands that weren't hands any more. Warming themselves.

They'd been here for years. He could tell. The way they stood. The way they'd worn a ring in the grey dust of the paving round the lanterns, with their not-feet, over and over, a hundred thousand times. Coming. Going. Coming back.

Six lanterns.

Given.

And every grey ghost in the Drowned Country had found them.

---

Antho stood at the edge of the square and cried.

He didn't mean to. It just came. Up out of his chest. Out of the place where the Murmur had lived, where the thing that ate him had lived, where there was room now, and quiet, and Shade, and a little grey girl's hand on his sleeve.

He stood there and cried, and the dead turned round, all of them, and looked at him. Grey faces. Faces that had forgotten they were faces.

And one by one, they bent.

Toward him. The way the bread-woman's dust had bent toward Pim. The way the dead at the rim bent toward Antho every morning when he counted the lanterns.

The way you bend to someone across a room to say *thank you*.

---

Then they turned.

All of them. At once. Away from the lanterns. Toward the edge of the square. Toward the street. Toward the river.

East.

And went.

Not drifting. Walking. Past him. On either side. A long grey river of the dead, flowing out of the square and down the street and down the stair to the water, to join the others. To go home. To Mal.

They'd waited. Antho understood. Watching them go. They'd waited here, round six small lights, for years. In the cold. In the grey. Because it was warm here. Because somebody had come back with a light, once, the way he'd promised. And they hadn't wanted to leave it.

Now they had somewhere better to go.

---

The square emptied.

The six lanterns burned on. Round the dead tree. Small and gold and steady. With nobody round them.

Antho went and stood in the middle of them.

He didn't pick them up. He didn't put them out. He just stood there. In the warm. Where she'd stood.

"*Thank you*," he said. To the lanterns. To the square. To the girl who wasn't there any more. "*For keeping them warm.*"

He felt something on his sleeve.

He looked down.

There was nothing there.

He went back to the boat.

---

The river went down.

Past the Drowned Country. Out of it. The towers falling away behind them into the mist. The dead going past them the other way, upriver, thousands, home. The oar going in and out.

And the mist began to thin.

And under it—

Light.

---

Antho saw it first.

Far ahead. At the bottom of the river. Where the grey water went down into the dark and the dark went down into something that wasn't dark.

Gold.

And white. And ten thousand threads of light, every colour, rising out of the ground like steam off a pond at dawn. Going up. Away. To the world.

Not moving.

Still.

---

The Heart.

Antho had been here before. Once. On the worst night of his life and the best. He'd stood on that white floor with his brother and his father and the eaten worlds rising round them in gold.

It looked smaller now. And colder. And emptier. A round white hall with no roof, at the bottom of the world. Seven steps. A gold anvil. A crown on it. A white chair.

And in the chair—

---

"*Ysy*," said Rhosyn.

Very softly. From the middle of the boat. With her bound hands in her lap.

---

The Glass Queen sat in the chair of the Root.

Clear. White. Still. Like a frost on a window. With the net bent round her like a bower of light. With her glass hands on the arms of the chair. With her eyes closed.

And on the seven steps below her, on the third step, in the middle—

A woman.

Sitting. Barefoot. In a shirt and leggings. With a fox-red braid down her back.

And in her lap, asleep, with one fist wound in her mother's shirt and the other in her mouth, glowing faintly gold in the white light of the Heart—

---

Lex made a sound.

Antho had never heard it before. Not from his brother. Not from anyone. A sound like iron cracking in a quench. Like something that had been held at heat for far too long, plunged into water, and broken.

He was on his feet. In the bow. With his ribs strapped and his hands wrapped and his face—

The boat didn't rock.

---

Wren looked up.

From the third step. Across the white floor. Across the threads of the net. Down to the river. To the boat. To the man standing in the bow.

She didn't move. She didn't call out. She had her daughter asleep in her lap and her arms round her.

She just looked at him.

And Antho saw her mouth move. One word. Across the whole of the Heart.

*Hammer*.

---

The boat touched the shore.

White stone. The edge of the Heart. Where the grey river ran in on one side and out on the other. The Ferryman held it there with the long oar. Steady.

Nobody moved.

Then the King stood up.

Slowly. Huge. Gold. In the middle of the boat. With the thin silver-grey crown in one hand and the white petal in the other.

He stepped out onto the white floor of the Heart.

And the white floor didn't go gold.

---

Antho stared at it.

At the King's foot. On the white stone. The way the snow had gone gold under him, and the flags of the Forge, and the gold path under Old Mag's tree. The way everything he'd stood on for nine hundred years had gone gold.

The white stone stayed white.

The King looked down at it too.

Then he looked up. Across the white floor. Up the seven steps. Past Wren and the sleeping child. To the chair. To the clear glass woman in it with her eyes closed.

"*Ysy*," said Oszric. Very gently. The way a big brother says a little sister's name when he's come to fetch her home from somewhere she shouldn't be. "*It's nearly dawn. Come down.*"

---

The Glass Queen opened her eyes.

They were clear. Like looking through a window at a white sky.

She looked down at her brother. Standing gold on the white floor of the Heart with a crown in one hand and a flower in the other. At her sister, getting out of a boat with her hands bound up in bloody linen. At the smith in the bow, broken, burning. At the Warden. At the Ferryman, at the oar, with the small gold flame on its post.

At the child asleep on the steps below her.

"*No*," said Ysenne.

And somewhere up above them—far up, through the grey water and the drowned city and the glass road, in the world, at the edge of everything—

The sky began, very faintly, to go pale.
