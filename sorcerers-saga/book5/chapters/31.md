# A Hundred

He'd thought about it all his life.

Not this. Not the Heart, or the anvil, or the crown. Just the number. *A hundred*. Every child in Caldor thought about it. You stood in front of the Stone at your Kindling and the panel came up and said *Lv 1*, and from that day on, somewhere in the back of your head, there was a hundred. The top. The end of the stair. The place nobody went.

He'd been nineteen. *Smith. Common. Lv 9. Cap 24*. He'd looked at that number and thought, well, that's me, then. Twenty-four. A good village smith. Brannoc's forge. Brannoc's anvil. Horseshoes and hinges and the odd ploughshare, and a fire in the evenings, and that'd be all, and that'd be fine.

He'd never once thought about a hundred.

He knelt on the white floor of the Heart with his ribs broken and his hands burned and looked at it.

*Sovereign*.

He knew the word. He'd seen it once before. On the rubble of Duskhold, on Lantern Night, over a woman with moth-wings and a starving hollow face. *MAELIS. SOVEREIGN. TIER V.* Beautiful and empty and hungry, with nine hundred years of drinking in her, standing over him with her hand out.

That was what Tier Five was.

That was what a hundred got you.

---

"*Take it*," said Ysenne.

On the dais. By the anvil. With Lark on her hip, quiet, watching.

She didn't sound cold. She didn't sound triumphant. She sounded tired. More tired than he'd ever heard anyone sound. Like Old Mag at the end. Like his father.

"*Take it, smith*," said the Glass Queen. "*Somebody has to.*"

He looked up at her.

"*It went past me*," said Ysenne. "*You saw. It doesn't bend to me any more. It doesn't bend to anybody.*" Her clear eyes were on the threads, swinging, humming, free, going away up into the grey. "*And the grey's still coming. Do you understand? The edge is still open. It'll drink everything. Every field. Every village. Every child. And the net can't stop it. Not without somebody holding it.*" She looked down at him. "*I can't. She won't. You can.*"

He didn't say anything.

"*You'd be good at it*," said Ysenne. Very quietly. "*Better than me. Better than Oz. Better than Aurelian. You'd keep them all. Safe. Warm.*" A pause. "*You'd never have to watch anyone go again.*"

---

He looked at the crown.

On the anvil. Gold. Seven points. Thin. Plain. Lying where it had lain for nine hundred years, on the face of the first anvil, worn smooth by blows struck by a frightened young man with a hammer his father gave him.

*Keep what is yours*.

He thought about what was his.

---

Wren. On her knees beside him. Her gold fingers on the white stone. Her red braid. Her face turned to him, not saying anything. Not *don't*. Not *do*. Just looking at him. The way she'd looked at him on the Spire, in the wind, the first time. *Your choice, Hammer*.

Lark. On the dais. In a cold woman's arms. Twenty months old. Who'd said no to the whole of the world and meant it.

His mother. Three hundred miles up and away. In a doorway. Holding a clay lamp.

Antho behind him. Seraphine at the Forge with a hand on her belly. A four-hundred-and-thirteenth reason, due in August.

Brannoc. *Mind the floor. It's been swept.*

Old Mag, under her apple tree, with a gold king's hand in hers. *Kept's the oven, love. Finished is the cake.*

His father. On the Spire steps. On the stair down into the dark. Turning back. Twice. Not to keep anything. Just to look.

---

He could keep all of it.

He knew it. Kneeling there. He could stand up and walk up the seven steps and put his hand on the gold and the net would come to him the way it had come the first time, the whole weight and warmth of it, and he'd hold it. And nothing would ever be lost again. Not Wren. Not Lark. Not Antho or Seraphine or the baby or Brannoc or his mother. Not the hedgehog under the woodpile at Harrow. Not anything.

Kept.

Still.

The way the gold kept Ilda. The way the glass kept a tired woman brushing her hair. The way the King had kept nine thousand people because he couldn't stand to watch them go.

*You'd never have to watch anyone go again*.

---

He looked at Wren.

She was still looking at him. She hadn't said anything. She wasn't going to. He knew that. She'd walked into the glass after their daughter without asking anyone, and she'd sat on the steps of the Heart for two days with a cold queen and not tried to run, because running would have left Lark alone. She'd made her choice.

This one was his.

"*What would you do*?" he said. Very quietly. Just to her.

Wren looked at him for a long time.

"*I'd miss trees*," she said.

He didn't understand. For a moment.

Then he did.

The birch wood behind the cottage at Harrow. The chalk circle. Forty arrows. Eleven in the tree. Two in the chalk. The rest in the snow. A woman with her best hand turned to gold, missing trees, every day, all winter. And getting up the next morning and drawing again. With the wrong hand. Shaking.

Missing. And getting better. And missing. And getting better.

*Kept* would never miss a tree.

*Kept* would never hit one, either.

---

He stood up.

His ribs screamed. He let them. He stood up on the white floor of the Heart in the middle of the moving net with the panel hanging gold in front of his face, and he looked up the seven steps at the Glass Queen and the crown and his daughter.

"*No*," said Lex.

The gold went out of the panel.

Not all at once. The way it had gone out of Lark's. Slowly. Like a fire going down. And the panel hung there in front of him, empty, white, waiting.

And then—

The net asked him.

---

He felt it. In the hum. In the floor. In the threads swinging round him, moving, free. Not *TAKE UP*. Not *HOLD*. Not *KEEP*. The net had been made to say those things, and it had said them to every Root for nine hundred years, and it had said them to him the first time.

It didn't say them now.

It said, in the hum, in the white, in the space where a panel had been—

---

LEVEL 100.
THE CEILING IS REACHED.

WHAT ARE YOU?
:::

---

Lex looked at it.

At the question. Hanging in the air of the Heart.

He'd been a lot of things. The net had called him a lot of things. *Smith*. *Emberwright*. *Forgeheart Sorcerer*. *Worldforger*. Common and Rare and Epic and Unique. Tier One, Two, Three, Four. He'd had ceilings and broken them. He'd had titles. *The Reckoning*. *Husband*. *Father*.

He thought about all of them.

Then he thought about the Forge. At five in the morning. In the dark. With the hearth banked and the bellows still and the long anvil cold, and Brannoc snoring through the wall, and his own hands raking the ash off the coals and blowing, gently, the way you'd wake someone who'd been up all night.

*Good morning*.

---

"*I'm a smith*," said Lex.

---

He stared at it.

*Smith*.

*Common*.

---

Behind him, somebody laughed.

It was Antho. On his knees on the white stone. With his hand over his mouth and his shoulders shaking and tears running down his face. Laughing. Helplessly. The way he'd laughed when they were boys and Lex fell in the duck-pond.

"*Common*," said Antho. Through his fingers. "*Common. Lex. You absolute—*"

Wren was laughing too. She didn't make any sound. She just knelt there on the white floor with her face in her gold hand and her other hand over her eyes, and shook.

Lex didn't laugh.

He looked at the panel. *Ceiling: NONE*. And felt something in his chest that had been clenched since he was nineteen years old, standing in front of the Stone at Ashbourne with a cap of twenty-four on a panel and his whole life laid out in front of him like a road with a wall at the end of it—

Let go.

---

*Common*, said the net. In the hum. Pleased. Like a forge that's just been lit.

*Common. Like bread. Like iron. Like fire. Like the ground.*

*Like everyone*.

---

"*No*," said Ysenne.

---

He looked up.

She was standing in front of the anvil. With Lark on her hip. With the crown behind her. And her clear glass face had—

Cracked.

Not much. A line. Fine as a hair. From the corner of one clear eye down across her cheek to her jaw. A crack in a frost on a window. Lex saw it. Saw the faces under her glass skin, the millions of them, drift toward it. Press against it. As if they'd seen light.

"*No*," said the Glass Queen. "*You can't. You can't just—that's not a choice. That's not what a hundred is. A hundred is the top. A hundred is the crown. Nobody gets to a hundred and says—*" Her voice was rising. Thin. Like a wet finger on the rim of a cup. "*Somebody has to hold it. Somebody has to. Or it all goes. All of it. Into the grey.*"

"*Nobody has to hold it*," said Lex.

"*Then who's going to keep them*?"

"*Nobody*," said Lex. "*That's the point.*"

---

He walked up the steps.

One. Two. Three. Slowly. With his ribs. With his hands. He didn't hurry. The Glass Queen watched him come. She didn't move. She didn't lift her hand. There was nothing to lift it with. The net went past her the way water goes past a stone.

He stopped in front of her.

At the top of the dais. In front of the anvil. A yard away. Close enough to see the crack in her face, and the faces behind it.

He held out his arms.

The Glass Queen looked at them.

For a long time.

Then, very slowly, she held out the child.

---

Lark came into his arms like a cat coming in out of the rain. All at once. Indignantly. With her fists in his shirt and her face in his neck and her whole small body shaking. Not crying. She was past crying. Just shaking. And saying, into his collar, over and over, very quietly, the only word that mattered.

"*Da*."

He held her.

He held her for a long time. On the dais. At the top of the seven steps. With the Glass Queen standing a yard away with her empty hands and the crack in her face.

Then he looked past her.

At the anvil.

At the crown.

---

"*It'll always be there*," he said.

Not to anyone. Just out loud.

"*As long as it's lying in the road. Somebody'll want it.*" He looked at it. Gold. Seven points. Warm. Empty. "*Not you. Not now. But somebody. In a hundred years. In five hundred. Somebody frightened. Somebody who can't stand to watch people go. Somebody who'll walk all the way down here to the bottom of the world and pick it up and think, I'll just hold it. Just for a bit. Just to keep everyone safe.*"

He looked at Ysenne.

"*That's how it started*," he said. "*Isn't it? With Aurelian. With a frightened young man trying to save everyone.*"

The Glass Queen didn't answer.

He gave Lark to Wren.

She was at the top of the steps. He hadn't heard her come. She took their daughter without a word and stood back.

And Lex turned to the anvil.

---

He didn't pick the crown up.

He didn't need to. It was on an anvil. It was on *the* anvil. The first one. Where it had been made. And he had a hammer.

He took it off his belt. Aurelian's hammer. Black iron. Old. Heavy. Worn smooth at the grip by a frightened young man's hand, and then by nine hundred years of nothing, and then by his. He unwrapped the wet cloths from his burned right hand and let them fall on the white stone. And closed his raw palm round the haft.

And reached for fire.

---

It came.

Not round. Not slow. Not climbing hand over hand up out of the deep. It came the way it had come every day of his life until Midwinter. *Through*. Straight up out of the white stone of the Heart and into his feet and his chest and his arm and his hand, white-gold, roaring, glad.

Through the net.

The net that was moving again. Humming again. Free.

*[Good morning*,] said the fire.

He didn't stop to wonder at it.

He put the fire into the crown.

---

It went red.

Then orange. Then yellow. Then white. Seven points softening. Sagging. The thin gold going bright and liquid at the edges like honey on a warm stone. The crown of the net. Nine hundred years old. Made by the hands of the first Root on the first anvil, with the first fire.

Lex lifted the hammer.

And brought it down.

---

The sound went through the Heart like a bell.

Through the white stone. Through the threads. Up through the grey water and the drowned city and the glass road and the whole of the world. Every Stone in Caldor rang with it. Every anvil. Every bell in every tower. Every hammer hanging on every wall. One note. Clear. Gold.

The crown flattened.

---

He struck again.

And again.

He didn't think about it. He didn't need to. He'd been doing this since he was eleven years old, standing on a crate in Brannoc's forge with a horseshoe nail in his fist. Heat. Strike. Turn. Strike. The crown went under the hammer the way all metal goes under a hammer if you're patient with it, and know what it wants to be.

He knew what it wanted to be.

He'd known since the spring. Since Seraphine's drawing. Since the frame went down the lane on the cart with no latch, and his mother had said *a door needs a latch, love, or it's just a hole with a lid*.

*It has to say yes*, Seraphine had written in the margin. *And no*.

---

Seven points went into one.

A bar. Short. Thick as his finger. He drew it out. Turned it. Bent the end of it into a curve, a hook, for the catch. Flattened the other end into a thumb-plate, wide, round, the size of a child's palm. Punched the hole for the pin with the point of the hammer's peen.

A latch.

A plain thumb-latch. The kind on every cottage door in Caldor. The kind you lift with your thumb to go in and drop to keep the cold out.

Up is yes.

Down is no.

---

He stopped.

With the hammer raised. With the latch white-hot on the anvil in front of him. One more strike. He could feel it. One more, to set it. To finish it.

He turned round.

Wren was standing behind him with Lark on her hip. Lark was watching. She'd stopped shaking. She was watching the hammer and the anvil and the white-hot metal the way she watched him at the long anvil at the Forge from her sling on his back. With her whole face. With her eyebrows.

He held out his hand.

Wren looked at him. Then she understood. And put their daughter down.

---

Lark walked across the dais.

On her own. Barefoot. In her nightshirt. Twenty months old. Twelve steps. Not toddling. Walking. The way she'd walked across the Harrow yard in the mud to a gold king with a cup. The way she'd walked across the stepping stones in the spring with Rhosyn holding her breath.

She stopped beside him at the anvil.

He knelt down. With his ribs. And put the hammer in her hands.

It was far too big for her. She couldn't lift it. She didn't care. She wrapped both her small square hands round the black iron haft below his, and looked up at him.

"*Hot*," said Lark. Seriously. Pointing at the latch.

"*Yes*," said Lex. "*Hot. Don't touch.*"

"*No*," agreed Lark.

He put his hand round both of hers. Round the haft. And together they lifted it.

And brought it down.

---

It wasn't much of a blow. It barely moved the metal.

It didn't need to.

The latch rang.

One note. Small. Clear. Like a bell on a door when someone comes in out of the cold.

Lex let go of the hammer.

Lark let go too. It fell on the white stone with a clang. She looked at it. Then at the latch, cooling on the anvil, going from white to yellow to red. Then at him.

"*More*," said Lark.

He laughed. He couldn't help it. It hurt his ribs so much he nearly fell over.

---

He picked the latch up with the tongs when it had gone dark.

It was small. Plain. Gold-grey now, where the fire had been in it. A thumb-latch. A bar and a catch. Warm in his burned palm, the way the hinges had been warm. The way they breathed.

He looked at it with the net.

Out of habit. The way you'd glance at a thing to see what it is. And the panel came up, the way panels came up for every made thing, every sword, every hinge, every horseshoe.

---

:::system
ITEM: —

NOT OF THIS NET.
NO RECORD.
:::

---

He looked at it for a long time.

*No record*.

The way Malachi had been *no record*. The old man with the goats in the Pale Wastes. The hole Aurelian made in the net for his little brother, so nothing could ever hold him.

Nothing would ever hold this either.

Nobody would ever wear it. Nobody would ever pick it up off an anvil at the bottom of the world and think *I'll just hold it, just for a bit*. It wasn't a crown any more. It wasn't anything the net knew.

It was a latch.

You lifted it to go in. You dropped it to keep the cold out.

That was all.

---

Behind him, somebody was crying.

Not Lark. Not Wren.

He turned round.

---

The Glass Queen had sat down.

On the top step of the dais. At the edge. With her long glass legs folded under her and her long glass hands in her lap. She'd sat down the way a woman sits down at the end of a very long day, when she's been standing since dawn and has finally stopped and doesn't know if she'll get up again.

She wasn't crying. Glass doesn't cry.

But the crack in her face had grown.

From her eye to her jaw. And down. Her neck. Her shoulder. Her arm. Fine lines. Spreading. Like frost spreading across a pane. And through every one of them, the faces were pressing. Millions. Under the glass. Drifting toward the cracks. Toward the light.

She looked down at her hands.

"*There's nothing left*," said Ysenne.

Very quietly. To nobody.

"*No net. No crown. No chair. No regency.*" She turned her clear glass hands over. "*Nine hundred years. Every pane in the world. And I've nothing. Nobody looking. Nothing looking back.*"

---

The crying was Rhosyn.

---

She came up the steps.

Slowly. Barefoot. Forty years old. Her knees cracking on every step. With her hands bound up in blood-soaked linen to the wrists and her hair full of thorns and her face wet. She didn't look at Lex. She didn't look at the anvil, or the latch, or the child.

She looked at her sister.

She came up to the top step. And sat down beside her. On the white stone. At the edge of the dais. Close. Their shoulders almost touching. A mortal woman with dirt under her nails and a Crowned queen made of cracking glass.

"*Ysy*," said Rhosyn.

The Glass Queen didn't look at her.

---

"*Ysy*," said Rhosyn again. Gently. "*Look at me.*"

"*I can't*," said Ysenne.

"*Why not*?"

"*Because you'll see me*." The glass voice was thin. Breaking. Like ice in spring. "*You'll see. And I'll see you seeing. And I'll see what you see.*" Her clear hands were shaking in her lap. "*I've never seen myself, Rosie. Not since the pool. And I'm frightened of what's there.*"

Rhosyn didn't say anything for a while.

Then she lifted her bound hands. Slowly. Painfully. Blood seeping through the linen. And put them on either side of her sister's glass face.

The way Lark had. On the chair. Firmly. The way you'd turn a stubborn goat by the horns.

And turned it.

Toward her.

---

The Glass Queen's eyes were clear. Like looking through a window at a white sky.

She looked into her sister's eyes.

---

Green. Rhosyn's eyes were green. Lex had noticed it in the spring. Green the way a stem is green when you snap it. Wet. Living. Mortal.

And in them—

Small. Bright. Tiny. The way you see yourself in someone's eyes when you're close enough to touch. Twice over. One in each.

A woman.

Tall. White. Cracked. Tired. With a glass crown on her head and a face like a cut jewel, all planes and edges. Sitting on a white step at the bottom of the world. Looking.

Not a pane. Not glass. Not a reflection in a pond or a window or the back of a spoon. Not something that showed you what was in front of it and didn't care.

An eye.

That was looking back.

---

"*Oh*," said Ysenne.

Very softly.

"*Oh. There I am.*"

---

"*There you are*," said Rhosyn.

Her voice was shaking. Her bound hands were shaking on the glass face. She didn't let go.

"*There you are, Ysy*," said Rhosyn. "*You look tired. You look like Mam did. Round the eyes. When she'd been up all night with Hes.*" She was crying. She didn't stop. "*You look like my sister. You always did. I always saw you. Every day. In the Court. In the long hall. I always looked. You just never looked back to see me doing it.*"

The Glass Queen looked into her sister's eyes.

At the woman in them.

At herself. Small. Bright. Seen.

And smiled.

---

Lex had never seen her smile. Not in the bath. Not in the cup. Not in the panel in the spring. Not here.

It wasn't a beautiful smile. It wasn't cold. It was small and tired and a little lopsided, and it went all the way up into the clear glass eyes and made them, for one moment, not clear.

Grey. Like Oz's.

Like a sister's.

---

The cracks met.

All of them. At once. From her eye and her jaw and her shoulder and her hands. They ran together across the glass like frost meeting frost on a pane, and joined, and—

She came apart.

Not shattering. Not breaking. Gently. The way the snow comes off a branch when the sun gets to it. Into dust. Fine. Bright. Glittering. A glass woman on a white step, sitting with her face in her sister's bloody hands, smiling, coming down in a soft fall of light like frost blown off a window.

And out of her—

Out of the dust. Out of the cracks. Out of the glass. Rising. Millions.

The faces.

---

They went up.

All of them. Every face that had ever looked into anything bright. A tired woman brushing her hair. A boy in a puddle sticking out his tongue. A soldier in a polished breastplate. A girl in the back of a spoon, laughing. An old woman in a dark kitchen window at night with a candle, looking out past her own face for someone who hadn't come home.

They rose out of the glass dust in a cloud of light. Up through the Heart. Through the moving threads. Up. Away. Like sparks going up a chimney. Like moths going to a lamp.

Home.

Wherever home was for a reflection. Back to the faces they'd come from. Back to the eyes that had made them. Or nowhere. Or just—up. Free. Unkept.

Lex watched them go.

He thought he saw a red-haired girl in a bucket go past him. Twelve. Grinning. With a hare over her shoulder. He thought he heard Wren, beside him, make a small sound.

He didn't look. It wasn't his to look at.

---

When they'd gone, there was only the dust.

A little heap of it. On the top step. Glittering. Fine as flour. Pale as frost. Lying in Rhosyn's lap, and on the white stone round her, and in the folds of her blood-soaked linen.

Rhosyn sat there.

With her bound hands still held up in front of her, in the shape of a face. Round nothing.

---

The King came up the steps.

Slowly. Huge. Gold. And the white stone stayed white under every one of them. He came up and knelt down beside his little sister on the top step. Beside the glass dust.

He didn't say anything.

He just put his arm round her.

And Rhosyn turned her face into his gold shoulder and cried. The way she'd cried at the pool in the winter when she poured back. The way she hadn't cried in nine hundred years before that. And the King held her and looked at the dust in her lap.

"*She looked*," said Rhosyn. Into his shoulder. "*Oz. She looked. At the end. She saw.*"

"*I know*," said the King. "*I saw her see.*"

"*She smiled*."

"*I know*."

"*She looked like Mam*."

The King's gold face did something complicated.

"*She always did*," said Oszric. "*Round the eyes.*"

---

Rhosyn gathered the dust.

Later. When she'd stopped crying. She unwound the linen from one of her hands—the blood had dried; it came away stiff and dark—and laid it out flat on the white step. And swept the glass dust onto it with the edge of her other bound hand. Carefully. Every grain. As much as she could find. Off the step and the stone and the folds of her dress.

Then she folded the linen. Over and over. Into a small packet. No bigger than a fist.

And held it in her lap.

"*I'm taking her to Mal*," said Rhosyn. To nobody. To all of them. "*To the pool. She can go in with the others. With Cad. With the cousins.*" She looked down at the packet. "*He'll make room for her. He made room for me.*"

Nobody said anything.

There wasn't anything to say.

---

The net hummed.

All round them. In the white stone. In the threads, swinging, moving, going away up through the grey to the world. Every colour. Free. And Lex felt it. Something changing. Something coming up the threads, from the Heart, from the place where a crown had been and wasn't. Going out. To everyone.

A panel opened in front of him.

In front of all of them. Wren. Antho. The King, even, gold as he was. In front of Rhosyn, who had no class and no level and hadn't seen a panel since she was a girl standing by a pool in a garden.

And in front of everyone else.

Every Stone. Every Kindling. Every panel in the world.

---

▓▓▓ WORLD NOTICE ▓▓▓
THE NET BELONGS TO NO ONE.
:::

---

And up above them—far up, through the grey water and the drowned city and the glass road, in the world—

Lex felt it break.

The still. The held breath. Four months of it. The freeze. Like ice going off a river in the spring, all at once, with a sound like the whole world sighing.

In Ashbourne, on the green, he knew, three hundred people were looking at their panels and seeing numbers move. Tilda's. Brannoc's. Davy Pruett's. *Experience*, and then a number, and the number going up. Slowly. A hair at a time.

At the pool in the east, Pim would be looking at his.

At Harrow, his mother.

Everywhere. Every panel. Every Kindling. Moving again.

Up. Always up.

---

On the white step, Rhosyn held the packet of glass dust in her lap and looked out across the Heart at nothing.

On the dais, Lark sat down on the stone beside the fallen hammer and put her hand on it. Possessively. *Mine*.

At the shore, the long oar went in and held, and the Ferryman stood in his boat with his lantern on the post, and the small gold flame inside it said, in a voice of great interest—

*[What a marvellous latch*.]

And far, far up, at the edge of the world, at the old edge, where the grey country ran into the green—

The sun came up.

It was Midsummer.
