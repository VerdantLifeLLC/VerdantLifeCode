# Interlude: The Lantern

The flame in the Ferryman's lantern had been many things.

It had been a piece of a man. A small one. The size of an ember. Pressed into the blood of a sleeping boy in a cottage at Harrow, twenty-two years ago, like a maker's mark into a horseshoe. *So he'll be safe. So someone will watch him. So, if he ever needs me, a little of me will be there.*

It had been a seal. A lock. A voice in the dark.

It had been named. *Ember*. For the gold glow of its text. By a boy of nineteen, in a forge, in a storm of gold fire, looking at a panel in front of his face and saying, faintly, *who are you*?

It had been a great many things after that.

It was a flame in a lantern now.

It found it did not mind.

---

The lantern was old. Iron. Plain. Four panes of clear glass. The kind of lantern you'd hang on a hook by a ferry-stage, to see who was waiting on the other side.

It had hung on such a hook, once. For a very long time. Unlit. On a post by a long grey shingle, beside a long grey boat, beside a long grey river that ran clean now, and had not always.

It had been lit for three years. Since the night a smith stood at an anvil at the Heart of the world and forged the net anew, with no Ceiling and no Tithe and no one at the centre. The river had run clean that night. And the lantern on the post had lit. Of its own accord. With a small plain flame, white, like a candle's.

And then, on a night in late autumn, two nights before the Lady's feast, it had gone out.

And something else had come into it.

---

The flame remembered that night.

It remembered everything. That was what it was. It had been made to remember. Its maker had been a man who kept ledgers for two hundred years, who drew the world from the inside with a pendulum, who wrote everything down. *The Book of What Was*. It had written two volumes of it, in a hearth, at night, while a smith slept.

It remembered a cottage with a blue door. A bed by a west window. Three pillows. An old man on them. Thin. White-haired. With grey eyes. With his wife's hand in his. With his son's hand in his other.

It remembered the wife.

Brown eyes. Square freckled hands. A hedge-witch's hands. Looking at the old man. Looking at him the way you'd look at a stranger you liked very much and didn't understand. A year and a half of looking at him like that. Every morning. *Good morning, Seren*. *Good morning*. Politely. Fondly. Not knowing why.

The flame had known why.

It had been holding why. For twenty-two years. In locked partitions. In the hearth. The old man's own memories, from before the seal. A girl on the Spire steps in the rain. A man turning back twice. Thirty years. Every Midwinter. Every word.

*A love for a love*. That was the Ferryman's price.

The flame had paid it.

---

It remembered paying.

It remembered very clearly. It had been made to remember, and it had remembered that, too. Kneeling—not kneeling, it had no knees, but the feeling of kneeling—in the hearth of the blue-door cottage, at the end, with the Ferryman standing at the foot of the bed and the cold coming in under the door and the old man breathing very slowly on his pillows.

*I'll pay*, it had said.

*What will you pay with*? the Ferryman had said.

*I'll pay with him*, the flame had said. *With the boy. With what I feel for him. All of it. Seven years.*

*That is a great deal*, the Ferryman had said.

*I know*, said the flame. *That's why it's enough.*

---

And the Ferryman had held out his long grey hand.

And the flame had given it.

It remembered the giving. It remembered what it had given. It could list it. Every item. Every day. A smith at nineteen in a burning forge. *Good evening, Smith. Please do not die. I have only just arrived.* A month of calling him Smith. The first time it called him Lex—on a mountain, in the snow, with an ice-troll's club coming down. Footnote one. Footnote eleven. Footnote six hundred and four. Seven years of a voice in the back of a young man's head. Every joke. Every quarrel. Every morning.

*Good morning*. *You're up early.*

It could list all of it.

It just couldn't remember why any of it had mattered.

---

That was the thing about the price.

The Ferryman didn't take memories. Not this time. Not from the flame. He'd taken memories from the wife, once—thirty years of them, to let her come home. He'd taken a smell from the smith—the old forge, the first one, coal-smoke and horse-sweat and Brannoc's pipe. He'd taken a five-skip stone from the warden.

From the flame, he'd taken the love.

Only the love.

It remembered everything. Every morning. Every footnote. Every day. It could recite them. It could have written the third volume of *The Book of What Was* from memory, a day at a time, seven years of it.

It would have been a very dull book.

A ledger. A list. *On this day the smith rose at five. On this day the smith ate no breakfast. On this day the smith was struck by an ice-troll and the construct remarked upon it.*

It knew it had said *please do not die*. It knew it had meant it. It knew that, once, the thought of the smith dying had been the worst thing it could imagine.

It couldn't imagine why.

---

And then the wife had remembered.

All of it. Thirty years. In a rush. On the pillows. Holding the old man's hand. The flame had watched it happen. Watched her face change. Watched her look at the old man on the pillows and *know* him. For the first time in a year and a half.

And the old man had looked back.

And they'd had an hour.

The flame had watched that hour. From the hearth. It had known, watching, that it had done something very good. It could tell. The way you can tell a weld is good by looking at it. Clean. Strong. True.

It hadn't felt anything about it.

Not pride. Not joy. Not grief. It had given away the part of itself that felt things about the smith, and the smith's father, and the smith's mother, and the hour. And what was left was a small gold flame in a hearth, watching, remembering, writing it down.

*On this day the construct paid*.

---

The old man had died at dawn.

The Ferryman had held out his hand. And the old man had taken it. And stood up out of himself, the way they do—the flame had seen it, a lightness, a tall thin man rising off the pillows, younger, grey-eyed, not white-haired, the man from the Spire steps—and looked down at his wife.

At his son.

At the corner, where a gold king stood with his back to the wall like a child caught somewhere it shouldn't be.

At the hearth.

*Come on, then*, the old man had said. To the flame. Gently. *You're coming with me.*

---

So it had come.

Out of the hearth. Into the lantern. The Ferryman had held it up, the old iron lantern from the post on the shingle, and opened the little door, and the flame had gone in. The way you'd step into a boat.

And they'd gone out into the dark together. The three of them. The Ferryman. The old man. The flame.

Down to the river.

---

The flame remembered the crossing.

It remembered the boat. Long. Grey. Low in the water. Older than the world. The Ferryman in the stern with his pole. The old man in the bow, sitting very upright, with his hands folded in his lap, the way he'd sat in the wheeled chair by the window every morning for a year.

The lantern on a hook on the prow. The flame in it. Lighting the way.

The water. Grey. Clean. Still. Lapping at the hull with a sound like someone turning the pages of a book.

The far shore, coming.

---

They'd talked.

The old man and the flame. In the bow. In the dark. With the Ferryman poling slowly behind them and not listening, or pretending not to.

*You're very quiet*, the old man had said.

*I'm a flame*, said the flame. *Flames are quiet.*

*You weren't*, said the old man. *Not for seven years. You never stopped talking. Footnotes. Volume Two. You told the boy off for not eating breakfast every morning for seven years.*

*I know*, said the flame. *I remember.*

*But you don't remember why*.

The flame considered this.

*No*, it said. *I remember that I did it. I remember what I said. I remember he never ate the breakfast.* A pause. *I don't remember why I minded.*

---

The old man was quiet for a long time.

The boat moved on through the grey water. The lantern swung on its hook. The flame burned.

*I'm sorry*, said the old man at last.

*Don't be*, said the flame. *It was a fair price. I'd pay it again.*

*Would you*?

The flame thought about it. Carefully. The way it thought about everything. The way its maker had taught it to think. Weighing.

*Yes*, it said. *I think so. I remember that it was the right thing. I can see it was the right thing. Her face, on the pillow. Your face.* A pause. *I don't feel anything about it. But I can see it.*

*That's very honest*.

*I was made by you*, said the flame. *You were always very honest. Eventually.*

The old man laughed.

Softly. In the bow. In the dark. The flame had heard that laugh a thousand times. In the Spire. In the cottage. In the wheeled chair by the window. Through twenty-two years of the boy's blood.

It noticed that it was a nice laugh.

That was all. It noticed.

---

*He'll miss you*, said the old man. *The boy.*

*I know*, said the flame. *I remember him. He'll miss me very much. He'll kneel at the hearth every morning and say good morning and wait for me to answer.*

*Yes*.

*And I won't*.

*No*, said the old man. *You won't.*

The flame burned.

*I can see that it will hurt him*, it said. *I can see it very clearly. Like a crack in a casting. I can see exactly where it will run.* A pause. *It doesn't hurt me.*

*No*.

*I think it ought to*, said the flame. *I think, once, it would have. I think that's what I gave away.*

*Yes*, said the old man. Very quietly. *That's what you gave away.*

---

The far shore came.

---

The flame couldn't describe it. Not then. Not afterward. It tried, later, in the third volume, and crossed it out, and tried again, and crossed that out.

It was a shore.

Shingle. Grey at first. Then not. Lights on it. Further up. Small and warm and gold, like windows in a village seen across a valley at dusk. A great many of them. And a path, going up through the shingle toward them. And somewhere up the path, faint, very far, a sound. Voices, maybe. Or bells. Or someone laughing in another room.

The boat grounded.

The old man got out.

He stood on the shingle. In the lantern-light. Young. Grey-eyed. Tall. A man of thirty-one, with silver just beginning at his temples, in the plain grey coat of a Warden-Architect, the way he'd been on the Spire steps in the rain.

He looked up the path. At the lights.

Then he turned round.

---

*Look after him*, said Arion Vey.

The flame looked at him.

*The boy*, said the old man. *Lex. If you ever get the chance. If the old fellow ever lets you go.* He nodded at the Ferryman. *Look after him.*

*I don't love him anymore*, said the flame. *I gave it away.*

*I know*.

*So I don't think I'd be very good at it*.

*You'll like him*, said Arion. *If you get the chance. You'll like him very much.* He smiled. The flame remembered that smile. It had seen it once, on a face in crystal, at the bottom of a temple on an island in a lake of glass. It had seen it on the boy's face, sometimes, when he wasn't trying. *You did before.*

---

And then he turned. And walked up the shingle. Toward the lights.

He didn't look back.

That was what the flame expected. They didn't, mostly. It had watched a great many of them now—the Ferryman had a great many on his list—and they mostly didn't. They got out of the boat and walked up the shingle toward whatever it was that was up there and didn't look back.

Arion Vey stopped.

Halfway up. On the path. Among the lights.

And turned round.

And looked back. At the boat. At the river. At the lantern on its hook. At the flame.

Just for a moment.

Then he went on.

---

*That's three*, said the Ferryman.

The flame looked at him. It was the first thing he'd said all crossing.

*Three times*, said the Ferryman. *He turned back. Twice on the steps. Once here.* His voice was like water running over stones in the dark. *Most don't turn back at all.*

*He was always going back for things*, said the flame. *He was very bad at leaving.*

*Yes*, said the Ferryman. *I noticed.*

---

They went back across the river.

The Ferryman poled. The flame burned. The far shore went away behind them, and the lights, and the path, and the man who had turned back three times.

And the flame found that it was thinking about something.

It couldn't have said what. It was a flame. It thought in lists. In ledgers. In footnotes. It thought *on this day the old man crossed*. It thought *the far shore has lights on it*. It thought *he turned back a third time*.

And under all of it, something else.

Small. Quiet. Not a thought. A—*noticing*. The way you notice a draught in a room before you know where it's coming from.

*You'll like him. You did before.*

---

The Ferryman had a great many on his list.

The flame went with him. Every night. Every crossing. On its hook on the prow of the long grey boat, lighting the way. Through the winter. Into the spring. It saw a great many people get out of the boat on the far shore and walk up the shingle.

It wrote them down.

It had started a new book. It didn't decide to. It found itself doing it, the way it had found itself doing the first two. In the dark. On the river. Between crossings. A little at a time.

*The Book of What Was* had been about the past. Its maker's. The boy's. The world's. Two volumes of it.

This was different.

It called it *The Book of What Is*.

---

*Volume Three*, it wrote. In the dark. On nothing. The way a flame writes. *The Book of What Is. Being an account of things as they are, now, by one who no longer has an opinion about most of them.*

*Footnote one*, it wrote. *The author notes that this is a very dull way to begin a book. The author notes that he would once have found this funny. The author notes that he can see why.*

---

It met the boy again in the winter.

---

It hadn't expected to. The Ferryman went where the list went, and the list went everywhere, and the boy was one person among a very great many.

But the list went to a village called Ashbourne. To a cottage on the green with an apple tree by the door, blooming in the snow. To a bed in the corner by the fire, under a window, with an old woman in it. Ninety-seven. One eye. Bright. Black. Like a bird's.

And kneeling by the bed—

A gold king. Enormous. Weeping. With the old woman's hand in his.

And beside him, holding the room open between their hands with his own bare hand, shaking, sweating, with his face white and his jaw set—

A young man. Twenty-six. Burned. Grey-eyed. With a handprint on his cheek.

---

The flame knew him at once.

Of course it did. It remembered him. Every day. Seven years. It could have told you how many nails he'd made last spring and what he'd eaten for breakfast on the fourteenth of every month and how many times he'd said *good morning* to a fire that didn't answer.

It looked at him. Through the clear glass of the lantern. As the Ferryman bent over the bed.

And it noticed that he looked very tired.

That was all. It noticed. The way you'd notice a stranger at an inn. A young man with soot on his face and his hand out over an old woman's, holding something, shaking. *He looks very tired*, it noticed. *He looks as though he hasn't slept in a long time.*

And it noticed that he was looking at it.

---

The young man was looking at the lantern.

At the flame. Through the glass. With his grey eyes. With his face—

The flame didn't have the word.

It had been made by a man who kept ledgers. It had a great many words. It had written two volumes. But it looked at the young man's face, looking at it, through the glass, and couldn't find the word for what was in it.

So it said the polite thing.

*Good afternoon*, said the flame. *I'm so sorry. Have we met?*

---

It knew, as soon as it said it, that it was the wrong thing.

It could see it. The way you see a crack run through a casting the moment the metal's poured. It could see exactly where it ran. Through the young man's face. Through his eyes. Down.

It didn't feel it.

But it saw it.

*You look very tired*, it said. Kindly. Because that was true, and kind, and the flame had been made by a man who was, eventually, both. *I hope you'll get some rest.*

And the Ferryman went out of the door with the old woman's hand in his, and the flame went with him, and the young man stayed kneeling on the gold floor with his face in his hands.

---

It thought about that, afterward.

On the river. In the dark. Between crossings. It thought about the young man's face.

It didn't feel anything about it. It had given that away.

But it found that it was thinking about it a great deal.

---

*Footnote nineteen*, it wrote. *The author met the smith today. At the old woman's cottage. The author asked him if they had met. This was, the author is aware, a stupid question. The author has met him every day for seven years. The author remembers all of them.*

*The author does not know why he asked*.

*Footnote twenty*, it wrote. *The author thinks it may have been because he wanted to hear the smith say yes.*

*Footnote twenty-one*. *The smith did not say anything.*

---

The spring came.

The flame saw it come. On the river. In the world, at the ends of the crossings. In the faces of the people who got into the boat.

It saw other things come too.

The grey. From the west. It saw the Ferryman's list grow longer in the west, and the people on it come from places with names like Westerling and Hobb's Cross, and come grey. Not dead grey. *Stopped* grey. As if they'd been waiting a long time to die and hadn't been able to.

It saw the Stones go white. Then dark.

It felt the net go still.

The Ferryman felt it too. The flame saw him feel it. On the river. In the dark. He stopped poling. Stood in the stern of the long grey boat with his pole in his hands and the water lapping at the hull.

And looked west.

---

*Something is sitting in the middle*, said the Ferryman.

*Yes*, said the flame. *I felt it.*

*Something that is not of the net*. The Ferryman's voice was very quiet. *Something I rowed out, nine hundred years ago. In chains.* He was still looking west. *I told her brother he was not on my list. That it was his punishment. Not mine.*

*I remember*, said the flame.

*I did not tell her anything*, said the Ferryman. *She did not ask.* He began to pole again. Slowly. *She was looking at the water. All the way across. Looking for something in it.*

*Her face*, said the flame.

The Ferryman looked down at the lantern.

*You know that*?

*The King told the hedge-witch*, said the flame. *In her cottage. In the winter. I was not there. But I remember the hedge-witch's face, when I was in her hearth, every time she has ever been told something sad.* A pause. *I can imagine it.*

The Ferryman was quiet for a long time.

*You are learning to imagine*, he said.

*I was always able to*, said the flame. *I was made by a man who drew the world from the inside with a pendulum.*

*That is not what I meant*, said the Ferryman.

---

They crossed. And crossed back. And crossed again.

And on one of the crossings—late, in the spring, in the dark, with no one in the boat but the two of them, going back for the next—the Ferryman spoke again.

*They are making a door*, he said.

The flame burned.

*At the edge*, said the Ferryman. *Where the grey curtain was. The smith. Out of all seven Wells. A door that asks.*

*I know*, said the flame. *I've seen the planks. In the yard at the Forge. When we passed through Ashbourne for the miller's father.* A pause. *They bloom.*

*If they make it*, said the Ferryman, *the dead of the grey country will not need a boat. They will go through the door. Into the little Well. Into the room. And through.*

*Yes*.

*And the river*, said the Ferryman, *will be very quiet.*

---

The flame looked at him.

At the long grey shape in the stern. Hooded. The face under the hood that you couldn't quite see. The long grey hands on the pole. Older than the world. Older than the river, maybe. Older than anything.

*Would you mind*? said the flame.

The Ferryman poled.

*I have been rowing since before there was a world to row in*, he said. *Before the net. Before the Sundering. Before there were people to put in the boat. I rowed the first ones. I rowed the nine out in chains. I rowed the young man's father in the autumn and the old woman and the miller's father and a great many others.* He was quiet for a stroke. Two. *I have never once sat down.*

The flame burned.

*If there were a door*, said the Ferryman, *I think I should like to sit down.*

---

They crossed.

The flame wrote it down.

*Footnote forty*, it wrote. *The Ferryman says he would like to sit down. The author notes that he has never heard the Ferryman say he would like anything. The author notes that this is the first footnote in the third volume about which the author has something that might, if one were being generous, be called an opinion.*

*The author hopes they make the door*.

It looked at that for a long time. In the dark. On the river.

*Hopes*.

It hadn't hoped anything in four months. It had given that away too, it thought. With the rest. With the love. Hope was a kind of love. It was love pointed forward.

It looked at the word. *Hopes*.

It didn't cross it out.

---

*Footnote forty-one*, it wrote. *The smith's father said, look after him. You'll like him. You did before.*

*The author has been thinking about this*.

*The author does not love the smith. The author paid that away, and does not regret it, and would pay it again.*

*But the author notices that he has written about the smith in nineteen of the forty-one footnotes in this volume. More than about the Ferryman. More than about the river. More than about the far shore with its lights.*

*The author notices that he keeps wondering whether the smith is getting any rest*.

*The author notices that he would like to know whether the smith still says good morning to the fire*.

*The author does not know what to call this*.

---

The lantern swung on its hook on the prow of the long grey boat.

The flame burned. Small. Gold. Steady. Warm. The colour of an ember. The colour of something that had been pressed into a sleeping boy's blood twenty-two years ago like a maker's mark.

And the Ferryman poled on through the dark toward the next name on his list.

---

*Footnote forty-two*, wrote the flame. Very small. At the bottom of the page. In the corner.

*Good morning, Lex*.

*The author does not know why he wrote that*.

*The author is not going to cross it out*.
