# The Lantern Road

Seren kept the window all night.

It wasn't a window. It was a door. The great doors at the end of the long hall of the Forge, oak and iron, twelve feet high, standing open onto the yard and the dark and the grey mist that had rolled up out of the cinders when the Ferryman came. She stood in them. With the clay lamp in her hands. Wenna's lamp. Cracked across the lip. Burning.

She didn't sit down. She didn't sleep.

Seraphine brought her a stool at midnight. She didn't sit on it. Brannoc brought her tea at two. She drank it standing. Tilda brought a blanket at four and put it round her shoulders without asking, and Seren let her, and went on standing in the doorway holding the lamp out into the dark.

*Somebody has to keep the window*.

---

At some point before dawn, the panels moved.

She felt it first. Not in her own—she didn't have one, not really, she'd never Kindled properly, Harrow women didn't—but in the people round her. A gasp. Down the long hall. Tilda, at the great hearth, with her gold hand held up in front of her face and a silver-blue light hanging over it.

"*It's moving*," said Tilda. Thin. High. "*Brannoc. Brannoc, my panel. It's—the number. It's moving.*"

Brannoc came out of the kitchen with a ladle in his hand and stood in the middle of the hall and looked at the air in front of his face. At the panel there. At whatever was written on it.

He didn't say anything for a long time.

Then he went and knelt at the great hearth, with his knees cracking, and blew on the coals. And the fire came up. Fast. Through. Without him reaching. A roar of white-gold that made Tilda jump back and Kestrel, asleep on a bench, sit up shouting.

*[Good morning*,] said the fire. Glad.

"*Morning*," said Brannoc Holt. Gruffly.

And wiped his face with the back of his hand.

---

Seren didn't move from the door.

She knew what it meant. She'd lived with Arion Vey for a year and a half and with the net all her life, and she knew. The still had broken. The freeze. Somebody, at the bottom of the world, had taken the Glass Queen's hand off the strings.

It didn't tell her who.

It didn't tell her whether they were alive.

She stood in the door with the lamp and waited.

---

The mist came back at sunrise.

Up out of the cinders. Grey. Moving. Knee-high. Rolling across the yard from the bottom, where the stream had been, where Brannoc took his quench-water. And out of it—

A light. Low. Gold. Swinging.

And a boat.

---

Lark came out of it first.

Of course she did. Before it touched. Before anyone could stop her. She went over the side of the black boat into the mist on her bottom, the way she went down the stairs, and stood up in the grey with the mist round her knees and her nightshirt filthy and her black hair sticking up at the back, and looked up the yard at the Forge doors.

At her Gan. In them. With a lamp.

"*GAN*," shrieked Lark.

And ran.

---

Seren didn't remember putting the lamp down. Afterward Seraphine said she'd set it very carefully on the flags by the door, the way you'd set down something precious, before she went down on her knees on the cinders. Seren didn't remember. She remembered the weight. Warm. Solid. Small arms round her neck. A wet face in her collar. A voice in her ear, furious, tremendous, telling her all about it.

"*Gan. Gan. Wa'er. Lady. Cold. No. No. Da. Hot. Ham'er. More.*"

"*I know*," said Seren. Into the black hair. Not knowing anything. "*I know, love. I know. I know.*"

---

They came up the yard behind her.

Wren, barefoot, with her hand on Lark's back and her face grey with tiredness and her mouth trembling. Antho, with his arm round Rhosyn, who was holding something in her lap—a packet, small, folded linen, no bigger than a fist—as if it were made of eggs. The King, gold, enormous, last out of the boat, with a white petal in one hand and Hes's crown in the other.

And Lex.

Walking slowly. With his ribs. With his right hand unwrapped, raw, blistered. With Aurelian's hammer on his belt and something in his other hand. Small. Gold-grey. Plain.

He stopped in front of her.

She looked up at him from her knees on the cinders with his daughter in her arms.

"*Are you all right*?" said Seren Harrow.

Lex looked down at her for a long time.

"*I'm common*," he said.

And sat down on the cinders beside her, and put his head on her shoulder, and started to laugh, and then to cry, and then both, and didn't stop for a long time.

---

The Ferryman didn't get out of the boat.

He stood in the stern with the long oar. Tall. Grey. Hooded. With the lantern on its post.

Seren looked at him over her son's head.

She'd met him once before. On the night Arion died. He'd stood at the foot of the bed with his hand out, and Arion had taken it, and the old man had looked at her—at her, Seren, sitting by the bed—and given her one memory. A gift. *The Spire steps*. *He turned back*.

He looked at her now.

"*Lady*," said the Ferryman.

"*Thank you*," said Seren. "*For bringing them home.*"

The Ferryman was silent.

Then: "*I'll be at the edge*," he said. "*When it's done. When the door's hung.*" The hood tilted. Very slightly. Toward the lantern on its post. Toward the small gold flame inside it. "*I'd like to see how it ends.*"

*[So would I*,] said the flame. Interested. Courteous. *[I've a great deal to write.]*

Seren's breath caught.

She'd known its voice for a year and a half. In the hearth at Harrow. In Lex's chest. Dry. Small. Precise. Telling Arion the dates of things. Correcting her about the Wells. Saying *good morning* to her son every day across the kitchen as if it had invented the words.

It looked at her now. From the lantern. Politely.

It didn't know her.

*[Good morning*,] said the flame. *[I don't believe we've met.]*

"*No*," said Seren. Very gently. "*Not yet.*"

The long oar went in. The boat turned. The mist went back down the yard and into the cinders, and took the light with it, and was gone.

---

They slept until noon.

All of them. Lex and Wren and Lark in a heap on the pallet in the tool-room, and nobody dared go in. Antho on the floor of the kitchen with his head in Seraphine's lap, and Seraphine asleep sitting up against the wall. Rhosyn on Seren's cot by the great hearth, with the packet of glass dust held against her chest in both hands.

The King didn't sleep. The King sat on the flags by the apple wood door, gold, with his back to the wall and his knees drawn up, and looked at it.

Seren sat beside him.

She didn't say anything. Neither did he. They sat there together in the long hall in the noon light, with the fires roaring in all twenty hearths because Tilda had lit every one of them out of pure joy, and looked at the door.

Seven feet high. Three and a half wide. Apple wood. Blooming. Hanging on its frame in the middle of the floor on hinges forged from the Key, which breathed. With no threshold under it. No bell. No paint.

No latch.

"*He made it out of the crown*," said the King.

Seren looked at him.

"*The latch*," said Oszric. "*He unmade the crown on the first anvil. And made a latch. He let the little one strike the last blow.*" His gold face did something complicated. "*Aurelian made that crown. On that anvil. I held the tongs.*"

Seren didn't say anything.

"*I thought it would be the most beautiful thing I'd ever see*," said the King. "*When I was a boy. When Aurelian made it. I thought, that's it. That's the most beautiful thing anyone will ever make.*" He looked at the door. At the empty place where the latch would go. "*I was wrong. It's a latch. A plain little latch. You lift it to go in.*"

He was quiet for a long time.

"*She looked*," he said. "*Ysy. At the end. In Rosie's eyes.*"

"*I know*," said Seren. "*Rosie told me.*" She'd told her at dawn. In the yard. In one breath. And then gone very white and sat down.

"*She smiled*," said the King. "*I'd forgotten her smile. I'd forgotten she had one.*" A pause. "*Nine hundred years.*"

Seren put her hand on his arm.

Gold. Cold. Hard. Like a doorknob.

She left it there.

---

The raven came at three.

Seren saw it from the yard. A black speck over the west, coming out of the grey, out of the picture of a sky, and then not a speck, a bird. Big. Ragged. Exhausted. It came in low over the lantern ring and the Forge roof and dropped onto the yard cinders at her feet like a sack of coal, and lay there with its wings out and its beak open, panting.

It had a paper tied to its leg.

Seren knelt and untied it. Gently. The bird let her. It had come three hundred miles from the east, and the last of them through the grey, and Seren didn't know how. She didn't think the bird knew either.

She unfolded the paper.

A small careful hand. In pencil. The longest note she had ever seen Pim write.

---

She read it twice.

Then she took it into the Forge, to the long hall, to the place by the apple wood door where the King was still sitting on the flags with his back to the wall.

She held it out.

The King looked at it.

"*It's for Lex*," said Seren. "*But I think it's for you.*"

---

He read it slowly.

His lips moved. He'd learned to read nine hundred years ago, in a palace, from a tutor with a stick, and he'd had nothing to read since. His gold finger went along under the pencil lines, one word at a time, the way a child's does.

*The King is practising. Letting go. We felt it. It hurts them. It frightens them. They go like sand.*

His finger stopped.

Went on.

*He has to do it all at once. He can't do it a bit at a time. Tell him.*

*When he does it, they shouldn't be on their own. Nobody should come apart on their own. Not again.*

*We'll hold their hands. Here. At the pool. As many as we can get. Ivy and Hask and Kit and me. And Ilda. And all the dead. Tell him to send them here. Tell him to send them all.*

*Tell him we're ready.*

*Tell him not to be sorry. Tell him it's all right to let go.*

*P.*

---

And under it. At the bottom. Very small.

---

The King's finger stopped.

It stayed there. On the last line. For a long time.

Seren watched his face. She'd watched it all winter. At Harrow. In her kitchen, on a gold circle on the floor, with a cup of tea going cold in front of him. In her garden, under the grave, with his gold hands on his knees. She'd seen it do a lot of things.

She'd never seen it do this.

---

"*Maudie*," said the King.

Very quietly.

"*Maud. The bread-woman. In the big kitchen. She sang.*" His voice was a whisper. "*She sang like a cat in a well. Agnes used to say. The cook.*" He stared at the paper. "*I knew that. I knew her. I used to go down to the kitchen at night when I couldn't sleep, when I was a boy, and she'd give me the heel of the loaf and tell me to get out from under her feet.*"

He looked up at Seren.

"*I forgot her name*," said Oszric. "*Nine hundred years. I walked past her on the floor of the hall while she came apart, with her daughter in her arms, and I kept the girl and I let her go and I forgot her name.*"

Seren didn't say anything.

"*A boy remembered it*," said the King. "*A boy who doesn't talk. Who wrote it down.*"

He folded the paper. Very carefully. Small. And held it in his gold fist with the white petal and Hes's crown.

And put his face in his other hand.

---

Seren sat with him while he cried.

It took a long time. Nine hundred years is a long time to save up.

---

When he'd finished, he wiped his face with his gold hand—it left no wet; nothing stayed wet on him—and looked at the door.

"*When it's hung*," said the King. "*When it's done. When it's at the edge, and open, and there's somewhere for them to go.*"

"*Yes*," said Seren.

"*I'll let go*," said Oszric. "*All at once. Like the boy says.*" He swallowed. "*All of them. Everyone I've got. Roole at his gate. The Ashby lad in his field. The smith's wife's fingers, and the girl's hand, and Brannoc's. And my household. All nine thousand.*" His voice was very steady. "*And me. I'll have to let go of me too. It's all one hand. I can't open it and keep one finger shut.*"

Seren looked at him.

"*You'll come apart*," she said. "*Like them. Nine hundred years.*"

"*Yes*."

"*Like sand*."

"*Yes*," said the King. "*But not on my own.*" He looked down at the folded paper in his fist. "*The boy says nobody should. Not again.*"

Then he turned his huge gold head. And looked across the long hall. To the cot by the great hearth, where his sister was sleeping with a packet of glass dust held against her chest.

"*Will you ask Rosie*," said the Gilded King, "*if she'll walk me to the door*?"

---

Rosie said yes.

She said it before Seren had finished asking. Sitting up on the cot with the packet in her hands and her hair full of thorns and her face creased from the blanket. She looked across the hall at her brother sitting by the apple wood door.

"*Of course I will*," said Rhosyn. "*Silly. Of course. Who else would?*"

---

They dug the Stone up the next morning.

All of Ashbourne came to watch.

---

It stood on the green where it had always stood. Since before the village. Since before the Lattice was the Lattice. A tall grey standing stone, eight feet, rough, leaning a little west, with a crack down its face from crown to foot that Lex had put there himself, the day he Kindled, when the net looked at a nineteen-year-old smith's boy and saw his grandfather's grandfather's grandfather's blood, and the Stone had rung like a bell and split.

It had been wrapped in canvas and chains since the spring. Since the Glass Queen came into the Stones and looked out of them at children.

Lex took the chains off himself.

---

Seren watched him do it. From the edge of the green. With Lark on her hip and Rhosyn beside her and Wren on her other side, leaning on her bow, which she wasn't using as a bow, because she could barely stand.

He did it slowly. With his ribs strapped and his right hand wrapped in fresh linen. Lock by lock. Link by link. Brannoc beside him, holding the ends. The chains came off one at a time and went on the grass in a heap. Then the canvas. Four layers. Then the sacking.

And the Stone stood there.

Grey. Cracked. Old.

Dark.

---

Lex put his hand on it.

His left. The one that wasn't burned. Flat. On the grey rough face. On the crack. The way you'd put your hand on the neck of a horse you hadn't seen in a long time.

The whole green held its breath.

Nothing happened.

No eye. No white light. No cold. No Glass Queen looking out of it at the children of Ashbourne. Just a Stone. Old. Grey. Cracked. Warm in the sun.

And then, faintly, from somewhere under Lex's palm—

A hum.

---

It wasn't loud. Seren barely heard it. A low note, deep, slow, like a bell a long way off across fields. Coming up out of the grey stone into Lex's hand.

And a panel opened over it.

Silver-blue. Small. Hanging in the air over the cracked crown of the Stone, where every child in Ashbourne for four hundred years had looked up and seen their name.

---

Lex stared at it.

"*It knows*," said Seraphine. Behind Seren. Very quietly. With her quill stopped on the page. "*It knows what it's for.*"

"*It's always known*," said Brannoc. Gruffly. "*It's a Stone. It's what they do. They stand at the edge of things and look at people going past and say what they are.*" He looked at the panel. "*Every child in this village stood on that patch of grass and walked past it into their life.*"

*Threshold: stone*, Seraphine had written in the spring, in the margin of her drawing. *Something that knows what people are*.

---

They didn't dig it out with spades in the end.

Tom Pruett had brought spades. And the Penn boys. And half the men in the village. And a cart, with six oxen, borrowed from three farms, and levers and rope and a block and tackle off the church tower.

They didn't need any of it.

Lex knelt on the green in front of the Stone. And put both hands flat on the grass at its foot. And reached. Not round the net. Not down past it. Through. The way he'd reached at the Heart. The way fire came now, and stone, and everything.

*Please*, he said. *Up. You're needed.*

And the Stone came up out of the ground.

---

The whole green went quiet.

It came up slowly. The way a man stands up after a long time kneeling. With its roots coming out of the earth after it—not roots, Seren saw, but the bottom of the stone, eight feet more of it, grey and rough and earth-stained, that had been underground for four hundred years. It rose out of the green with the grass parting round it and the soil falling off it in clods, sixteen feet of grey stone with a crack down the middle, and stood on the grass beside its own hole.

And then, gently, as if it had been asked—

Lay down.

On its side. On the green. Sixteen feet long. A long grey stone. Cracked. Old. The length of a doorway.

The panel hung over it. *THRESHOLD*.

---

Lark wriggled down off Seren's hip.

Nobody stopped her. She went across the green on her own, barefoot, in a clean nightshirt that wouldn't stay clean for a minute, to the long grey stone lying on the grass. And climbed onto it. And stood on it. In the middle. Over the crack. With her arms out for balance and her eyebrows down.

And looked round at everybody.

Very pleased with herself.

"*Mine*," said Lark.

The whole green laughed.

---

They left on the third day after Midsummer.

Everyone.

---

Seraphine had done the sums. She'd done them on the floor of the long hall with chalk, crawling about on her knees round her belly with her horn spectacles slipping down her nose, while Antho sat on a bench and fretted.

"*It's a hole*," said Seraphine. "*The edge. Where the Veil was. It's not a line. It's a hole. The world's room is going out of it the way water goes out of a bucket with a hole in it. That's what the grey is. That's why it spreads. It's the world draining.*" She drew a long line on the flags. "*Two hundred miles west. The old edge. That's where it's going out. That's where the door has to go.*"

"*Two hundred miles*," said Antho. "*Through the grey.*"

"*Yes*."

"*With a door. And a sixteen-foot stone. And a pregnant woman.*"

"*I'll be fine*," said Seraphine.

"*You'll be*—"

"*I'll be fine*," said Seraphine. "*I've got four hundred and twelve reasons. And one more coming in August.*" She sat back on her heels. "*And I've walked further. With worse.*"

---

The grey had slowed. Since the freeze broke. A mile a day, Elowen said, riding back from the stakes. Less, some days. But it hadn't stopped. It still lay round Ashbourne on every side, a picture of fields, a picture of woods, all the way west to the edge and further east every day toward the Vale and Fen Hollow and the Teeth.

Two hundred miles of it.

"*We'll walk*," said Seren.

---

So they walked.

---

Seren went first.

She hadn't meant to. She'd meant to walk with Rhosyn, somewhere in the middle, behind the carts, where she could keep an eye on Lark. But on the morning they left, when the whole of Ashbourne was gathered on the green at dawn with their packs on their backs and their lanterns in their hands, waiting, somebody said *who's going first*, and somebody else said *the Lady*, and everybody looked at her.

So she went first.

---

She walked out of the lantern ring at the west of the village. Past the mill-pond, grey and hard as slate. Past the last of the lanterns. Onto the grass beyond, which was a picture of grass.

And held her hands out. One to each side.

Rosie took one. Wren took the other.

And Seren walked into the grey.

---

It went round her.

The way it had at the grave. Not two yards. Less. A ring of green round her feet, moving with her. She didn't know how she did it. She didn't know what it was. Something in her hands. Something she'd been carrying for thirty years. A Harrow thing.

She walked. And behind her, the green stayed.

Not all of it. Not the whole width of her ring. A path. A thin green path where her feet had been, a yard wide, curving away behind her across the grey field like a ribbon dropped on a page.

And behind her, Rosie. And behind Rosie, the King, gold, enormous, with a white petal in his hand. And behind the King, the first cart.

---

It was Tom Pruett's cart. Six oxen. Eight wheels. Built in a week by every carpenter in the Vale. And on it, lashed upright in the middle with ropes and wedges and blankets—

The door.

Seven feet high. Three and a half wide. Apple wood. Blooming. Petals coming off it in the wind of their walking and blowing back over the oxen and the carters and the road, white, pink at the edges. Hanging on its frame on its breathing hinges. Swinging, a little, as the cart went over the ruts. Out. And back.

Lex walked beside it. With his hand on the frame.

Behind it, the second cart. Twelve oxen. And on it, the Stone. Sixteen feet of grey. Cracked. With a small girl sitting on the very end of it in a sling of blankets Brannoc had rigged, watching the oxen, telling them what to do.

And behind that—

---

Everyone.

---

Seren turned round at the top of the first rise and looked back.

She'd meant to look for a moment. She stood there for a long time.

They came out of the lantern ring of Ashbourne in a long line, onto the green path she'd made through the grey, and every one of them had a lantern.

Hollis Penn with his barrow. Tilda with Brannoc's hammer on her back and a forge-lantern on a pole. Kestrel. Teo, who'd come up from the coast with his fish in a jar of water, because he wouldn't leave Barnaby with anyone. Davy Pruett, holding his mother's hand, and his mother with the new baby in a sling and a jam-jar candle in her other hand. Jem Tolley the beekeeper, whose lantern had been crushed by his own glass face, carrying a new one, tin, rough, that Lex had made him the night before. Old Mag's neighbour with her great-grandmother's iron lantern with the cracked pane. Nan Ashby. With the HERE lantern Lex had given her in the spring.

Three hundred. Four. More. Coming out of the villages round about, out of the grey, in twos and threes, with their own lanterns—Fen Hollow and Hobb's Cross and Westerling and Thistledown—falling in at the back of the line.

And as they walked, the lanterns did what given lanterns did.

They held a circle. Each one. Round its bearer. A ring of real ground. A ring of green. Small. A yard. Two. Overlapping. Joining. Each ring touching the next, the way hands touch in a line of people passing buckets.

And behind them, where they'd been—

The green stayed.

---

A road.

A green road. Through the grey. Wide as a lane. With real grass on it, and real dust, and real dandelions coming up at the edges in the summer sun. Curving away east behind them across the grey fields, back to the gold ring round Ashbourne. Like a lantern line. Like a seam. Like a thread of colour stitched across a page.

And every mile, somebody stopped.

Seren saw it from the rise. A woman at the back of the line. An old man. A boy. They'd stop, at the side of the green road, and set their lantern down on a stone or a stump or a fencepost. Lit. Given. And step back. And look at it. And walk on, without it, into the ring of the next person's light.

A lantern every mile.

So that the road would stay.

---

"*It's the Keep*," said Rhosyn. Beside her. Very softly. Looking back. "*Isn't it? The eleven hundred. Your boy's.*"

"*Yes*," said Seren.

"*It's every lantern in every window*," said Rosie. "*Every one anybody ever left on for somebody coming home.*"

Seren didn't trust herself to answer.

She turned round. And held her hands out. And Rosie took one, and Wren the other.

And they walked on. West. Into the grey. Toward the edge of the world.

---

It took nineteen days.

---

Seren didn't remember most of them. Afterward. They ran together. Walking. Always walking. The grey on either side, a picture of fields, a picture of farms, a picture of villages where people stood still in their doorways and their gardens, as they'd stood since the grey came, not dead, not alive, kept. The green road behind. The lanterns, one every mile, going back east in a line of gold as far as you could see on a clear night.

There were no clear nights. Grey country doesn't have stars.

But the lanterns did.

---

She remembered some things.

She remembered the first village they came to, on the fourth day, where a woman stood in a garden in the grey with a washing basket on her hip and a shirt held up to the line, and had stood there since the spring. And the line of lanterns going past her, and the green road going past her gate. And Hollis Penn stopping. And setting his lantern down on her garden wall. And the ring of it going out—two yards, three—and reaching the woman with the basket.

And the woman blinking.

And pegging the shirt on the line.

And turning round, and looking at four hundred people with lanterns going past her gate, and saying, very clearly, "*Well, I never.*"

---

She remembered the King.

Walking. Every day. All day. Behind her and Rosie. Never tiring. Gold. The green road going gold under his feet in a trail, a long gold stripe down the middle of the green. And then, around the tenth day—

Not going gold.

She noticed it at a ford. The King stepped out of the water onto the bank and she looked down out of habit, at the grass under his foot, to see it turn.

It didn't.

She looked at him. He was looking at it too.

"*Since the Heart*," said the King. Quietly. "*The white stone. It didn't go. And now this.*" He held up his hand. The one with the petal in it. White. Pink at the edges. Not gold. Still. After all this time. "*It's not coming out of me any more, Seren. The gold. It's not spreading.*"

"*Why*?"

"*I don't know*," said Oszric. He looked at the petal. "*I think I've stopped wanting to keep things.*"

---

She remembered the ninth night. Wren crying in her sleep. And Lex holding her. And Lark, between them, waking up, and putting her hand on her mam's face, the way she'd put it on the Glass Queen's, and saying *Mam* very firmly until Wren woke and stopped.

She remembered the fourteenth day. When they passed the place where Elowen's first stake had been, in the winter, with a red rag on it. *Westerling*. *Nine miles in*. And Nan Ashby's croft, on the hill above the village, with a gold man in the field—Col Ashby, Nan's boy, gilded in the spring when he'd stood between the King's steward and his mother—standing in his barley with his hand up. And Nan going up the hill to him. On her own. With the HERE lantern. And sitting down on the wall of the field beside her gold son. And not coming back down.

They left her there. She'd catch them up, she said. After.

---

She remembered the seventeenth day, when they began to feel the draught.

---

It was very faint at first. A breath on the back of her neck. Going west. As if someone had opened a window somewhere a long way ahead.

By the eighteenth day it was a wind. Steady. Cool. Going past them, from behind, from the east, from the whole of the world, west. Pulling at their clothes. At their hair. At the lantern flames, which leaned, all four hundred of them, in one direction, like grass leaning in a current.

Leaning toward the edge.

"*It's the room*," said Seraphine. Walking. With one hand on her belly and one on the side of the door-cart. "*Going out. That's what it feels like. The world's room. Draining.*" She was very white. "*We're nearly there.*"

---

On the nineteenth day, at noon, Seren came over a long low rise in the grey and stopped.

---

The road ended.

Not their road. The world's road. The old road. The one that had run west from the Vale for a thousand years, through Westerling and Hobb's Cross and the last farms and the last fields, to the place where every map in Caldor stopped and wrote, in old brown ink, *Here the world ends*.

It ended.

In front of her. Fifty yards off. Not in a cliff. Not in a wall. Not in anything.

In a seam.

---

She could see it. Just. If she didn't look straight at it. A line in the air. Running north and south, as far as she could see in both directions. Faint. Like a crease in a page. Like the place where the Veil had hung, for nine hundred years, a curtain of grey light at the edge of the world.

The Veil was gone.

The crease was still there.

And through it—past it—beyond it—

Nothing.

Not grey. Grey was something. Grey was the colour of a world that had gone still. This was the colour of a world that had never been. Flat. Endless. A white road running away across it toward towers on the horizon that she could just make out, glass and iron and gold and bone, the Court of the Crowned. Empty now.

And the wind going into it. Past her. Past all of them. Out of the world, into the nothing, through the seam, like water going down a drain.

---

The King came up beside her.

He stood there. On the rise. Gold. Looking at the seam. At the white road. At the towers.

"*I walked out of there*," said Oszric. "*In the winter. Down that road. With nine thousand people behind me.*" His voice was very quiet. "*I didn't look back.*"

Rosie came up on Seren's other side. With the packet of glass dust held against her chest.

She looked at it too.

Nobody said anything.

---

Behind them, the oxen came up the rise. Slowly. Tom Pruett's cart. The door, on its frame, lashed upright, swinging. Out. And back.

Lex walked beside it. He stopped at the top of the rise. And looked down at the seam. At the end of the road.

He didn't say anything either.

Then he walked down the slope. Fifty yards. To the end of the road. To the last cobble of the world's road, cracked and old and mossed, with a picture of grass growing round it.

And stood there. With the seam a yard in front of him. With the wind going past him into the nothing.

He looked down at the ground under his feet.

"*Here*," said Lex Vey.

---

And round his neck, on its cord, under his shirt—

Malachi's bell rang.

On its own. Once. Small. Clear.

The way a bell on a door rings when somebody is coming in out of the cold.
