# Ilda

Pim counted things.

He'd always counted things. When he was small, before he could talk, before the Keep, before anybody had looked at him long enough to notice he was there, he'd counted. Steps. Stones. Cracks in a ceiling. Breaths. Counting was quiet. Counting didn't take up any room. You could count in your head all day and nobody would ever know.

He counted lanterns now. Five hundred and fifty-six. Round the pool in a ring. In the long green grass. He walked round them every morning before the others were up, the way Antho had walked round the eleven hundred at the Keep, and touched each one. Not to light it. They didn't need lighting. Just to say *I see you*.

He counted the lights in the water. Three. Down in the middle of the pool. Under the surface. Where Mal kept them. The bright steady one, Malachi. The gold one, the old king, Aurelian. And the small fierce one that flickered like a candle in a draught, which was Kell, who had held on.

He counted the dead.

That was harder. They moved. Grey columns of dust standing on the grass in the morning mist, all round the rim, like a crowd at a fair waiting for something to start. Swaying. Drifting. Bending to look at things. A dozen came every day. Out of the west. Down out of the grey sky. Settling round the pool like leaves. And every day, a dozen went in. Into the water. Into the dark. And came apart, gently, and were gone, and Mal's voice under the water went a little deeper. A little warmer.

Pim stopped counting the dead after the first week. There were too many. It wasn't fair to them, to be a number.

He counted the days instead.

It had been sixty-one days since he'd brought Ilda to the pool.

---

She was in the doorway.

She was always in the doorway.

The Coldwater house stood on the north side of the pool, thirty yards up the slope. Hask had built it. Hask and Kit and the Coldwater crew, the autumn after the rim. Stone walls. A slate roof. A fat chimney. One big room with a long table and a hearth and seven bunks along the back wall and Kell's stick on two pegs over the door. And in the window, on the sill, a lantern. Plain tin. With a word scratched round the base in the lad's hand.

*HOME*.

Ilda stood in the door under it.

Eight years old. Small. Thin. Barefoot. In a kitchen girl's smock with a burn-mark on the hem. Gold. All of her. Hair, skin, eyes, smock, the soles of her bare feet. Smooth and bright and cold, as if somebody had dipped her in it. As if she were something on a mantelpiece.

She'd been gold for nine hundred and twelve years.

She stood in the doorway with one gold hand on the frame and looked down the slope at the pool.

And at the edge of the pool, on the grass, between the lanterns, a column of grey dust stood and looked back at her.

It didn't drift. The others drifted. This one didn't. It stood on the rim. Bent a little. At the top. Toward the house. The way a woman bends over a cot. The way a woman bends to look in an oven to see if the bread's done.

It had been standing there for sixty-one days.

---

"*Morning*," said Pim.

He sat down on the step at Ilda's feet. With his knees drawn up. The way he always did. And didn't say anything else for a while.

That was the thing about Ilda. You didn't have to. She didn't mind. She'd been standing on a road for nine hundred years with eight thousand nine hundred and ninety-nine other people and none of them had said anything to her at all. A boy on a step who didn't talk was company.

"*Morning*," said Ilda.

Pim looked up.

She'd never said it before. Not in sixty-one days. *Mam*, she said. And *Pim*, sometimes. And *no*, once, when Kit tried to carry her down to the water. And *bread*, a lot. But never *morning*.

"*The lady says it*," said Ilda. Very slowly. Her voice was small and rusty. Like a gate that hadn't been opened. "*The crutch lady. Every day. To the water.*"

Ivy. Ivy said good morning to Mal every day. Leaning on her crutch on the rim. *Morning, Mal. Sleep well?* As if the pool were a lodger.

"*Yes*," said Pim.

"*Is it morning*?" said Ilda.

Pim looked at the sky. Pale. Pink in the east. Mist on the grass. The lanterns glowing in their ring.

"*Yes*," he said.

Ilda looked at it too. For a long time. With her gold eyes.

"*It was always the same*," she said. "*On the road. It was never morning.*"

---

Pim had tried everything.

He'd tried the first week. Every day. Cupped his hands in front of her face, the way he had on the white road, the way that had made her cry. Made room. A little pocket of quiet, soft and dark and still, right in front of her, and let her look into it.

She looked. She cried, sometimes. She took a step.

She always took a step. One. Out of the door. Onto the grass. Toward the pool. Toward her mam.

Then another.

And another. Thirty yards. Down the slope. Through the long grass. With the dew not wetting her feet, because it couldn't. Past the lanterns. To the rim.

And there she stopped.

Every time.

---

The first time, she'd tried to go in.

Pim had been holding her hand. Gold. Cold. Hard. Like holding a doorknob. He'd walked her down to the rim and she'd looked at the grey column of dust bending over her and said *Mam*, and the column had bent lower, and Pim had felt it. The way you feel someone smile in the dark.

And Ilda had stepped off the rim. Into the water.

And the water hadn't let her.

It didn't splash. It didn't part. It went flat under her foot, hard and smooth, like the top of a table. Like ice. Like glass. She stood on it. On the surface of the pool. A gold girl, standing on the water, with her mother's dust a foot away, bending, and the water would not let her down.

She'd stood there. Staring at her feet.

Then she'd started to scream.

---

*[...I can't...]* Mal had said.

Under the water. Under the ground. In the soft deep voice that had been a little boy's voice when Pim first heard it, a whisper on the wind at the Keep, and was bigger now. Much bigger. Since the thorn lady. Since the cousins and the four hundred and the lady with the bronze face. A voice like the sound in a well when you drop a stone and listen.

*[...I can't take her...she's held...]*

Ivy had been on the rim. Leaning on her crutch. White.

"*Held by what*?" said Ivy.

*[...him...]* A slow ripple went out across the water. Sad. *[...he's holding her...like a hand round a bird...I can't take what's held...I'm room...room can't take what isn't let go...]*

"*Then he has to let go*," said Ivy.

Silence.

Then, very softly, under the water, as if he were ashamed of it—

*[...if he lets go of her...he lets go of all of them...it's one hand...]*

---

So Ilda stood in the doorway.

And her mother stood on the rim.

And neither of them moved.

Sixty-one days.

---

"*She made bread*," said Ilda.

Pim waited.

That was another thing about Ilda. If you waited, things came. Slowly. Up out of nine hundred years. Like stones coming up out of a field in the spring frost. One at a time.

"*In the big kitchen*," said Ilda. "*At the palace. The King's palace. Before.*" She frowned. Her gold forehead creased, and it made a tiny sound, like a coin being bent. "*She got up in the dark. Every day. Before everybody. To light the ovens. And I got up with her. Because I was little and I didn't want to be on my own.*"

"*Yes*," said Pim.

"*I sat on the flour sacks*," said Ilda. "*And she made the dough. On the big table. And she sang.*" Her gold eyes were far away. "*She sang very badly. Everybody said. The cook said. The cook said Maudie, you sound like a cat in a well.*"

Pim smiled. He didn't mean to. It just came.

"*Maudie*," he said.

"*That's her name*," said Ilda. She looked down the slope. At the column of grey dust on the rim. Bending. "*Maud. But everybody said Maudie.*" A pause. "*I said Mam.*"

---

Down on the rim, the dust stirred.

It didn't drift. It didn't bend more. It just stirred. All through. Like a field of barley when the wind goes through it. Like someone hearing their name called across a crowded room.

Pim watched it.

He'd known the bread-woman's name for nine hundred years' worth of no-time. Nobody had. Not the King. Not the Glass Queen. Not the cousins. Not anybody who'd walked past her on the floor of the empty hall while she came apart in the grey with her daughter in her arms.

Somebody knew it now.

He took the stub of pencil out of his pocket. And the scrap of paper he always kept there. And wrote it down.

*Maud. Maudie. Bread. Sang like a cat in a well.*

He folded it up. Very small. And put it back in his pocket.

You didn't let a name get lost twice.

---

Hask came out at seven.

Big. Grey-bearded. Rumpled. His right arm in its sling across his chest, gold to the elbow, catching the light. Kell's stick in his left. He stood in the doorway behind Ilda and looked at the morning and breathed.

There was still a catch at the bottom of it. There always would be. The grey-lung had left that much behind when it went. But it was a small catch now. A hitch. Like a step on a stair you know is there.

"*Morning, boy*," said Hask.

"*Morning*," said Pim.

"*Morning*," said Ilda.

Hask stopped breathing.

Just for a second. Then started again. He looked down at the top of Ilda's gold head. For a long time.

"*Well*," said Silas Hask. Very gruffly. "*Morning, then.*"

He went down the step past Pim and stomped off across the grass toward the woodpile, and Pim saw him wipe his face on his good sleeve when he thought nobody was looking.

---

They came in the afternoon.

The way they'd been coming for six weeks.

Out of the west. Along the old Duskhold road. Over the rise and down into the green circle. Walking. Slowly. One at a time. Two. Five. Ten.

Gold.

The kept.

Pim stood up on the step and counted them. He couldn't help it.

Fourteen.

---

They'd started coming in March.

The first one had been an old man. Gold. In a steward's coat with a chain on it. He'd walked down the slope in the evening with his gold face blank and his gold mouth humming, the way they all hummed, the one note, *home*, and walked to the rim, and stood there.

And a column of dust had come to meet him.

Out of the crowd of grey on the grass. Drifting. And then not drifting. Stopping. In front of him. Bending.

The old man had stopped humming.

Ivy had cried for an hour.

---

They'd been walking for four months. Since the Veil fell. Down the white road and out of the grey and into the world. Nine thousand of them. Humming. *Home*. Walking east. Because home was east. Because nine hundred years ago, before the pool and the drinking and the long grey, home had been here. In the world. In Caldor and Vhessa and the old kingdoms before there were kingdoms. Villages that weren't there any more. Houses that had fallen down and been built over and fallen down again. Graves nobody remembered.

Home wasn't there.

So they kept walking.

And some of them—a few at first, and then more, and then more—had turned. Somewhere out in the world. On a road. In a field. Had stopped humming for a moment. And turned their gold heads. And looked east. Further east. As if they'd heard something.

And come here.

Because their dead were here.

---

There were three hundred and eight of them now.

Standing round the pool. In the grass. Among the lanterns. On the rim. Gold. Each one with a column of grey dust in front of them. Bending. Waiting.

Three hundred and eight gold people and three hundred and eight grey ones. Facing each other. A foot apart. Not touching. Not moving. In the long green grass, in the evening light, with the lanterns glowing round them.

Like a dance that had stopped.

Like a dance where the music had stopped in the middle and nobody knew how to start it again.

---

Pim went down to meet the new ones.

He always did. He hadn't meant to. It was just that the first week, when they started coming, nobody else had. Hask didn't know what to say to them. Kit was frightened of them. Ivy couldn't walk that far on the slope without her hip seizing.

So Pim went.

He walked up to the rise in the afternoon light and stood at the top of the road and waited for them to reach him. And when they did, he cupped his hands.

Not to make them cry. Not to make them walk. Just to make a little room. A little quiet. Right in front of each gold face. So that for a second, a heartbeat, the hum would stop. And they'd see him.

And he'd say: "*What's your name*?"

---

Most of them didn't know.

That was all right.

He'd wait. A bit. And if nothing came, he'd say, "*That's all right. Come on.*" And walk them down. To the rim. To the lanterns. And stand with them while they looked at the dust.

And sometimes—not always, but sometimes—one of the grey columns would stir. And drift. Out of the crowd on the grass. And come. And stop in front of them. And bend.

And the gold face would change.

Not much. You had to be looking. But Pim was always looking.

---

The fourteen today were mostly from the palace.

He could tell. They had the look. Livery. Aprons. A footman's buttons. A gardener's leather knee-pads. Two laundry maids, holding hands, gold fingers fused together where they'd been holding hands for nine hundred years.

And a boy.

Small. Six, maybe. Gold. In a shirt too big for him and no shoes. He walked at the back of the fourteen with his gold eyes blank and his mouth humming and his hand out, a little, in front of him, the way you'd hold your hand out to somebody walking beside you.

There wasn't anybody walking beside him.

Pim cupped his hands in front of the boy's face.

Quiet. Soft. Dark. A little room. And the hum stopped.

The gold eyes found him.

"*What's your name*?" said Pim.

The boy looked at him. For a long time.

"*Jory*," said the boy.

Pim took the pencil out. Wrote it down.

"*Hello, Jory*," he said. "*Come on.*"

---

He walked the boy down to the rim.

Slowly. Through the grass. Through the three hundred and eight. Past the gold steward and his grey wife. Past the two laundry maids, who'd stopped at the edge and stood looking at two columns of dust that had come drifting to meet them side by side. Past a gold soldier on one knee in front of a small grey column no taller than his shoulder.

Down to the rim.

Jory stood there. With his hand out in front of him. Humming, a little. Looking at the dust.

Nothing came.

Pim waited.

A long time. The sun went down a bit further. The lanterns got brighter. Up at the house, Hask lit the HOME lantern in the window, and Kit came out with a bucket.

Nothing came.

"*That's all right*," said Pim.

He sat down on the grass next to the boy. With his knees drawn up.

And waited some more.

---

It came at dusk.

Not out of the crowd on the grass. Out of the west. Down out of the sky. One of the late ones. A thin grey wisp of dust, drifting over the rise and down the slope, the way they all came, the way they'd been coming for four months.

It drifted over the lanterns.

And stopped.

In the air. Over the pool.

And turned.

---

Pim didn't breathe.

The wisp came down. Slowly. Out of the air. Onto the grass. In front of the boy. And stood there. Thin. Small. Not much taller than Jory. Not much more than a smudge on the evening.

It bent.

Jory's hand was still out in front of him. Gold. The way you'd hold your hand out to somebody walking beside you.

The dust bent over the hand. Over the gold fingers.

And stayed there.

---

"*Brother*," said Jory.

---

Pim wrote it down.

His hand was shaking. The pencil skidded. He wrote it anyway.

*Jory. And his brother. Holding hands.*

Then he put the paper in his pocket and put his face in his knees and stayed there for a while. In the grass. With the lanterns glowing round him and a gold boy and a little grey column of dust a foot away, bending, holding a hand that couldn't hold back.

He'd had a brother.

Not by blood. He'd never had anybody by blood. He'd been left on a step in Wickhollow in a basket with nothing in it but him, and he'd been nobody's for twelve years, and then a man with a flute had come out of the snow at the Keep and looked at him. Really looked. And said *you can stay if you like*.

He knew what it was. To walk with your hand out. For somebody who wasn't there.

---

"*You're crying*," said Ilda.

Pim looked up.

It was dark now. The lanterns glowed. And Ilda was standing beside him on the grass.

She'd come down. On her own. Out of the doorway. Down the slope. Thirty yards. Without his hands. Without the room.

She was looking at him. With her gold eyes.

"*You're crying*," said Ilda again. Wonderingly. As if it were a thing she'd heard of but never seen. "*Why?*"

Pim wiped his face on his sleeve.

"*Because it's sad*," he said.

Ilda thought about that.

"*Is it*?" she said.

---

She sat down next to him.

It took her a long time. Gold doesn't bend easily. Her knees made the bent-coin sound, both of them, and her hips, and her back. But she did it. She sat down on the grass beside him with her gold knees drawn up, the way his were. Copying.

They sat there together. In the dark. A boy and a gold girl. With the dead all round them and the pool shining.

"*I don't feel sad*," said Ilda after a while. "*I don't feel anything. I haven't felt anything for a long time.*" She looked at her gold hands on her gold knees. "*I think I'm supposed to.*"

"*Yes*," said Pim.

"*Mam would know*," said Ilda.

She looked down the rim. Twenty yards. To where the column of grey dust stood among the lanterns. Bending toward her. The way it always bent.

"*She always knew what I was supposed to feel*," said Ilda. "*When I burned my hand. When the cook shouted. When the King gave me a sweet.*" Her gold voice was very small. "*She'd say, Ilda, you're cross, or Ilda, you're tired, or Ilda, that's sad, love, it's all right to be sad. And then I'd know.*"

Pim didn't say anything.

"*I want to ask her*," said Ilda. "*What this is. All of it.*" She looked at the dust. "*But I can't get to her.*"

---

They made bread the next day.

It was Ivy's idea. Ivy had a lot of ideas, most of them involving her crutch and the back of somebody's knees, but some of them were good ones.

"*She keeps saying bread*," said Ivy. At breakfast. At the long table in the Coldwater house. With her porridge going cold. "*Bread bread bread. Every day. She remembers her mam making bread.*" She jabbed her spoon at Pim. "*So let's make bread.*"

"*Bread won't rise*," said Kit. Through a mouthful. "*The letters say. Out west. Since the grey came. Nobody's had a loaf rise since Midwinter.*"

"*Out west*," said Ivy. "*This isn't west. This is here.*" She banged the table. "*This is the realest bit of ground in the world. Mal's under it. The smith said. Mal makes room. Room for a baby, a bud, a loaf. He said.*" She glared at Kit. "*It'll rise.*"

It did.

---

Pim had never made bread.

Neither had Kit, or Hask, or Ivy, it turned out, though Ivy claimed she had and was proved a liar inside ten minutes when she put a whole cup of salt in.

They started again.

Hask had flour. A sack. From the carter who came out from Fenmoor every fortnight. And there was yeast, of a kind, in a jar at the back of the shelf that one of the Coldwater crew's wives had left behind, which smelled like feet. And water from the spring. And a table.

And Ilda.

---

She stood at the end of the long table and looked at it.

At the flour in its heap. At the well in the middle. At the water in the jug and the yeast in its cup going frothy and grey.

Pim had brought her in. Not with his hands. Just with words. *We're making bread. Do you want to come?* And she'd looked at him for a long time, and then she'd turned round in the doorway, out of the evening light, into the house, for the first time in sixty-two days.

She stood at the table.

"*That's wrong*," said Ilda.

Everybody stopped.

"*The water*," said Ilda. "*It's too cold. You'll kill it.*" She pointed a gold finger at the yeast. "*It has to be warm. Like a bath. Like your hand. Mam put her elbow in. To tell.*"

Hask, who'd poured the water from the spring, looked at the jug.

Then he took it, without a word, and put it by the fire.

---

She talked them through it.

All of it. Slowly. In her small rusty voice. Up out of nine hundred years. One thing at a time. Like stones coming up in a field.

Warm the water. Feed the yeast. A bit of honey. Wait till it's frothy. Make a well. Pour it in. Not all at once. Mix with your fingers. Like this. No, like *this*.

She couldn't do it herself. She tried. She put her gold hand into the flour, and the flour went gold where she touched it. A gold handprint. A little heap of gold dust in the heap of white. She took her hand out and looked at it. And at the gold in the flour.

Nobody said anything.

"*You do it*," said Ilda to Pim. Quietly. "*I'll tell you.*"

---

So Pim made bread.

With Ilda at his elbow. Telling him. *More flour. Less. Push it away with the heel of your hand. Fold it back. Turn it. Push. Fold. Turn. No, don't tear it, you're tearing it, Mam would hit you with the spoon.*

His arms ached. His back ached. The dough stuck to his fingers and the table and his sleeve and somehow his ear. Kit laughed at him. Ivy laughed at Kit. Hask sat by the fire with his gold arm in his lap and watched and didn't laugh but his beard moved.

And Ilda talked.

Not just about bread. Other things came up with it. In the folding and the turning. The big kitchen. The great black ovens that were taller than a man. The cook, who was called Agnes and had a moustache. The pot-boy, Tam, who could whistle through his fingers. The cat that lived under the flour bins and was called Duchess and had kittens every spring in the King's best linen cupboard and was never once caught.

"*Mam fed her*," said Ilda. "*Duchess. Scraps. Under the table. The cook didn't know.*" A gold smile. Small. Cracking. "*The cook knew.*"

---

They left it to rise.

In a bowl. By the fire. Under a damp cloth. Ilda made them put a cloth over it. *It doesn't like to be looked at. Mam said. Bread's shy.*

And they sat round the table, the five of them, and waited.

An hour.

Nobody looked.

Then Ivy couldn't stand it. She leaned over on her crutch and lifted the corner of the cloth.

And went very still.

---

It had risen.

It had come up over the edge of the bowl. Pale and round and soft and smooth, like a sleeping cat. Like a cloud. Pressing up against the cloth. Alive.

Ilda looked at it.

For a long time. Without moving. With her gold hands at her sides.

Then she put out one gold finger and pressed it, very gently, into the side of the dough. The way you'd test a cheek for fever.

The dough sighed. A little. And slowly—very slowly—the dent came back. Filled in. Smoothed over.

Where her finger had been, there was a tiny gold fleck. Like a star.

"*It's ready*," said Ilda.

---

They baked it.

It came out of Hask's oven an hour later, brown and cracked and lopsided, with a gold fleck on its side like a star. It was the worst loaf any of them had ever seen. It was the best thing any of them had ever smelled.

The smell went everywhere.

Out of the house. Out through the door. Down the slope. Over the grass. Through the lanterns. Round the pool.

Bread. Hot bread. On a spring evening. In a green field.

---

Pim felt it before he saw it.

A stir. All round the pool. All through the dead.

He went to the door.

Three hundred and something columns of grey dust, standing on the grass among the lanterns, facing three hundred and something gold people—and all of them, every one, had turned. Toward the house. Toward the smell.

Bending.

The way you turn when someone opens an oven door in another room. The way you turn when you smell your mother's kitchen in a strange street.

And at the rim—

At the rim, the bread-woman's dust wasn't bending any more.

It was moving.

---

It came up the slope.

Slowly. Drifting. Out of the lanterns. Through the long grass. Thirty yards. Toward the house. Toward the door. Toward the smell of bread. It hadn't left the rim in sixty-two days, and it came up the slope now like a woman who's heard the oven door and knows by the sound that somebody's opened it too soon.

It came to the step.

Pim moved out of the way.

It came to the door.

And stopped. On the threshold. Under the HOME lantern. Swaying. Grey. Looking in.

At the long table. At the loaf on it, brown and cracked and lopsided, with a gold star on its side.

At the gold girl standing beside it.

---

"*Mam*," said Ilda.

---

The dust came in.

It came across the floor. Through the warm room. Past the fire. Past Hask, who'd stood up and was holding the back of his chair with his good hand. Past Kit. Past Ivy.

It came to the table.

It stood over the loaf. And bent. And Pim felt it—they all felt it—the way you feel a smile in the dark. Rueful. Fond. *Oh, love. Look at that. You've let it go lopsided.*

Then it turned to Ilda.

And bent over her. All of it. All the way down. The way a woman bends over a cot. Round her. Close. Grey dust all round a small gold girl, not touching, not able to touch, a quarter of an inch away. Like arms. Like arms that couldn't close.

Ilda stood very still in the middle of it.

"*Mam*," she said. "*Mam, I made bread. I told them. I remembered. I told them about the water and the elbow and the cloth.*"

The dust swayed.

"*I can't feel anything*," said Ilda. Her gold voice cracked. Right down the middle. "*Mam, I can't feel anything. What am I supposed to feel? Tell me what I'm supposed to feel.*"

---

The dust bent closer.

And Pim heard it.

Not with his ears. Not words. He'd never heard the dead speak in words. Antho had. Antho said they said their names. Pim had only ever felt them. The way you feel weather.

But he heard this.

Because it was in the room. Because the room was small and warm and full of the smell of bread. Because it came from nine hundred years down, from a floor in an empty hall, from a woman coming apart in the grey with her daughter in her arms, who had held on to one thing all that time—

Not to her own name. She'd let that go. Not to her face. Not to her hands.

One thing.

*Ilda, love. You're happy. That's happy. It's all right to be happy.*

---

Ilda's gold face crumpled.

All of it. At once. Like foil. With a sound like a hundred coins bending.

And she cried.

Not the way she'd cried on the white road, for the first time in nine hundred years, out of shock, out of a little room in front of her face. Not the way she'd screamed on the surface of the pool, when the water wouldn't take her.

She cried the way an eight-year-old girl cries when her mother's come home late and she's been waiting up. Big gulping sobs. Hiccups. Her gold hands over her gold face. Gold tears—real ones, wet, gold as honey—running down between her gold fingers and dripping on the table and the floor and the loaf.

And the dust held her.

As near as it could.

A quarter of an inch away.

---

Nobody moved.

Hask stood holding the back of his chair, with his beard working and his eyes shut. Kit stood by the hearth with his hands over his mouth. Ivy had sat down on the floor, crutch and all, and was crying so hard she'd got the hiccups too.

Pim stood in the doorway.

He didn't count anything. There wasn't anything to count.

He just stood there and watched a mother and a daughter in a warm room with a lopsided loaf, a quarter of an inch apart, and felt something in his chest that had been shut since before he could remember come open a little bit further.

---

The dust didn't go back to the rim.

It stayed. In the Coldwater house. That night and the next and the next. By the hearth. By the table. By Ilda. Wherever Ilda was.

Ilda didn't stand in the doorway any more.

She sat by the fire. With the dust beside her. And talked. To it. To Pim. To anybody. Things came up. Faster now. The kitchen. The palace. The garden, where there'd been a fountain with fish in it, orange ones, and she'd been told never to put her hand in and always did. The day the King's brother came to supper and spilled wine on the Queen-to-be and everybody pretended not to see. The pool. The night of the pool. Everybody going quiet. Mam holding her hand. The light.

And after.

"*It was grey*," said Ilda. "*After. Everything. And Mam got thinner. And thinner. Not thin like hungry. Thin like a drawing. Like somebody rubbing you out.*" She looked at the dust. "*And she held me. On the floor. In the hall. For days. And I could feel her going. In her arms. Like sand.*"

The dust swayed.

"*And then he came*," said Ilda. "*The King. Walking. Down the hall. He was crying. He was crying all the time then. He looked at everybody. And everybody he looked at went—*" She held up a gold hand. "*He looked at me. And I went. And Mam didn't.*"

Silence.

"*He kept me*," said Ilda. "*Because he couldn't stand it. He told me. On the road. Once. Before the grey got in his mouth. He said, I couldn't stand to watch you go, little one. I'm sorry. I couldn't stand it.*"

Pim wrote it down.

---

*[...he's practising...]*

Mal said it. On the fourth night. Under the water.

Ivy was on the rim. With her lantern. Saying goodnight. *Night, Mal. Sleep well.* And the pool had rippled. Slow. Thoughtful. And said it.

*[...he's practising...]*

Ivy went still.

"*Who is*?" she said.

*[...the big gold one...]* A long ripple. Out from the middle. Through the three lights. To the rim. *[...he's been to the forge...he's been to see the old man...the one with the gold fingers...]* A pause. *[...he came out holding something white...]*

"*And*?" said Ivy.

*[...and now he's practising...]* The water went very still. *[...letting go...a little...every night...and then holding on again...I can feel it...through all of them...]*

---

Pim felt it himself, the next night.

He was in his bunk. In the dark. Not asleep. Listening to Hask breathe and Kit snore and Ivy mutter to herself and the fire tick in the hearth.

And then—

Everything shifted.

Not the house. Not the ground. Something under that. Something that ran through all of them. Through every gold person on the grass round the pool. Through the gold fingers of the laundry maids and the gold knee of the soldier and the gold hand of the boy Jory held out to his brother. Through Hask's arm, in the bunk across the room. Through Ilda, on her pallet by the fire, with the dust beside her.

A loosening.

Like a fist opening. A little. One finger.

---

Ilda cried out.

Pim was out of his bunk before he knew it. Across the room. On his knees by her pallet.

She was sitting up. With her gold hand held out in front of her. Shaking.

"*It's cold*," she said. "*Pim, it's cold, it's—*"

Her little finger.

The smallest one. On her left hand. It had gone pink.

Not gold. Pink. Skin. A child's skin. With a nail on the end of it, small and dirty, and a crease at the knuckle, and the very faint white line of an old burn across the pad where she'd touched a spit-iron nine hundred and twelve years ago and cried and been given a sweet.

It was shaking. It was cold. It was alive.

And all round it, at the edge, where the pink met the gold—

The gold was coming apart.

Not peeling. Not flaking. Fraying. At the edges. Like old cloth. Like the dust on the grass. Like a drawing being rubbed out. Grey. Fine. Drifting.

---

"*Pim*," said Ilda. Very high. Very frightened. "*Pim, I'm going. Like Mam. I'm going like sand.*"

---

Outside, across the grass, all round the pool, in the dark among the lanterns—

Pim heard it.

The hum.

Three hundred and something gold people. Who had stopped humming, one by one, as their dead came to meet them. Who had stood in the long grass for weeks, facing their dust, quiet.

All humming again. At once. The one note. Rising. Shrill. Frightened.

*Home. Home. Home.*

---

And then it stopped.

All at once.

The loosening. The fist closed again. The finger. Pim felt it go—a tightening, everywhere, all through the gold, like a hand gripping a rope it's nearly dropped.

Ilda's little finger went gold again.

Just like that. Pink, and then gold. Smooth and bright and cold. The fraying at the edges stopped. The grey dust that had drifted up off it settled on the blanket. A pinch. Less than a pinch.

Outside, the hum faded.

Ilda sat on her pallet with her gold hand held out in front of her and stared at it.

---

"*He's sorry*," said Ilda.

Pim looked at her.

"*I felt it*," said Ilda. Shakily. "*When he let go. Just the finger. I felt him. He's somewhere a long way off and he's sitting in the dark holding a petal and he's sorry.*" She swallowed. "*He tried to let go of just me. Just a little. And it wasn't just me.*"

Hask was sitting up in his bunk. Holding his gold arm against his chest with his good hand. Grey-faced. Breathing hard.

"*Felt it*," said Hask. "*In the arm. Went cold. Right to the shoulder.*" He looked at Pim across the dark room. "*That's all of us, then. That's all nine thousand. All at once. If he does it.*"

"*Yes*," said Pim.

"*And the old ones*," said Hask. "*The ones that were all-over gold. Nine hundred years.*" He looked at the fire. At the dust beside it. At Ilda. "*They'll go like that. Like sand. Won't they.*"

Ilda didn't say anything.

The dust bent over her.

---

Pim didn't sleep.

He sat on the step outside the door, under the HOME lantern, in the dark, with his knees drawn up, and looked down at the pool.

At the lanterns. Five hundred and fifty-six. At the three lights in the water. At three hundred and some gold people standing in the grass, quiet again, and three hundred and some grey ones in front of them, bending. At the dance where the music had stopped.

He thought about the King.

Sitting in the dark somewhere a long way off. Holding a petal. Practising.

Letting go of one finger. And feeling nine thousand people start to come apart. And grabbing hold again. Because he couldn't stand it. Because he'd never been able to stand it. Because the first time, nine hundred years ago, in an empty hall, he'd looked at a little girl coming apart in her mother's arms and he'd held on, and he'd been holding on ever since.

He'd have to let go all at once.

Pim knew it. Sitting on the step. The way he knew things sometimes. Not in words. You couldn't do it a finger at a time. You couldn't do it gently. It wasn't that kind of hold.

When the King let go, he'd have to let go of everything. Everyone. At once.

And nine thousand people would come apart. All at once. All over the world. On roads and in fields and in the long grass round a pool. Frightened. Cold. Going like sand.

And there'd be nobody holding their hands.

---

Pim sat on the step until the sky went pale in the east.

Then he took his pencil out of his pocket. And his scraps of paper. All of them. Sixty-five days of them. Folded very small.

*Maud. Maudie. Bread. Sang like a cat in a well.*

*Jory. And his brother. Holding hands.*

*Ellen and Bet, laundry, Ellen's mam and Bet's gran.*

*Corporal Fitch. His girl. She was four.*

*Old Hobb the steward. His wife Joan. Fifty years.*

Three hundred and eight names. More. Some of them just *don't know*. Some of them just *a man in a red coat*. But all of them written down. All of them somebody.

He found a clean scrap. And wrote on it. Slowly. In his small careful hand. The longest note of his life.

---

*To Lex. At the forge.*

*The King is practising. Letting go. We felt it. It hurts them. It frightens them. They go like sand.*

*He has to do it all at once. He can't do it a bit at a time. Tell him.*

*When he does it, they shouldn't be on their own. Nobody should come apart on their own. Not again.*

*We'll hold their hands. Here. At the pool. As many as we can get. Ivy and Hask and Kit and me. And Ilda. And all the dead. Tell him to send them here. Tell him to send them all.*

*Tell him we're ready.*

*Tell him not to be sorry. Tell him it's all right to let go.*

*P.*

---

He folded it.

Then he unfolded it again, and looked at it for a long time. And added one more line. At the bottom. Very small.

*Tell him her name was Maudie.*

Then he tied it to the leg of Antho's raven, who'd been sitting on the Coldwater chimney for three days eating Hask's bacon rind and waiting to be useful, and threw her up into the pink morning sky.

And watched her go.

West.

Toward the forge. Toward the King. Toward the door.

Toward the grey, coming east to meet her.
