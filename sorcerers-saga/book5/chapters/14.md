# Back Down the White Road

Pim didn't like talking.

It wasn't that he couldn't. He could now. He'd been able to for four years, since the winter at the Keep when Antho played the flute and something in Pim's chest that had been shut since before he could remember came open a crack and a word came out. *Come back*. That was the first one. He'd said it to Antho, on the mountain, in the snow.

He'd said a lot of words since. Hundreds. Thousands, maybe.

He still didn't like it.

Words were loud. They went out of you and into the air and you couldn't get them back. They took up room. And Pim had spent twelve years learning how to take up no room at all.

So he didn't talk much.

He left notes instead.

---

He left one on the rim of the pool. Under a stone. For Antho. *Gone to fetch Ilda. And the thorn lady. She asked for help.* He thought about it for a long time and then added *Back when I can*. And then, because he knew Antho, and he knew Lex, *Don't follow. You need to do the door.*

He signed it. *P.*

Then he picked up his pack and his stick and three of Ivy's Hush-cups wrapped in wool and walked out of the green circle in the dark, west, on foot.

---

He got eleven miles.

Then a cart came up behind him on the road. A Coldwater cart. Big. With two horses. And on the box, a big grey-bearded man with a gold arm in a sling and a dead woman's staff across his knees. And beside him a thin solemn boy of nineteen with coal-dust in the creases of his knuckles.

The cart stopped.

Pim stopped.

Hask looked down at him from the box. For a long time. Breathing. The catch at the bottom of it.

"*Get in*," said Silas Hask.

Pim didn't move.

"*You left a note*," said Hask. "*Under a stone. On the rim.*" He shifted on the box. "*Ivy found it. Ivy came and found me. Ivy said, he's gone west on his own, and I said, he's fifteen, and Ivy hit me with her crutch.*"

Kit snorted.

"*So get in*," said Hask.

"*The note said don't follow*," said Pim.

"*The note said the smith shouldn't follow*," said Hask. "*The smith's got a door to make.*" He looked west. Down the road. Into the dark. "*I haven't got anything to make. I've got a pick I can't swing and a pool I can't help and a dead woman's stick.*" He looked back at Pim. "*Get in the cart, boy.*"

Pim got in the cart.

---

It took twenty-two days to get to the edge.

Pim didn't mind. He liked the cart. He liked the horses, which were big and slow and patient and smelled of hay. He liked sitting in the back on the sacks with his knees drawn up, watching the world go by. Snow. Fields. Villages. Woods.

He liked that nobody made him talk.

Hask didn't talk much either. He drove. With his good hand. His gold one lay in its sling across his chest, smooth and bright and cold, from the fingertips to the elbow, with the black seam down the forearm where an old iron scar had gone gold too. Sometimes Pim saw him look at it. Not often. A glance. Then away. The way you'd look at a dog that had bitten someone.

Kit talked. Kit talked to the horses, and to the road, and to people in the villages, and to Pim, sometimes, at night by the fire, about Coldwater and the mines and his mother and a girl called Nell who'd whistled two notes at a window and died in the Gloam years. Hask's sister. Pim knew about Nell. Everyone at the pool knew about Nell.

Kit didn't seem to mind that Pim didn't answer.

He just talked. And Pim listened. And that seemed to be enough for both of them.

---

They saw the lanterns on the eighteenth day.

West of Hobb's Cross. In the snow. A long line of them. Small. Gold. Running away across the fields north and south, over the hills, out of sight. Every few hundred yards. Every fifty. Every twenty. Each one in its own little circle of white.

Pim made Hask stop the cart.

He got down. He walked to the nearest one. Iron. Old. On the ground by a white-topped stake. He crouched beside it. Didn't touch it.

He felt the room round it.

A yard. White snow. Real wind. Moving. And outside the yard, the grey. Flat. Still. Drinking. Pim could feel it drinking. Pulling. West. The way you feel a draught under a door.

He could feel his own room pulling too.

The little room. Inside him. The one he'd made out of being hurt, in the years before the Keep, before anyone was kind. A hollow in his chest where nothing could get in. Moths couldn't drink from it. Gold couldn't set in front of it.

The grey wanted it.

He felt it wanting it. Soft. Patient. Endless. Like a cold hand on the back of his neck.

He didn't let it.

He'd had twelve years of practice at not letting things in.

---

"*Smith's work*," said Hask. From the box. Looking at the line of lights. "*And his mother's.*"

"*Given*," said Pim.

Hask looked at him.

"*They're given*," said Pim. It was a long speech for him. "*That's why.*"

Hask looked at the lanterns for a while.

Then he grunted. And clicked his tongue at the horses. And Pim got back in the cart.

---

They went into the grey on the nineteenth day.

---

It was quiet.

Pim had known it would be quiet. He'd been there before. In the summer. In the Court. With the brothers. But that had been on the other side of the Veil. In the grey country itself. Where it was supposed to be quiet.

This was the world.

This was fields and hedges and farms, and a road, and snow. And it was quiet the way the Court was quiet. The horses' hooves made no sound. The cart wheels made no sound. The snow lay on the hedges like snow in a painting, not falling, not settling, not anything, with the wind going past it.

Kit stopped talking.

Pim saw him stop. Halfway through a word. About a pie. He just stopped, with his mouth open, and looked round. At the silent hedges. At the snow. And shut his mouth. And didn't open it again.

Hask didn't say anything either.

He drove. With his good hand. With his jaw set. And every so often, Pim saw him breathe in—deep, slow, the grey-lung catch at the bottom of it—as if to make sure he still could.

---

They passed the gold ones.

Walking east. Across the fields. Through the hedges. A finger's width at a time. Grooms and cooks and nursemaids and gardeners, gold, in clothes from nine hundred years ago, all facing east, all humming.

*...home...*

Pim heard it. Even through the silence. He'd heard it on the white road in the summer. Thousands of gold throats. One note. Worn smooth.

He looked at every one they passed.

Every face. Every gold face. Looking for a round one. A snub nose. Hair scraped back under a cap. Seven, eight. A grey dress too big for her.

A wooden spoon.

---

They found her on the twenty-first day.

---

Two miles from the edge. On the old road. Past Westerling. Past the silent mill and the empty farms and the stakes crowding closer and closer together. Walking.

Not a finger's width. A step. A real step. Slow. Stiff. Scraping. Gold. In a grey dress too big for her. With a wooden spoon in her hand.

East.

Pim saw her from the cart.

He didn't say anything. He didn't need to. He got down while the cart was still moving. Hask swore and hauled on the reins. Pim didn't hear him. He was running.

Down the road. In the silent snow. Toward the small gold figure walking toward him.

---

He stopped in front of her.

A yard off. On the road. In the snow.

She didn't stop.

She came on. A step. Slow. Scraping. Her gold eyes on the road ahead. On the east. On the world.

And then on him.

---

He saw them move.

Her eyes. Gold. In her gold face. Moving. Off the road. Up. To him. Standing in front of her in the snow. Small. Thin. Dark-haired. Fifteen.

And stopping.

She knew him. He saw it. The way you see a light come on in a window.

---

He cupped his hands.

In front of her face. The way he had in the summer. On the white road. A little room. A space in the air. Where something could breathe.

He didn't say anything.

He didn't need to.

Her gold eyes filled.

And one tear, gold, ran down her gold cheek. Slowly. And dropped. Off her chin. Onto the snow.

The snow stayed white.

---

"*Mam*," said Ilda.

---

It came out of her like a breath. Very small. Very cracked. A gold whisper from a gold throat that hadn't said anything but *home* for nine hundred years.

Pim's eyes stung.

"*I found her*," he said.

Ilda's gold eyes went very wide.

"*Your mam*," said Pim. "*She's at the pool. In the green circle. Waiting. I asked. She bent down. She said your name.*" He swallowed. It was the most words he'd said in a row in his life. He didn't care. "*She's waiting for you. I told her you're coming.*"

The gold eyes stared at him.

"*I promised*," said Pim. "*Remember? I said I'd come back. I said when it's big. When there's room.*" His voice cracked. "*It's not big yet. But I came anyway. Because you're walking. And it's a long way.*"

---

Hask came up behind him.

Breathing. Leaning on Kell's staff. With his gold arm in its sling. He stopped. Looked at the girl. At the gold tear on her gold cheek.

He didn't say anything for a long time.

"*She's walking east*," he said at last. Gruffly. "*At a mile a day. Give or take.*"

"*Yes*."

"*The pool's five hundred miles*."

"*Yes*."

"*That's a year and a half*," said Hask. "*More. Walking.*"

Pim looked at him.

"*We're going west*," said Hask. "*Into the grey. To the glass palace. For the thorny one.*" He looked at Ilda. "*Then we're coming back. This way. On this road.*" He shifted his weight on the staff. "*She'll be a bit further on by then.*"

"*And then*?" said Pim.

Hask looked at the cart. At the two big slow horses. At the sacks in the back.

"*And then she can ride*," said Silas Hask.

---

Pim stood in the road.

Then he turned back to Ilda. And cupped his hands again. In front of her face.

"*Wait for me*," he said. "*Keep walking. Slowly. We'll come back. In a few days. With a cart.*" He tried to smile. He wasn't good at it. "*You can ride the rest.*"

The gold eyes looked at him.

And Ilda took a step.

East. Past him. On the road. Slow. Scraping.

But as she passed, her eyes stayed on him. All the way. Turning. Following him. As she went by. Until she couldn't turn them any further.

Then they went back to the road.

*Scrape*. *Scrape*.

---

They crossed the edge at noon on the twenty-second day.

There was nothing there. That was the thing Pim couldn't get used to. In the summer, there'd been the Veil. Grey. Hanging from nowhere. With a door in it that two brothers had opened with their blood.

Now there was a gold field with forty sheep and a boy. And a woman on a stool beside a little iron lantern, in a circle of real falling snow, talking. And past the field, the snow just—went grey. Without any line. Without any seam.

And the white road started.

---

Pim stopped the cart there. At the start of the road. And unwrapped the Hush-cups.

Three of them. Horseshoe iron, beaten round, with the Hollow glyph cut in the bottom and seven Hush-runes round the rim and an iron lid that sighed when you lifted it. Ivy had filled them at the pool. Before Pim left. From the dark water. Each one heavy with nothing.

*Room*. A flask of room. Three days, for one person. Ivy had said. Maybe less, now. Mal was so little.

He gave one to Kit.

He held one out to Hask.

Hask shook his head.

"*Don't need it*," he said. "*Never did. Not in the summer. Not much.*" He looked down the white road. "*I drank her light, boy. The moth queen's. Two years ago. There's not much room left in me.*" His mouth twisted. "*The grey doesn't bother pushing.*"

Pim looked at him.

Then he put the third cup back in his pack. For Rhosyn.

He didn't take one himself.

He had his own.

---

The grey country was the same.

The same as the summer. A plain of grey-gold dust, fine as flour, lying in drifts. Flat. Going on to the edge of everything. A white road through it, broad and straight as a ruler, the white of old bone. A low grey sky. No sun. No stars.

And far ahead, on the edge of the sky, towers.

Glass. Iron. Gold. Bone. And one wrapped in brown dry thorns.

---

But it wasn't the same.

Pim felt it as soon as they were on the road. The wind.

Behind them. From the east. From the world. Pouring in. Past the cart. Past the horses. Down the white road. Into the grey. Steady. Soft. Endless. Lifting the grey-gold dust off the drifts in long low streamers and carrying it west, toward the towers, the way smoke goes up a chimney.

And the grey country drinking it.

He could feel it drinking. Even from here. A great dry patient thirst. As wide as the world. Drawing the wind in and in and in, and never being full.

---

They walked the horses.

Hask wouldn't let them trot. *Not on that road. Not in this.* So they walked. Slowly. Along the white road. With the dust going past them on the wind and the towers getting no closer.

The kept were walking the other way.

Fewer, here. Most of them were out in the world now, scattered across the Westmarch. But some were still on the road. Slow ones. Old ones. Gold. Walking east. A finger's width at a time. Humming.

Pim looked at every one.

---

And the dead.

He hadn't expected the dead. He'd thought they'd all gone east. To the pool. On the first wind.

They hadn't. Not all.

Some were still here. In the dust. Lying in it. Grey columns, fine as flour, that didn't stand up and sway like the ones at the rim. That just lay there. In the drifts. Like dust. Like the floor.

Pim could feel them. Faintly. The way he'd felt the bread-woman bend over him in the green circle.

*Too tired*, he thought. *Too tired to stand up. Too tired to go.*

He stopped by one. Knelt. In the dust beside the white road. And cupped his hands. Over it. A little room.

The dust stirred.

Just a little. Lifted. A breath of grey. Like someone turning over in their sleep.

Then lay down again.

"*Later*," said Pim. To it. Very quietly. "*When it's big. I'll come back.*"

He didn't know if it heard.

He said it anyway.

---

They camped by the road.

There wasn't anything to camp with. No wood. No water. No grass for the horses. They'd brought hay and water in barrels. The horses ate and drank and stood with their heads down and their ears back and their eyes rolling, hating it.

Pim lay on the sacks in the back of the cart and looked up at the grey sky.

No stars.

Kit lay beside him. With his Hush-cup on his chest. He'd lifted the lid three times. Each time the room came out of it with a sigh, and Kit breathed it in, and his face changed—went pink, went alive, went *Kit*—for a while. Then the grey pushed it out of him again and he went pale and quiet and still.

"*How do you stand it*?" said Kit. In the dark. Very low. "*You haven't got a cup.*"

Pim thought about it.

"*Practice*," he said.

---

It took two days to reach the towers.

Pim knew it was two days because he counted sleeps. There wasn't any day or night in the grey country. Just grey. But they slept twice, in the cart, by the road. And Hask said *it'll be longer out there. In the world. A lot longer.* And Pim knew that too. He'd been in the Court in the summer. He'd come out to find the world ten times older than he'd left it.

It wasn't ten times now. He could feel that. The wind coming in had changed it. Near the edge, time was nearly ordinary. Deeper in, it got slower. Thicker. Like walking into a pond.

By the towers, he thought, it might still be ten.

He tried not to think about what that meant. For Ilda, walking east. For the brothers. For the door.

---

The glass palace stood at the end of the white road.

Tallest of the towers. Spires on spires. Glittering. Clear. Every surface a window. Every window showing the window behind it, and the window behind that, and the window behind that, until the eye gave up.

Pim had been inside it once. In the summer. With the brothers. When the Glass Queen showed each of them a mirror. Lex as Root, crowned. Antho in his mad years. Hask his sister Nell.

She'd shown Pim one too.

He'd never told anyone what was in it.

---

It was empty now.

He felt that as soon as they came to the great clear doors. Not the people. The *looking*. In the summer, every pane in that palace had been looking at you. You'd felt it. On your skin. Cold. Curious. The Glass Queen in every window, watching.

The windows weren't looking now.

They were just glass.

"*She's not here*," said Pim.

Hask looked at him. "*How do you know*?"

"*Nobody's watching*."

---

Orrin met them in the first hall.

---

He was standing in the middle of it. On the clear floor. Tall. Thin. Bent. A shape of dust and dry grey silk, like a pile of dead leaves that had stood up and decided to be a man. With his long patient face, the colour of old candlelight. With his twig-joints. With his chain of office, grey-gold, round his neck. With his hands folded in front of him.

Waiting.

He'd been waiting, Pim thought, for a long time.

---

Hask stepped in front of Pim.

He did it without thinking. The way he'd done it in the crack in the summer, with gold dust coming down. His big body between Pim and the dust-man. Kell's staff in his good hand. His gold arm in its sling.

"*Stay there*," said Hask.

Orrin bowed.

The old bow. Low. Courteous. One twig-arm folded across his middle.

"*Young sirs*," he said. His voice was a whisper. Like dry leaves blowing across a floor. "*Her Majesty is not at home.*"

---

"*Where is she*?" said Kit.

Orrin straightened. Slowly. His joints creaking. Dust sifting off him onto the clear floor.

"*In the window*," he said.

"*What window*?"

"*All of them*." Orrin looked round. At the hall. At the clear walls showing the clear walls behind them. "*She went into the window on the night the edge came down. Into the stones. In your world.*" He looked back at them. "*She hasn't come out.*"

"*And the thorn lady*?" said Pim.

Orrin looked at him.

He looked at him for a long time. Longer than he'd looked at Hask, or at Kit. His candlelight eyes moving over Pim's face. Slowly. The way you'd read a page.

"*You're the boy*," said Orrin. "*From the road. With the hands. Who made the spit-girl cry.*"

Pim didn't say anything.

"*Her Majesty watched you*," said Orrin. "*Through the glass. In the summer. Kneeling on the road.*" A pause. "*She stood in front of that window for a very long time.*"

---

"*Where's Rhosyn*?" said Hask.

Orrin didn't answer him.

He was still looking at Pim.

"*The hall of the long mirror*," he said at last. "*At the foot of a pillar. On a chain.*" His dry voice dropped. "*Her Majesty told me to keep her.*"

"*Are you going to*?" said Pim.

---

Orrin was quiet.

He stood in the middle of the clear hall, dust and silk and twig, with his hands folded and his chain of office round his neck, and was quiet for so long that Kit shifted his feet and Hask's good hand tightened on the staff.

"*I was a steward*," said Orrin. At last. "*Before. In the King's house. In the world. Nine hundred years ago. I kept his keys and his cellars and his household.*" He looked down at his hands. Twig. Dust. "*Then the boat came. And Her Majesty Maelis fed me light so I would not come apart. And I was hers. Mothwing. For nine hundred years.*" He looked up. "*And then she burned. In your world. In a lamp. And I was nobody's.*"

Nobody said anything.

"*And then His Majesty woke*," said Orrin. "*And I was his again. And I went where he sent me. I went to your world with his gold. I offered old women forever.*" He bowed his head. "*One of them said no. In her kitchen. And gave me tea.*"

"*Old Mag*," said Pim.

"*I did not know her name*," said Orrin. "*She did not tell me.*" He was quiet for a moment. "*She is gone now, I think. I felt something go. A while ago. From the gold. A small thing. An old woman's hand that was gilded, and is not anymore.*"

"*She's finished*," said Pim.

Orrin looked at him.

"*Finished*," he said. As if trying the word. "*Yes. She said that. In her kitchen. She said, I don't want to be kept, dear. I want to be finished.*" He looked down at his own twig hands. At the dust sifting off them. "*I have thought about it a great deal.*"

---

"*Let us take her*," said Pim.

---

He didn't know he was going to say it. It came out of him. Loud. Five words. Into the clear silent hall.

Orrin looked at him.

"*Her Majesty told me to keep her*," he said.

"*I know*."

"*Her Majesty will be very cross*."

"*I know*."

"*And what will I be*," said Orrin, "*if I do not do what Her Majesty tells me?*"

Pim thought about it.

"*Nobody's*," he said. "*Again.*"

Orrin went very still.

"*Maybe that's all right*," said Pim. He didn't know where the words were coming from. He didn't usually have this many. "*Maybe you could be nobody's for a while. And see what it's like.*"

---

The dust-man looked at him for a long, long time.

Then he turned.

And walked away down the hall. Slowly. Creaking. Toward a tall clear arch at the far end. And stopped there. And looked back over his shoulder. At the three of them.

"*This way*," whispered Orrin. "*Mind the floor. It shows you things, if you look down.*"

---

The hall of the long mirror was very long and very cold and very empty.

Clear walls. Clear floor. Clear pillars going up to a clear vaulted roof a hundred feet overhead. And down one side, the whole length of the hall, a mirror. Silver. Old. Taller than the pillars. Showing the hall back to itself. And them.

Pim didn't look at it.

He'd learned that. In the summer.

He looked at the floor instead. At the foot of the pillars. And at the foot of the ninth one—

---

A woman. Lying on the clear floor. Curled up. On her side. With her knees drawn up and her thin arms round them. Like a child asleep.

Grey. All of her. Not grey-green. *Grey*. Like ash. Like a dead branch. In rotten silk. With a crown of thorns crooked on her grey hair. Brown. Dry. Dead.

And round her wrists, a chain. Glass. Fine. Links like icicles. Looped round the foot of the pillar.

She didn't move when they came in.

---

Pim knelt beside her.

He didn't touch her. He looked at her face. Thin. Hollow-cheeked. Her eyes closed. Her mouth a little open. Like Old Mag on the morning she died.

But she was breathing. Pim could see it. Very slow. Very shallow. A breath like a leaf turning over.

And in her crown—in the dead brown thorns—there was a place. Near the front. Where something had been. A bud. A green one. Pim could see the scar of it. A little broken place on the stem, raw, pale, where something had grown, and been spent, and gone.

*She sent it*, he thought. *The rose. To Harrow. By the grave. With the silk.*

*That was the last one*.

---

"*Rhosyn*," he said.

Her eyes opened.

Green.

The only green thing in the grey country. Two green eyes in a grey face, opening slowly, the way a door opens in a house where someone's been very ill.

She looked at him.

"*Oh*," she said. A breath. A whisper. "*Oh. You came.*"

"*You asked*," said Pim.

---

Hask came and stood over them.

He looked at the chain. At the fine glass links round her wrists. Looped round the pillar. He looked at it the way he'd looked at rock, once, in the Coldwater mines, before the Gloam, before the Delves, before the moth. The way a hewer looks at a seam. Slowly. All of it. Every line. Every link.

Then he knelt. Heavily. On one knee. With his breath catching. And put his good hand flat on the clear floor beside the chain. And leaned close.

"*Pick*," he said. To Kit. Without looking round.

Kit held it out.

---

It was a small one. Not his old pick. His old pick had a haft as long as Pim was tall and a head as big as a cat. He couldn't swing it now. Not with one arm. Not with his left.

This one was little. A rock-hammer. A prospector's pick. With a short haft and a point on one end like a bird's beak. The smith had made it for him. In the autumn. At the Harrow forge. *For your left*, Lex had said. *Till you learn.*

Hask took it in his left hand.

He didn't swing it.

He held it close to the chain. An inch off. And moved it. Slowly. Along the links. Not touching. Listening. The way Lex listened to iron with the little hammer. The way Pim listened to rooms.

*Fault-Finder*. That was the skill. DELVEBREAKER. Epic. Hask could find the flaw in anything. Rock. Iron. A Delve core. A Moth Queen. The one place where a thing was weakest. Where it wanted to break.

He moved the pick along the chain.

And stopped.

---

The ninth link from her left wrist.

Pim couldn't see anything different about it. Clear. Fine. Like an icicle. Like all the others.

Hask could.

He looked at it for a long time.

"*She made this in a hurry*," he said. Quietly. "*On the road. The night the edge fell. Angry.*" He touched the point of the pick to the link. Very lightly. "*Glass you make angry has a fault in it. Always. Like rock that cooled too fast.*"

He tapped.

*Tink*.

---

The chain fell apart.

Not shattered. Not exploded. It just—came undone. Link by link. All the way along. From the ninth link out. Like a necklace when the thread breaks. The fine glass rings sliding off each other and off Rhosyn's wrists and off the foot of the pillar and landing on the clear floor with a sound like small bells.

*Chime*. *Chime*. *Chime*.

And then it was a heap of glass rings on the floor. And Rhosyn's wrists were bare. Thin. Grey. With red marks round them.

---

She didn't move.

She lay on the floor and looked at her wrists.

"*Oh*," she said again.

Then she started to cry.

---

Pim had seen people cry. Antho. Ivy. Wren, once, at the Keep, when she thought nobody was looking. Kit, at the river, when it started moving again.

He'd never seen anyone cry like that.

It wasn't loud. It hardly made any sound at all. It was just—*everything*. The whole of her. Shaking. Curled on the floor with her bare wrists against her chest and her face pressed into the clear glass and her thin shoulders heaving. Nine hundred years of something coming out of her all at once.

Pim knelt beside her.

He didn't touch her.

He cupped his hands. Over her. Not in front of her face. Over all of her. A little room. As big as he could make it. A space in the air. Where something could breathe.

He held it there.

And after a long time, she stopped.

---

"*I want to give it back*," said Rhosyn.

They were in the first hall. Orrin had brought them there. With Hask carrying Rhosyn in his good arm, the way you'd carry a child, and Kit carrying the pick and the third Hush-cup, and Pim walking beside them.

She was sitting on the clear floor with her back against a clear pillar. With the Hush-cup in her lap. Kit had lifted the lid for her. She'd breathed the room in, the way Kit did, and her face had changed. Not much. A little colour. A little life. Her green eyes brighter.

She looked at Pim.

"*What I drank*," she said. "*At the pool. Nine hundred years ago.*" Her voice was a thread. "*I want to give it back.*"

Pim looked at her.

"*I knew*," said Rhosyn. "*At the pool. Hes told me. The night before. She came to my room and sat on my bed and said, don't, Rosie. Please don't. It isn't ours. It's the door new things come in by. If we drink it there won't be any new things.*" She looked down at her hands. "*And I listened. I did. I lay awake all night thinking about it.*"

"*And then you drank*," said Pim.

"*And then I drank*," said Rhosyn. "*Because Oz was drinking. And Mae. And Ysy. And Cad. And I didn't want to be the only one left. I didn't want to grow old and die and leave them.*" Her mouth twisted. "*I didn't want to be Hes.*"

She looked at the empty spaces in her crown. The brown dead thorns.

"*Thorns for the gardener*," she said. "*Who listened. And drank.*"

---

Nobody said anything.

Orrin stood a little way off. In the middle of the hall. With his hands folded. Watching.

"*There's a pool*," said Pim.

Rhosyn looked at him.

"*In a green field*," said Pim. "*In the world. It's the Well. The one you drank. It came back. It's little. It's frightened.*" He swallowed. "*And there are thousands of the dead round it. Waiting. And it can't let them in. Because it's too little. Because you've still got it.*"

Rhosyn stared at him.

"*The grey's coming*," said Pim. "*Into the world. Through the edge. Drinking everything. Bread won't rise. Cuts won't close.*" It was the longest speech of his life. He didn't stop. "*And the dead are waiting. And Ilda's walking home to her mam. And the smith's trying to make a door. And the pool's too little for any of it.*"

He looked at her.

"*If you gave it back*," he said, "*it'd be a bit bigger.*"

---

Rhosyn didn't say anything for a long time.

She sat against the pillar with the Hush-cup in her lap and looked at him. At the small dark boy kneeling on the clear floor in front of her. With his hands in his lap.

"*It would kill me*," she said. Quietly. "*Wouldn't it? If I gave it back. I'd stop being—this. I'd start being—*" She stopped.

"*Old*," said Hask. From the side. Gruffly. "*You'd start getting old.*"

"*I'm nine hundred years old*."

"*You'd start getting old from where you are*," said Hask. "*Not from where you were.*" He looked at her. "*Caddoc fell. With the thread. And what he drank went home. And he died.*" A pause. "*But he'd stepped off a thread at the bottom of the world.*"

Rhosyn looked at him.

"*You're not on a thread*," said Hask. "*You're sitting on a floor.*"

---

She looked down at her hands.

Thin. Grey. With red marks round the wrists where the chain had been.

"*Hes poured hers back*," she said. "*At the pool. And she didn't die. She just—wasn't deathless anymore. She was a girl. Seventeen. And she walked away and lived. In the world. And had daughters. And died, in the end, an old woman, in a cottage, somewhere.*" She swallowed. "*Thirty houses. And never left a light on.*"

"*She had a good life*," said Pim. "*Seren says.*"

Rhosyn looked at him.

"*She did*," she said. Wondering. "*Didn't she? Hes. She had a whole life.*"

---

She closed her eyes.

She sat against the clear pillar with her head back and her eyes closed and the Hush-cup in her lap, and was quiet for so long that Pim thought she'd gone to sleep.

Then she opened them.

Green. Very bright.

"*Take me to the pool*," said Rhosyn.

---

Orrin bowed them out.

At the great clear doors of the glass palace. On the white road. With the wind going past them, into the grey, and the dust going up.

He bowed low. The old bow. One twig-arm across his middle.

To Rhosyn. In Hask's arm.

"*Highness*," he whispered.

Rhosyn looked at him.

"*Orrin*," she said. "*Come with us.*"

The dust-man was quiet.

"*Her Majesty told me to keep the palace*," he said.

"*Ysy isn't here*."

"*No*," said Orrin. "*But she told me.*" He looked up. At the spires. At the glass going up and up into the grey. At every surface a window, and every window showing the window behind it. "*And I have been keeping things for nine hundred years, Highness. I do not know how to stop.*"

He looked down at Pim.

"*The boy says I could try being nobody's*," he said. "*For a while. And see what it's like.*"

"*Will you*?" said Pim.

Orrin looked at him for a long time.

"*I will think about it*," whispered Orrin.

And bowed again.

And went back inside.

---

They found Ilda fifteen miles east of where they'd left her.

It had been four days for them. Two in, two out. Pim had counted sleeps.

It had been a lot longer for her.

She was past Westerling. Past the line of lanterns. Walking. East. Through the snow. In the real winter. Slow. Stiff. Scraping. Gold. With her wooden spoon.

Hask stopped the cart beside her.

He got down. He stood in front of her. In the road. A yard off. She came on. A step. Her gold eyes on the road. Then on him. Then past him. On the cart.

On Pim. In the back. On the sacks. Beside a grey thin woman with a crown of thorns.

---

Hask bent down.

He put his good arm round her. Round her middle. Gently. The way you'd lift a child. And lifted.

She was heavy. Gold. All of her. Pim saw his face go red. Saw the cords stand out in his neck. Heard the grey-lung catch at the bottom of his breath.

He didn't stop.

He lifted her off the road and into the back of the cart. And set her down. On the sacks. Beside Pim. Standing. Mid-step. With her wooden spoon in her hand.

Then he leaned on the side of the cart, breathing hard, and looked at her.

"*There*," he said. "*You can ride the rest.*"

---

Ilda's gold eyes looked at him.

Then at Pim.

Then at the grey woman on the sacks beside her. With the crown of thorns.

And the gold eyes went very wide.

Pim saw it. Saw her know. Nine hundred years ago, she'd turned a spit in a king's kitchen. She'd have seen them. All of them. The royal children. Running through the kitchens. Stealing bread. A girl with dirt on her hands and roses in her fists.

Rhosyn looked at her.

"*Oh*," she said softly. "*Oh, I know you. You're the bread-woman's girl. You burned your hand on the spit. Oz gave you a sweet.*"

The gold eyes filled.

"*I'm sorry*," said Rhosyn. "*I'm so sorry. For all of it.*" She reached out. Thin. Grey. And put her hand on the gold girl's arm.

Nothing happened. Rhosyn wasn't Oz. Her touch didn't keep anything. It just rested there. Grey on gold.

"*We're going home*," said Rhosyn. "*Both of us. To the pool. To give it back.*"

---

Hask got back on the box. Kit got up beside him. Pim sat on the sacks with his knees drawn up, between a gold girl and a grey queen.

The cart went east.

Through the lanterns. Through the snow. Into the world.

And behind them, faint, far off, on the edge of the grey sky, the glass palace glittered. Every surface a window. Every window showing the window behind it.

And in one of them—high up, in a round pane like a porthole—Pim thought he saw something.

A shape. Tall. Thin. Dust and silk.

Standing at the glass. With its hands folded.

Watching them go.
