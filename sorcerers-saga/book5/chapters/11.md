# The Stones Look Back

Posy Tull was sixteen on the fourteenth day of the new year, and her mother had been planning her Kindling since she was four.

Lex knew this because everyone in Ashbourne knew it. The widow Tull had talked about nothing else for a month. The new dress, blue, with lace at the cuffs that she'd unpicked from her own wedding gown. The ribbons. The honey-cakes for after, two dozen, from Hollis Penn, paid for in advance. The order of who would stand where on the green, and who would cheer first, and how loud.

Posy had wanted to be a Weaver. Like her grandmother. Everyone knew that too.

In the old days, before the net was reforged, she'd have been told what she was and that would have been the end of it. Common or Uncommon, most likely. Ceiling 24. A Weaver if she was lucky, a Spinner if she wasn't, and the rest of her life at a loom in the back room of her mother's cottage.

Now there were no Ceilings. Now a girl could put her hand on the Stone and be told what she was and then *grow*. Past twenty-four. Past fifty. Past anything. Dunny Tobbin had been Kindled a Berserker at fourteen and was Level 41 by the summer. Ivy Moss had been Kindled with a class nobody had ever heard of and was keeping a Well.

Anything could happen at a Kindling now.

That was what the widow Tull said. *Anything could happen*.

---

The Stone had been white for two days.

It had come on in the night. Wren had seen it from the dragon's back, coming home from the Teeth. Every Stone in Caldor, lighting up one after another across the dark like lanterns in windows. White. Cold. Hard. Like frost under the moon.

And in the crack down the middle of Ashbourne's Stone—

*An eye*.

---

Lex had gone down to the green in the dark. With Antho. With Wren behind them, Lark asleep on her chest, and Kestrel with her fire.

He'd stood in front of the Stone in the snow and looked at it.

Pale. Tall as a man. Clouded like old ice. Glowing white from inside, so bright it hurt to look at directly, so cold he could feel it on his face from three paces off. Like standing in front of an open door in a house in winter. Like standing in front of the sea.

And in the crack down the middle—

Nothing.

He'd looked for a long time. Close. With his face a foot from the cold white glass. He'd seen the crack. Straight as a ruler, from top to bottom, where the two halves didn't quite meet. He'd seen the glow coming out of it, white and hard.

He hadn't seen an eye.

"*It was there*," said Wren. Behind him. Very quietly. "*Ice-grey. Wide open. Looking up at us.*"

"*I believe you*."

"*It saw us*. *Lex, it saw her.*" She had her arms round the sleeping baby. "*It looked straight at her.*"

---

So they'd covered it.

That night. Hedda came down from the Forge with a bolt of sacking and a roll of chisels and her grey plaits tucked into her collar, and they wrapped the Ashbourne Stone from top to bottom in three layers of sacking and lashed it with rope. And Hedda cut a rune into the frozen earth at the foot of it. Four strokes. A hush-rune. The one Pim used. The one that meant *not here*.

The white glow came through the sacking anyway. Dim. Like a lamp behind a curtain.

But nobody saw the eye again.

---

The Keepers were doing the same, all over Caldor. Seraphine had sent ravens out from the Forge before dawn. *Cover the Stones. Sacking. Canvas. Anything. Hush-runes if you've got anyone who can cut them. Don't look into them. Don't touch them.*

*Don't let anyone Kindle*.

---

The widow Tull had not got the raven.

Or she had, and hadn't read it. Or she had read it and decided it didn't apply to her daughter. Lex never found out which.

He found out the way everyone found out. At noon on the fourteenth day of the new year, with the sun white on the snow and the whole village going about its business, when a shriek went up from the green so loud and so high that half of Ashbourne dropped what it was holding and ran.

---

He was at the Forge.

He ran too.

Down the lane. In the snow. With Antho beside him and Tilda behind and the bell under his shirt bouncing on its cord. Past the chapel. Onto the green.

---

The sacking was off.

It lay in the snow at the foot of the Stone in a heap, the ropes cut, the hush-rune scuffed out by boots. And round the Stone, a crowd. Thirty people. Forty. The widow Tull's whole family and half her street, in their best coats, with ribbons in their hats, and a trestle table set up by the pond with two dozen honey-cakes on it under a cloth.

And in the middle, by the Stone, a girl.

Sixteen. Thin. Freckled. Mousy-haired. In a blue dress with lace at the cuffs. With her right hand flat against the white glowing glass of the Stone.

Not moving.

---

The widow Tull was screaming.

Not words. Just sound. Standing a yard behind her daughter with both hands over her mouth and her eyes wide and the sound coming out between her fingers. High. Thin. Endless.

Nobody else was making any noise at all.

Lex pushed through.

"*Posy*—"

The girl didn't turn round.

She was looking at the Stone. At the white glass. Right in front of her face, a foot away. Her hand flat against it. Her mouth a little open. Her eyes—

He saw her eyes.

Wide. Fixed. Not blinking. The way you'd look at something you couldn't look away from. The way a rabbit looks at a stoat.

He looked where she was looking.

---

The Stone was showing her her face.

---

Not a reflection. Not the way a window shows you your face at night, faint and grey with the dark behind it. This was *clear*. Bright. Sharp. Every freckle. Every eyelash. Every pore. A face hanging in the white glass a foot in front of her, exactly her size, exactly her shape. Thin. Freckled. Mousy-haired. Sixteen.

Looking back at her.

And under it, in the glass, in letters—not silver-blue, not the Lattice's own clean glyphs, not anything Lex had ever seen in seven years of panels—in letters like frost on a window, white and fine and cold:

*YOU ARE POSY TULL*.

*THIS IS WHAT YOU ARE*.

---

Nothing else.

No class. No grade. No level. No skills. None of the things a Stone gave you, a Kindling gave you, that told you what you could do, what you could become.

Just her face.

And her name.

And *this is what you are*.

---

"*Posy*," said Lex. Very gently. "*Posy, take your hand away.*"

The girl didn't move.

"*I can't*," she said. A whisper. She didn't take her eyes off the face in the glass. "*I can't stop looking. Smith, I can't stop looking at it.*"

"*Take your hand away*."

"*It's me*," said Posy Tull. "*It's just me. That's all I am. That's all I'll ever be. It's showing me.*" Her voice cracked. "*It says I'm not anything. It says I'm just this. Just Posy. It says that's all.*"

"*It's lying*."

"*It's not*," said the girl. "*It's true. Look at it. It's true.*" A tear ran down her face. The face in the glass wept with her. Exactly. Every drop. "*I'm not a Weaver. I'm not anything. I'm just—this.*"

---

Lex put his hand on her wrist.

It was cold. Ice-cold. The cold coming out of the Stone and into her hand and up her arm. He could feel it. Like frost spreading across a pane.

He pulled.

Her hand didn't come away.

Not stuck. Not frozen. He could feel it wasn't stuck. It was just—*staying*. Her own hand, pressed flat against the glass, staying there because she couldn't bear to take it away. Because the face in the glass was hers, and true, and she couldn't stop looking.

He pulled harder.

"*Don't*," said Posy. Faintly. "*Don't. I want to see.*"

---

"*Wren*," said Lex.

He didn't know where she was. He hadn't seen her in the crowd. He said it anyway. Not loud.

She was beside him.

He didn't know how. She hadn't been there, and then she was. With Lark on her back in the sling and her bow over her shoulder and her face white. She looked at the Stone. At the face in it. At the frost-letters.

She leaned past Lex.

And breathed on it.

---

Mist. Grey. Soft. Cold. Rolling out of her mouth like fog off a river in October. Over the white glass. Over the face. Over the letters.

*Fog on glass*.

The face blurred. The letters blurred. The white glow behind them went grey, went soft, went—

Posy Tull's hand came away.

All at once. As if a string had been cut. She fell back. Into Lex. And he caught her, and she twisted in his arms and buried her face in his coat and screamed.

---

It took an hour to get her home.

Her mother carried her. Lex walked beside them. The whole street followed. Nobody said anything. Nobody looked back at the Stone. The honey-cakes stayed on the trestle by the pond under their cloth.

At the door of the Tull cottage, Posy stopped screaming.

She stood in the doorway. In her blue dress with the lace at the cuffs. And turned round. And looked at Lex.

Her face was blotched and wet and swollen. Her eyes were very wide.

"*It was true*," she said. "*Wasn't it? It wasn't lying. It showed me what I am.*"

Lex didn't know what to say.

"*It showed you what you are now*," said Wren. From behind him. Quietly. "*Not what you'll be. Glass can't show you that. Nothing can.*"

The girl looked at her.

"*Then why did it feel like that was all*?" said Posy Tull.

And went inside.

---

They covered the Stone again.

Four layers of sacking this time. And canvas over that. And chains. Brannoc brought the chains down from the Forge himself, on his shoulder, and wrapped them round and round and padlocked them, and stood back and looked at it with his gold fingertips in his armpits.

"*There*," he said. "*Now she'll have to knock.*"

Nobody laughed.

The white glow came through the canvas anyway. Dimmer. Like a lamp in a fog.

---

Seraphine came that evening.

On Ashka, with Antho. From the Forge, where she'd been since dawn with her eleven books and a hundred ravens going out and coming back. She came into the kitchen at Harrow with snow on her shoulders and ink on her fingers and her spectacles fogged, and sat down at the table without taking her coat off.

"*Thirty-one*," she said.

Lex looked at her.

"*Thirty-one Kindlings*," said Seraphine. "*Since the Stones came on. Two days. All over Caldor. Before the ravens got there, or after, or because people didn't believe them.*" She took off her spectacles. Rubbed her eyes. "*Thirty-one children put their hands on a Stone and saw their own faces. And their names. And this is what you are.*"

"*And*—"

"*And nothing*," said Seraphine. "*No class. No level. No skills. Nothing.*" She put her spectacles back on. "*Their threads are there. I checked. With the Keepers' readers. The net's got them. It knows they're there.*" She looked at him. "*It just won't tell them what they are. It shows them their faces instead.*"

---

"*It's her*," said Antho. From the door. Where he was leaning, with his arms folded, the way he leaned when he was frightened. "*It's Ysenne.*"

"*Yes*," said Seraphine.

"*She's in the Stones*."

"*She's in the net*," said Seraphine. "*Through the Stones. The way the rose said.*" She pulled one of her books out of her satchel. Not the Codex. A Warden's manual. Old. Grey cloth. *On the Structure and Maintenance of the Lattice Stones*. She'd stolen it from the Spire archive eight years ago, the night she fled. "*Every Stone is a window. Onto the net. That's what they were built for. That's what a Kindling is. The child puts her hand on the window and the net looks through it, at her, all the way down.*"

She opened it. To a diagram. A Stone, drawn in cross-section. Threads going down from it. Down and down. Into the ground. Into the net.

"*She's turned them round*," said Seraphine.

"*What*?"

"*The windows*." She tapped the diagram. "*They're supposed to let the net look at the child. Now they're looking at the child for her. The Glass Queen. She's in the net, behind every Stone, and when a child puts her hand on the glass, it's not the net looking back.*" She looked up. "*It's her. And she shows them what she shows everyone.*"

"*Their faces*," said Wren.

"*Their faces*. *What they are.*" Seraphine closed the book. "*Glass can't show you what you could be. It can only show you what you are.*"

---

Nobody said anything for a long time.

Lark was asleep in the cradle in the loft. Seren was on the settle by the fire with the grey book. Kestrel was on the ladder. The water barrel had its lid on. The windows were horn.

"*She's walking*," said Seraphine at last.

Lex looked at her.

"*In the net*," she said. "*I can feel it. A little. Through Mal. Hollow Speaking.*" She touched her chest. "*Every thread she touches goes—cold. Like glass. Like frost spreading. It's very small. Very slow. But she's moving. Down. Thread by thread. Toward the middle.*"

"*The crown in the road*."

"*The crown in the road*." Seraphine swallowed. "*The Root. The centre. Where you let go.*" She looked at him. "*She's going for it. And she's going the long way. Through every thread. Because the net won't let her straight in. It doesn't know her. She's not of the net. She can only walk where it's glass.*"

"*How long*?"

Seraphine looked at her notebook. At columns of figures. At a map of Caldor with thirty-one small crosses on it.

"*At this pace*," she said, "*two months. Perhaps less. Perhaps she'll find a quicker way.*" She closed the notebook. "*The spring. The equinox. Round about then.*"

---

"*And when she gets there*," said Lex, "*it won't let her in.*"

Seraphine looked at him.

"*The Root's Vey*," said Lex. "*Always. Aurelian built it that way. It only ever answered to his blood. That's why it was ours. That's why I could give it away.*" He put his hands flat on the table. "*She can walk to the middle. She can stand there. She can look at it. But she can't pick it up.*"

"*No*," said Seraphine. "*Not without—*"

She stopped.

She looked up. At the ceiling. At the loft.

They all did.

---

"*Not without a Vey hand*," said Seren. Quietly. From the settle.

Nobody answered.

---

Lex went up the ladder.

Kestrel moved aside for him. Without a word. She had a little flame in her palm, the size of a candle-flame. She held it up so he could see.

The loft was dark and warm and close. Wren's quilt on the bed. The little window under the roof, horn-paned, with the stars blurred behind it. And the cradle. Ash and iron. With *KNIT* on the rockers.

He looked in.

Lark was asleep. On her back. With her arms flung up over her head, the way she always slept, as if she'd been dropped from a height and landed like that and couldn't be bothered to move. Her fluff of hair. Her round face. Her mouth a little open.

Her hands.

One on either side of her head. Open. Palms up. Square. Broad. Too big for the rest of her. A smith's hands. The fingers curled a little, loose, in sleep.

*Vey hands*, his father had said. *Aurelian had hands like that. In the paintings.*

---

He stood over the cradle for a long time.

He didn't touch her. He didn't want to wake her.

He thought about the eye of the storm. About the wind lifting her out of the sling and spinning her in the bright air, laughing. About *don't*, and Wren's hand on his arm, and letting go.

*You cannot hold her*, the wind had said. *You can hold the door open so she can go through it. But you cannot hold her.*

He'd believed it. Up there. In the eye. With the sky blue overhead and his daughter laughing.

He stood in the dark loft with the white glow of a covered Stone faint on the horizon through the horn window, and tried to believe it now.

---

*Status*, he thought.

He didn't know why. Habit. Seven years of habit. When he was frightened, when he didn't know what to do, he looked at the panel. As if it would tell him. As if it would have something at the bottom, in the corner, in gold.

It came up.

---

Not silver-blue.

---

White.

Cold. Fine. Like frost on a window. The frame of the panel, the lines of it, the letters, all of it white and glittering and hard, the way the letters on the Stone had been. Hanging in the dark of the loft over his daughter's cradle.

And it didn't say WORLDFORGER.

It didn't say Level 99.

It said:

---

:::error
HELLO, SMITH.
:::

---

He didn't move.

He stood over the cradle with his hands at his sides and the white panel hanging in front of him and didn't move at all.

The letters changed. Melted. Re-formed. Like frost growing across a pane.

---

:::error
YOU'RE FRIGHTENED.
YOU LAUGHED YESTERDAY. IN THE STORM.
THE FIRST TIME SINCE HE DIED.
:::

---

*Get out*, he thought.

---

:::error
I'M NOT IN. NOT YET.
I'M IN THE GLASS. AT THE EDGE.
YOUR NET HAS SO MUCH GLASS IN IT.
:::

---

*Get out of my panel*.

---

:::error
IT ISN'T YOURS.
NOTHING IN IT IS YOURS.
YOU GAVE IT AWAY. REMEMBER?
:::

---

He reached for fire.

He didn't think. It came up in him the way it always came when he was frightened. Heat. Weight. The roar of a forge. He could have burned the loft down. He could have burned the cottage down. He felt it come up into his hands, white-gold, and stopped it. Just. With his daughter asleep a foot away.

He stood there with fire in his hands and nowhere to put it.

The white letters didn't flicker.

---

:::error
THAT WON'T WORK. I'M GLASS.
FIRE MAKES GLASS. IT DOESN'T BREAK IT.
:::

---

And then the letters went.

All of them. Melted away. And the white panel hanging in the dark in front of him was empty. Clear. Like a pane of glass in a window with nothing behind it.

And then it wasn't empty.

---

A face.

In the panel. Where the letters had been. Clear and bright and sharp. Every line. Every scar. A face he knew.

His own.

Twenty-six. Grey-eyed. Burned. A handprint on the left cheek, grey-gold, five fingers and a palm. Dark hair cut badly. Tired. Older than he felt.

And on his brow—

Gold.

A crown. Not a circlet. Not a band. *Threads*. Gold threads, fine as hair, thousands of them, millions, coming up out of his head and going out in every direction, into the dark, into the world, like the rays of the sun in a child's drawing. Every thread humming. Every thread a person. A name. A life.

The Root.

His face. Crowned with the net.

Looking back at him.

---

:::error
THIS IS WHAT YOU COULD HAVE BEEN.
YOU GAVE IT AWAY.
I WONDER IF YOU'D GIVE IT AWAY AGAIN.
:::

---

He couldn't breathe.

He stood in the loft and looked at the face in the panel. His face. Crowned. Holding every thread in the world. Able to touch any of them. Fix any of them. Wren's fingers. Brannoc's. Tilda's. The boy in the field. The bread at Westerling. The cut on Davy Pruett's hand.

Ember.

He could reach down any thread in the world and find a flame in a lantern and—

*No*.

He didn't think it. He *felt* it. Under his breastbone. Where the Wells were. Fire and stone and water and wind, all at once, saying it.

*No*.

---

The face in the panel smiled.

Not his smile. Not quite. Something colder. Something curious. As if someone were wearing his face to see how it fit.

Then the letters came back. Over the face. Small. Fine. White. At the bottom of the panel. In the corner. Where, for seven years, there had been a line of warm gold text.

---

:::error
YOUR DAUGHTER'S HANDS
ARE VERY SQUARE.
:::

---

And the panel went out.

---

Lex stood in the dark.

Kestrel's flame was gone. She'd closed her hand. The loft was black. The horn window. The stars. The faint white glow on the horizon, far off, where a covered Stone stood on a village green.

And in the cradle, his daughter. Asleep. On her back. With her arms flung over her head and her square hands open on the pillow.

He knelt down beside the cradle.

He didn't touch her. He didn't want to wake her.

He knelt there in the dark, beside her, all night. With his hands in his lap. With fire still in them. With nowhere to put it.
