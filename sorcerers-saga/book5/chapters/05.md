# The King on the Green

The whole of Ashbourne was standing at the edges of the green, and nobody was on it.

Lex saw them as he came down the lane from the Forge. Packed into doorways. Crowded at windows. Lined up three deep along the front of the Crooked Kettle, with Benno the landlord in front in his apron holding a poker. Children on shoulders. Old men on walls. Women with their arms wrapped round themselves and their breath smoking. A hundred people. Two hundred. The whole village.

All of them looking at the middle of the green.

And nobody moving.

---

The green at Ashbourne was a lopsided triangle of grass between the mill road and the chapel and the lane up to the Forge. Under snow now. Trampled into paths at the edges, by the well and the chapel door. A lantern-pole on every corner, still hung with Lantern Night lights. A bench by the pond.

And in the middle, the Stone.

Pale. Tall as a man. Clouded like old ice. Cracked from top to bottom down the middle in a line as straight as if someone had laid a ruler on it. The Lattice Stone of Ashbourne, where every child of the village had put their hand at sixteen for three hundred years and been told what they were.

Where Lex had put his, at sixteen, ten years ago. And the Stone had lit, silver-blue, dim, and said *SMITH*. *Common*. *Ceiling 24*.

He'd believed it for three years.

---

The King was sitting beside it.

---

On the snow. Cross-legged. With his back very straight and his great hands resting on his knees, the way a man sits who has been told to keep still and is trying very hard to do it.

Even sitting, he was taller than the Stone.

Gold. All of him. Not gilded. Not painted. *Gold*, the way a coal is red. Glowing. Faintly, steadily, from inside, like metal in a crucible just before it pours. His hair was gold. His beard. His skin. His robe, which had once been something rich and deep and was now gold to the hem. And his eyes—

Lex didn't look at his eyes. Not yet. He'd learned that in the autumn. You looked at the King's eyes when you were ready to.

Round him, on the snow, a circle. Gold. A yard across. Where he'd sat down. The snow under him and round him gone gold, and the grass under the snow, and, Lex supposed, the earth under the grass, a little way down. A gold circle on a white green, with a gold man in the middle of it.

He had a cup in his hand.

---

Lex stopped at the edge of the green.

Behind him, he could hear the others coming down the lane from the Forge. The Queen's guards. Kestrel's quick light step. Brannoc, breathing. He didn't look round.

"*Stay here*," he said.

"*Lex*—" Wren.

"*Stay here*. *Please.*" He looked at the King. The King hadn't moved. Hadn't turned his head. Was looking, Lex thought, at the Stone. "*He's not here to hurt anyone. He'd have done it already.*"

"*You don't know what he's here for*."

"*No*," said Lex. "*So I'm going to ask him.*"

He stepped onto the green.

---

The snow was very loud under his boots.

Two hundred people watching. Not a sound from any of them. Just his boots in the snow, *crunch*, *crunch*, and the wind in the bare elms by the chapel, and somewhere a dog barking and someone hushing it.

He walked straight across. Not hurrying. The way you'd walk up to a horse you didn't know.

He stopped at the edge of the gold circle.

---

"*Smith*," said the Gilded King.

He said it without turning his head. Warm. Deep. A voice like a bell.

But not like a bell. Not quite. Lex heard it. The thing his mother had heard at the window, and Wren in the high country, and Nan Ashby's neighbour over her wall with her clay pipe. Under the warmth. Under the depth. A flaw. A hairline crack down the middle, the way a bell sounds when it's been dropped once and still rings but not quite true.

"*Majesty*," said Lex.

"*Oh*, *don't*." The King turned his head. Slowly. Looked up at him. "*Nobody's called me that since the boat. Except Orrin. And Orrin calls the furniture Majesty if you leave him alone with it long enough.*"

And Lex looked at his eyes.

---

Furnace eyes. Molten. Gold-white at the centre, going to orange at the edges, like the heart of a forge with the bellows going. He remembered them from the autumn. From the floor of his mother's cottage, with the King kneeling on the gold flags and his hand out to a baby. You couldn't look at them for long. They burned. Not cruelly. The way the sun burns.

They'd dimmed.

He saw it at once. In the autumn they'd been like looking into a furnace. Now they were like looking into one that had been banked for the night. Still gold. Still hot. Still too bright to hold. But lower. Deeper. With something in them that hadn't been there before.

Lex didn't have a word for it.

He thought, later, that the word was *tired*.

---

"*Sit*," said the King. "*If you like. Not too close.*" He lifted one hand off his knee and gestured. At the snow beyond the edge of the gold. "*I've been very careful. I've been careful for two months. I haven't touched a single person.*" He looked at the circle round him. "*Things, yes. I can't help things. But not people.*"

Lex sat.

In the snow. A yard off. Just outside the gold. Cross-legged, the same as the King. With the cracked Stone beside them both and two hundred people watching from the edges of the green.

---

The cup was lopsided.

He saw it now. In the King's great gold hand. Small. Too thick in the walls. With a handle that a smith had got wrong, too high, at a slant. And a thumb-print in the clay near the rim, where someone had pressed too hard.

Gold. All of it. And the tea in it. A gold disc. Still.

The King saw him looking.

"*I can't put it down*," he said. Simply. "*I've tried. I put it on a wall outside a farm near Greyfall, and walked twenty paces, and came back for it.*" He turned it a little in his fingers. Very carefully. The way you'd turn a bird's egg. "*Your mother made it. Did you know?*"

"*Brannoc made the handle*."

"*Brannoc*." The King considered this. "*The big one. With the fingers.*" He looked down at the cup. At the slanting handle. "*I gilded his fingers.*"

"*Your steward did*."

"*Orrin does what I want*." The furnace eyes didn't move off the cup. "*It's the same thing.*"

---

They sat for a while.

The wind blew. The elms creaked. Two hundred people breathed at the edges of the green.

The King looked at the Stone.

"*It's glass*," he said. "*Did you know that? All the way down. Very old glass. Very strange.*" He tilted his great head. "*Ysy would like it. She likes anything you can see through.*"

"*Where is she*?"

"*I don't know*." He said it simply. Not hiding anything. Lex didn't think the King had ever hidden anything in his life. He'd never needed to. "*She went back to the Court. With Rosie. On a chain.*" A pause. "*I haven't heard her since. Not in a puddle. Not in a window. Not anywhere.*" He frowned. A great slow gold frown. "*Ysy's never quiet. Not for two months. She's never been quiet for two days.*"

He looked at the Stone again. At the crack down the middle.

"*I don't like it*," said the Gilded King.

---

"*Cad's gone*," he said.

Lex didn't answer.

"*I felt it*. *At the edge. When it fell.*" The King looked at the cup. "*Rosie felt it more. Through her roots. She said he fell with the thread. All the way down. She said he nodded.*" A pause. "*At Hes. At her crown. On someone else's head.*"

"*Elric's*."

"*Elric's*." The King tried the name. "*Mae's friend. The grey one. With the bird.*" He shook his head slowly. "*I never met him. Cad met him. On the thread. Cad said he was the most stubborn thing he'd ever tried to move.*" A dry sound. Not a laugh. "*Cad said that about mountains, mostly.*"

"*He let us go*," said Lex. "*Your brother. At the end. He held us and he let us go.*"

The King was quiet.

"*He held Father's hand*," he said. "*At the end. Every night. For a month. Not saying anything. He was eleven.*"

"*I know*. *You told us.*"

"*Did I*?" The King looked at him. "*At the cottage. Yes. I told the old man.*" He looked down. "*I tell everyone. I've told four hundred people since the edge fell. On their walls. At their gates. In their yards.*" The cracked bell of his voice went very low. "*Cad held his hand. And I stood outside the door.*"

---

"*Four hundred*?" said Lex.

"*Four hundred and twelve*." The King didn't hesitate. "*I've been counting. Smiths count, I'm told. So do kings, it turns out. When there's nothing else to do.*" He turned the cup. "*Four hundred and twelve old women and old men and a few young ones, on their walls, at their gates, giving me tea in their worst cups because they knew it would go gold.*"

"*And you asked them*—"

"*How their mothers died*." The furnace eyes came up. "*Their grandmothers. Their husbands. Their children, some of them.*" He looked at Lex. "*If it hurt. If they were frightened. If anyone held their hand.*"

Lex didn't say anything.

"*You want to know why*," said the King.

"*Yes*."

The King looked at the Stone for a long time.

"*Because I sat with the old man*," he said. "*Your father. On the floor. At the end. And I watched it.*" His voice was very quiet now. "*I'd never watched it. Not once. In nine hundred years. Not Father. Not the cousins at the edge. Not one of my household in the first year, coming apart in the halls. I put my hand on them before they could. All of them. So I'd never have to.*"

He looked down at his hands. Gold. Enormous. Holding a lopsided cup.

"*And then I sat on the floor of that cottage and watched an old man do it*," said the Gilded King. "*With his wife holding one hand and his son holding the other. And it wasn't—*"

He stopped.

"*It wasn't what*?"

"*I don't know*." The King's face worked. Gold. Slowly. Like metal cooling. "*I don't know what it wasn't. That's why I keep asking.*"

---

Lex looked at him.

At the biggest man in the world, gold, glowing, sitting in a circle of gold snow on a village green with a lopsided cup in his hand. Who had kept nine thousand people in gold for nine hundred years so he'd never have to watch one of them go. Who'd walked across half the world in the snow, stopping at walls and gates, asking old women how their grandmothers died.

He thought of a boy in a field. Mid-step. His eyes moving.

He thought of Wren's fingers. Brannoc's. Tilda's whole hand. Hask's arm. Roole in the gate.

He thought of a bed by a west window. And a voice saying *you should have gone in. He'd have liked you there*.

He didn't know what he felt.

---

"*I need to ask you something*," he said.

The King looked at him.

"*Ask*."

---

He told him.

All of it. The grey. The bread. The boy's hand. The stakes. A mile a day and faster. The dead at Mal's rim, thousands, saying their names, with the snow going grey round them. Mal, crying in the dark, too little.

And Caddoc. The heavy cold piece of the Well coming home on Lantern Night. *Hesper's*. And the room getting bigger. Like a breath.

The King listened.

He didn't interrupt. He didn't move. He sat in his circle of gold with the cup in his hands and his furnace eyes on Lex's face and listened to all of it. The way Lex's father used to listen. The way Seraphine listened. With his whole attention, as if nothing else in the world were happening.

When Lex had finished, he was quiet for a long time.

"*So I'm full*," he said at last. "*Of your pool.*"

"*Yes*."

"*And it's empty*."

"*Nearly*."

"*And my dead are standing round it*," said the Gilded King. "*Waiting. Because I drank their room nine hundred years ago, and never gave it back.*"

Lex didn't say anything.

The King looked down at the cup.

"*And you want it back*," he said.

"*I'm asking*," said Lex. "*Not taking. I couldn't take it if I wanted to. Nobody can.*" He swallowed. "*I'm asking you to give it back. Like Hesper did.*"

---

The King didn't answer.

He sat very still. In the snow. Looking at the cup. For a long time. Long enough that Lex heard the chapel clock strike the half hour behind him. Long enough that a child at the edge of the green started crying and was hushed.

Then he spoke.

"*Do you know what happens*," said the Gilded King, "*if I give it back?*"

"*You'd stop being deathless*."

"*Yes*." The King nodded slowly. "*Yes. That's the first thing. I'd stop. I'd start. I'd get old. I'd get sick. One day I'd sit in a chair by a window and go through the door, and the man with the boat would come for me at last.*" He looked at the cup. "*I'm not afraid of that.*"

He said it so simply that Lex believed him.

"*Then*—"

"*That's not the only thing*." The furnace eyes came up. "*Smith. What do you think keeps them?*"

Lex didn't understand.

"*My household*," said the King. "*Nine thousand of them. Walking across your fields. Humming.*" He lifted his free hand. Not far. A few inches off his knee. Gold. Enormous. "*What do you think holds them? In the gold? What do you think keeps them from coming apart, after nine hundred years? Keeps the boy in the field from finishing his step? Keeps your wife's fingers? Keeps the man in the gate?*"

He opened his hand. Slowly. Palm up.

"*Me*," he said. "*It's me. It's one hold. I put it on them the night of the boat, all of them, at once, and I've never let go. Not for a moment. Not in nine hundred years.*" He looked at his open hand. "*It's what I drank. It's what I'm full of. That's what the keeping is.*"

---

Lex went cold.

"*If I give it back*," said the King quietly, "*the hold goes. All at once. Everywhere.*"

"*They'd be free*—"

"*They'd be nine hundred years old*," said the Gilded King.

---

Silence.

On the green. In the snow. Two hundred people breathing.

"*The boy in your field*," said the King. "*Fine. He'd finish his step. He'd go home to his mother. He was only kept a year.*" He closed his hand. "*Your wife's fingers. The big smith's. The girl's hand. The miner's arm. They'd wake. They'd bend.*"

He looked east. Over the elms. Over the chapel roof. Toward the world.

"*And my cooks*," he said. "*My grooms. My nursemaids. Ilda. Nine thousand people who were born before your world had a name. They'd wake up. All at once. With nine hundred years on them.*" His voice cracked. Properly, this time. Down the middle. "*And they'd come apart. Like the ones in the halls. Like the cousins at the edge. Right there. In your fields. In front of me.*"

He looked at the cup.

"*All of them*," said the Gilded King. "*At once. And I'd be standing there. Outside the door.*"

---

Lex couldn't speak.

He'd thought about it. At the rim. On the dragon. In the cot at Greyfall. He'd thought about the Crowned and what they'd drunk and what it would mean to give it back. He'd thought it would cost them their deathlessness. He'd thought that was the price.

He hadn't thought about the nine thousand.

---

"*They want to go home*," he said. Hoarsely. "*Your household. They're humming it. Home. All of them.*"

"*They are home*," said the King. "*With me. In the gold. Safe.*"

But he didn't say it the way he'd said it in the autumn. On the floor of the cottage. *They're safe*. Like a bell.

He said it the way you say a prayer you've said so many times the words have worn smooth and you've stopped hearing them.

"*Ask me something else*," he said. "*Anything. I'll do anything else.*"

"*There isn't anything else*."

"*Then I'm sorry*," said the Gilded King. "*I'm very sorry, smith. I am.*" He looked down at the cup in his hands. "*But I can't stand outside nine thousand doors.*"

---

Lex sat in the snow.

He didn't argue. He couldn't think of anything to say. He sat beside the King in the snow on the green at Ashbourne with two hundred people watching and the cracked Stone between them, and didn't say anything at all.

*We ask*, he'd said.

He'd asked.

---

"*Oi*," said a voice.

---

They both looked up.

Across the green. From the far side. From the row of cottages that faced the chapel, with their gardens running down to the pond.

An old woman was coming across the snow.

Very slowly. With a stick in one hand and a cat under her other arm. In three shawls and a man's coat and a pair of boots so big they must have been her husband's. Bent nearly double. One eye. Bright. Black. Like a bird's. The other closed for good under a fold of skin she'd stopped bothering to cover forty years ago.

The whole village held its breath.

She came straight across the green. *Thump* went the stick. *Crunch* went the boots. *Mrrp* went the cat, under protest. She didn't hurry. She didn't stop. She came right up to the edge of the gold circle, on the King's other side, opposite Lex, and stood there, leaning on her stick, breathing hard, and looked down at the Gilded King with her one black eye.

"*You*," said Old Mag. "*You're the one with the steward.*"

---

The King looked up at her.

For a moment—just a moment—Lex saw something in the furnace eyes he'd never seen there. Not in the autumn. Not on the floor of the cottage. Not ever.

The King looked *afraid*.

"*Mistress*," he said.

"*Don't you Mistress me*. *Your steward did that. In my kitchen. In the spring. Bowed to me after.*" She shifted the cat. "*Came in a storm of your gold. Blew my door in. Sat on my hearth like a pile of dead leaves and offered to keep me.*"

"*I know*," said the King. "*He told me.*"

"*And what did he tell you*?"

The King looked at her for a long time.

"*He said you said you weren't greedy*," said the Gilded King. Very quietly. "*He said you said you didn't want to be kept. You wanted to be finished.*" He swallowed. Gold. "*He said, there's a difference.*"

"*There is*," said Old Mag.

"*I've been thinking about it*," said the King. "*Ever since. For eight months.*" He looked down at the cup. "*I've walked across half your world thinking about it. I've asked four hundred and twelve people. I still don't know what it means.*"

Old Mag looked at him.

Then she sniffed. Loudly. And turned round.

"*Well*, *you won't find out sitting in the snow*," she said. "*Come on. My step's just there. I'm not making tea out here, I'm ninety-six. Ninety-seven Tuesday.*" She started back across the green. *Thump*. *Crunch*. "*Bring your cup. I've only got the one good teapot and I'm not having it go gold.*"

---

Nobody moved.

Not the King. Not Lex. Not the two hundred people round the edges of the green.

Old Mag stopped. Ten paces off. Looked back over her shoulder. One black eye.

"*Well*?" she said.

---

The King got up.

Slowly. Carefully. The way a very big man gets up when he's afraid of breaking something. One knee. Then the other. Then all of him, unfolding, up and up, until he stood over the green taller than the chapel door, gold, glowing, with the snow sliding off his shoulders in gold flakes and the circle round his feet blazing.

The whole village stepped back. A hundred people. Two hundred. All at once. Like a field of grass in the wind.

The King didn't seem to notice.

He was looking at the old woman. At her stick. At her husband's boots. At the cat.

He followed her.

---

Lex followed too. He didn't decide to. He just got up and went.

Across the green. Behind the King. The King walked very slowly, very carefully, putting each great gold foot down in the snow as if the snow were glass. Each footprint gold. A gold path across the white green, a step wide, following an old woman with a stick.

At the edge of the green, Old Mag's garden. A low wall. A gate, standing open. A path. And at the end of the path, a cottage. Small and crooked and whitewashed, with a thatch like an old hat, and a step—one step, worn hollow in the middle by a hundred years of boots—up to the door.

And beside the door, an apple tree.

Old. Black-barked. Bent like its owner. Covered in snow.

And under the snow—Lex saw it as he came up the path, and stopped, and stared—

Blossom.

---

White. Pink at the edges. In thick clusters along every black branch. In the snow. In Midwinter. Not withered. Not frozen. *Open*. As if it were May.

It had done it two winters ago. The winter of the weddings. The winter the Hollow started coming up through the ground in Wild Delves and the world went strange—a spring in the forge yard, a second chimney on the mill—and Old Mag's dead apple tree, which hadn't flowered in thirty years, had burst into blossom in the snow.

Lex had thought it had stopped.

It hadn't. It was blooming now. In the grey Midwinter dusk. With the snow lying on it and the blossom showing through, white on white.

The King had stopped too.

He stood at the end of the path with his cup in his hand and looked up at it.

"*Oh*," said the Gilded King.

---

"*Don't touch it*," said Old Mag. Sharply. From the step. "*It's mine. It's the only one I've got.*" She sat down on the step. Heavily. With a grunt. Put the cat in her lap. "*Sit there. On the path. You can look at it all you like. You can't touch it.*"

The King sat down on the path.

The flagstones went gold under him.

He didn't look at them. He was looking at the tree.

---

"*Thirty years*," said Old Mag. Stroking the cat. "*Thirty years that tree was dead. My husband planted it the year we married. Gave apples like you wouldn't believe for forty years. Then stopped.*" She looked up at it. "*Then two winters ago it woke up. Middle of the snow. Like this. Hasn't stopped since. Flowers all year round. Never gives an apple. Not one.*" She sniffed. "*I don't mind. I'm too old for apples anyway. I like the flowers.*"

The King looked at the tree.

"*Why*?" he said.

"*Don't know*." Old Mag shrugged. "*The smith says it's the Hollow. The new Well. Making room. Possibilities, he calls them.*" She looked at Lex. "*Didn't you?*"

"*Yes*."

"*So there you are*." She looked back at the King. "*Possibilities*."

---

The King sat on the gold path and looked at the blossom for a long time.

"*It's new*," he said at last. Very quietly.

"*It's old*," said Old Mag. "*Older than me. Older than my husband'd be.*"

"*The flowers are new*."

Old Mag looked at him. One black eye.

"*They're new every morning*," she said. "*That's the thing about flowers.*"

---

Lex stood at the gate and watched them.

An old woman on a step with a cat in her lap. A gold king on a gold path with a lopsided cup in his hands. An apple tree between them, blooming in the snow.

And behind him, at the edges of the green, two hundred people. Watching. Not making a sound.

---

They talked for an hour.

Not about anything. Lex couldn't have said, after, what about. The cat. The tree. Her husband, who'd been a thatcher and fallen off a roof at sixty and lived another twenty years out of spite. Her daughters, both married, both gone south. Her son.

"*Went to sea*," said Old Mag. "*At seventeen. On a salt-boat out of Saltreach. Never came back.*" She stroked the cat. "*Forty years ago. More. Storm off the Teeth, they said. All hands.*"

The King looked at her.

"*Were you frightened*?" he said. "*When they told you?*"

"*No*," said Old Mag. "*I was angry. He never wrote. Not once. Six months on that boat and not one letter.*" She sniffed. "*I'm going to tell him off. When I see him.*"

The King went very still.

"*When you see him*," he said.

"*At the bottom of the sea*," said Old Mag. "*Or wherever he's got to. Wherever you go.*" She looked at the King. Steadily. One black eye in a face like a dried apple. "*Not long now. I can feel it in my feet.*"

---

The King didn't move.

He sat on the gold path in front of the old woman's step with the cup in his hands, and Lex watched the gold of his face go very still, the way metal goes still when it stops glowing and starts to set.

"*Your feet*," he said.

"*Cold*," said Old Mag. "*All the time. Since the autumn. Can't get them warm. Not by the fire. Not in bed. My mother had it. Her mother.*" She shrugged. "*It's how we go, in my family. Feet first.*" She almost grinned. "*Before Lantern Night, probably. The next one. Maybe before the spring.*"

"*I could*—" said the King.

He stopped.

"*No*," said Old Mag. Gently. "*No, you couldn't, dear. We've had that talk. Me and your steward.*" She scratched the cat behind its ears. "*I don't want to be kept. I want to be finished.*"

"*I don't know what that means*," said the Gilded King. And his voice broke. "*I keep asking. I don't know what it means.*"

---

Old Mag looked at him for a long time.

Then she did something nobody on the green would forget.

She leaned forward on her step. Over her cat. Toward the King. Until her old face was a foot from his great gold one. Not touching. Not quite. Close enough that Lex, at the gate, saw the light of the King's skin on her cheek, gold, and saw her not flinch.

"*Then you'd better come and watch*," said Old Mag.

The King stared at her.

"*When it's time*," she said. "*Come and sit with me. Here. By the fire. Hold my hand.*" She lifted her own hand off the cat. Old. Knotted. Brown-spotted. Held it out. Not to him. Just out. In the air between them. "*And don't keep me. Whatever you do. Don't you dare keep me.*"

She looked at him with her one black eye.

"*Sit with me, lad*," said Old Mag. "*When it's time. And you'll see.*"

---

The Gilded King looked at her hand.

At the old knotted fingers. Held out. In the cold. A foot from his own.

He didn't take it.

He didn't move at all.

And in the blooming apple tree over the step, a little wind came through the branches, from somewhere, from the east, and shook the snow off the blossom, and a few petals came down. White. Pink at the edges.

They fell on the gold path round the King.

And stayed white.
