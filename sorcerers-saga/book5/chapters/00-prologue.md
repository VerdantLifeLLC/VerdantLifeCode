# Prologue: The Glass Queen

Ysenne of the Glass had not seen her own face in nine hundred years.

She thought about it on the white road, on the night the edge came down, with her sister on a chain behind her and her brother walking away.

She thought about it the way you think about a tooth that has been gone so long you've stopped putting your tongue in the gap. Not often. Not on purpose. Only sometimes, at night, when everything else was quiet, and the gap was all there was.

---

Oz didn't look back.

She watched him go. East. Across the snow. Off the grey-gold dust and onto the white, where the Veil had stood for nine hundred years and now stood nothing at all. The biggest man in the world, gold, glowing like a coal, walking into the dark with a gold cup in one hand and his other hand pressed flat against his chest.

The snow went gold where he stepped.

Of course it did.

She watched the gold footprints go away from her across the white. One. Two. Ten. Fifty. A gold road, a step wide, wandering east toward a sky full of tiny lights.

Lanterns. Every one of them lit. A whole world of little windows with fire in them.

*We go and see everyone*, he'd said.

He'd said it in the hall, too. Nine hundred years ago. And again a year ago, when he woke, with Mae's crown in his hand and green coming through the crack. *Then we'll go and see everyone.* Warm. Deep. Certain. Like a bell.

It hadn't sounded like a bell tonight.

It had sounded like a bell with a crack in it.

---

"*He's gone*," said Rhosyn.

Ysenne didn't turn round.

"*Yes*."

"*He didn't say goodbye*."

"*Oz never says goodbye*," said the Glass Queen. "*He doesn't know how. He thinks if he doesn't say it, it doesn't count.*"

Behind her, in the dust, a small dry sound. The chain. Glass links touching glass. *Chime*. Rhosyn shifting where she sat.

"*He's like that because of Father*," said Rhosyn.

"*Everyone's like everything because of Father*."

"*You're not*."

Ysenne turned then.

Her sister was sitting on the white road. In the dust. With her thin knees drawn up and her arms wrapped round them and her crown of thorns crooked on her grey hair. With the chain running from her wrists across the dust to Ysenne's own clear hand.

She looked terrible. She'd always looked terrible. Even as a girl. All elbows and knees and dirt under her nails, out in the gardens with the under-gardeners when she should have been at her lessons, coming in at dusk with leaves in her hair and roses in her fists. Mother used to despair.

She looked worse now. Grey. Not the grey-green she'd been for nine hundred years. *Grey*. Like ash. Like the inside of a dead tree. She'd spent every thorn she had in that last hour at the tower, holding back a thousand of Oz's gold servants so two Harrow boys could run. Every stem. Every root.

There was nothing left in her.

"*I'm not what*?" said Ysenne.

"*Like you are because of Father*." Rhosyn looked up at her. Green eyes. The only green thing left in the grey country. "*You were like this before.*"

"*Like what*?"

Rhosyn didn't answer.

She didn't need to. Ysenne knew what she meant. She'd always known what everyone meant. That was the trouble with being the one who saw.

---

The wind was still coming in.

She felt it on her face. On her clear cheeks, on her clear hands, on the glass of her. Cold. Wet. Smelling of snow and woodsmoke and something green and growing under the snow. Pouring west past her, past Rhosyn, along the white road. Into the grey-gold dim. Into the Court.

The first wind in nine hundred years.

It was going *in*.

She stood on the white road and felt it go past her, endless, soft, like a river finding a hole in a dam, and she thought: *it isn't stopping*.

She thought: *it isn't ever going to stop*.

And under it, faint, going the other way—out, east, into the world—the dust. Grey. Fine as flour. The dust of the Court. The dust of a closed room nobody had walked through in nine hundred years.

Going out.

Into all those little windows.

---

"*The dead are going*," said Rhosyn. Very quietly. "*Look.*"

Ysenne looked.

Grey. Rising. All over the plain. Columns of dust standing up out of the drifts and swaying, the way smoke sways in a room with a window open, and leaning, all of them, east. And going.

Past them. Past the place where the edge had been. Into the world.

Thousands.

She hadn't known there were so many. She'd never looked. You didn't look at the dust. You walked through it, on the white road, between the palaces, and it came up round your feet and settled again behind you, and you didn't think about who it had been.

She thought about it now.

The first year. Before Oz went out with his hands. Before Mae went out with her moths. The ones they hadn't reached in time. Cooks and grooms and pages and nursemaids, coming apart in the silent halls because there was nothing in the Court to eat and nothing to drink and nothing to be. Lying down in the corridors. Not getting up.

Becoming the floor.

She'd walked on them for nine hundred years.

She watched them rise and lean and go east into the world, on the first wind, toward the lanterns.

"*Where are they going*?" she said.

"*Home*," said Rhosyn. "*The warden told them. The tall one with the scarred hands. There's a pool. In a green field. Full of room.*" A pause. "*He said it's still very little.*"

"*Then it will drown*," said the Glass Queen.

"*Maybe*."

"*Thousands of them. And a pool still very little.*" Ysenne looked east. "*It'll fill up and stop. And they'll be standing round it. Forever. Like the kept. Waiting at a door nobody can open.*"

"*Maybe*," said Rhosyn again.

"*You don't care*?"

"*I care*." Her sister put her chin on her knees. "*I just don't know anything. So I say maybe. It's what people say when they don't know.*" The green eyes moved. "*You should try it, Ysy.*"

"*I always know*."

"*I know*," said Rhosyn. "*That's what I mean.*"

---

They walked back.

West. Along the white road. Ysenne first, tall and clear and glittering, and the chain running back from her hand, and Rhosyn on the end of it, stumbling. Barefoot. Her rotten silk dragging in the dust.

She could have carried her. Ysenne thought about it. She was glass, but glass is strong, if you know where to hold it. She could have lifted her sister the way you'd lift a sack of bones and carried her the forty miles to the palaces and set her down somewhere soft.

She didn't.

*She let them go*, she thought. *She let them go. She held the thorns for them. She's been sending roses through the crack since the spring. She gave them the tower.*

*She chose them. Over us.*

*Over me.*

She walked a little faster.

The chain went tight. Rhosyn stumbled. Fell. Got up again. Said nothing.

Ysenne didn't look back.

---

The kept were walking the other way.

She passed them on the road. Hundreds. Then thousands. Gold. In the grey-gold dim. Coming toward her out of the west and going past her east, on both sides, slow, so slow, a finger's width at a time.

*Scrape*. *Scrape*. *Scrape*.

Oz's household. The household of the Gilded King, kept in gold on the night of the exile so that none of them would come apart in the grey. Cooks with gold spoons. Grooms with gold bridles. Nursemaids with gold children on their hips. Soldiers with gold spears held upright. Gardeners with gold trowels.

She'd walked past them for nine hundred years too.

Lining the white road. Standing. Their eyes moving. Following you. Saying something you couldn't hear.

They weren't standing now.

They'd turned. All of them. When the edge fell. When the wind came in. And now they were walking. East. Toward the place where the Veil had been. Toward the world.

And humming.

One note. From thousands of gold throats. Low. Worn smooth.

*...home...*

---

"*They'll never get there*," said Ysenne. "*At that pace.*"

"*They've got time*," said Rhosyn. Behind her. On the chain. "*They've got all the time in the world. That's what Oz gave them.*"

"*He gave them safety*."

"*He gave them time*," said her sister. "*Nine hundred years of it. Standing by a road. Watching us walk past.*" A pause. "*I used to talk to them. Did you know? When I was out in the thorns. The ones nearest the tenth tower. A laundress. Two boys from the stables. A man with a gold harp.*" Her voice cracked. "*I don't think they could hear me. But I told them about the roses.*"

Ysenne said nothing.

"*What did you tell them, Ysy*?"

"*Nothing*."

"*In nine hundred years*?"

"*I didn't need to tell them anything*," said the Glass Queen. "*I showed them.*"

And it was true. She had. Every one of them. Every gold face along the white road. She'd stopped, sometimes, on her walks between the palaces, and stood in front of one. A kitchen girl. A soldier. And held up her clear hand, like a mirror, and let them see.

Themselves. Gold. Stopped. Kept. Their own eyes moving in their own gold faces.

Some of them had wept. You couldn't see it—gold doesn't weep—but you could see it in the eyes. That was the thing about the eyes. They still did everything.

She'd thought it was a kindness.

The truth is always a kindness. That was what she'd believed for nine hundred years. The truth is cold and hard and clear and it cuts, but it is never, ever a lie, and a lie is the one unkind thing.

She'd shown them the truth.

She walked a little faster.

---

She thought about the girl.

The first of them. An hour ago, at the edge, before Oz went. Out ahead of all the others, small, in a grey dress too big for her. Gold. With a wooden spoon in her hand.

Walking. Not a finger's width. A step. A real step. Slow and stiff and scraping, but a step, and another, and another, down the white road, east, toward the edge.

*Ilda*, thought the Glass Queen. *The spit-girl. The one the boy made cry.*

She'd watched it. In the summer. Through the glass of the Court. A small dark boy with a cupped hand, kneeling on the white road in front of a gold kitchen girl, making a little room in front of her face. And the girl's gold eyes filling. And one tear, gold, running down a gold cheek.

And a word.

*Mam*.

Nobody had ever made any of them cry. Not Oz. Not Mae. Not her. She'd tried. Once. Nine hundred years ago, in the first year, with a gold page-boy. She'd shown him himself, and shown him himself, and shown him himself, until his eyes went still and wouldn't move anymore.

She hadn't tried again.

And a boy from the world had knelt down on the road with his hands cupped and done it in an afternoon.

---

The girl had walked past her.

Close. A yard off. At the foot of the white road, where the edge had been. And as she passed, Ysenne had turned—she hadn't meant to, she hadn't decided to—and held up her clear hand. Flat. Between them. The way she'd held it up to a thousand gold faces on this road.

*Look*, she'd thought. *Look at yourself. Look at what you are.*

Ilda hadn't looked.

She'd walked past the Glass Queen's raised hand as if it were a branch, or a post, or nothing at all, and stepped off the dust onto the snow, and gone on east toward the lanterns.

Toward a boy with a cupped hand who'd promised he'd come back.

---

Ysenne lifted her hand now. On the white road. Walking. She didn't mean to.

Her own hand. Clear. Glass. With the grey-gold dim showing through it, bent, the way the world bends through a lens. With the white road behind it, and the walking gold, and the dust going up.

Everything showing through it.

Everything but her.

---

"*Ysy*," said Rhosyn. Softly. From the end of the chain.

She lowered her hand.

She hadn't stopped walking. She didn't stop now.

---

The glass palace stood where it had always stood. At the end of the white road. Among the towers. Glass and iron and gold and bone and the tenth tower wrapped in its dead brown thorns.

Hers was the tallest. Of course it was. Oz had built his gold one first, so she'd built hers higher, and he'd laughed and said *you would*, and she'd said *I did*. Spires on spires. Halls of mirrors. Floors you could see through, all the way down. Walls that showed you the hall behind them, and the hall behind that, and the hall behind that, until the eye gave up.

Every surface of it a window.

Every window looking out.

That was the thing about glass. She'd learned it in the first year, alone in her palace with nothing to drink. Glass wasn't a wall. It looked like a wall. People treated it like one. They put it in their windows and thought it kept the world out.

It didn't keep anything out.

It let the light through. And if the light came through, so could she.

---

She chained Rhosyn in the hall of the long mirror.

Not cruelly. She wasn't cruel. She looped the glass chain round the foot of a pillar and left her sister enough slack to lie down, and Rhosyn lay down, at once, on the clear floor, and curled up like a child, and closed her eyes.

"*You'll stay there*," said Ysenne.

"*Where would I go*?"

"*To them*. *Your Harrow boys.*"

Rhosyn opened one green eye.

"*Ysy*," she said. "*I can't stand up.*"

Ysenne looked at her. At the grey face. At the hands, thin as twigs, lying on the glass. At the crown of thorns, brown, dry, not a single green leaf on it.

She went away down the long hall and didn't look back.

---

Orrin was waiting at the end of it.

He was always waiting at the end of things. That was what stewards were for. A tall stooped shape of dust and silk, grey-gold, hunched under a chain of office that had once been silver and was now the colour of everything else. With his long patient face and his hands folded in front of him and his wings—what was left of his wings—folded down his back like an old cloak.

Mae had made him. Fed him on stolen light, on the long road out, so he'd never come apart. He'd been the Gilded King's steward before that. And after Mae went, and after Oz woke, he'd been everyone's.

He bowed.

"*Majesty*."

"*The King has gone into the world*," said Ysenne.

"*I saw, Majesty*."

"*He won't be coming back*."

A pause.

"*No, Majesty*," said Orrin. "*I didn't think he would.*" He lifted his long grey face. His eyes were the colour of old candlelight. "*Did he leave instructions?*"

"*He said, we'll go and see everyone*."

Orrin waited.

"*That's all*," said the Glass Queen.

"*Ah*."

"*So there are no instructions*." She walked past him. Into the next hall. The hall of a thousand panes. "*Except mine.*"

Orrin followed. As he always did. A respectful three steps behind. The dust of him drifting.

"*Yes, Majesty*," he said.

---

The hall of a thousand panes was round.

And tall. And lit from nowhere. Its walls were windows. Hundreds on hundreds of them, small and large, square and round and arched, set into the glass of the walls from the floor to the ceiling a hundred feet overhead. Each one a different shape. Each one a different glass.

She'd made each one herself.

Nine hundred years. Nothing else to do. Nothing to eat or drink or grow or kill. Just glass, and the long grey dim, and the slow discovery of what glass could do.

That every pane is a door. That every still pool is a pane. That every lantern-glass, every mirror, every polished brass plate and dark window at night and spoon held up to a fire is a pane, and every pane is the same pane, if you know how to look.

That if you stand in front of one, you can look out of all of them.

---

She stood in the middle of the round hall and lifted her hands.

And looked.

---

The world came in.

All of it. All at once. Through every pane in the hall, every window in the walls. Not the Court. Not the grey. The *world*.

She'd only ever had a crack before. A finger's width of Seren Harrow's blood carried west by Mae's last moth, and the light through it thin and hard to hold, like looking at a lit room through a keyhole. She'd made it work. She'd stood in the Harrow boys' windows. She'd stood in the water barrel. She'd reached her hand out of a dark pane at night and touched a sleeping child's cheek.

But it had been a keyhole.

Now there was no edge.

Now there was nothing between her glass and theirs.

---

A thousand windows.

Ten thousand. A hundred thousand. Every pane in every house in every town in Caldor. Every lantern-glass on every sill. Every mirror on every wall. Every puddle in every lane. Every pond and trough and bucket and the still black water in every well.

Lit. Every one. It was the night they lit their lanterns. The Lady's night. Every window in the world had a light in it, and every light was looking out, and she was looking in.

At them.

---

A kitchen in a mill-town. A woman at a table with her head in her hands and a lantern in front of her, and a man's coat on the back of the chair across from her that nobody was wearing.

A barracks. Twenty young men round a fire, singing, too loud, out of tune. A lantern on a nail, and a name cut into it.

A nursery. A child asleep. A woman in a chair beside the bed, asleep too, with her hand through the bars.

A village green. A cracked old Stone. A hundred lanterns hung round it on poles and strings, and children running between them, and an old man on a bench with a pipe, watching.

An inn. A hall. A palace. A ship. A tent. A cave on a mountain hung with eleven hundred little lights, every one of them lit, and a tall thin woman with ink on her fingers standing at the mouth of it, alone, looking up at the sky.

---

She looked at all of them.

She'd always been able to see what was in people's faces. Since she was a child. She'd sit at the long mirror in the nursery and look at the room behind her in it, over her shoulder. At her nurse. At her brothers. At Mae doing her hair. At Oz, sprawled on the window seat with his boots on the cushions.

And tell them what they were thinking.

*You're cross because Father didn't come to your fencing.*

*You're sad about the dog.*

*You don't like her, you only say you do.*

They'd hated it. All of them. They'd thrown cushions. Oz had laughed and said *Ysy, you're a monster*. Mae had said *it's rude, darling, people are allowed to have insides*.

She'd never understood that.

Why would anyone want insides nobody could see?

---

She looked at a hundred thousand faces in a hundred thousand windows and saw what was in every one of them.

Grief. Joy. Fear. Love. Hunger. Hope. Boredom. Lust. Rage. The thin bright fear of a mother at a cot. The thick dull ache of a man at a grave. The fizz of children. The hush of the very old. The hard set of a jaw that has decided something. The soft fall of a face that has stopped deciding anything at all.

All of it. Laid out. Like goods on a table.

Every one of them looking out of their window into the dark, at the night, at the lanterns, not knowing she was there.

Every one of them, if they'd only turned round, with something behind them in the glass.

Themselves.

---

She lowered her hands.

The windows went dark.

She stood in the middle of the round hall in the grey dim, and the thousand panes in the walls showed her the hall.

The floor. Clear. The walls. Clear. Orrin, three steps behind her, grey-gold, stooped, with his hands folded. Reflected in every pane. A thousand Orrins.

And in the middle of the hall, where she stood—

Nothing.

The far wall. Through the place where she was. Bent a little, the way things bend through glass.

That was all.

---

It had happened at the pool.

She didn't remember it happening. Nobody did. That was the thing about the Drinking. You knelt at the edge of a black pool in a hidden garden, nine of you, with your crowns on, and cupped your hands, and drank. And it was cold and it was sweet and it was the most beautiful thing you'd ever tasted. Like being *made*. Like being made again, new, forever.

And then you stood up.

And you were what you were going to be. Forever. Fixed. Finished.

And you went in, laughing, all nine of you, to the long hall with the long mirror, and stood in front of it, arm in arm, to see what deathless looked like.

And she'd looked.

And she wasn't there.

---

Oz was there. Huge and laughing, gold already at the edges. Mae was there, tall and grey and luminous, her eyes like lit windows. Cad, square and dark. Rosie, at the end, not laughing, with dirt on her hands. Their four cousins. The empty space where Hes should have been.

And between Mae and Oz, where Ysenne was standing, with their arms through hers—

The wall behind.

*Ysy*, Oz had said. Laughing. Looking down at her. *Ysy, where've you gone?*

And she'd laughed too. Because it was a joke. It was a trick of the light. It would pass.

It hadn't passed.

---

She'd thought, for a while, it was the glass. The mirror. Something wrong with the silver. She'd had it taken down and another put up, and another, and another.

She'd thought it was the pool. Something she'd drunk that the others hadn't. Too much. Too little.

She'd thought, in the long first year, in the grey, that if she made enough glass, if she learned enough about it, if she understood every pane and every surface and every angle of every light, she'd find it. The trick. The flaw. The place where her face had gone.

Nine hundred years.

She knew everything about glass now. She could walk through it. Look out of it. Reach through it. She could stand in a window two hundred miles away and look out of a lantern into a child's nursery.

She'd never found it.

And at some point—she didn't remember when—she'd stopped looking.

She'd started showing other people theirs instead.

---

"*Majesty*," said Orrin. Softly. Three steps behind.

"*What*."

"*You've been standing there a long while*."

She looked at him. In the thousand panes. A thousand old stooped men, patient, grey-gold, with their hands folded.

"*Orrin*," said the Glass Queen. "*Do you know what's in the world?*"

He considered it. He always considered things. It was why Oz had kept him.

"*People, Majesty*," he said. "*A great many people.*"

"*And what else*?"

"*I don't know, Majesty*."

"*A net*," said Ysenne.

---

She walked to the nearest pane.

Not a window. A small square of dark glass, low in the wall, no bigger than her hand. She'd made it in the summer. After the crack. After she'd looked through the keyhole for the first time and seen what was out there.

She knelt in front of it.

And looked.

---

Not at a window. Not at a lantern. Not at a face.

At a *stone*.

Pale. Tall. Clouded, like old ice. Standing on its own on a village green, cracked from top to bottom down the middle, with lanterns hung round it on poles. And inside it, under the cloud, deep, the faintest glimmer.

Silver-blue.

Threads.

---

She'd found them in the summer. Through the keyhole. She hadn't known what they were.

Stones. All over the world. In every village. On every green and every crossroads and every square. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Pale and clouded and humming very faintly with a light that came from inside.

The world called them Lattice Stones.

The world put its children's hands on them when they were sixteen, and the Stones looked at the children, all the way through, and told them what they were.

*Kindling*, they called it.

She'd watched one. In the autumn. Through the crack. In a mill-town in the south. A boy. Thin, nervous, sixteen, in his best coat, with his whole village watching. He'd put his hand on the Stone, and the Stone had lit up from inside, silver-blue, and the boy had gone very still, and then he'd laughed.

Laughed and laughed. And turned round to his mother with his whole face shining.

She'd known what was in his face. She always did.

*It saw me*.

---

*It's glass*, she'd thought. In the autumn. Kneeling at the keyhole. *It's only glass. Very old. Very deep. Very strange. But it lets the light through.*

*So it's a pane*.

*And every pane is a door*.

---

She put her clear hand flat against the small dark square in the wall.

And reached.

Through the glass. Through the grey. Out of the Court and across the Sundered Lands and over the snow and down into a village green two hundred miles away, where a cracked old Stone stood among the Lady's lanterns.

And in.

---

It was cold inside.

Cold and enormous and humming. Like standing in a cathedral made of frost. Like being inside a bell after it's been struck. Threads everywhere. Silver-blue. Running away from her in every direction, fine as spider-silk, crossing and recrossing, knotting and parting, millions on millions of them. Each one humming its own small note.

Each one a *person*.

She could feel them. Every thread. A name on it. A class. A level. A life. The whole world, written out in light, strung across the dark like dew on a web at dawn.

She'd never seen anything so beautiful.

She'd never seen anything so *clear*.

---

*Oh*, thought Ysenne of the Glass.

*Oh, it's a mirror*.

*It's a mirror of everyone*.

---

That was what the net was. She saw it all at once, the way you see the shape of a room when someone lights a lamp. Not a cage. Not a lid. Not a gift. Not any of the things they called it in the world.

A mirror.

The greatest mirror ever made. Held up to every living soul. Showing each one what they were. Their name. Their class. Their level. The shape of them. Written in light, for them to see.

*It saw me*.

---

And at the middle of it—

She felt it. Far off. Deep down. At the bottom of the world, under the grey river, under the drowned country, where every thread came in and crossed and met.

A centre.

Warm. Gold. Like the hearth at the heart of a house.

And empty.

Nobody there. No hand on the threads. No one holding the mirror. Just a place where someone used to be, and the threads running into it and out of it, humming, untended, the way a harp hums in a room where nobody plays.

The crown in the road.

Cad had told them about it. In the hall. When they woke. Before he went down the thread. *The Root of the net is empty, Oz. The smith gave it away. It's lying there. Whoever turns the Key, the net offers them all of it.*

And Oz had said *the child first*. And Cad had said *the Key first*. And they'd argued, the way they always argued, and Cad had gone down the thread, and the Key had been turned by an old man in a crown that said *no*, and Cad had fallen with the thread into the dark, nodding.

And nobody had picked it up.

It was still lying there.

---

*If I held it*, thought the Glass Queen. Inside the Stone. In the frost and the humming. *If I held the mirror. If I were the centre. If every thread in the world ran through my hands*—

*Then it would see me*.

*Every thread. Every soul. Every name. Every one of them would look through the mirror and see—*

*Me*.

---

She reached for it.

Down. Along the threads. Toward the warm gold empty centre at the bottom of the world. Her clear hand going through the frost, through the humming, through the millions of fine bright lines, the way it went through any glass, easily, coldly, like a knife through water—

And the net turned her away.

---

Not hard. Not angry. Nets don't feel things. It just—turned. The way a door turns on its hinges when you push the wrong side. The way a lock turns a wrong key. Her hand slid off the centre like rain off a window.

And something came up the threads to meet her.

Not words. Not a voice. A *shape*. Gold. Very old. Stamped into the warm empty place at the middle of the net, the way a maker's mark is stamped into a horseshoe.

A name.

*VEY*.

---

She pulled her hand back.

Out of the Stone. Out of the frost. Back across the snow and the Sundered Lands and the grey, into the round hall of a thousand panes, where she was kneeling on the clear floor in front of a small dark square of glass with her hand pressed flat against it.

She took her hand away.

It was shaking. She looked at it. She'd never seen it shake. Nine hundred years.

"*Majesty*?" said Orrin.

---

*Vey*.

She knew the name. Everyone in the Court knew the name. Aurelian Vey, who wove the net that cast them out. The Listener with the little hammer, who stood on the shingle while the boat came and wouldn't look at them.

His blood was in the centre. Of course it was. He'd built it. He'd put himself in it like a stone in the foundation of a house. And the house remembered. Nine hundred years later, the house still opened only to his blood.

*The smith gave it away*, Cad had said.

The smith. The tall one with the burned arms and the grey eyes and Mae's handprint on his cheek. Lex Vey. Aurelian's blood, and Arion's.

And Hes's.

And the warden. His brother. With the scarred hands and the patience.

And—

---

Ysenne stood up.

She walked across the round hall. Past Orrin. Past a thousand reflected Orrins. To a pane she knew. High on the wall. Round, like a porthole. Clear.

She looked into it.

---

A valley. At the end of the world. Snow on the ground. A birch wood. A stream. A small stone cottage with one storey and an oak ramp and a blue door.

And in the window, on the west side—

A lantern. Horn-paned. A crooked hinge. Lit.

She couldn't see through horn. Horn doesn't let the light through clean; it scatters it, it softens it, it turns it to a glow with no shape in it. She'd learned that in the summer, at that very cottage, when the smith hung horn in every window and she couldn't find a way in.

But the stream outside was clear.

And still, at the edges, where it ran under the ice.

And from the stream she could see the window.

And through the window, past the horn lantern, in the firelight—

---

A man asleep in a chair by the hearth. Big. Burned arms. Grey-gold handprint on his cheek. Asleep with his mouth open.

And on his chest, asleep too, with one square fist closed round the collar of his shirt and the other round a nail on a ribbon at her own neck—

A child.

A year old. A fluff of dark hair standing up like a bird's crest. Round face. Small. New.

*New*.

---

Oz had wanted her.

From the very first night. Standing in the hall under the split cocoons with Mae's crown in his hand. *Bring me the smith's child.* Because she was new, and the Crowned can never make anything new, not ever again, because they drank the Well that new things come in by.

He'd wanted to keep her. Gold. Forever. Safe. His heir.

He'd gone to the cottage with the blue door. He'd sat on the floor by the fire and drunk tea from a lopsided cup. And the child had looked at him and said *no*.

And he'd come away with the cup instead.

---

Ysenne looked at the child through the window, through the stream, through the ice, two hundred miles away.

At the fist round her father's collar. At the other fist round the nail.

*Vey*, she thought.

*Vey on her father's side. Harrow on her grandmother's. Crowned, all the way back, to Hes.*

*And new*.

*Nobody's ever been all three*.

---

The child stirred.

In her sleep. On her father's chest. Turned her head. And opened her eyes. Dark grey. Going green at the edges. Like a field going from frost to grass.

And looked at the window.

At the horn lantern. At the stream beyond it. At the ice. At the still clear water under the ice, where, two hundred miles away, through a round pane high in a glass wall, a woman with no reflection was looking back.

Ysenne went very still.

The child looked at her.

Not past her. Not through her. *At* her. Steadily. With the frank enormous interest of the very small, who have not yet learned what they are not supposed to see.

As if there were something there.

---

Then she yawned. And put her fist in her mouth. And closed her eyes again. And went back to sleep on her father's chest.

---

Ysenne stood at the round pane in the high glass wall for a long time.

Behind her, Orrin waited. Three steps back. His hands folded.

In the hall of the long mirror, at the foot of a pillar, her sister slept on the clear floor with her crown of thorns crooked and a chain of glass round her thin grey wrists.

In the world, the wind went on coming in.

---

"*Orrin*," said the Glass Queen.

"*Majesty*."

"*How long does a child take to grow?*"

He considered it.

"*Some years, Majesty*," he said. "*I believe. It's been a long time since I had any to do with them.*"

"*Too long*," said Ysenne.

She turned away from the window.

"*Majesty*?"

"*The net opens to the blood*," said the Glass Queen. "*To his blood. Aurelian's. The house remembers its builder.*" She walked past him. Out of the round hall. Into the long one. "*So I'll need a Vey.*"

"*The smith, Majesty*? *The warden?*"

"*They'd never*," said Ysenne. "*Not freely. Not for me. And they're very strong now, Orrin. Too strong to make.*"

She stopped at the door.

Looked back. At the round pane, high on the glass wall, with the valley in it and the stream and the window and the sleeping child.

"*No*," she said. "*I'll need a small one.*"

---

Down the long hall, in the dim, Rhosyn opened her eyes.

She didn't move. She lay on the clear floor at the foot of her pillar, in her chains, grey as ash, and listened to her sister's footsteps going away through the glass.

And in the dead brown thorns of her crown, very faint, so small that nobody would have seen it unless they'd been looking—

A bud.

Green.
