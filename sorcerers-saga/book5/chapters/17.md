# What the King Kept

Oszric of the Crowned had never looked at them.

He knew that now. He'd known it since the night the edge came down, standing in the snow with a cup in his hand, watching a spit-girl walk past him into the world. *I hadn't looked at any of them in nine hundred years. I'd walked past them every day and never once looked.*

So he looked now.

---

They were everywhere.

All across the west of the smith's kingdom. In the fields. In the woods. On the roads. In the middle of villages, in the middle of farmyards, in the middle of a mill-pond once, up to his gold waist in the water with ducks swimming round him. Walking. East. A finger's width at a time.

His household.

He went among them.

Slowly. Carefully. Putting his feet down in the slush so as not to gild more than he had to. Through the fields and the woods and the villages, in the last of the winter, with the cup in his hand. Stopping by each one. Standing in front of them.

And *looking*.

---

A groom. Tall. Stooped. With a gold bridle in his hand. Walking across a ploughed field toward a hedge.

Oszric stopped in front of him.

He didn't know his name. He'd had four hundred grooms. Five hundred. He'd never known any of their names. They'd held his horse and he'd got on it.

The groom's eyes moved. Up. To him.

And held.

Oszric stood in the ploughed field and looked into the gold eyes of a man who had held his horse nine hundred years ago, and been kept in gold on the night of the boat, and stood by a white road in a grey country with no sun for nine hundred years watching his king walk past.

He didn't know what was in the eyes.

He'd never known what was in anyone's eyes. That was Ysy. Ysy always knew. Ysy could look at a face and tell you everything in it, laid out like goods on a table.

He couldn't.

He looked anyway.

And after a while he thought—he wasn't sure—he thought it might be *tired*.

---

A nursemaid. With a gold child on her hip. Three or four. A boy. Both of them kept. The boy's gold fist in the nursemaid's gold hair.

He knew this one.

Not her name. Her face. She'd been nurse to his cousins' children. To Merrow's little brother. He remembered her in the nursery corridors, scolding, laughing, with her sleeves rolled up.

He stood in front of her for a long time.

The child's eyes moved. To him. Wide. Gold. Three years old for nine hundred years.

He had to look away.

---

A cook. A gardener. Two boys from the kennels, holding hands. An old man with a gold stick, so slow he'd barely moved since the edge came down—a mile, maybe, in three months. A soldier with a gold spear held upright. A laundress with a gold basket.

A man with a gold harp.

He stopped longest in front of that one.

He remembered the harp. He remembered the man. Leolin. The only one of them whose name he knew. Leolin had played in the long hall every night for twenty years. Before the pool. Before the boat. He'd played at Oszric's naming-day and his father's funeral and Mae's wedding.

He'd played on the boat.

All the way out. Across the grey water. In chains, like the rest of them. With the Ferryman in the stern and the shore going away. Leolin had sat in the bottom of the boat with his harp in his lap and played. Very quietly. A song Oszric didn't know. For the whole crossing.

And on the far shore, when the grey country closed round them, Oszric had put his hand on Leolin's shoulder. To keep him. So he'd never come apart.

The harp had gone gold first. Then Leolin.

He'd never played again.

---

Oszric stood in front of him in a muddy lane in the smith's kingdom with the rain coming down and looked at the gold harp.

The strings were gold. All of them. Stopped. Mid-note, maybe. Mid-chord. Leolin's gold fingers on them.

Leolin's eyes moved.

Up. To him.

And Oszric heard it. Very faint. Under the rain. Under the hum—*home*—that came from every one of them.

Not a song. Not a note.

The *shape* of one. The shape of the song Leolin had been playing on the boat when the gold came up his arm. Hanging there. In the gold strings. Nine hundred years. Never finished.

---

He walked on.

He didn't know what else to do.

---

He went to Greyfall.

He hadn't meant to. His feet took him. West. Along the old road. Through the line of the smith's lanterns—small and gold, each in its own circle of real winter—and into the grey. Past the silent mill. Past the empty farms. Past the stakes crowding closer and closer together.

To the crag above the old edge. To the outpost. To the gate.

---

The man was still standing in it.

Gold. In the gateway. Square in the middle. Feet planted. A staff held crosswise in both hands, chest-high, barring the way.

There was a lantern at his feet. Small. Iron. Lit. In a circle of white snow on the grey stone.

Oszric stopped in front of him.

---

*You're not coming through this gate*.

He'd said that. In the summer. When Oszric came up the road from the crack in the Veil. One man. Alone. With a staff. In front of the Gilded King and the whole gold storm of him. Everyone else gone east. Sent. *Somebody has to stand somewhere*.

And Oszric had thought it was beautiful.

He'd stood in front of this man in the summer and thought *you should never stop. You should stand there forever. Exactly like that.*

And reached out. Very gently. And touched his face.

---

The man's eyes moved.

Up. To him.

Grey. Not gold—his eyes had been grey, Oszric remembered, grey and hard, and they were gold now like the rest of him, but they'd been grey. And they looked at Oszric now the way they'd looked at him in the summer.

*You're not coming through this gate*.

They hadn't changed. Nine months. Kept. Stopped. Standing in a gateway with a staff. And they hadn't changed at all.

---

"*Roole*," said Oszric.

He'd learned the name. From the Knight. The one with the sword, who came every few days and brushed the snow off the man's shoulders and talked to him. *Evening, Commander*. *Line moved a mile today*. *I'll be back on Thursday.*

"*Daxian Roole*," said Oszric. "*Commander of the Keepers of the Stones.*"

The grey-gold eyes held him.

"*I'm sorry*," said Oszric.

---

He hadn't known he was going to say it. It came out of him the way things came out of him now. Since the old woman's cottage. Since the step. Since *there, see, not so bad*.

"*I thought it was beautiful*," he said. "*You. Standing here. In the summer. With your staff. One man. In front of me.*" His voice was very low. "*I thought, he should stand there forever. Like that. Exactly like that. So no one ever has to see him fall.*"

The eyes didn't move.

"*So I kept you*," said the Gilded King. "*Because I thought it was beautiful.*"

---

He stood there in the grey.

In front of the gate. In front of the gold man with the staff. With the little iron lantern burning at the gold man's feet in its circle of real snow, and the wind going past, west, endless.

"*Do you want to come out*?" said Oszric.

---

The eyes moved.

Not up. Not away. They moved *in*. Narrowed. The way a man's eyes narrow when he's listening very hard.

Then they moved. Down. To the staff. Crosswise. In his own gold hands. Barring the gate.

Then back up. To Oszric.

And Oszric knew.

He didn't have Ysy's eyes. He couldn't read faces. He'd never been able to. But he knew this one. He'd seen it in the summer. On the road. In the gate. A man who had decided something and was not going to undecide it.

*Yes*.

*Yes, I want to come out*.

*And I'm still not letting you through this gate*.

---

Oszric laughed.

It came out of him cracked. Wet. Astonished. The way the old woman had laughed on her step. He stood in the grey in front of Greyfall gate and laughed until there were gold tears on his face.

"*All right*," he said. "*All right. Commander.*"

He held out his hand.

Not to touch. He'd learned that. An inch off. Over the gold hands on the staff. Palm down.

And he tried to let go.

---

He'd never tried.

That was the thing. In nine hundred years. He'd put his hand on his household on the night of the boat and *held*, and he'd never once tried to open it. Not for a moment. Not for one of them.

He didn't know how.

He stood with his hand over Roole's and thought *let go*. *Just this one. Just him. Open your hand. Let him out.*

And he felt for it.

The keeping. The hold. The thing he'd drunk at the pool, nine hundred years ago, cold and sweet and the most beautiful thing he'd ever tasted. The thing that came out of his hands into everything he touched and *stopped* it. Kept it. Forever.

He found it.

It was in his hand. In his chest. In the middle of him. A grip. Huge. Steady. Closed. Like a fist that had been closed so long the fingers had forgotten they could open.

He tried to open it.

Just a little. Just one finger. Just for this one man in this one gate.

---

It moved.

He felt it move. The grip. Loosen. A hair. A breath.

And he felt Roole—

He felt something in the gold under his hand *stir*. The way a sleeper stirs. A breath going in that hadn't gone in for nine months. A heartbeat. One. Slow. Like a drum in a far valley.

*Yes*.

---

And then he felt the others.

---

All of them. At once. Everywhere.

Because it wasn't nine thousand grips. It never had been. He'd known that, on the green, with the smith. *It's one hold. I put it on them the night of the boat, all of them, at once, and I've never let go.*

He'd known it.

He hadn't *felt* it.

He felt it now.

---

Nine thousand people. Across the smith's kingdom. In fields and woods and lanes and farmyards and a mill-pond. A groom with a bridle. A nursemaid with a child on her hip. Two boys holding hands. An old man with a stick. A soldier. A laundress.

Leolin. With his harp.

All of them. *Stirring*. As the grip loosened. A hair. A breath.

And under the stirring—

*Coming apart*.

---

He felt it start.

In all of them. At once. The way a frost-cracked wall starts to crumble when the thaw comes. A loosening. A softening. At the edges. Their gold going—not to flesh. To *dust*. Nine hundred years. Nine hundred years of time they hadn't lived, waiting behind the gold like water behind a dam. And the dam cracking.

The nursemaid. The child on her hip. He felt the child—*three years old*, *three years old for nine hundred years*—felt the gold of the small fist in the nursemaid's hair go soft. Grey at the edges. Like ash. Like the dust in the halls in the first year. Like the cousins at the edge.

Leolin's fingers on the harp-strings. Going grey.

---

He closed his hand.

---

He didn't decide to. It happened. The way you snatch your hand back from a fire. The grip slammed shut. All the way. Harder than before. *Holding*. With everything he had. Every one of them. All nine thousand. Every grain of gold in every one of them. *Stop*. *Stay*. *Don't*.

The stirring stopped.

The grey went out of the edges.

The child's fist in the nursemaid's hair went back to gold. Hard. Bright. Kept.

---

And Roole—

The heartbeat. The one slow drum in a far valley. The breath going in.

Stopped.

---

Oszric stood in the grey in front of Greyfall gate with his hand out over the gold man's hands and his whole body shaking.

He couldn't breathe.

He'd felt them. All of them. Coming apart. Starting to. For a breath. For less. In fields and woods and lanes across the world. Because he'd tried to let one man out of a gate.

*It's one hold*.

*If I open my hand, I open it all the way*.

---

Roole's eyes were on him.

Grey-gold. Steady. Hard.

They'd felt it too. Oszric saw that. The heartbeat. The breath. *Yes*. And then the grip slamming shut again, and the breath stopping, and the gold closing back over everything.

The eyes didn't blame him.

That was the worst of it. Oszric looked into them and saw—he didn't have Ysy's eyes, he'd never been able to read a face, but he saw this—he saw that Daxian Roole, Commander of the Keepers, standing in a gate for nine months with a staff, didn't blame him at all.

He just looked.

The way you'd look at a man who'd tried to lift something too heavy for him and dropped it.

---

"*I can't*," said Oszric.

His voice cracked. All the way through.

"*I can't let you go*. *Not just you. Not one.*" He was shaking. "*It's all of them. If I let you out, I let them all out. And they'd come apart. All of them. Nine hundred years. In front of me.*" He looked at his own hand. Gold. Enormous. Shaking. "*Leolin. The nursemaid. The child. All of them. At once.*"

The eyes held him.

"*And me*," said Oszric. Very quietly. "*It's me too. It's what I drank. It's what I am. If I opened my hand all the way—*"

He didn't finish.

He didn't need to.

---

He stood there for a long time.

In the grey. In front of the gate. With the wind going past, west, endless. With the little iron lantern at Roole's feet in its circle of real snow.

*Kept's the oven*, the old woman had said. *Finished is the cake.*

He'd thought he understood. In the cottage. Holding her hand. Watching her go. *She was finished. Like a cake.*

He hadn't understood.

He understood now.

To finish Roole, he'd have to finish all of them. Every one. Leolin and the nursemaid and the child and the two boys holding hands and the old man with the stick. Nine thousand cakes he'd left in the oven for nine hundred years.

And himself.

---

He walked away from Greyfall at dusk.

He didn't look back.

He wanted to. He didn't.

---

Ysy came that night.

---

He was sitting on a hill above the old road. In the grey. Where nobody would come. With his back against a gold rock that had been a grey one an hour ago, and the cup in his lap.

He looked into it.

He didn't know why. He always looked into it, now. At night. In the dark. The lopsided white cup the hedge-witch had made, with the big smith's wrong handle and her own thumb-print in the rim. Gold. All of it. And the tea in it, a gold disc. Flat. Still. Polished.

He looked into it the way you'd look into a fire. Not for anything. Just to look.

And she was there.

---

"*Oz*," said his sister.

Her face. In the gold. Small. Clear. Like a face seen through ice. With her white hair and her narrow crown of glass and her ice-grey eyes.

"*Ysy*."

"*You went to the gate*." Her voice was cold. Clear. Like a bell under ice. "*At Greyfall. You tried to let him out.*"

He didn't answer.

"*I felt it*," said Ysenne. "*All the way down here. In the net. Every one of them. Your whole household. Starting to come apart.*" A pause. "*You nearly killed them, Oz. All of them. For one Keeper in a gate.*"

"*I know*."

"*Why*?"

He didn't answer.

---

"*It's Rosie*," said Ysenne. "*Isn't it. She's put ideas in your head.*" The ice-grey eyes narrowed. "*She gave it back. Did you know? At the pool. In the smith's green field. She knelt down and opened her hand and poured it all back. Nine hundred years.*" Her voice went very cold. "*And now she's a woman of forty with dirt on her hands, eating bread in a hedge-witch's cottage, getting older every day.*"

"*I know*," said Oszric. "*I felt that too.*"

"*She's going to die, Oz*."

"*I know*."

"*In thirty years. Forty. In a bed. By a window.*" The face in the cup was very still. "*Like the old woman you sat with. Like the smith's father.*" A pause. "*Rosie. Our Rosie. She's going to go through the door.*"

He didn't say anything.

He looked at his sister's face in the cup and didn't say anything at all.

---

"*Bring me the child*," said Ysenne.

---

He'd known she'd say it. She'd said it before. Twice. On the green, the first time, in the dark. Then again, last night, by the stream at the bottom of the hedge-witch's yard, in the rain.

*Bring me the child*.

"*I'm nearly there*," said the Glass Queen. "*The middle. The crown in the road. I can feel it. Down the threads. Warm. Gold. Empty.*" Her voice was very soft now. "*A few weeks. That's all. And then I'll be standing in front of it.*"

"*And it won't let you in*."

"*No*. *Not without a Vey hand.*" The ice-grey eyes held his. "*So bring me one. A small one. The smallest. She won't even know.*"

"*She'd know*," said Oszric.

"*She's a baby*."

"*She said no to me*," said the Gilded King. "*In the autumn. On the floor of her grandmother's cottage. I held out my hand and she took her fist out of her mouth and said no.*"

"*She says no to everything, Oz. She's one.*"

"*She said it to me*."

---

Ysenne was quiet.

Then her face in the cup changed. Softened. The ice going out of it a little. The way it used to, when they were children, in the nursery, at the long mirror, when she'd stopped being clever and was just his little sister.

"*Oz*," she said. "*Listen to me. Please.*"

He listened.

"*If I hold the net*," said Ysenne, "*I can keep everyone. Do you understand? Not just your household. Everyone. Every thread. Every person in the world.*" Her voice was very gentle. "*Nobody would ever have to go through the door again. Not old women. Not old men in beds by windows. Not Rosie.*"

He didn't move.

"*Not you*," said his sister. "*Not your household. Not one of them would ever have to come apart. Leolin. The nursemaid. The child on her hip. You'd never have to open your hand. Not ever.*" A pause. "*I'd hold it for you. Forever. With the net.*"

"*Like gold*," said Oszric.

"*Like glass*," said Ysenne. "*Clear. True. Everyone exactly what they are. Forever. Nothing lost. Nothing finished.*" Her ice-grey eyes were very bright. "*No more doors, Oz. Not for anyone. Not ever. You'd never have to stand outside one again.*"

---

He looked at her face in the cup.

He thought about his father. In the room at the end of the corridor. For a month. With Cad holding his hand. And Oszric outside the door. Every night. With his hand on the latch.

He thought about the old woman. On her pillows. *Kept's the oven. Finished is the cake.*

He thought about the smith's father. On three pillows. By a boarded window. With his wife holding one hand and his son the other. *You should have gone in. He'd have liked you there.*

He thought about Hes.

In a garden door. In the dark. All night. *In case one of you wanted to come out*.

---

"*I'll think about it*," he said.

---

His sister's face went still.

Not cold. *Still*. The way glass goes still. The way water goes still in a pond when the wind drops.

"*You said that last night*," said Ysenne.

"*I know*."

"*And the night before*."

"*I know*."

"*I haven't got long, Oz*," said the Glass Queen. "*A few weeks. And then I'll be standing in front of the crown in the road with no hand to pick it up.*" A pause. "*And if you won't bring her—*"

She stopped.

He waited.

"*Think quickly*," said his sister.

And was gone.

---

He sat on the hill in the dark for a long time.

With the cup in his lap. With the gold disc of tea in it, flat, still, polished, showing nothing now. Just the dark sky. Just his own great gold face, looking down.

*If you won't bring her—*

He knew what came after. He'd known Ysy for nine hundred years. Nine hundred and nineteen. Since she was born, red-faced and screaming, in the queen's bedchamber, and the nurse had held her up and said *a girl, a girl, look at her, isn't she pretty*. And Oz, four years old, had looked and thought *no, she's red and cross and her face is all squashed*.

And then she'd opened her eyes. Grey. Ice-grey. And looked at him. And he'd thought *oh*.

*If you won't bring her, I'll fetch her myself*.

---

He got up.

He walked.

East. Through the grey. Through the lanterns. Into the world. Through the night and the next day and the next. Along the old road. Up the Vale. Through the villages, where people stood in their doors and watched him go by and pulled their children in. Through Ashbourne, past the green with its Stone wrapped in sacking and chains, glowing white through the canvas. Past an old woman's garden with an apple tree's stump by the door, and petals still falling from it, white, onto the gold path.

Up the Vale road. Into the birch wood. Up his own gold footprints.

To the hedge-witch's cottage.

---

He stopped at the bottom of the yard.

By the stream. Where the birches ended. In the rain. At dusk.

He could see it all. The cottage with the blue door and the oak ramp and the horn lantern in its west window. The other cottage, across the stream, with a horn lantern in its east window—no. The east window was dark. He looked again. Dark. Empty. Something had been there, and was gone.

The grave by the stream. The grey stone. Twenty lanterns round it. A black soft thing that had been a turnip. Two roses. Red. Growing.

And on the ramp of the blue-door cottage, in the doorway, in the light from inside—

The smith's wife. With her bow. Looking at him.

---

He didn't come closer.

He stood at the bottom of the yard in the rain with the cup in his hand and called across to her. Not loud. He didn't need to be loud. He'd never needed to be loud.

"*Tell the smith*," he said. "*She came again. Last night. In the cup.*"

The smith's wife didn't move.

He told her. All of it. *If I bring her the child, the net will keep everyone. Forever. No doors at all.*

"*Tell him I'm thinking about it*," said the Gilded King.

And the smith's wife looked at him across the yard for a long time. With her bow in her hand and her face white. And then she turned and went inside. And he heard her voice, low, fast. And the other woman's. And then a door at the back. And footsteps, running, away down the track through the birch wood toward the Ashbourne road.

Going to fetch the smith.

---

He stood there all night.

He didn't sit. He didn't move. He stood at the bottom of the yard by the stream in the rain with the cup in his hand and watched the cottage.

The smith came at midnight. On foot. Running. Up the track. He stopped in the yard. Between Oszric and the door. With his hands at his sides.

He didn't say anything.

Neither did Oszric.

They stood there. The two of them. In the rain. Across the yard. All night.

---

At dawn, the door opened.

---

Not the smith's door. The other one. The blue one. On the ramp. In the grey light.

And down the ramp, very fast, in a nightshirt so long it trailed behind her like a train, falling forward and catching herself and falling forward and catching herself with her arms out and her square fists clenched and a look of fierce concentration on her round face—

The child.

---

Oszric went very still.

So did the smith.

So did the hedge-witch, in the doorway behind her, with her hand out, too late. So did the grey-haired woman beside her, who had been Rosie, and was a woman of forty now, with dirt on her hands. So did the smith's wife, on the ramp of the other cottage, with her bow coming up.

Nobody moved.

The child came down the ramp. *Thump*. *Thump*. Into the yard. Into the mud. Across it.

Toward him.

---

He didn't move.

He couldn't. He stood at the bottom of the yard with the cup in his hands and watched her come. Small. Square. Fourteen months. With her fluff of dark hair standing up like a bird's crest and her nightshirt dragging in the mud and her grey-green eyes fixed on him.

*Don't*, he thought. *Don't. Don't come close. Don't touch me. I'll keep you. I won't mean to. I'll keep you and you'll stand in this yard forever in your nightshirt with your eyes moving.*

He put his hands behind his back.

Both of them. With the cup. Behind him. Out of reach. And held them there. So tight his gold shoulders ached.

---

The smith hadn't moved.

Oszric saw him. A yard to the side. Every muscle in his body locked. His face white. His hands open. Ready. *Ready*. To throw himself between them.

He didn't.

He stood there. Shaking. And let her come.

Oszric didn't understand. Then he did. *He's letting her*. *He's terrified, and he's letting her.*

---

The child stopped.

A yard off. In the mud. In front of him. Swaying a little, the way she did, catching her balance.

She looked up at him.

All the way up. Her head tipping back and back. At the biggest thing she'd ever seen. Gold. Glowing. Taller than the birches, nearly. With his hands behind his back.

She looked at him for a long time.

Then she looked past him.

At his hands. Behind his back. Where the cup was.

---

She couldn't have seen it. He was holding it behind him. Out of sight.

She looked at it anyway.

Her eyes went to it the way they'd gone to it in the autumn. On the floor of the hedge-witch's cottage. Past his outstretched hand. To the cup.

And her face changed.

---

Oszric felt it before he understood it.

The cup. In his hands. Behind his back. Going cold.

Not the cold of gold in the rain. A different cold. A *clear* cold. Like ice on a pond. Like frost on a window.

*Ysy*.

She was there. In the cup. In the gold disc of tea. He could feel her. Looking out. Out of the cup. Past his hands. Past his back.

At the child.

---

And the child looked back.

Straight at it. At the cup. At the gold. At whatever was in it. With her grey-green eyes wide and steady and her mouth set in a line.

The way she'd looked at the stream, Ysy said, on the night the edge fell. Through the ice. Straight at her.

*As if there were something there*.

---

The child took her fist out of her mouth.

"*No*," said Lark Vey.

---

She said it to the cup.

Not to him. He felt it. She said it *past* him. Through him. To the thing in the gold behind his back.

Loud. Clear. Furious. With her whole small body. With her square fists clenched at her sides and her face red and her feet planted in the mud of the yard like a smith at an anvil.

"*No*."

---

The cold in the cup—

*Flinched*.

He felt it. Felt it go through the gold in his hands. A shiver. A start. Like someone who's been caught looking through a keyhole. Like a face at a window jerking back.

And then nothing.

The cup was just gold. Warm, a little, from his hands. Gold tea in it. Still.

She'd gone.

---

He stood there.

In the mud. In the dawn. With his hands behind his back.

The child looked up at him again. At his face. Her own face had gone back to round and calm and serious, the way it always was. As if she'd just put a goat out of the kitchen.

Then she turned round. And went back across the yard. *Thump*. *Thump*. Toward the hedge-witch, who had come down the ramp now, white-faced, with her arms out.

The child walked into them.

And put her fist back in her mouth.

---

Oszric brought his hands round from behind his back.

Slowly. With the cup in them.

He looked into it.

Gold. Flat. Still. Polished. His own face looking back. Great and gold and wet.

And under it, faint—so faint he almost didn't see it—

A crack.

---

Thin. A hair. Across the gold disc of tea. From one side of the cup to the other.

Gold doesn't crack. Gold bends. Gold dents. Gold never cracks.

It had cracked.

---

He looked at it for a long time.

At the crack in the gold. At the place where his sister's face had been, and a child had looked at it, and said no.

He thought about the old woman. *There, see, not so bad*.

He thought about Hes. In the door. All night.

He thought about Roole. In the gate. With his staff. *Yes, I want to come out. And I'm still not letting you through.*

He looked up.

The smith was looking at him. Across the yard. White-faced. Shaking. With his hands open at his sides.

---

"*Ysy*," said Oszric of the Crowned.

He said it to the cup. Quietly. To the crack. To wherever she'd gone. Down the threads. Into the net.

"*No*."

---

It was very small.

One word. In the rain. At dawn. At the bottom of a hedge-witch's yard.

He'd never said it to her. Not once. Not in nine hundred and nineteen years. Not when she was four and wanted his pony. Not when she was nine and wanted him to tell her she was pretty. Not when she was nineteen and knelt beside him at the pool and said *drink, Oz, drink, it's the most beautiful thing, drink with me*.

He'd never said it to anyone.

He'd never had to. He was the King. He was the eldest. Gold for the eldest. Everyone else said yes.

He looked at the crack in the cup.

"*No*," he said again. Louder. To the smith. Across the yard. "*I won't bring her. Tell your wife. Tell your mother. I won't.*"

---

The smith didn't say anything.

He stood in the mud with his hands open and looked at him. And Oszric saw his face change. Slowly. The way metal changes when it comes out of the fire and starts to cool. White to red to grey.

Not relief.

Something else. Something Oszric didn't have a word for.

He thought, later, the word might have been *thank you*.

---

He turned to go.

And stopped.

And looked back. At the smith. At the cottage. At the child in the hedge-witch's arms. At the woman beside them with green eyes and dirt on her hands and grey in her hair.

At Rosie.

She was looking at him. Across the yard. Very steadily.

"*Oz*," she said.

"*Rosie*."

"*That's the first time*," said his sister. "*Isn't it? You've never said no to anyone.*"

"*No*," said the Gilded King.

Then he heard himself. And laughed. Cracked. Wet. Astonished.

"*No*," he said again. "*No, I haven't.*"

His sister smiled at him. Brown. Weathered. Tired. Forty. With roses in her hair.

"*Hes would be so proud*," said Rhosyn.

---

He walked away down the gold path through the birch wood.

He didn't look back.

But as he went—as he came down through the white trunks of the birches toward the Ashbourne road—he heard something behind him. Very faint. Very far. Off-key. From under the smith's shirt, across the yard, against his breastbone, on a cord.

*Ting*.

He stopped.

He stood on the gold path in the birch wood and listened.

*Ting*.

It rings when someone's sad, the smith had said.

Oszric of the Crowned stood very still. And listened to the bell. And put his hand flat against his own chest. Over Mae's crown. Over the place where his heart was.

And understood, for the first time in nine hundred years, who it was ringing for.

It wasn't him.

It was her.

It was Ysy.
