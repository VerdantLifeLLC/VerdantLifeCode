# Ninety-Seven Years

The King was sitting on the step.

Lex saw him from the air. In the first grey light. As Ashka came down over the Vale and the elms and the chapel roof, over the green with its cracked Stone and its gold circle in the snow, over the row of cottages facing the pond.

Old Mag's garden. The low wall. The gate. The path. The apple tree, blooming. White on white.

And on the step—the one step, worn hollow in the middle by a hundred years of boots—a gold man. Enormous. Sitting with his back to the door and his knees drawn up and his arms wrapped round them. Like a child on a doorstep waiting to be let in.

Glowing.

The step under him was gold. The path in front of him was gold, a long bright stripe of it from the gate to the door where he'd walked it every day for two weeks. The snow round him was gold in a circle. And on the gold, scattered, white, pink at the edges—

Petals.

---

The bell under Lex's shirt was still ringing.

It had rung all night. All the way from the coast. Four hundred miles of dark and cold and stars, flat on Ashka's back with his brother in front of him and his wet clothes freezing to his skin, and the bell ringing the whole way. Soft. Steady. Off-key.

*Ting*. *Ting*. *Ting*.

He'd known who it was ringing for before they were halfway.

---

Ashka came down on the green.

The village was awake. It had been awake all night, Lex thought. Lights in every window. People in doorways. Benno at the door of the Crooked Kettle with a lantern. Nobody on the green. Nobody near the garden. They'd learned that in two weeks.

But they were watching.

Lex slid down off the dragon's back and nearly fell. His legs had gone stiff in the cold. Antho caught his arm.

"*Go*," said his brother.

"*You're not coming*?"

"*She asked for you*." Antho let go of his arm. "*And him. Not me.*" He looked across the green. At the garden. At the gold man on the step. "*I'll be here.*"

Lex went.

---

Tilda met him at the gate.

Square-built, soot-faced, twenty years old, with Brannoc's old hammer in her belt and her gold hand tucked into her apron and her eyes red. She'd been Old Mag's neighbour all her life. She'd carried her water and split her wood and pulled her cart on Lantern Night with the brass lantern in it, *for Arion Vey, from Mag, who was ninety-six and never greedy*.

"*She's awake*," said Tilda. "*She's been awake all night. Asking for you.*" She wiped her nose on her sleeve. "*Her feet went cold on Tuesday. On her birthday. She said, that's it, then, that's the sign. And then she made me bring her a slice of cake in bed and ate the whole thing.*"

"*Ninety-seven*," said Lex.

"*She made me write it on the wall*," said Tilda. "*By the bed. In charcoal. She said she wanted to see it.*"

She looked past him. Up the path. At the gold man on the step.

"*He won't go in*," she said. Very low. "*Three days. Since her feet went. He came and sat on the step and he won't go in. She keeps calling for him. Through the door. And he keeps sitting there.*"

Lex looked at the King.

The King hadn't moved. Hadn't turned his head. He was sitting on the step with his arms round his knees and his great gold face turned up to the apple tree. To the blossom. And his eyes—

Lex could see his eyes from the gate.

He was crying.

---

He went up the path.

On the gold. He didn't think about it. It was just stone now, gold stone, hard under his boots. He walked up it to the step and stopped. And stood there. In front of the biggest man in the world.

The King didn't look at him.

"*She's calling for you*," said Lex.

"*I know*."

"*Then go in*."

"*I can't*."

The bell rang. *Ting*. Under Lex's shirt. The King flinched. Lex saw it. The great gold shoulders twitching, as if at a sound only he could hear.

"*What is that*?" said the King. Hoarsely.

"*A bell*."

"*It's been ringing all night*. *I could hear it. Very far off. Getting closer.*" The King turned his head at last. Looked at him. The furnace eyes swimming. Gold tears on gold cheeks. Falling. Landing on his knees, on his robe, on the step. "*Why is it ringing?*"

Lex looked at him.

"*It rings when someone's sad*," he said.

---

The King stared at him.

Then he laughed. Or tried to. It came out of him cracked. Broken. Like a bell dropped on stone.

"*Oh*," said the Gilded King. "*Oh, that's Hes. That's Hes's bell. Isn't it? From her belt. She dropped it in the kitchen when she was nine and bent the clapper. She used to wear it so the nurses would know where she was.*" He put his face in his hands. "*She gave it away. To that boy. In the garden. I always wondered.*"

He sat there. On the step. With his face in his hands.

"*It's been ringing for me*," he said. Through his fingers. "*Nine hundred years. And I never heard it.*"

---

From inside the cottage, a voice.

Old. Cracked. Thin as paper. But not weak. Not yet.

"*Is that the smith*?"

"*It's me, Mag*."

"*About time*. *Come in. Bring the big one.*" A pause. A wheeze. "*Is he still sitting on my step like a lump?*"

Lex looked at the King.

"*Yes*," he said.

"*Tell him if he doesn't come in*," said Old Mag, "*I'll get up and come out there and he won't like it.*"

---

The King lifted his head.

He looked at the door. Old. Oak. Grey with weather. With a latch, iron, black, that someone had mended a hundred times. Standing a little ajar. Firelight coming through the crack.

Outside the door.

"*I can't*," he said. Very quietly. To Lex. Not to the door. "*Smith. I can't. I'll touch her. I'll want to. I won't be able to help it. I'll take her hand, and she'll go gold, and she'll be kept, and she'll lie there in that bed forever with her eyes moving, and I'll have done it to her.*" His voice broke. "*She asked me not to. She made me promise. On the step. Two weeks ago. Don't you dare keep me.*"

"*Then don't*."

"*I can't help it*," said the Gilded King. "*Everything I touch. Everything. Since the pool. I can't stop it. I tried. The cup. Your mother's cup. I tried so hard. I held it and I thought, not this, please, not this one, I want to drink from it.*" He looked at his hands. "*And it went gold anyway.*"

---

Lex looked at him.

At the great gold hands. At the tears. At the cracked step and the gold path and the white petals lying on it, white, not gold.

*Make room in front of it*, he thought. *Before it sets.*

He'd done it on the green. In the spring. With gold dust coming down and a child in front of him, a little girl, Jenna Tull, with her hands up and the dust falling toward her face. He'd cupped his hands in front of her—not touching—and *made room*. A space. A hollow. Between the dust and the child. A place where something new could happen. And the dust had fallen into the room and not set. Just fallen. Grey. Harmless.

He'd learned it in a breath, with the seventh Well three hundred miles off answering him, frightened, small. *Speaking*.

He'd never done it for a touch.

He'd never done it for the King.

---

"*Give me your hand*," said Lex.

The King looked at him.

"*Not to touch*," said Lex. "*Just hold it out. Like this.*" He held out his own. Left. Burned. Palm up. "*Over mine. Not touching. An inch.*"

"*Smith*—"

"*Please*."

---

The King held out his hand.

Slowly. Shaking. Enormous. Gold. Glowing like metal in a crucible. He held it out over Lex's, palm down. An inch above. Lex felt the heat of it. Not fire. Not heat like a forge. Heat like the sun on a stone at noon. Steady. Patient. *Keeping*. He could feel it reaching for him. Down. Across the inch. The gold wanting to come down and close over his skin, over his bones, over the blood moving in them, and *stop* it. Gently. Lovingly. Forever.

He didn't move his hand.

He reached for the room.

---

It came.

From three hundred miles off. From a pool in a green circle under snow. From a young Well, frightened, small, too full already, with thousands of the dead standing round its rim. It came up through the ground under Old Mag's step and up through Lex's boots and into him and out through his hand.

*[...smith...]*

*I need room*. *Just a little. Just an inch.*

*[...for what...]*

*For an old woman to hold a king's hand*.

The Well was quiet.

Then—

*[...oh...]* said Mal. *[...oh...yes...]*

---

The room opened.

Between Lex's palm and the King's. In the inch of air. A space. A hollow. Not empty—*waiting*. The way the inside of a mould waits. The way a cupped hand waits. The way the dark in a hearth waits for the fire.

And the gold came down into it.

He felt it come. The keeping. Reaching. Pouring down out of the King's hand like heat off a stone, toward Lex's skin, toward the blood moving in it, wanting to close over it and hold it, still, forever—

And it fell into the room.

And didn't set.

---

It hung there. In the inch between their hands. Lex could feel it. Gold. Warm. Patient. Hanging in the hollow like a coin dropped into a well that never reaches the bottom. Not touching him. Not able to. Because there was room in front of it. Room for something to happen. And gold doesn't set where something's going to happen.

The King stared at their hands.

"*What*—"

"*Room*," said Lex. "*In front of it. Before it sets.*" He was sweating. In the cold. It was like holding a door shut against a wind. Like holding a hot bar steady on the anvil with the tongs while someone else struck. "*It can't keep what's still happening.*"

"*How long can you*—"

"*Not long*." His hand was shaking. "*An hour. Maybe. If she—*" He stopped.

"*If she what*?"

"*If she doesn't take longer than that*," said Lex.

---

The King looked at their hands for a long time.

At the inch of air between them. At the gold hanging in it, not setting.

Then he stood up.

---

It took a while. He was very big, and the step was very small, and he was very careful. He stood up into the blossom of the apple tree, so that the flowers brushed his gold hair, and stayed white. He stood with his hand held out flat in front of him, palm down, and Lex's hand under it, palm up, an inch apart, and the room between.

And he turned to the door.

And put his other hand on the latch.

---

It went gold.

Of course it did. The old black mended iron. Gold. Under his fingers. Bright and smooth and cold.

He didn't look at it.

He pushed the door open.

---

The cottage was small. One room. Low-beamed. Smoke-dark. A fire in the hearth, banked low. A table. A chair. A dresser with plates on it, blue and white, chipped. A rag rug. And in the corner by the fire, under the window, a bed.

Old Mag was in it.

Small. So small. Smaller than Lex had ever seen her. Propped on pillows. In a white nightcap and a white nightgown and three shawls, with a patchwork quilt pulled up to her chin. Her one black eye open. Bright. Like a bird's. The other closed for good under its fold of skin.

The cat was on the quilt. Biscuit. Ginger. Huge. Curled on her feet.

On the wall by the bed, in charcoal, in Tilda's big square letters:

*97*.

---

"*Well*," said Old Mag. "*You took your time.*"

The King stood in the doorway. Bent nearly double under the low lintel. Gold. Filling the door. With the morning behind him and the blossom over his head and his hand held out flat in front of him over Lex's.

"*Mistress*," he said.

"*Don't you Mistress me*," said Old Mag. "*I've told you.*" The black eye moved. To their hands. To the inch of air between them. Narrowed. "*What's that?*"

"*Room*," said Lex.

"*Room for what*?"

"*For him to hold your hand*," said Lex. "*Without keeping you.*"

---

Old Mag looked at their hands for a long time.

Then she looked at the King.

"*Is that so*," she said.

"*I think so*," said the King. His voice was shaking. "*I don't know. The smith says. I've never—I've never held anyone's hand. Not since the pool. Not without—*" He stopped.

"*Well*, *don't stand in the door*," said Old Mag. "*You're letting the warm out. Come and sit.*" She jerked her chin at the chair. By the bed. Low. Rush-seated. With a cushion on it. "*That chair. Mind the cat.*"

---

He came in.

It took a long time. He had to bend so far his head was nearly at his knees. He had to turn sideways to get between the table and the dresser. Lex went with him, step by step, backward, with his hand held up under the King's and the room between them, sweating, shaking, holding.

The floor went gold where the King stepped. The rag rug. The flags. A leg of the table, where his robe brushed it.

He reached the chair.

He looked at it. Low. Small. Rush-seated. Built for an old woman.

He didn't sit in it. He'd have broken it. He knelt. On the floor. Beside the bed. The way he'd knelt on the floor of Lex's mother's cottage in the autumn, with his hand out to a baby.

The flags went gold under his knees.

He knelt beside the bed and looked at Old Mag.

And she looked at him.

---

"*Now*," said Old Mag. "*Give it here.*"

She took her hand out from under the quilt.

Old. Knotted. Brown-spotted. Thin as a bundle of twigs. With a wedding ring on it, gold, worn so thin it was nearly a thread, that had been on that finger for seventy-five years.

She held it out.

---

The King looked at it.

He looked at it the way Lex had seen men look at a sword they'd been told to fall on. The way his father had looked at the black door at the bottom of the Hollowmaw, the first night, with his wife behind it.

"*Smith*," he said.

"*I've got it*," said Lex.

He didn't know if he had it. He didn't know anything. He knelt beside the King on the gold flags with his hand under the King's hand and the room in the inch between them, and Mal's voice faint and far in the back of him, *[...yes...yes...]*, and he moved.

He turned his hand. Slowly. Palm up still. And slid it under Old Mag's. So his palm was under hers. Under her knotted old fingers. Holding them up. Like a tray.

And the King's hand came down.

Over hers. An inch above. With the room between.

---

He closed it.

---

Slowly. So slowly. The great gold fingers coming down round the old brown ones. Curling. Closing. Lex felt them come. Felt the gold reaching. Felt the keeping pouring down out of the King's palm like heat off a stone at noon, toward the old woman's skin, toward her thin slow blood, wanting to close over it and *hold it*—

And he held the room.

He held it the way he'd held the hammer at the Heart. With everything. With his whole body. With the seventh Well coming up through the floor of the cottage from three hundred miles off and pouring through him, *[...yes...room...here...]*, into the inch, into the hollow, into the space between a king's hand and an old woman's, so that the gold fell into it, and fell, and fell, and never reached the bottom.

The King's fingers closed.

Round hers.

Touching.

---

Old Mag's hand stayed brown.

---

Nobody breathed.

Not Lex. Not the King. Not Tilda, in the doorway, with her gold hand over her mouth.

Old Mag looked down at her hand. In the King's. At the gold fingers wrapped round her brown ones. Huge. Gentle. Holding. At her own skin, wrinkled and spotted and brown and alive, showing between the gold.

She looked at it for a long time.

"*Well*," she said. "*There.*"

---

The King made a sound.

Lex had never heard a sound like it. Not from a man. Not from anything. It came up out of the great gold chest like something breaking. Like ice breaking on a river in spring. Like a bell cracking all the way through.

He put his head down. On the quilt. Beside their hands. And wept.

Not quietly. Not the gold tears on the step. *Wept*. The whole of him. Shaking. His great shoulders heaving. His face pressed into the patchwork. His hand still closed round hers—gently, so gently—and not letting go.

Old Mag let him.

She put her other hand on his head. On the gold hair. Lex saw it. Her old free hand, the one Lex wasn't holding, coming up off the quilt and settling on the King's bowed head.

It went gold.

Lex saw it happen. Saw the gold come up into her fingers from his hair. Up to the first knuckle. The second.

She didn't take it away.

"*Doesn't matter*," said Old Mag. Softly. Looking at her gold fingers in his gold hair. "*Doesn't matter, dear. It's only the one hand. I'll not be needing it.*"

And stroked his hair.

---

He wept for a long time.

Lex knelt beside him and held the room. His arm was shaking. His back was screaming. Sweat ran into his eyes. He didn't care. He held it.

Biscuit the cat got up off the old woman's feet. Stretched. Walked up the quilt. Looked at the King's bowed gold head. Sniffed it.

Lay down against it. Purring.

The cat's fur stayed ginger.

---

"*Tell me*," said the King. At last. Into the quilt. Muffled. "*Tell me what it means. Please. I've asked everyone. I don't understand.*"

"*What what means, dear*?"

"*Finished*." He lifted his head. His face was a ruin. Gold. Wet. "*You said you didn't want to be kept. You wanted to be finished. You said there's a difference.*"

Old Mag looked at him.

"*There is*," she said.

"*What is it*?"

---

She thought about it. Lex watched her think. Her one black eye going to the window, to the apple tree, to the blossom in the snow. To the wall by the bed. *97*.

"*A cake*," said Old Mag.

The King stared at her.

"*You make a cake*," she said. "*You put it in the oven. You wait. You take it out when it's done. And it's done. It's finished. It's a cake.*" She looked at him. "*You don't leave it in. Forever. Because you love it. Because you can't bear for it to be done. You'd burn it. Or you'd have a cake that was never a cake. Just an oven with something in it, waiting.*"

The King said nothing.

"*Kept's the oven*," said Old Mag. "*Finished is the cake.*" She squeezed his hand. Very weakly. Lex felt it through the room. "*I'm the cake, dear. I'm done. Ninety-seven years. Husband. Daughters. Boy at sea. This cottage. That tree. Eleven cats.*" A pause. "*Twelve. Biscuit's twelve. Don't tell him.*"

"*But you'll be gone*," said the Gilded King.

"*Yes*."

"*Forever*."

"*Yes*," said Old Mag. "*That's the point, dear. That's what finished means.*" She looked at the ring on her finger. Worn to a thread. "*If I went on forever I'd never get to see my boy and tell him off. Or my Will. Or my mother. I'd lie here forever with my feet cold.*" She shook her head on the pillow. "*No. I've had my share. I want to go and see them.*"

---

The King looked at her for a long, long time.

Then he looked down. At their hands. At the gold round the brown. At the room between them that Lex was holding, shaking.

"*I stood outside his door*," he said. Very quietly. "*My father. Every night. For a month. With my hand on the latch.*"

"*I know*," said Old Mag. "*Your steward told me. In my kitchen. Before he bowed.*"

"*Cad went in*. *He was eleven. He held Father's hand. Every night. Not saying anything.*" The King's voice was barely a whisper. "*And I stood outside the door. I couldn't. I couldn't watch it. I couldn't watch him be finished.*"

"*And what happened*?"

"*He was finished anyway*."

"*There you are*," said Old Mag. "*It happens anyway. Whether you go in or not.*" She squeezed his hand again. Weaker. "*Only if you go in, someone's holding your hand when it does.*"

---

She went at noon.

---

It wasn't sudden. It wasn't anything. She talked for a while. About her husband, Will, who'd been a thatcher and fallen off a roof. About her daughters. About the tree. She asked Tilda for a sip of water, and Tilda gave it to her, holding the cup to her lips with her good hand. She asked for the cat to be given to the Pruett boy with the cut hand, because he'd like something to hold. She said the tree was Lex's.

"*Yours*," she said. Looking at him. At last. Her one black eye. "*The tree. When I'm gone. It's no good to Tilda, she's got a forge to run. And it won't give apples.*" A wheeze. "*Make something with it. Something that matters.*"

"*I will*."

"*Promise*?"

"*I promise*."

"*Good lad*," said Old Mag. "*You always were. Even when you were a sulky little thing with your hair in your eyes.*" She closed her eye. "*Tell Brannoc he still owes me for the pie dish.*"

Then she was quiet for a while.

Then she opened her eye one more time. And looked at the King. Kneeling by her bed. With his great gold hand round hers.

"*There*," she said. "*See? Not so bad.*"

And she breathed out.

And didn't breathe in.

---

It was very quiet in the cottage.

The fire ticked. The cat purred, against the King's bowed head. Outside, in the garden, the wind moved in the apple tree, and a few petals came down past the window. White. Pink at the edges.

Old Mag lay on her pillows. Small. Still. Her one black eye closed now too. Her mouth a little open. Her hand in the King's.

Brown.

---

Lex let go of the room.

He didn't decide to. It just went out of him. All at once. The way breath goes out of you when you've been holding it too long. His arm dropped. His whole body sagged. He knelt on the gold floor beside the bed and couldn't hold himself up and put his head down on the edge of the mattress and breathed.

The King didn't let go.

He knelt beside the bed with Old Mag's hand in his. And looked at it. And Lex lifted his head and saw it too.

The gold reaching. Now. With the room gone. Out of the King's fingers. Toward her skin. Toward the old brown hand, still and cooling.

And not setting.

It slid over her skin like water over glass. And didn't take. Didn't hold. Didn't keep. Because there was nothing to keep. She was finished. Done. There was nothing in that hand anymore that the gold could stop.

The King saw it.

He looked at it for a long, long time.

Then, very gently, he opened his fingers. And laid her hand on the quilt. And took his away.

Her hand lay on the patchwork. Brown. Old. Knotted. Still. With the thin gold ring on it.

Finished.

---

He knelt there.

Lex didn't know for how long. He knelt beside him. Tilda stood in the doorway with her gold hand over her mouth and tears running down her face. The fire ticked.

And then it got cold.

---

Not the fire. The fire burned. But the cold came in under it. Through the door. Through the window. A grey cold. A river cold. The cold of a long grey shingle in the dark, with water lapping on it, and a smell of wet stone.

Lex knew it.

He'd felt it in the autumn. In a cottage with a blue door. Two nights before Lantern Night. With his father's hand going slack in his.

He looked up.

---

The Ferryman was standing at the foot of the bed.

---

Tall. Grey. Hooded. Taller than the King, almost, though the King was kneeling. A long grey robe that moved, always moved, the way water moves at the edge of a river. A face you couldn't quite see under the hood. Long grey hands.

And in one of them, a lantern.

Old. Iron. Plain. With four panes of clear glass. The kind of lantern you'd hang on a hook by a ferry-stage to see who was waiting on the other side.

It was lit.

A flame burned in it. Small. Gold. Steady. Warm. Not the colour of a candle-flame or a lamp-flame. The colour of an ember. The colour of something that had been pressed into a sleeping boy's blood twenty-two years ago, like a maker's mark into a horseshoe.

Lex stopped breathing.

---

The Ferryman looked at Old Mag.

He bent. Slowly. The grey robe flowing. And held out his long grey hand over the bed. Not touching. Waiting.

And something rose out of her.

Lex couldn't have said what. Not a light. Not a shape. A *lightness*. The way a room goes lighter when someone opens a window. The way the air changes when the last guest goes home. It rose out of the small still shape on the pillows and stood for a moment by the bed—Lex thought, for a moment, he saw a woman there, small and bent and fierce, with two eyes, both bright, black as a bird's—and looked down at the King.

And at Lex.

And grinned. Three teeth.

And put her hand in the Ferryman's.

---

The Ferryman straightened.

He looked at the King. Kneeling. Gold. Enormous. Wet-faced. With his empty hands on his knees.

The King looked back.

"*Not you*," said the Ferryman. His voice was like water running over stones in the dark. Very quiet. Very old. "*Still not you.*"

"*I know*," said the King.

The Ferryman looked at him a moment longer.

"*You held her hand*," he said.

"*Yes*."

"*You did not keep her*."

"*No*," said the Gilded King. "*She asked me not to.*"

The Ferryman was silent.

Then—so slightly that Lex nearly missed it—he inclined his hooded head. Toward the King. The way you'd nod to someone across a room. The way a man nods to another man who has done something hard.

"*I noticed*," said the Ferryman.

---

He turned to go.

And the lantern in his hand swung as he turned, and the light from it fell across the bed and across the floor and across Lex, kneeling, with his head up and his eyes on it.

And the flame in the lantern turned too.

Toward him.

---

It was small. Gold. Steady. A flame on nothing. The way unlatticed fire burns, that nobody ever taught to need a wick.

It looked at him.

He knew it was looking at him. The way you know someone in a crowd is looking at you. He knelt on the gold floor of an old woman's cottage with sweat drying on his face and his arm still shaking and looked back at it. And couldn't speak. And couldn't move.

*Ember*, he thought.

He didn't say it. He couldn't.

---

And the flame spoke.

Not in his head. Not anymore. Out loud. In the cottage. A small dry voice, precise, gentle, with an old-fashioned courtesy, that came out of the lantern the way the warmth came off it. A voice he'd heard every day for seven years in the back of his skull, and hadn't heard for sixty-seven days.

*[Good afternoon*,] said the flame in the Ferryman's lantern.

It paused. Politely.

*[I'm so sorry*,] it said. *[Have we met?]*

---

Lex didn't answer.

He couldn't.

He knelt on the floor and looked at it and didn't answer at all.

*[You look very tired*,] said the flame. Kindly. The way you'd say it to a stranger at an inn. *[I hope you'll get some rest.]*

And the Ferryman went out of the door into the garden, with an old woman's hand in one of his and the lantern in the other, and the cold went with him, and the grey, and the smell of wet stone.

And the light went down the gold path under the apple tree, and through the gate, and was gone.

---

The fire ticked.

The cat got up. Off the bed. Stretched. Walked across the gold floor and sat down in front of the hearth and began, very carefully, to wash its face.

Tilda was crying in the doorway.

The King knelt by the bed with his empty hands on his knees.

And Lex knelt beside him. On the gold floor. With his face in his hands.

---

After a long time the King spoke.

"*Smith*," he said.

Lex didn't look up.

"*That flame*," said the Gilded King. Very quietly. "*In his lantern. It knew you.*"

"*No*," said Lex. Into his hands. "*It didn't.*"

The King was silent.

Then—slowly, carefully, the way he did everything now—he lifted one great gold hand. And held it out. Not touching. An inch from Lex's bowed shoulder. Hanging there. In the air. Shaking.

He didn't put it down.

He just held it there. An inch away. For a long time.

Not keeping anything.
