# Through the Glass

The first thing Wren learned about the glass road was that it went down.

Not like a stair. Not like a slope. Like falling into a well where the well falls with you. She sank through the grey slate of the mill-pond the way mist sinks into a cold hollow, and the glass let her through without noticing, the way a window lets the fog press against it all night and never feels a thing, and then she was—

Under.

In.

Somewhere.

---

It was bright.

That was the second thing. She'd expected dark. She'd been braced for dark, the way you brace for cold water. She'd thought the inside of a mirror would be black, like the back of one.

It was white.

White like a page. White like the sky over snow. White and clear and endless in every direction, and full of light that came from nowhere, that didn't cast shadows, that lay on everything evenly like a coat of paint.

And full of faces.

---

Panes.

Thousands of them. Millions. Hanging in the white. Not on walls. Not in frames. Just hanging. At every angle. Every height. Near and far. Like leaves falling, if leaves fell forever and never reached the ground. Square ones and round ones and long narrow ones. Some as big as a barn door. Some no bigger than a thumbnail.

And in every one, a face.

Looking out.

---

She saw a woman brushing her hair. In a long pane, tall, the shape of a looking-glass on a bedroom wall. A tired woman with grey in her hair, looking at herself, not seeing herself, thinking about something else. Brushing. Brushing. The same stroke. Over and over.

She saw a boy in a puddle. Upside down. Six or seven. Leaning over to look at himself, making a face, sticking out his tongue. The same face. The same tongue. Over and over.

She saw a man in a shop window. A soldier in a polished breastplate. A girl in the back of a spoon. An old woman in a dark kitchen window at night, with a candle, looking out past her own face into the dark for someone who hadn't come home.

Every face that had ever looked into anything bright.

Kept.

---

Wren hung in the white. As mist. A thin grey breath of cold fog among the falling panes.

And understood.

This was where they went. All of them. Every reflection. Every time you looked in a glass and saw yourself, and looked away, and the glass kept looking. Every face in every window. Every face in every pond. They didn't go away when you stopped looking. They came here.

And she kept them.

Ysenne. Nine hundred years. Every pane. Every face. Every one of them, stopped, at the moment someone looked—a tired woman brushing her hair, a boy pulling a face—stopped and kept and hung here in the white forever, so that the Glass Queen could walk among them and look at every face in the world.

Except one.

---

*Where's Lark*.

She made herself think it. Hard. Like a nail.

*Where's Lark*. *Not faces*. *Not panes*. *Lark*.

She turned. As mist turns. Slowly. In every direction at once.

And there—

Down. Far down. Through the falling panes. A long way off. Small. Soft. Gold.

A light. Going away.

---

She went after it.

She didn't know how. She'd never mistwalked anywhere like this. She'd mistwalked through fog banks and rain and the grey country's edge and once, terribly, through the inside of a storm over the Teeth with Lark in her belly. You went between. You went through. You let the cold carry you the way the wind carries a breath.

There was no wind here.

There was no cold, even. The white was neither warm nor cold. It was just *still*.

So she went the way she'd gone the first time. When she was ten. On the hill above Thistledown. When her father was three days dead under the rock-wyrm's boulder and her mother was coughing in the house and she'd gone out into the fog in her nightdress, barefoot, and walked until she couldn't see the house any more, and kept walking, and found she wasn't walking. Found she was the fog. Found she could go anywhere, so long as she didn't think about where she'd come from.

She didn't think about the mill-pond. Or the bank. Or Lex on his knees in the mud.

She thought about the light.

And went.

---

Down through the panes.

Past faces. Past thousands of faces. They didn't see her. They couldn't. They were made of seeing, Kestrel said, and mist didn't throw a reflection, and she slid past them like a draught under a door. A baker in a copper pan. A child in a window. An old man in a pair of spectacles—she knew him, she knew that face, wise and wry and tired—and she went past it before she could think *Malachi*.

Past a pane with a face in it that she knew so well her heart stopped.

Red-haired. Freckled. Grinning. Twelve. Thirteen. In a bucket of water by a well in Thistledown, with a fresh-shot hare over one shoulder and a bow taller than she was over the other.

Her.

Kept.

---

She didn't stop.

She wanted to. God help her, she wanted to. That girl in the bucket was the last time she'd looked at herself and been glad. Before Lex. Before the Spire. Before the hand.

She didn't stop.

*That's not what I am*, she thought. *That's what I was*. She'd said it to the glass thing on the lantern line, on her knees in the snow, with her own five-fingered reflection holding a glass arrow at her heart. *This is what I was. That's all right. I don't need it back.*

She went past it.

The pane turned. Slowly. As she went. As if, for just a moment, the red-haired girl in the bucket had felt something go past her. A breath. A draught. And looked up.

---

The light was getting closer.

She could see it now. Not just a speck. A shape. A small gold shape, glowing soft, held. Held against something white. Something tall. Something walking down through the falling panes as if they were a stair.

Wren slowed.

Went thin. Thinner. As thin as breath on a window. And came down through the panes behind them.

Very quietly.

---

Ysenne was singing.

---

Wren hadn't expected that.

She'd expected—she didn't know what. Cold. Triumph. A queen with a stolen child, walking fast to her throne. She'd expected to find Lark screaming.

Lark wasn't screaming.

She was on the Glass Queen's hip. The way a child sits on its mother's hip. Glowing. With one fist wound in a fold of the white gown, and the other in her mouth, and her black hair sticking up at the back. And her square eyebrows down. Hard. In the scowl she'd had since the day she was born, that Lex said was Wenna's and Seren said was Wren's and Wren said was nobody's but her own.

She was looking at the panes going past.

At every one.

And at every one, round the fist in her mouth, she said:

"*No*."

---

"*That's a woman brushing her hair*," said Ysenne. Softly. Walking. "*In Saltreach. Eight hundred years ago. She was thinking about her son, who'd gone to sea.*"

"*No*," said Lark.

"*That's a soldier*. *He polished his breastplate every morning for forty years. He liked to see his face in it. It made him feel he was still there.*"

"*No*."

"*That's a girl in a spoon*." A pause. "*She thought she looked funny upside down. She laughed.*"

Lark took her fist out of her mouth.

Looked at the girl in the spoon.

"*No*," said Lark. Very firmly.

And put the fist back.

---

The Glass Queen laughed.

It wasn't a cold laugh. That was what made Wren go still in the white, a breath among the panes, and stay still. It was a small laugh. Surprised. As if it had got out of her without asking. Like water out of a crack in ice.

"*You don't like any of them*," said Ysenne.

"*No*," said Lark.

"*Why not*?"

Lark took her fist out of her mouth again. And thought about it. With her eyebrows. The way she thought about everything.

"*Gan*," said Lark.

"*Your grandmother*?"

"*Gan*," said Lark. Insistently. And pointed. Back. Up. The way they'd come. Through the panes. To the place where a pond at the bottom of a garden had shown her a face she knew. "*Gan. Mam. Da.*" And then, with tremendous scorn, flapping her hand at all the faces in the world—"*No*."

---

Ysenne stopped walking.

She stood among the falling panes with a child on her hip and looked at her. For a long time. With her clear glass face. With her eyes that were nothing, like looking through a window at a white sky.

"*Not these*," said the Glass Queen. Slowly. "*You only want the ones that are yours.*"

"*Mam*," said Lark.

"*Not every face*," said Ysenne. "*Not all of them. Not kept. Just the ones that look back.*"

"*Da*," said Lark.

The Glass Queen didn't say anything.

And Wren, hanging in the white a dozen yards behind her, saw her lift her free hand—long, clear, glass—and touch, very lightly, one of the panes beside her. A small one. Round. Like the back of a spoon.

There was nothing in it.

---

She walked on.

Wren followed.

---

It went on for a long time.

She didn't know how long. There was no day in the white. No night. No sun. No hunger. No tiredness. She didn't breathe—mist doesn't—and she didn't sleep, and the panes went past and the panes went past, and Ysenne walked down through them as if down a stair that had no bottom, and Lark said no to every one.

Hours. Days. She couldn't tell.

At some point Lark fell asleep. On the glass shoulder. With her fist still wound in the white gown. Glowing softly. The way she glowed in the cot at night.

Ysenne walked more slowly after that.

Wren noticed. She didn't want to. She noticed anyway. The Glass Queen shifted the child on her hip, the way you shift a sleeping child when your arm's gone dead, so the head's in the crook of your neck. And slowed. And stopped singing. And walked the rest of the way in silence, very carefully, through the falling panes, so as not to jolt her.

Wren hung in the white behind them and hated her.

And watched her do it.

And couldn't stop watching.

---

*Take her*.

She thought it a hundred times. A thousand. Hanging there.

*Take her now. While she's asleep. Go in as mist and come out as you and grab her and run.*

*Run where*?

That was the thing. That was what stopped her, every time. *Run where*. Up? There was no up. There was only the white, and the panes, and the glass road going down. Back to the mill-pond? She didn't know the way. She'd come down without thinking about where she'd come from. That was how mistwalking worked. You didn't look back.

And Ysenne was the white. Ysenne was every pane. If Wren came out of the mist and became a woman with a red braid and a child in her arms, she'd throw a reflection in every pane in the world. And the Glass Queen would turn round. And look at her. And—

*YOU ARE WHAT YOU ARE*.

She'd seen what happened. Posy Tull on her knees in the snow with her own face screaming at her out of a Stone. The lantern line. Ada Fell.

She couldn't take Lark. Not here. Not in the glass.

So she followed.

And waited.

And was there. That was all. Was there. A breath. A draught. A grey fog going down through the white, a dozen yards behind her daughter, where her daughter couldn't see her and didn't know.

*I'll stay with her*, she'd said to Lex. *She's not going to be on her own down there. Not for one minute*.

She wasn't.

Even if she didn't know it.

---

The white began to go grey.

Wren noticed it at the edges first. The light changing. Thinning. The panes going further apart. Fewer of them. Older. The faces in them stranger—clothes she didn't know, hair she'd never seen done that way, languages on the lips that she couldn't read.

And under the white—

Water.

Far down. Below. Grey. Dark. Moving. A huge slow grey current, like a river seen from a mountain. And in it—Wren looked, and looked away, and looked again—buildings.

Towers. Roofs. Domes. Streets. A city. Under the water. Grey and drowned and enormous, old beyond anything she had a word for. With grey shapes drifting in its streets like fish. Like smoke.

The Drowned Country.

Antho had told her. On the night of the double wedding, drunk, at two in the morning, with his head in Seraphine's lap. *There's a city under the Hollow. Before the Lattice. Before the Wells were tapped. Drowned. And the dead drift in it. And there's a little girl. Grey. I promised her a light.*

The glass road went down over it. Through it. A road of panes. Falling. Past the drowned towers. Past the drowned dead. Down.

To the bottom.

To the Heart.

---

She saw it from a long way off.

Gold.

The only colour in the grey. A long way down. At the bottom of everything. Where the glass road ended and the drowned city ended and the grey river ran out of the dark on one side and into it on the other.

A hall.

Round. Huge. Roofless. Its walls were white stone, the way the Spire was white stone, the way every Lattice Stone in Caldor was white stone—and Wren understood, looking down at it, that they'd all been cut from here. That every Stone in the world was a chip off this. And in the middle of the floor, on a dais of seven steps—

An anvil.

Gold. Huge. Old. Plain. Worn smooth on its face by blows struck nine hundred years ago. And on it—on the anvil, on the face, as if somebody had set it down a moment ago and gone to fetch something—

A crown.

Gold. Seven points. Thin. Plain. No jewels.

Warm. Empty.

---

And beside the anvil, on the top step, a chair.

White. Stone. High-backed. Plain. The chair of the Root. The chair where Lex had sat, once, with the whole net in his hands. Where the old king had sat, for nine hundred years, dead in his tomb and holding on.

Empty now.

Empty, because the woman who'd been sitting in it for four months was walking down the last of the glass road toward it, with a child asleep on her shoulder.

---

Ysenne stepped off the last pane.

Onto the white floor of the Heart.

And the hall woke up.

---

Light.

Out of the floor. Out of the walls. Out of the white stone. Ten thousand threads. Silver. Gold. Blue. Every colour. Rising out of the stone like steam off a pond at dawn, and going up, and out, and away—to every Stone in the world. To every Kindling. To every panel. To every hand.

The net.

Wren had never seen it. Not like this. Lex had. Lex had sat in the middle of it. But Wren had only ever felt it, the way you feel your own pulse. A hum. A warmth. A panel in the air in front of you saying *Lv 73* in silver-blue.

She saw it now.

Ten thousand threads of light, rising out of the white stone, out of the floor of the Heart, going away up through the grey water and the drowned city and the glass road to the whole of the world.

And all of them—

Still.

Not moving. Not humming. Not pulsing like a heart. Hanging. In the air. Taut. Like the strings of a harp when someone has put their whole hand flat across them.

Held.

---

Ysenne walked up the seven steps.

Slowly. Carefully. With the child asleep on her shoulder. Past the chair. To the anvil.

She stood in front of it.

She looked down at the crown.

She didn't touch it.

Wren saw her not touch it. Saw the long glass hand come up—and stop. A finger's breadth away. Hovering. Over the gold. Shaking, a little.

Then go back down.

---

"*Not yet*," said Ysenne. Softly. To the crown. To herself. "*Not without the hand.*"

She turned.

She sat down in the white chair.

Carefully. With Lark asleep in her lap. The way you'd sit down in a rocking chair with a sleeping child, so as not to wake her. And the threads of the net bent toward her. All ten thousand. Leaning. The way the dead had leaned toward Antho on the hill. The way the flames had leaned toward Lark at Harrow on the night the Veil fell.

The Glass Queen sat in the chair of the Root with the net bent round her like a bower of light and a child asleep in her lap, and closed her eyes.

---

Wren hung in the grey air at the edge of the Heart.

At the edge of the glass road. Where the last pane hung over the white floor. As mist. A breath. A draught.

And waited.

---

She didn't know how long.

The light in the Heart didn't change. The threads hung still. The grey river ran in on one side and out on the other, slow, silent, with the drowned city dark above it. The crown lay on the anvil. The Queen sat in the chair. The child slept.

Wren thought about Lex.

On the bank. In the mud. With his hands burned and his side broken and his face—she'd never seen a face like that. Not on him. Not on anyone. As if somebody had cut him open and taken something out.

She'd kissed him and gone.

She thought about that kiss. She thought about the first one. On the Spire. In the wind. She thought about the night after the wedding, and the morning she'd told him, on the roof, *I'm carrying*, and his face. Twelve years old. Crooked smile. She thought about Lantern Night, and the lanterns in every window at Harrow, and the small furious red thing in her arms that had opened its mouth and yelled at the whole world as if it had a complaint to make.

She thought: *two days*.

She didn't know if it had been two days. She didn't know if it had been two hours. She didn't know if, up there, in the world, in Ashbourne, it was still the night of the mill-pond, or Midsummer, or a hundred years later and everyone she loved was a picture of themselves standing still in a grey field.

She waited.

---

Lark woke up.

---

Wren knew before she moved. Mist knows things. The way fog knows the shape of a valley. She felt the small body in the Queen's lap go from soft to stiff. Felt the breath change. Felt the eyebrows come down.

Lark sat up.

In the Glass Queen's lap. In the white chair. In the middle of the net. Glowing. Furious. With her hair flattened on one side from sleeping on a glass shoulder and her fists clenched. And looked round.

At the white hall. At the threads of light. At the gold anvil. At the grey river.

At the Glass Queen.

Who opened her eyes, and looked back.

---

"*Good morning*," said Ysenne.

Lark looked at her.

Then she looked away. Past her. Past the chair. Past the anvil. Past the threads of light. Across the white floor of the Heart to the edge, where the glass road ended, where the last pane hung over the white stone.

Where nothing was.

Where a breath of grey fog hung in the still air. Thin. Cold. Faint. Like your breath on a winter window.

Lark's eyebrows went up.

---

"*Mam*," said Lark.

And pointed.

---

The Glass Queen turned her head.

Slowly. The way glass turns. And looked where the small finger was pointing. At the edge of the Heart. At the last pane. At the empty air.

She looked for a long time.

Wren didn't move. Didn't breathe. Mist doesn't. She hung there in the grey and was nothing. A draught. A cold. A smudge on the air. Nothing that threw a reflection. Nothing that could be seen.

The Glass Queen's eyes were clear. Like looking through a window at a white sky.

They didn't find her.

---

"*There's nobody there, little one*," said Ysenne. Gently.

"*Mam*," said Lark. Insistently. Pointing.

"*I can see everything*," said Ysenne. "*Every pane. Every face. Everything that's ever looked into anything.*" Her voice was very soft. Very sure. "*There's nobody there.*"

Lark looked at her.

Then she did a thing Wren would remember for the rest of her life.

She put both her small square hands on either side of the Glass Queen's face. On the clear cold glass. And turned it. Firmly. The way you'd turn a stubborn goat by the horns. Away from the empty air. Toward her own face.

And looked at her. Very hard. From six inches away.

"*Mam*," said Lark. Slowly. Clearly. As if to an idiot. "*There.*"

---

Ysenne went very still.

Under the small square hands. With her glass face turned. Looking into the eyes of a child of twenty months who glowed in the dark.

And Wren saw something happen to the glass.

Not a crack. Not yet. Something smaller. A blur. A breath. As if someone very close had sighed on it.

---

"*You can see her*," said the Glass Queen. Very quietly.

"*Mam*," said Lark.

"*I can't*."

Lark frowned. As if that were a ridiculous thing to say. As if anybody could see her mam, if they'd just look.

"*I can't*," said Ysenne again. Her voice had changed. Something in it had gone thin. "*I can see every face in the world. I can't see her. I can't see—*"

She stopped.

The small hands were still on her face.

"*Who are you looking at*?" said the Glass Queen. Barely a whisper. "*Little one. Right now. When you look at me. What do you see?*"

---

Lark considered her.

Seriously. With her eyebrows. For a long time. The way she considered a new food, or a strange dog, or the question of whether a fire was hot.

Then she took her hands away from the glass face.

And said, very firmly, the only answer she had—

"*No*."

---

The blur went off the glass.

The Glass Queen sat in the chair of the Root with the net bent round her and a child in her lap, and her face was clear again. Cold. Clear. Like a window.

But her hands, on either side of the child, were shaking.

"*Midsummer*," said Ysenne. To nobody. To the net. To the crown. "*Midsummer, and then you'll see. Everyone will see. Everyone will look. And I'll be in every pane.*"

She lifted her head.

And spoke to the edge of the Heart. To the last pane. To the empty air where a breath of grey fog hung, and did not throw a reflection, and could not be seen.

"*Mistwalker*," said Ysenne. Not loudly. Not cruelly. Tiredly. "*I can't see you. Not when you're mist. I couldn't on the lantern line, until you stopped to look at yourself. I can't now.*"

Wren didn't move.

"*But she can*," said the Glass Queen. "*So you might as well come and sit down. It's a long time till dawn. And I'd like her to stop pointing.*"

---

Wren hung in the grey.

She thought about it. For a long time. About everything she'd decided. About mist and reflections and *YOU ARE WHAT YOU ARE* and Posy Tull on her knees. About the glass road and the panes and the face of a red-haired girl in a bucket.

She thought: *she can't see me*.

She thought: *if I come out, she'll see me in every pane in the world*.

She thought: *my daughter's pointing at me*.

---

She came out.

Not all at once. Slowly. The way the fog comes off a field in the morning and leaves a hedge behind. Grey, thinning. And under it, a woman. Barefoot. In a shirt and leggings. With a fox-red braid down her back and three flesh fingers on her right hand and two gold fingers and a gold thumb on her left.

Standing on the white floor of the Heart.

At the edge of the net.

Throwing a reflection in every pane in the world.

---

Lark shrieked.

Pure joy. The big belly-laugh. The one that came up out of her like water out of a spring. She scrambled down out of the Glass Queen's lap—Ysenne didn't stop her—and tumbled down the seven steps on her bottom, one at a time, bump, bump, bump, the way she went down the stairs at the Forge, and got up at the bottom and ran.

Across the white floor. Through the threads of the net. Glowing.

Into Wren's arms.

---

Wren went down on her knees.

And held her.

And held her.

And over her daughter's head, over the black hair sticking up at the back, she looked up the seven steps at the Glass Queen in the chair of the Root.

Ysenne was looking at her.

Really looking. For the first time. With her clear glass eyes. At a woman on her knees on the white floor, with a child in her arms, both of them throwing a reflection in every pane in the world.

Wren waited for it. For the cold. For the panel. For her own face, five-fingered, the best bow in Thistledown, screaming out of the air at her. *YOU ARE WHAT YOU ARE*.

It didn't come.

---

"*Sit down, Mistwalker*," said the Glass Queen. Very quietly. "*Please.*" She looked at the child in Wren's arms. "*I'm not going to hurt either of you. I never wanted to hurt anyone. I only wanted—*"

She stopped.

She looked down at her own hands. Clear. Glass. Shaking.

"*At dawn*," said Ysenne, "*she'll put her hand on the anvil. And the net will take her. And I'll pick up the crown for her and hold it till she's grown. And everyone will be safe. And kept. And still. Forever.*" Her voice was very steady. "*You can hold her till then. I'd like you to. She's happier.*"

"*She'll say no*," said Wren.

"*I know*," said Ysenne. "*She always does.*"

She leaned back in the white chair. In the middle of the still net. With the crown on the anvil beside her, warm and gold and empty.

"*That's why it has to be her hand*," said the Glass Queen. "*And not her answer.*"
