# Rosie

Rhosyn woke up tired every morning, and every morning it surprised her.

She lay on the cot in the corner of the blue-door cottage, under the west window, under the horn lantern with TOO LATE cut into its base, and felt it. In her arms. In her legs. In the small of her back. A heaviness. A *slowness*. As if she'd been carrying something all night and had only just put it down.

Tired.

She hadn't been tired in nine hundred years. Nobody in the Court had. You couldn't be tired when nothing was happening. Tired was what happened when time went through you and took something with it.

She lay there and felt time going through her. Every morning.

She'd never felt anything so wonderful.

---

She got up.

Slowly. Her knees cracked. She was forty. She'd been forty for six weeks. Her knees had been nine hundred and something before that, but they'd been nine hundred and something without ever bending in the cold, or kneeling on a stone rim, or squatting in the birch wood to look at a snowdrop. They were forty now, and they'd found out about it all at once.

She sat on the edge of the cot and rubbed them.

*Ow*, she thought.

And laughed.

---

Seren was already up.

Seren was always already up. *Thirteen years of getting up at dawn in the dark*, she said. *I don't know how to stop.* She was at the hearth with the kettle, in her grey shawl, with her square freckled hands doing the thing they did every morning—warming the pot, one spoon each and one for the pot, water just off the boil, not on it—without her looking at them at all.

She didn't turn round.

"*Your knees*?" she said.

"*They cracked*."

"*They'll do that*," said Seren. "*Mine started at thirty. You're doing well.*"

Rhosyn laughed again.

Seren turned round then. And looked at her. With her brown eyes. Hes's eyes. Not the colour—Hes's had been grey, like Oz's—but the *looking*. The way Hes had looked at people. Steadily. Kindly. As if she had all the time in the world and was glad to spend it on you.

"*What*?" said Seren.

"*Nothing*," said Rhosyn. "*You just looked like her.*"

Seren didn't say anything. She turned back to the kettle.

But Rhosyn saw her shoulders change. Just a little. The way a plant changes when you move it into the sun.

---

They had tea.

At the table under the west window. With the horn lantern on the sill between them, burning on nothing. Seren in her chair. Rhosyn on the stool. With bread. Real bread. From Hollis Penn's. Which Seren had brought up from Ashbourne the day before, and which was a day old now, and going hard at the edges.

Rhosyn ate it anyway.

She ate it the way she'd eaten it the first day. With both hands. With her eyes shut. Every bite. The crust. The crumb. The hard edges. She'd forgotten bread. Not what it was. What it was *like*. The weight of it. The chew. The way the crust cracked between your teeth and the inside was soft. The taste of it—yeast and salt and wheat and fire—going all the way down.

Nine hundred years of nothing. And then bread.

"*You're crying again*," said Seren.

"*I know*," said Rhosyn. With her mouth full. "*It's very good bread.*"

---

She went out to the grave after.

She did every morning. Across the stepping stones, in the grey spring light, with the stream loud and brown and high from the thaw. Past the other cottage, the smith's, with its empty east window. To the grey stone by the water.

*ARION VEY*.

*He turned back twice*.

Twenty lanterns round it. Still lit. Hollis Penn still came up every second day with his can of oil, though they didn't need it. The black soft lump that had been Pim's turnip. Nobody had moved it.

And the roses.

Two of them. Hers. The first, the one she'd sent through the snow with a scrap of silk on it, the last green thing she'd had. And the second, the one she'd planted the morning after she came, from a cutting of her own crown.

They'd grown.

Not slowly. Not the way roses grow. They'd come up the side of the grey stone like ivy, in six weeks, and over the top, and down the other side. A tangle of green stems and dark leaves and thorns. And flowers. Red. Dark red. Nearly black at the heart. A dozen. Two dozen. Open. In March. In April. As if it were midsummer.

She knelt by them.

She put her hand in the earth at the foot of the stone. Her brown hand. Her dirty hand. Into the cold wet ground. And felt them.

Growing.

---

She'd always been able to feel it. Growing things. Since she was a child. Since before the pool. She'd lie on her stomach in the palace gardens with her cheek on the grass and feel the roots under her moving. Slowly. Patiently. Reaching. Feel the sap rising in the stems. Feel a bud deciding to open.

The under-gardeners thought she was mad. *The little princess talks to the roses*.

She did. She talked to everything. She talked to the roses and the cabbages and the old apple tree by the kitchen wall. And they leaned toward her. A little. The way flowers lean to the sun.

And then she drank.

And everything she touched grew thorns.

---

Nine hundred years of thorns.

She'd tried to grow roses. In the Court. In the grey. She'd knelt in the dust with her grey hands and reached for the thing she'd always been able to reach for, and it had come—it always came—but it came *wrong*. Twisted. Hard. Dry. Brown. Thorns. Miles of them. Round the tenth tower. Round anything she loved. Round anything she wanted to keep.

That was what keeping did. To growing things. It made them thorns.

She'd known that. In the Court. For nine hundred years. She'd known it the way you know a sore tooth.

She hadn't known it would stop.

---

She knelt by the grave with her hand in the earth and felt the roses grow. Up the stone. Over it. Reaching. Not hard. Not dry. *Soft*. Green. Alive. Leaning toward the light coming through the birch wood.

Thorns, still. Roses have thorns. That's just what they are.

But flowers, too.

---

"*Good morning*," she said. To the stone. To the man under it, whom she'd never met. Hes's girl's husband. Who sounded, from everything she'd heard, like the kind of man Hes would have liked. *She liked men who cried.*

"*It's April*," she said. "*The roses are doing well. I think they like you.*"

She didn't know why she said it. The smith said it to his fire every morning. *Good morning*. To nobody. To something that couldn't answer.

She'd started doing it too.

It felt right. It felt like what you did. When you were in a world where things ended. You said good morning to the ones who'd gone. In case.

---

The child found her there.

She heard her first. *Thump*. *Thump*. *Thump*. Across the stepping stones. Fast. Too fast for stepping stones. Rhosyn turned round, alarmed, half up off her knees—

The child was across.

Standing on the bank. In a nightshirt too long for her. Swaying. With her fluff of dark hair standing up and her square fists clenched and a look of tremendous satisfaction on her round face. As if she'd crossed the sea.

Behind her, on the far bank, the smith's wife. With her bow. White-faced. Running.

The child looked at Rhosyn.

"*No*," she said. Proudly.

---

Rhosyn laughed.

She couldn't help it. It came out of her the way it came out of her now, all the time, rough and cracked and astonished. She sat back on her heels in the wet earth by the grave and laughed.

The child came over. *Thump*. *Thump*. And stopped. And looked at the roses. At the dark red flowers climbing over the grey stone.

And reached out.

"*Careful*," said Rhosyn. "*They've got thorns.*"

The child's hand stopped. An inch from a stem. She looked at Rhosyn.

"*Thorns*," said Rhosyn. She touched one. Lightly. With her fingertip. "*Sharp. Ow.*"

The child considered this.

Then she reached past the thorn. Very carefully. With one square finger. And touched a petal. Dark red. Soft.

And smiled.

---

The smith's wife arrived. Out of breath. On the bank. With her bow.

"*She got out*," said Wren Ashdown. "*Again. She got out of the cot and down the ladder and out the door and I turned my back for—*" She stopped. Looked at her daughter. Touching a rose. Smiling. "*Oh.*"

"*She's all right*," said Rhosyn. "*She knows about thorns now.*"

Wren looked at her. Then at the child. Then she sat down on the bank. Heavily. In the wet grass. And put her face in her good hand.

"*She's going to be the death of me*," she said. Into it.

"*No*," said Rhosyn. "*She's going to be the life of you. That's much harder.*"

---

They sat by the grave.

The three of them. In the spring light. With the stream loud beside them and the birch wood going green behind and the lanterns burning round the stone. The child sat in Rhosyn's lap—she'd climbed there, without asking, the way she climbed everything—and played with the dirt. Picking it up in her square fists. Letting it fall. Picking it up again.

"*Is it true*?" said Wren. After a while. Not looking at her. "*About Seraphine?*"

Rhosyn looked at her.

"*Antho came up last night*," said Wren. "*At midnight. He couldn't stop talking. He walked up and down the kitchen for an hour. Lex had to sit on him.*" She almost smiled. "*He said you put your hand on her and knew.*"

"*I knew*," said Rhosyn.

"*How*?"

Rhosyn thought about it.

"*It's growing*," she said. "*It's a growing thing. I can feel growing things. I always could.*" She looked down at the child in her lap. At the dirt in her fists. "*It's very small. Very new. Like a seed in March. It's only just decided to be.*" She was quiet. "*It's the first new thing I've felt in nine hundred years that wasn't a rose.*"

Wren was very still.

"*Will it*—" She stopped. "*With the net still. With the grey coming. The midwife at Hobb's Cross says the babies near the grey are—*"

"*It's not near the grey*," said Rhosyn. "*Not yet.*" She looked west. Over the birch wood. Toward the edge of the world. "*And it's not of the net. Babies aren't. They're of the room. Of the Well. The one I drank.*" She swallowed. "*The one I gave back.*"

---

The child dropped her dirt.

And looked up. Past Rhosyn. Past Wren. At the stream.

And went very still.

---

Rhosyn felt her go still. In her lap. The small square body. Rigid. The fists clenched. The round face turned toward the water.

She looked where the child was looking.

The stream. Loud. Brown. High with the thaw. Running fast over the stones. Not still. Not anywhere. Not a single still place in it.

Except—

There. At the edge. By the bank. Where a root of the birch went into the water and made a little pool behind it. A pool no bigger than a dinner-plate. Out of the current. Brown. Calm.

Still.

---

And in it, a face.

---

Not the child's. Not Rhosyn's. Not Wren's.

Narrow. Pale. Clear. Like ice. White hair. Ice-grey eyes. A crown of glass like a line of frost.

Looking up.

Not at the child.

At Rhosyn.

---

Wren was on her feet. Rhosyn heard her. Heard the bow come off her shoulder. Heard her breathe in—

"*Don't*," said Rhosyn.

Wren stopped.

"*Take her*," said Rhosyn. Very quietly. She lifted the child out of her lap. Gently. And held her out. Without looking away from the little pool. "*Take her inside. Please. It's not her she wants. It's me.*"

---

Wren took the child.

Rhosyn heard her go. Back across the stepping stones. Fast. With the child on her hip saying *no*, *no*, *no*, crossly, over her shoulder, at the water.

Then the door of the blue-door cottage. Opening. Shutting.

Then nothing. The stream. The birches. The lanterns round the grave.

And her sister's face in a pool the size of a dinner-plate.

---

"*Hello*, *Ysy*," said Rhosyn.

---

The face in the water didn't answer for a long time.

It looked at her. The ice-grey eyes. Moving over her face. Slowly. The way they'd always moved. The way Ysy had always looked at everyone, since she was four and sitting at the long mirror in the nursery. Reading. Laying it all out. Like goods on a table.

Rhosyn let her.

She knelt by the grave with her dirty hands in her lap and her knees in the wet earth and let her sister read her.

---

"*You're old*," said Ysenne.

"*I'm forty*."

"*You look older*." The ice-grey eyes moved. "*There's grey in your hair. Lines round your eyes. Your hands—*" A pause. "*Your hands are filthy, Rosie.*"

"*I've been gardening*."

"*You've been getting old*," said the Glass Queen. Her voice was cold. Clear. Like a bell under ice. "*Every day. I can see it. Every day you're a little older. A little closer.*" The face in the water was very still. "*To the door.*"

"*Yes*," said Rhosyn.

"*You'll die*."

"*Yes*."

"*In thirty years. Forty. In a bed. By a window. Like the smith's father. Like the old woman Oz sat with.*" The ice-grey eyes didn't blink. "*You'll go through the door, Rosie. And I'll never see you again.*"

---

Rhosyn looked at her.

At her sister's face. In a pool the size of a dinner-plate behind a birch root at the edge of a stream at the end of the world.

Ysy had always been the prettiest. Everyone said so. Mother said so. The nurses. The ladies of the court. Oz said so, every morning, at the long mirror, because she made him. *Am I pretty, Oz? Am I the prettiest? Tell me.*

She *was* pretty. She'd been the prettiest girl in the world.

She was glass now. Clear. Cold. You could see through her.

And her face—her face was still pretty. The same face. The same high brow and narrow chin. Mae's chin. The same ice-grey eyes.

But Rhosyn looked at it now—really looked, the way she'd learned to look at things in six weeks of being forty and tired and alive—and saw something she'd never seen.

Ysy was *frightened*.

---

"*Come with me*," said Rhosyn.

The face in the water didn't move.

"*Come with me*," said Rhosyn again. "*To the pool. Give it back. Like I did. Like Hes did.*" She leaned forward. Over the little pool. Close. "*Get old with me, Ysy. Forty. Fifty. Sixty. Get grey hair and bad knees and dirt under your nails. Eat bread.*" Her voice cracked. "*Go through the door with me. At the end. Holding hands. Like we did when we were little and Mother made us go to bed in the dark.*"

"*No*," said Ysenne.

"*Why*?"

The Glass Queen didn't answer.

---

"*You chose them*," she said instead. "*The Harrow boys. The smith. The warden. You held your thorns for them and let me chain you, and then you went to their pool and poured yourself out and came here.*" The ice-grey eyes narrowed. "*To a hedge-witch's cottage. To eat her bread and sleep on her floor and dig in her dirt.*"

"*Yes*."

"*Over us*," said Ysenne. "*Over Oz. Over me.*"

"*I didn't choose them over you*."

"*You did*."

"*I chose to stop*," said Rhosyn. "*That's all. I chose to stop being what I was. I chose to start being something else.*" She looked at her dirty hands. "*I chose roses. Instead of thorns.*"

The face in the water was very still.

"*You could too*," said Rhosyn. Softly.

---

"*I can't see myself*," said Ysenne.

---

Rhosyn went still.

The face in the water had changed. Not much. The ice-grey eyes. The set of the mouth. Something in it had cracked. A hair. Like ice on a pond in the first warm day.

"*In the pool*," said the Glass Queen. "*At the end. When we went in. To the long hall. To the mirror.*" Her voice was very low now. "*You were there. You saw.*"

"*I saw*."

"*Everyone was there. In the glass. Arm in arm. You and Oz and Mae and Cad and the cousins. And Merrow. All of you. Laughing.*" The ice-grey eyes didn't blink. "*And I was in the middle. And there was nothing there. The wall. Just the wall.*"

"*I know*, *Ysy*."

"*Oz laughed*," said Ysenne. "*He said, Ysy, where've you gone? He thought it was a trick. Everyone laughed.*" A pause. "*I laughed too.*"

Rhosyn didn't say anything.

"*Nine hundred years*," said the Glass Queen. "*I learned everything there is to know about glass. Every pane. Every surface. Every angle of every light. I can walk through it. Look out of it. Reach through it. I can stand in a window two hundred miles away and see a child's face in a bath.*" Her voice was shaking now. "*And I've never once seen my own.*"

---

Rhosyn knelt by the stream with her hands in her lap.

She'd known. She'd been there. She'd seen the mirror. She'd laughed with the others.

She'd never once thought about it again.

Nine hundred years. In the Court. In the grey. With her sister in the glass palace at the end of the white road, building windows. Making them. Every surface. Every pane. Thousands. And Rhosyn out in her thorns, round the tenth tower, never once going to the glass palace, never once asking.

*What are you looking for, Ysy*?

---

"*The net*," said Rhosyn. Slowly. "*That's why. Isn't it?*"

The face in the water didn't answer.

"*It's not the net you want*," said Rhosyn. "*It's not the crown. It's not keeping everyone. You don't care about keeping everyone.*" She leaned closer. "*You want it to look at you.*"

---

The face in the water went very still.

"*The net looks at everyone*," said Rhosyn. "*That's what it's for. It looks at every child at sixteen and tells them what they are. It looks at them every day. Every thread. It's a mirror. Of everyone.*" She swallowed. "*And if you held it—if you were the middle, the crown, the Root—then every thread in the world would run through you. Every person. And every time they looked at their panel, they'd be looking through you.*"

Nothing.

"*They'd be looking at you*," said Rhosyn. "*All of them. Everyone in the world. All the time.*" Her voice cracked. "*And you'd know you were there.*"

---

The Glass Queen didn't say anything for a long time.

Then she said, very quietly, so quietly that Rhosyn barely heard it over the stream:

"*They looked at me*. *In the panels. The first night. When I sat down in the chair.*"

"*I know*."

"*Everyone*. *Every face in the kingdom. In the dark. Looking at me.*" The ice-grey eyes were very bright. "*At the same time.*"

"*I know*, *Ysy.*"

"*It was the first time*," said Ysenne. "*In nine hundred years. The first time anyone—*"

She stopped.

"*And it wasn't enough*," said Rhosyn. Gently.

The face in the water flinched.

"*They were looking at a face in a panel*," said Rhosyn. "*Not at you. At the glass. Like you've been looking at the glass for nine hundred years. Like you looked at the mirror in the long hall.*" She leaned closer. Over the little pool. Until her own face was a foot from the water. From her sister's. "*It wasn't enough. Was it? It's never going to be enough. Not if every face in the world looks at you forever.*"

---

"*Stop it*," said the Glass Queen.

"*Ysy*—"

"*Stop it*. *Stop looking at me like that.*"

"*Like what*?"

"*Like you can see*," said Ysenne. "*Like you know. You don't know. You were out in your thorns for nine hundred years. You never came. Not once. You never came to the palace. You never asked.*" Her voice was rising. Cold. Cracking. "*You don't know anything about me, Rosie.*"

"*No*," said Rhosyn. "*I don't. You're right.*" She didn't move back. "*I never came. I never asked. I'm sorry.*"

The face in the water stared at her.

"*I'm asking now*," said Rhosyn. "*What are you looking for, Ysy?*"

---

Her sister didn't answer.

She looked up out of the little pool behind the birch root with her ice-grey eyes and her glass crown and her face that was still the prettiest face in the world, and didn't say anything.

And then she did.

"*I don't know*," said Ysenne of the Glass.

---

It was three words. Very small.

Rhosyn had never heard her say them. Not once. Not in nine hundred and nineteen years. Not when she was four. Not when she was nineteen at the pool. Not ever.

*I always know*. That was what Ysy said. That was what she'd always said. *I always know*.

*I don't know*.

---

"*Then come and find out*," said Rhosyn. "*With me. Not in the net. Not in the glass.*" She held out her hand. Over the water. Brown. Dirty. Open. "*Come out, Ysy. Come and be forty with me. Come and be tired. Come and find out.*"

The face in the water looked at her hand.

For a long time.

Rhosyn didn't move. She knelt there with her hand out over the little pool, the way she'd seen the smith hold out his hand over a gold king's, and Seren hold out hers over a dying man's. Not touching. Not taking. Just there.

---

Then the face changed.

Hardened. The crack in the ice closing over. The cold coming back into the ice-grey eyes. The set of the mouth going straight and thin.

"*No*," said the Glass Queen.

"*Ysy*—"

"*I have a crown in front of me*," said Ysenne. "*I've walked for four months through every thread in the world to get to it. It's there. In front of me. Warm. Gold. Empty.*" Her voice was very cold now. Very clear. "*All I need is one small hand.*"

"*She said no*," said Rhosyn. "*The child. In the yard. To you. In Oz's cup.*"

"*She's a baby*. *She says no to everything.*"

"*She said it to you*," said Rhosyn. "*She looked at you, Ysy. In the cup. Straight at you. And said no.*" She leaned closer. "*Nobody else has ever looked at you like that. Have they? Not Oz. Not Mae. Not me. Nobody's ever looked straight at you.*"

The face in the water went white. Whiter. Like frost thickening on a pane.

"*She did*," said Rhosyn. "*And she said no.*"

---

The little pool behind the birch root—

*Cracked*.

Not the water. Water doesn't crack. The *stillness*. The flat calm surface. It broke. All at once. As if someone had dropped a stone in it. Rippled. Shivered. And the face in it went to pieces. A hundred bright broken pieces of ice-grey and white and glass. Scattered across the ripples.

And gone.

And the stream ran in. Over the root. Into the little pool. Brown. Fast. Loud.

Not still anywhere.

---

Rhosyn knelt by the stream.

With her hand still out. Over the water. Brown. Dirty. Empty.

She took it back. Slowly. And put it in her lap. And looked at it.

*I don't know*, Ysy had said.

---

Seren found her there.

An hour later. Still kneeling. By the stream. By the grave. With the roses climbing over the stone beside her and the lanterns burning and the sun going over toward noon.

Seren didn't say anything.

She sat down. On the bank. In the wet grass. With her knees cracking. And put her arm round Rhosyn's shoulders.

They sat there for a long time.

---

"*She's frightened*," said Rhosyn at last.

"*I know*," said Seren. "*Oz told me. In the winter. About the mirror.*" She was quiet. "*I said, oh, the poor thing.*"

Rhosyn turned her head and looked at her.

"*You said that*?"

"*Nobody else ever had*," said Seren. "*Oz said.*"

Rhosyn looked at her for a long time. At Hes's girl. Hes's girl's girl. Twenty generations down. With brown eyes and square hands and a widow's grey shawl.

"*She can't see herself*," said Rhosyn. "*She never could. Not since the pool. She's been looking for nine hundred years.*" She swallowed. "*She thinks if the whole world looks at her, she'll know she's there. She thinks the net can do it.*"

"*Can it*?"

"*No*," said Rhosyn. "*It's glass. It's all glass. It'll show her everyone else. It'll never show her.*" She looked at the stream. "*Nothing that's glass ever will.*"

"*Then what will*?"

---

Rhosyn didn't answer.

She thought about it. Sitting on the bank. With Seren's arm round her shoulders.

She thought about the child. In the yard. In the mud. In her nightshirt. Planted in front of a gold king with her fists clenched. Looking straight at the cup. At the face in it.

*No*.

She thought about the little pool behind the birch root. Her sister's face. *Stop looking at me like that. Like you can see.*

She thought about the smith's father. Whom she'd never met. Looking at his wife every morning for a year and a half, across a table, *like the sun coming up*. Every morning. And her not knowing why.

*He didn't need me to know him*, Seren had said. *He needed me to have a good morning.*

---

"*Someone looking back*," said Rhosyn slowly.

Seren looked at her.

"*Not glass*," said Rhosyn. "*Not a mirror. Not a panel. Glass just shows you what's in front of it.*" She looked down at her own hands. "*Someone looking back. At you. Who can see you. And says what they see.*"

"*Like the child did*," said Seren.

"*Like the child did*," said Rhosyn. "*And she said no.*" She almost laughed. "*Of course she said no. She's one. She says no to everything.*"

She was quiet for a long time.

"*It should be someone who says yes*," she said.

---

She got up.

Slowly. Her knees cracking. She stood on the bank of the stream at Harrow with the roses climbing over the grave beside her and the sun at noon and Seren looking up at her.

"*When they go*," she said. "*The smith. The warden. When they go to her. To the middle. To the crown.*" She looked west. Toward the edge of the world. Toward the place, very far down, under the grey river, under the drowned country, where her sister sat in an empty chair looking out of every window in the world. "*I'm going with them.*"

Seren didn't say anything.

"*She's my sister*," said Rhosyn. "*Nine hundred years she's been looking. And I never once came.*" She wiped her face with the back of her dirty hand. "*I'm going to go and look at her.*"

"*And then*?"

Rhosyn didn't answer at once.

"*And then I'm going to tell her what I see*," she said.
