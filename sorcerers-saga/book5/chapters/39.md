# Epilogue: A Forge, a Child, a Lantern

Six years later, on the morning of Lantern Night, Lark Vey made her first lantern.

---

She did it at the long anvil. In the Forge at Ashbourne. At five in the morning. In the dark. Because that was when smiths worked, she said, and she was going to be a smith, and she wasn't going to start being one at a lazy hour like *nine*.

She was eight.

She stood on the crate. The same crate. Brannoc's crate, that Lex had stood on at eleven with a horseshoe nail in his fist, that had stood at the end of the long anvil for forty years for apprentices too short to reach. Lark was nearly too tall for it now. She'd grown a hand's width since the spring. Wren said she'd be taller than both of them, and furious about it.

She had her father's hands. Square. Short strong fingers. Wide palms. Burns on the backs of them, already, little white crescents, from the first year she'd been allowed near a fire and hadn't listened.

She had her mother's eyes. Green. And her mother's way of looking at a thing she was about to do. As if it had insulted her.

And she had her own eyebrows. Square. Down.

"*Don't tell me*," said Lark.

"*I wasn't going to*," said Lex.

"*You were*. *You were going to say the tin's too thick.*"

"*The tin's too thick*."

"*I know*," said Lark. "*I want it thick. I want it to last.*"

---

*[She's right, you know*,] said the fire.

From the great hearth. Gold. Dry. Small. Amused.

*[Thick tin holds the heat better at the seams*,] said Ember. *[The Saltreach lantern-makers used a gauge nearly twice what you use, in the old days. They said a lantern ought to outlive the person who made it.]*

"*Whose side are you on*?" said Lex.

*[Hers*,] said Ember. *[Obviously. She's much better company. She doesn't get ash on her nose.]*

"*I do*," said Lark. "*Sometimes. I just wipe it off.*"

*[Exactly*,] said Ember. *[She wipes it off. Six years, Lex. Six years and you have never once wiped it off.]*

---

He sat on the bench by the wall and watched her work.

He didn't help. She didn't want him to. He'd learned that the first year, when she was five, and had wanted to make a nail, and he'd put his hand round hers on the hammer the way he had at the bottom of the world, and she'd turned round on the crate and looked at him with her eyebrows and said *no*.

So he sat on the bench.

She cut the tin. Badly. Then better. She bent it on the horn of the anvil, the way he'd shown her, and the way Tilda had shown her, and the way Ember had explained at length, with diagrams drawn in the ash. She soldered the seams with the little iron, her tongue between her teeth, the way her grandmother wrote. The first seam was lumpy. The second was less lumpy. The third was good.

She scraped the horn for the panes herself. She'd been doing it all week. In the evenings. At the kitchen table at Harrow. With a knife and a lamp. Scraping, scraping, until the horn was thin enough to see the flame through, yellow-brown, like old honey.

She put it all together on the anvil as the sky went grey in the Forge windows.

And then she stood back. On the crate. And looked at it.

---

It was a lantern.

It wasn't a good one. The door hung a little crooked. One of the horn panes was thicker than the others, so the light would come out lopsided. The handle was too big, because she'd wanted it big enough for a grown man's hand.

It was a lantern.

"*It needs a word*," said Lark.

---

She'd been thinking about the word for a month.

Lex knew. He'd watched her think. At the table. At the stream. On the stepping stones. On the swing, which she was much too old for and still went on, high, every evening, until the bough creaked. Frowning. With her eyebrows. The way she'd frowned at the gold panel at the bottom of the world when it asked her a question.

He'd asked her, once, what she was going to cut.

She'd looked at him with scorn.

*It's a surprise*, she'd said. *Obviously*.

---

She picked up the nail.

A horseshoe nail. Old. Square-headed. Bent a little at the tip. Brannoc had given it to her when she was six weeks old, in a cradle at the Forge, on a winter afternoon. *A smith's first nail*, he'd said. *Every smith has one. Mine was my father's. Lex had one. Now she has one.* She'd kept it in a tin under her bed ever since.

She turned the lantern over on the anvil. And held the nail to the tin of the base, the way Lex had shown her, the way he'd cut every word he'd ever cut. *HERE*. *HOME*. *STAY*. *TOO LATE*. *MORE*.

And cut.

Slowly. Carefully. Pressing hard. Three letters. Square. Deep. In a hand that was going to be very like his.

---

She turned it the right way up. And held it out to him across the anvil.

He took it.

He turned it over. And read the base.

---

*YES*.

---

He looked at it for a long time.

He thought about a gold panel at the bottom of the world. *WILL YOU HOLD IT*? And a girl of twenty months, with her hand on an anvil, looking down seven steps at her father.

*No*.

He thought about a thorn-woman, kneeling on the rim of a pool in the morning, handing a small silver-grey crown to a boy who didn't talk. *It'll have a yes next*.

"*Why yes*?" he said. When he could.

Lark looked at him. As if he were being very slow.

"*Because I said no to everything*," she said. "*When I was little. Gan says. To bread and baths and the cold lady and the King and the whole net.*" She shrugged. "*I'm eight now. I thought I'd better start saying yes to things. Or nobody'll ever ask me anything again.*"

*[That's very sensible*,] said Ember.

"*I know*," said Lark.

---

"*Who's it for*?" said Lex.

He knew. He didn't want to know. He asked anyway.

Lark took the lantern back. Gently. Out of his hands. And held it against her chest. The way you'd hold a moth.

"*Gramp*," she said.

---

Brannoc Holt was seventy-one.

He'd been tired since the spring. Not ill. Not anything you could put a name to. Just tired. The way the old get tired, Seren said. The way a fire gets tired at the end of a long night, when the coals are all ash on the outside and red only right at the heart.

He'd stopped coming to the Forge in the summer. He'd gone to live at Harrow, in Seren's loft, where Pim used to sleep before Pim went back to the pool to keep it with Rosie and Ivy. He sat in the garden in the afternoons. On the bench by the grave. With his old scarred fingers in his lap. Looking at the roses.

In September he'd said, at supper, quite calmly, between one mouthful and the next: *I'd like to go to the door, I think. Before the snow.*

Nobody had said anything.

*I've had a good long go*, said Brannoc. *Seventy-one years. Forty-five of them at that anvil. A boy came in out of the snow when I was fifty, with nobody, and I got to watch him grow up.* He looked at his plate. *I'd like to see the door again. And sit by it a bit. And then I'd like to go and see Mattie.*

Lark had been six then. No—seven. She'd sat at the table with her spoon halfway to her mouth and looked at him for a long time.

Then she'd put the spoon down and gone out to the Forge without a word, in the dark, at seven o'clock at night. And Lex had found her there an hour later, on the crate, cutting tin.

---

They went on Ashka.

The storm-mother's daughter was huge now. Bigger than her mother had been. She came down out of the October sky over Harrow on the morning of Lantern Night in a long slow spiral, the way she always did, and landed in the meadow by the stream with a thump that shook the stepping stones, and put her great blue head down on the grass in front of Brannoc. And waited.

Brannoc looked at her.

"*I don't like dragons*," he said.

"*I know*," said Lex.

"*Never have*. *I walked two days once so I wouldn't have to fly on one.*"

"*I know*."

Brannoc looked at the dragon for a long time. Then at the stream. Then at the cottage, with its blue door and its horn lantern in the window. Then at the grave, with the roses climbing over it.

"*Well*," said Brannoc Holt. "*It's a long walk. And I'm a bit tired.*"

And let Lex help him up.

---

Six of them. Brannoc in the middle, wrapped in three blankets, with Lark in front of him, holding the YES lantern in her lap, and Lex behind, with his arms round both of them. Wren on Vharuun beside them, with Mattie—four, small, red-haired, silent as Pim and twice as stubborn, named for a weaver Lex had never met—asleep against her chest. Seren on Vharuun too. With the clay lamp.

They flew west.

Over the Vale. Over Westerling, where a young man was getting in the last of the barley on a hillside, and his old mother was sitting on the wall watching him, with a lantern in her lap. Over the green road, with its lanterns every mile, still burning, six years on, because people kept them. Somebody always did. Over farms and villages and fords and mills. Over a land that was green and gold and brown and alive, with smoke going up from chimneys and birds going up out of hedges and children running out of houses to wave at the dragons going over.

Brannoc slept most of the way.

Lark didn't. She sat in front of him with the lantern in her lap and her back against his chest, and every so often she turned round to check he was still breathing.

He always was.

---

There was a village at the door now.

Antho had been right. Lex had known he would be. It had started with one house—two rooms and a loft, a fat chimney, a lantern in each window—and then Tom Pruett's men had stayed on and built themselves somewhere to sleep, and then their wives had come, and then somebody had opened a shop, and then somebody else had opened an inn, and now there were forty houses on the long rise above the end of the road and a market on Thursdays and a man who sold little wooden doors on sticks.

The inn was called the Last House. Roole kept it. He said he'd stood at enough gates to know how to run one. It had the best ale for two hundred miles and a rule, written on a board over the bar in Roole's square grim capitals: *THE DEAD DRINK FREE*. Nobody had ever seen a dead man drink. Roole said that wasn't the point.

---

They came down out of the sky in the afternoon.

Into the meadow behind Antho's house. With the whole village coming out to look, the way it always did when the dragons came. Children running. Dogs barking. Seraphine in the doorway of the house with her horn spectacles and her quill, already writing.

And Antho.

He came across the meadow. In his old coat. Grey at the temples now—grey early, the way his father had, the way Seren said Vey men always did. With a boy at his heels. Six. Thin. Black-haired. With ears that stuck out at the top.

"*Uncle Lex*," shouted Ari Vey. "*Uncle Lex, Uncle Lex, I can skip a stone seven times, Da counted—*"

"*Six*," said Antho.

"*SEVEN*."

"*Six and a half*," said Antho. "*The last one sank.*"

He stopped at the dragon's side. And looked up. At Brannoc. Wrapped in his blankets. Grey-faced. Smiling.

"*Brannoc*," said Antho. Very gently.

"*Doorkeeper*," said Brannoc. "*I've come to sit on your step. If that's all right.*"

Antho looked at him for a long time.

"*It's what it's for*," he said.

---

They sat by the door all evening.

---

Brannoc on the threshold. On the grey stone. With his back against the frame, the way the Ferryman had sat. Wrapped in his blankets. With his old scarred fingers in his lap and the evening sun on his face.

The door stood shut beside him. Seven feet. Three and a half. Apple wood. Still blooming. Six years, and every morning new flowers, white and pink, and every evening the petals coming off in the wind, and never once a bare branch. Painted blue. Painted over and over. Every stroke anyone had ever added in six years. Loaves and hammers and fish and bees and roses and names, so many names, so many little pictures, layer on layer, until the door was thick with them, a whole world in blue. And low down, under the latch, small and square and faded now, a child's handprint.

Lark sat on the grass in front of him. With the YES lantern in her lap. Not lit yet. Waiting.

The rest of them sat round. On the slope. On the grass. Lex and Wren and Mattie. Seren with her lamp. Antho and Seraphine and Ari. Roole, who'd shut the inn. Half the village, sitting further back, quietly, with their own lanterns, the way people sat now, at the door, when someone came to it at the end.

Nobody said much.

---

"*Mattie liked roses*," said Brannoc.

At sunset. To nobody. Looking at the door.

"*She grew them*," he said. "*Up the front of the weaver's cottage. Red ones. Dark. Nearly black in the middle.*" He smiled. "*She'd have liked the thorn-woman's.*"

"*She'd have liked you*," said Seren. "*Mattie. Grown old. She'd have liked you old.*"

Brannoc didn't say anything for a while.

"*I went in every night*," he said. "*When she was ill. Thirty years ago. Every night. I sat by the bed. And every morning I went back to the forge and lit the fire. Because somebody had to.*" He looked at his hands. "*And then one night I went in and she'd gone. In the night. On her own. While I was asleep in the chair.*" A pause. "*I always thought that was the worst of it. That I wasn't awake.*"

Lark put her hand on his knee.

"*You can be awake this time*," she said. "*I'll stay up. I always stay up on Lantern Night.*"

Brannoc looked down at her. At her square hand on his blanket.

"*Aye*," he said. Gruffly. "*You do.*"

---

The sun went down.

The lanterns came out. All over the village on the rise. In every window. Along the road. Down the slope. Round the door. The way they did every Lantern Night now, at the end of the road. Hundreds of them. Gold.

Lark lit hers.

She did it with a spill from Seren's lamp. Very carefully. Opened the crooked little door of the lantern and held the spill to the wick and watched it catch. And closed the door. And held it up.

The light came out lopsided. Through the thick pane on one side more yellow than the rest.

It was the most beautiful lantern Lex had ever seen.

---

She gave it to Brannoc.

She didn't say anything. She just held it out. In both hands. With the YES turned toward him, so he could see.

He took it. In his old scarred hand. The handle was big enough. She'd made sure.

He held it up. And looked at it. At the light. At the crooked door and the lumpy seam and the lopsided pane.

He turned it over. And read the base.

Lex saw his face.

He'd known Brannoc Holt for twenty-two years. He'd seen him angry, and tired, and drunk on his own terrible ale. He'd seen him on his knees at a hearth at midnight saying *evening* to a fire for the first time in forty-five years. He'd seen him stand on a stone at the end of the world and plant his feet and close a crack.

He'd never seen him look like this.

"*Thick tin*," said Brannoc. When he could. His voice had gone. "*Good. It'll last.*"

"*That's what I said*," said Lark.

---

He went just before dawn.

---

Lex was awake. Everyone was. Nobody had slept. They'd sat on the slope all night under the lanterns, wrapped in blankets, while the stars went round. Ari had fallen asleep in Antho's lap. Mattie in Wren's. Seraphine had written all night, by the light of her lantern, and then stopped, and just sat.

Lark hadn't slept.

She'd sat on the grass at Brannoc's feet all night, the way she'd said she would. With her chin on her knees. Awake. Watching him. And every so often he'd open his eyes and look down at her and she'd be looking back, and he'd say *still up*? and she'd say *still up*, and he'd close his eyes again.

The YES lantern sat in his lap all night. Burning.

---

In the grey before dawn, he opened his eyes and didn't close them.

He looked at the door.

Lex followed his look.

There was someone standing on the slope below the threshold.

---

Tall. Grey. Hooded. With a long stick in one hand, like a walking stick, worn smooth at the grip. No lantern. No oar.

Lex hadn't seen him come. Nobody had. He was just there. Standing in the grass at the foot of the threshold, in the grey light, with the dew on the hem of his robe and mud on his feet from a long way of walking.

He looked older. Lex hadn't thought that was possible. Older and browner, as if six years of mornings had got into him. There was a sprig of something in the band of his hood. Heather. From the Teeth, maybe. Or further.

He looked at Brannoc.

"*Smith*," said the Ferryman.

"*Not any more*," said Brannoc. "*Retired.*"

"*Ah*," said the Ferryman. "*So am I.*"

---

"*You came back*," said Antho. Very quietly. From the slope.

The Ferryman turned his hooded head.

"*I said I would*," he said. "*Everyone does.*" He looked at the door. At the blue paint. At the blooming. At the bell. "*I've been seeing mornings. Six years of them. I've seen them over the sea at Gull's Reach and over the Pale Wastes and over the Teeth and over a city in the Sunreach where they ring a bell for every one.*" A pause. "*They're very good. I recommend them.*"

He looked back at Brannoc.

"*But I heard there was a smith at the door*," said the Ferryman. "*A good one. Who held a fire for a king once. I thought I'd come and sit with him. If he didn't mind.*"

Brannoc looked at him for a long time.

"*Mind the step*," said Brannoc Holt. "*It's been swept.*"

---

The Ferryman sat down beside him.

On the threshold. On the grey stone. With his back against the other side of the frame and his stick across his knees. Two old men on a doorstep in the grey before dawn. Not saying anything.

Brannoc looked at the lantern in his lap.

Then at Lark. On the grass at his feet. Awake. Watching him.

"*Still up*?" said Brannoc.

"*Still up*," said Lark.

He smiled.

Then he looked at Lex.

---

Lex went down the slope.

He didn't know he was going until he was there. Kneeling on the grass in front of the threshold, beside his daughter, in front of the old man who'd found him in the snow at eleven with nobody and taken him in and given him a crate to stand on and a fire to wake every morning and a hammer and a trade and a life.

Brannoc held out his hand.

The one without the lantern. Old. Scarred. Burned at the tips. Not gold. Never gold again.

Lex took it.

---

"*Mind the quench*," said Brannoc.

"*I always do*."

"*You never do*." The old man's grip was very weak. "*You never have. You put it in too hot and too fast and you swear when it cracks.*" A breath. "*Good lad.*"

He looked at the door.

"*Doorkeeper*," said Brannoc.

---

Antho came down the slope.

Slowly. With his hands open at his sides. The way he walked toward a frightened animal. The way he'd walked toward a little grey girl in a drowned street. He stepped up onto the threshold, past the two old men sitting on it, and put his thumb on the latch.

The latch lifted.

The door swung. Out. On its breathing hinges. And the bell rang. Once. Small. Clear.

And behind it, the room.

Soft. Dark. Deep. Full of stars. With shapes moving in the gaps between them, going in, going on. And far off, at the bottom, a pool. In a green field. With a ring of roses round it, now, as well as lanterns, red, nearly black at the heart. And a thin dark-haired young man of twenty-one sitting on the rim with his knees drawn up, and a woman of forty-six beside him with dirt on her hands, kneeling among the roses.

They looked up. Both of them. Through the soft dark. At the open door.

Pim lifted his hand.

---

And just inside the door—a yard in, no more, in the soft dark, among the stars—

A column of grey dust.

Small. A woman's height. Standing very still. Bending. A little. At the top. Toward the threshold. Toward the old man sitting on it.

The way a woman bends over a man asleep in a chair, to see if he's awake.

---

Brannoc saw her.

Lex felt it. Through his hand. The old fingers tightening. Once. Hard. The way they'd tightened on a hammer for forty-five years.

"*Oh*," said Brannoc Holt. Very softly. "*There you are.*"

---

He stood up.

Lex didn't know how. He'd been too weak to stand since the spring. He stood up off the threshold with the YES lantern in one hand and Lex's hand in the other, and he was straight. Not stooped. Not tired. A big grey-haired man in a leather apron, the way he'd been on the morning a boy came in out of the snow.

He let go of Lex's hand.

He looked down at Lark.

"*Thank you for the lantern*," said Brannoc. "*I'll take good care of it.*"

"*It's thick tin*," said Lark. Her voice was shaking. Her eyebrows were down. Hard. "*It'll last.*"

"*Aye*," said Brannoc. "*I know. I can tell.*"

---

He turned round.

He stepped through the door.

The Ferryman, on the step, inclined his hooded head. Very slightly. As Brannoc went past. The way you'd nod to a man across a room who has done something hard.

The grey column bent. All the way. Close. Arms. Arms that could close, now.

And they went down into the soft dark together. A big old man with a lantern, and a woman made of grey dust, with her arm through his. Slowly. Not hurrying. Not late. Down between the stars. The YES lantern swinging from his hand, lopsided, thick-tinned, burning gold.

They watched it go down.

Smaller. Smaller. A gold speck among the stars.

It didn't go out.

It just got further away.

---

Antho closed the door.

Gently. The latch dropped. *Click*. The bell didn't ring. It only rang for opening.

He stood there with his hand flat on the blue wood for a long time.

---

Lark didn't cry.

Not then. She knelt on the grass in front of the threshold with her square hands on her knees and her eyebrows down and her jaw set, looking at the closed door, and didn't cry.

Lex knelt beside her.

He didn't say anything. He'd learned that much. From Pim. From the dead. From an old man on a bench by a grave. Sometimes you didn't say anything.

He just put his arm round her.

After a while, she leaned against him.

After a while longer, she said, very quietly, into his shoulder—

"*He took it with him*."

"*Yes*."

"*The first one I ever made*."

"*Yes*."

Lark was quiet for a long time.

"*Good*," she said.

---

The sun came up.

Over the world. Over the green road. Over the village at the end of it, and the lanterns going pale in the windows, and the slope, and the threshold. Over two old men's places on a doorstep, one empty. Over the Ferryman, who had closed his eyes and turned his face to it, the way you'd turn your face to a fire.

Over the door.

The blue went bright. The apple wood went gold. The petals lit up, every one, white and pink, new this morning, the way they were new every morning.

The hinges breathed.

Out. And back.

---

Lark stood up.

She wiped her face with the back of her hand. Fiercely. As if it had insulted her. And looked at the door for a long time.

Then she turned round. And looked up at her father.

"*I want to make another one*," said Lark Vey.

"*Another lantern*?"

"*Lots*," said Lark. "*Every one I can. For everyone. So nobody has to go through without one.*" She frowned. Her eyebrows. "*I'll need more tin. And better horn. And you have to show me how to hang a door properly, mine was crooked.*"

Lex looked down at her.

Eight years old. Square hands. Green eyes. Burns on her knuckles. Ash on her nose.

"*All right*," he said.

---

They walked back up the slope together in the morning sun. Toward the village. Toward the Last House and Roole's terrible breakfast and the dragons in the meadow and the long flight home.

Toward Harrow. And the stream. And the stepping stones. And the blue door of a cottage across the water with a horn lantern in its window.

Toward the Forge. At five in the morning. In the dark. With the hearth banked and the long anvil cold and a crate at the end of it for anyone too short to reach. And a small dry voice in the fire that would wake when he blew on it, and say good morning, and tell him he had ash on his nose.

He would.

He always would.

---

Behind them, at the end of the road, at the edge of the world, the door stood shut in the sunlight on its threshold of grey stone. Blue. Blooming. Breathing.

Waiting for the next one.

And on the step, in the sun, with his eyes closed and a sprig of heather in his hood, the oldest thing in the world sat with his back against the frame. Not going anywhere. Not rowing. Not waiting at the edge of anything.

Just sitting on a doorstep, on a fine morning, keeping someone company.

In case anybody wanted to come out.

---

*Here ends* The Sorcerer's Dawn, *and the Lattice Chronicles.*
