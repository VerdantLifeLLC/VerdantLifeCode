# The Lantern Line

The raven came at noon, and it was Elowen's, and there was blood on it.

Wren saw the blood first. On the black feathers of its breast. Dried. Brown. Not the bird's. The bird was fine. Furious, exhausted, ruffled, perched on the edge of the water barrel in the yard at the Forge with its beak open and its wings half-spread, panting.

Not its blood.

She took the paper off its leg with her good hand. It was crumpled. Torn at one corner. Written in pencil, fast, sliding off the edge of the page.

---

*Wren.*

*They're coming out of the ice.*

*At the line. Every puddle, every ditch, every rut with a skin of ice on it. Since yesterday. Since dawn.*

*They look like us. Exactly. Our faces. Our clothes. Glass.*

*They're breaking the lanterns. Not drinking. Breaking. Kicking them over. Stamping.*

*Fire doesn't touch them. Swords crack them and they come back out of the next puddle.*

*Ada Fell is dead. Her own face did it.*

*We need mist. Please.*

*E.*

---

Wren read it twice.

Then she turned round.

Lex was at the door of the Forge. With soot on his face and the little hammer in his hand and Lark on his hip, chewing the end of his apron string. He'd seen the raven come in. He'd seen her face.

He held out his hand for the paper.

She gave it to him.

---

He read it. She watched him read it. Watched his face go still.

Then he looked at her.

She didn't say anything. She didn't need to. She'd been his wife for two years. She'd been beside him for seven. She knew what he was going to say before he opened his mouth.

*I'll go*.

"*I'll go*," said Lex.

"*No*," said Wren.

---

He stared at her.

"*She says mist*," said Wren. "*She says please. She doesn't say fire.*" She took the paper back. "*Fire doesn't touch them. She says that too.*"

"*Black heat*—"

"*Kestrel can do black heat*." Wren folded the paper. Small. Put it in her coat. "*Kestrel can come with me.*"

"*Wren*—"

"*And you can't*," she said. "*You can't, Lex. You've got to stay here.*" She looked at the baby on his hip. At the square fist round the apron string. "*With her.*"

---

He didn't say anything.

She watched it go through him. All of it. *She's going west. Into the grey. To fight glass things that wear your own face and kill you with it. And I'm staying here.*

*With a baby. With a stream at the bottom of the yard and a Glass Queen who's said she'll fetch her herself*.

She watched him want to argue. Watched him want to say *I'll go and you stay*. Watched him want to say *we'll both go and take her*. Watched him want to put himself between all of it, the grey and the glass and the ice and the dark, and hold it off with his bare hands.

She watched him not.

"*He said no*," said Lex. Very quietly. "*The King. Three days ago. In the yard. He said he won't bring her.*"

"*I know*."

"*So she'll come herself*."

"*I know*."

"*And you'll be two hundred miles away*."

"*And you'll be here*," said Wren. "*With her. With Kestrel's fire in every hearth and your mother and Rosie and Brannoc and every bucket lidded and every pane horn.*" She looked at him. "*And with you. That's more than she had in the summer.*"

---

He looked down at Lark.

Lark looked back. Chewing the apron string. Grey-green eyes. Steady.

"*No*," she said. Around the string. Conversationally.

Something went out of Lex's face. Wren saw it go. Not the fear. The fear stayed. But something else. Something that had been holding his jaw shut.

"*Go*," he said.

---

She went at dawn.

On a horse. Not the dragon—Antho had Ashka at Mal's pool, and the dragon was needed there. A Keepers' horse, grey, fast, with Kestrel on another beside her in her singed red coat, and four more Keepers behind, and the road west going away in front of them in the thin spring light.

She didn't look back.

She did, actually. Once. From the top of the hill above Ashbourne. At the Forge, with its twenty chimneys smoking. At the yard. At the small figure in the yard by the gate, big-shouldered, burned, with a smaller figure on his hip.

Lark waved.

Her whole arm. Like someone flagging down a cart.

Wren waved back.

Then she turned the horse west and rode.

---

It took four days.

Four days of road and mud and thaw. The snow gone everywhere now except the grey places, where it lay flat and finished like snow in a painting. Four days of villages with their doors shut. Of gold figures in the fields, walking east, humming. Of fewer gold figures, the further west they went, and more grey.

On the third day they saw the first of the old lanterns.

---

Islands.

That was what they looked like. Kestrel said it first. *Islands*. Small gold lights in the grey. Each one in its own little circle of real ground. A yard across. Two. White where there'd been snow, green where it had gone. Real grass. Real mud. A real wind, moving in it, stirring the grass.

And all round each one, the grey.

They'd been the line, once. The first line. Five hundred and fifty-six of them, set in the snow outside Westerling by Lex and his mother in the winter. And the grey had come up against them, and slowed, and gone round.

And kept going.

Past them. Round them. East. A mile a day, then less, then more. Leaving them behind. Little gold islands in a grey sea, each one holding its own yard of world. Still lit. Still given. Still not drunk.

But behind the line now. Not on it.

---

"*It goes round*," said Kestrel. Riding beside her. Looking at them. "*It can't take them. So it goes round.*"

"*Lex said it would*."

"*Lex said it would slow it*." Kestrel's mouth twisted. "*It's slowed it. A bit. A mile a day instead of two.*" She looked ahead. East. Toward the new line. "*It hasn't stopped it.*"

Wren didn't answer.

She looked at the islands. At the small gold lights going past on either side, in the grey. Each one holding its yard.

*Nothing ever is*, Seren had said. *Enough. You do it anyway.*

---

They reached the line on the fourth day at dusk.

---

She heard it before she saw it.

Not a sound. A *lack* of one. The grey was silent—it always was—but this was a different silence. Ahead. Over the next hill. A held breath. The silence of a field after a battle. Of a house after a scream.

Then a sound.

*Crack*.

Like ice on a pond. Like a cup dropped on stone. Sharp. Clear. Carrying.

*Crack*.

---

They came over the hill.

---

The line ran across the valley below them.

North and south. As far as Wren could see. Small gold lights in the dusk. Hundreds of them. Every twenty paces. Every ten. Each one in its own circle of real ground. A string of beads laid across the grey.

And Keepers. Along the line. Grey-cloaked. With staffs and swords. Standing between the lanterns.

And among them—

Wren pulled up her horse.

---

Glass.

People. Made of glass. Clear. Glittering in the last of the light. Tall and short and broad and thin. Standing among the Keepers along the line. *Moving*.

And every one of them wore a face she knew.

---

She saw Elowen.

Two Elowens. One in grey, with mail and a sword and blood running down her face from a cut over the eye. Swinging. Hard. Desperate.

And one in glass.

Clear. Perfect. Every line of her. The set of her shoulders. The way she held her sword—Oathkeeper, Garrick's sword, reforged by Lex in the Thornwood seven years ago—held exactly the same, in a glass hand, a glass sword. Her face. Her own face. Clear as ice. Cold. Smiling.

They were fighting.

Elowen swung. The glass Elowen swung. The swords met—steel on glass—*crack*—and the glass sword shattered.

And the glass Elowen stepped back. Into a rut. A wheel-rut in the mud, with a skin of ice on the water in the bottom.

And stepped out again.

With a new sword. Whole. Glass. Clear.

---

All along the line.

Keepers. Fighting themselves. Their own faces. Their own swords. Their own staffs. Their own way of standing, of moving, of breathing. Every Keeper on the line with a glass shadow in front of them, perfect, cold, wearing their face like a mask.

And between the fights, the lanterns.

Wren saw a glass man—a Keeper's face, young, thin, with a sergeant's knot on his glass shoulder—walk up to a lantern. Iron. Small. Lit. In its circle of real grass. And stop. And look down at it.

And lift his glass boot.

And bring it down.

---

*Crack*. Not the glass. The *lantern*. The iron crushed. The little horn panes split. The flame—

The flame didn't go out.

Wren saw it. The flame didn't go out. It lay there. On the grass. In the wreckage of the lantern. Small. Gold. Burning on nothing. Given.

But the circle round it—

Shrank.

The yard of real grass. Shrinking. The grey coming in from the edges. A yard. Half a yard. A hand. Until it was just a little flame on a patch of green the size of a plate. Still lit. Still holding.

But the line had a gap in it now.

And the grey was coming through.

---

"*She can't drink them*," said Kestrel. Low. Beside her. "*So she breaks them.*"

"*Yes*."

"*Clever bitch*," said Kestrel Varn. And kicked her horse forward down the hill.

---

Wren followed.

She didn't think about it. She didn't think about anything. She rode down the hill into the grey with her bow coming off her shoulder into her left hand—her gold fingers curling round the grip the way they did, frozen, like claws—and an arrow coming out of the quiver into her right.

She didn't draw.

There was no point. Not at glass.

She breathed.

---

Mist.

From the bottom of her lungs. From the place under her breastbone where the wind lived. Grey. Soft. Wet. Cold. Rolling out of her mouth in a great cloud as she rode, the way fog rolls off a river in October. Gale Commanding. Mistwalker. Born in a storm.

It went out ahead of her. Down the hill. Over the line.

Over the glass.

---

The glass Elowen stopped.

Wren saw it. Mid-swing. With its glass sword up and its glass face smiling. The mist rolled over it. And it *stopped*. Froze. The way you freeze when someone blows out the candle in a dark room. Its glass face blurring. Going grey. Going soft.

*Fog on glass*.

Elowen didn't hesitate. She'd been a knight since she was seventeen. She saw the glass thing stop and she swung. Not at its sword. At its *face*.

*Crack*.

The glass Elowen shattered.

All of it. Not just the sword. All of it. From the face down. Into a thousand glittering pieces. Into the grey grass. Into the mud.

And didn't come back.

---

Elowen stood over the pieces with her sword down and blood running into her eye. Breathing hard.

She looked up. Into the mist. At Wren, riding out of it on a grey horse.

"*You came*," she said.

"*You said please*," said Wren.

---

It took all night.

---

Wren rode the line. North first. Then south. Breathing. Mist pouring out of her. Rolling over the Keepers and the lanterns and the glass. And everywhere it rolled, the glass stopped. Blurred. Went blind. Froze.

And the Keepers broke them.

Kestrel rode with her. Behind her. Watching. And when a glass thing stepped out of a rut or a ditch or a frozen puddle, out of the mist's reach, Kestrel lifted her hand.

And nothing came out of it.

That was black heat. Fire with no light. Fire so deep and hot it burned past glowing into dark. Brannoc's lesson, in the deep, two winters ago. *A fire you can't see. A fire that throws no reflection.* Kestrel held up her hand and the air in front of it went thick and wavy and *dark*, like the air over a road in summer, and the glass thing walked into it.

And couldn't see it.

And melted.

Not shattered. *Melted*. The glass going soft and red and running. Slumping. Like a candle in a fire. Into a puddle of glowing glass in the grey grass. And going dark. And cooling. Into a lump. Clear. Flat.

Nothing in it.

---

"*It can't see it*," said Kestrel. Grimly. After the fourth. "*It's a reflection. It's made of seeing. It can't see something that doesn't throw light.*"

"*So it walks right into it*."

"*So it walks right into it*." Kestrel shook out her hand. Steam came off it. "*Your husband could do this. Better than me.*"

"*My husband's minding his daughter*," said Wren.

Kestrel looked at her sideways.

"*Good*," she said.

---

At midnight, Wren met herself.

---

She was riding south along the line. In the dark. With the mist pouring out of her and the lanterns going past on either side, small and gold in their circles. With Kestrel behind. And a Keeper ahead with a torch.

And something stepped out of a ditch in front of her horse.

The horse reared. Wren kept her seat. Just. With her gold-fingered hand wrapped in the reins and her bow in her right.

And looked down.

---

A woman.

Glass. Clear. Glittering in the torchlight. Twenty-seven. Thin. Wiry. In a glass coat and glass breeches and glass boots. With a glass braid down her back.

And a glass bow in her left hand.

Drawn.

---

Wren knew the stance.

The feet. The shoulders. The angle of the head. The way the left arm held the bow, straight and steady, and the right hand drew the string back to the cheek. She'd stood like that ten thousand times. Since she was nine. With her father's bow, too big for her.

She hadn't stood like that since the summer.

Since the Night of Glass. Since gold came down on her drawing hand.

The glass woman stood like that now. Perfect. Left hand on the bow. Right hand at her cheek. A glass arrow on the string. Pointed at Wren's heart.

And her left hand—

All five fingers. Clear. Curled round the grip. Bending.

---

Wren stared at it.

At the glass hand. Her own hand. The way it had been. Before. Five fingers. All of them moving. Curled round a bow the way hers used to curl, easily, naturally, without thinking.

Not gold.

Not stopped.

*Whole*.

---

"*Hello, Mistwalker*," said the glass woman.

In Wren's voice. Exactly. Every note. But cold. Clear. Like a bell under ice.

"*This is you*," said the reflection. "*Isn't it? This is what you are. The best bow in Thistledown. A hare through the eye at sixty paces in a snowstorm.*" The glass face smiled. Wren's face. "*Not a woman who misses trees.*"

Wren didn't move.

"*She can give it back*," said the reflection. "*Did you know? The Glass Queen. When she holds the net. She can show everyone what they are. And make them that. Forever.*" The glass arrow didn't waver. "*Your hand. The way it was. The way it really is. Underneath. All you'd have to do is look.*"

---

Wren looked.

She couldn't not. She sat on her horse in the dark with the mist curling off her lips and looked at the glass woman's hand. Her hand. Five fingers. Curled round the grip. Bending.

She'd dreamed about it. Every night for nine months. Lying awake. Feeling her gold fingers on the blanket beside her face, cold, like something that didn't belong to her. And dreaming about the way it had been.

*All you'd have to do is look*.

---

"*Wren*," said Kestrel. Behind her. Very quiet. "*Wren. Breathe.*"

She didn't breathe.

She was looking at the hand.

---

And then she thought of the tree.

Not the mark. The tree. In the birch wood behind the cottage at Harrow. With the chalk circle the size of a plate. Forty arrows. Eleven in the tree. Two in the chalk. The rest in the snow.

She thought of her right arm. Shaking. Six months of it. Every day.

She thought of the twenty-third arrow. And her teeth together. And drawing again.

She thought of the eye of the storm. Lark turning in the bright air. *Don't*. Her own hand on Lex's arm. *Let go*.

She thought of Lark in the yard. Three days ago. In the mud. In her nightshirt. Planted in front of a gold king like a smith at an anvil.

*No*.

---

"*That's not what I am*," said Wren.

The glass woman's smile didn't move.

"*That's what I was*," said Wren Ashdown. "*Before.*" She looked at the glass hand. At the five clear fingers. "*It was a good hand. I miss it. I miss it every day.*" She swallowed. "*But it's not what I am.*"

"*Then what are you*?" said the reflection.

Wren lifted her own bow.

In her left hand. With her three gold fingers wrapped round the grip like claws. Stiff. Cold. Frozen.

And drew with her right.

Not well. Her right arm shook. It always shook. Six months, and it still shook. She drew the string back to her cheek with the wrong hand and the wrong muscles and the wrong everything, and held it there, trembling, with the arrow pointing at the glass woman's face.

"*This*," said Wren.

---

She didn't loose.

She breathed.

---

The mist came out of her like the breath of a river at dawn.

All of it. Everything she had. Rolling out over the bow and the arrow and the glass woman in the ditch. Grey. Soft. Wet. Cold.

Over the glass face. Her face.

Over the glass hand. Her hand. The five clear fingers.

*Fog on glass*.

The glass woman blurred. Went grey. Went soft. Froze. With her glass bow drawn and her glass smile on her face. Blind.

And Wren loosed.

---

She missed.

She'd known she would. Right arm. Shaking. She missed by a foot. The arrow went past the glass woman's shoulder and into the ditch behind her with a hiss.

It didn't matter.

The glass woman was frozen. Blind. Grey with fog.

And Kestrel was beside her. With her hand up. And the air in front of it going thick and dark and wavy.

The glass woman melted.

Slumped. Ran. Into the ditch. Into a puddle of glowing glass. Went dark. Cooled. Into a lump. Clear. Flat.

Nothing in it.

---

Wren sat on her horse and looked at it.

She was shaking. All of her. Not just her right arm. She sat there and shook and couldn't stop.

"*You missed*," said Kestrel. Gently.

"*I know*."

"*By a foot*."

"*I know*, *Kestrel.*"

"*Good*," said the fire-witch. "*If you'd hit it, I'd have worried.*"

Wren laughed.

It came out of her cracked and wet and sudden. She put her face in her good hand and laughed until she was crying and crying until she was laughing, on her horse, in the dark, in the grey, with a lump of cooling glass in the ditch in front of her that had worn her face.

By dawn it was over.

The glass had stopped coming. Not all at once. Fewer, and fewer, out of the ditches and ruts and frozen puddles along the line. Then none. As if whoever was sending them had got tired. Or angry. Or had somewhere else to be.

The Keepers sat down where they stood. In the grey. Between the lanterns. With their swords across their knees and their heads in their hands.

Wren rode the line one more time. North. Then south. Slowly. Looking.

Eleven lanterns broken. Eleven gaps in the line. Eleven little flames lying in the grey grass in the wreckage of their lanterns, still burning, each in a circle of green no bigger than a plate.

And the grey coming through the gaps.

Not fast. Not a flood. A seep. Like water through a cracked dam.

---

They set new lanterns.

There were always new lanterns now. That was the thing. Every village they'd passed through on the road west had come out to the road with lanterns. Old ones. New ones. Iron and tin and clay. *For the line*. Given. Set in the carts without a word. They had two hundred in the carts.

They set eleven in the gaps.

And buried Ada Fell.

---

She was forty-four. A Keeper for twenty years. Marta Fell's cousin. She'd watched the Ashbourne Stone for Roole for three years, in the winter of the weddings. She'd been kind to Ivy Moss on the day she Kindled.

Her own face had done it. Her own glass face. With her own staff. In the dark. While she stood in front of a lantern.

They buried her by the lantern.

Elowen read the words. With a bandage round her head and blood still on her collar. *Into the Lady's keeping, with a light left on.*

And they left the lantern burning on the grave.

---

Wren sat by the grave after.

In the grey. In the dawn. With her bow across her knees and her gold fingers lying on the wood.

She looked at them.

Three. Gold. Smooth and bright and cold. Curled like claws. Not bending.

She'd seen them whole. Last night. In glass. Five fingers. Curled round a bow. The way they used to be.

She thought about it for a long time.

Then she lifted the hand. And turned it over. And looked at the palm. At the gold fingertips. At the place on the side of the first finger where the bowstring used to cut her, every day, for eighteen years, and leave a callus.

The callus was gold too.

She'd never noticed that.

She ran her right thumb over it. Gold. Smooth. A little ridge. Where the string had been. Kept. Exactly as it was on the Night of Glass. Every line. Every year.

*This is what you are*.

She closed her hand. As much as it would close. And held it against her chest.

"*No*," said Wren Ashdown. Quietly. To nobody. To the grey. "*This is what I was. I'm what comes next.*"

---

Kestrel came and sat beside her.

Without a word. In her singed red coat. With a heel of bread in one hand and an apple in the other. She held out the bread.

Wren took it.

They ate. In the grey. By Ada Fell's grave. With the lantern burning.

"*She'll be angry*," said Kestrel. After a while. "*The glass one. You broke her toys.*"

"*I know*."

"*She'll do something else*."

"*I know*."

"*Something worse*." Kestrel bit into the apple. "*They always do something worse. That's what they're like, the ones who've lived too long. They get bored. They get angry. They get worse.*"

Wren didn't answer.

She looked east. Toward the world. Toward the Vale. Toward a forge with twenty chimneys and a yard with a gate and a man in the yard with a baby on his hip.

*He said no*. *So she'll come herself.*

---

The Stones went dark at noon.

---

Wren didn't see it. Not the Stones. There were no Stones in the grey. But she felt it. Everyone on the line felt it.

A *cold*.

Not the grey's cold. Not the flat still painting-cold of the snow that didn't melt. A *moving* cold. Like a wind off ice. Like a draught from a door opened somewhere very far away, very deep. Coming up out of the ground. Through her boots. Through her bones. Into the place under her breastbone where the wind lived.

And going out.

All at once. Everywhere. Like a candle being snuffed.

---

Elowen's raven came at dusk.

Not Elowen's. Seraphine's. From the Forge. Black. Ruffled. Panting. With a paper on its leg that Wren unrolled with shaking fingers and read by the light of Ada Fell's lantern.

Seraphine's hand. Tiny. Neat. Shaking too.

---

*Every Stone in Caldor went dark at noon. All at once. The white glow. Gone.*

*Not because she's left them.*

*Because she's gone through them. All the way. Down.*

*I can feel it through Mal. The cold's not spread out anymore. It's not in the edges. It's in one place. Deep. At the bottom. In the middle.*

*She's there, Wren.*

*She's at the centre.*

*She's standing in front of the crown in the road.*

---

And under it, in a different hand. Square. Heavy. Pressing too hard. A smith's hand.

---

*She's fine. Lark's fine. I'm with her. I'm not letting go of her hand.*

*Come home.*

*L.*
