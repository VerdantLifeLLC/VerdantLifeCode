# The Bread That Wouldn't Rise

The dough sat in the bowl.

Lex looked at it. Wren looked at it. Elowen Dunmore, Knight of the Keepers, in her mail and her grey cloak with snow on the shoulders, looked at it with her arms folded and the face of a woman who'd already looked at it a great deal and was tired of it.

It was a good bowl. Brown glaze, chipped at the lip. A clean cloth folded back from it. A kitchen as warm as a forge, with a big black range and copper pans and bunches of thyme hanging from the beams, and the smell of woodsmoke and flour.

And in the bowl, a round pale lump of dough.

"*Four days*," said the baker.

Her name was Marta Fell. Keeper Ada Fell's cousin. A broad brisk woman of fifty with forearms like hams and flour to the elbow and a mouth that had set itself, over four days, into a straight hard line.

"*I made it on Midwinter morning*," she said. "*Before dawn. Like always. Good flour, from the mill at Hobb's Cross. Barm fresh from the brewer that morning. Warm water. Salt. Kneaded it a quarter hour.*" She folded her arms. "*Covered it. Set it by the range. Went to bed.*"

They all looked at the dough.

"*Four days*," said Marta Fell again. "*By the range. And it hasn't moved a hair. Not up. Not down. Not sour. Not crusted. Nothing.*" She reached out and lifted the cloth all the way off. "*Smell it.*"

Lex leaned down and smelled it.

Yeast. Flour. Warm water. The clean sharp smell of new dough, the smell of every bakehouse he'd ever walked past at dawn.

Fresh.

As if it had been made a minute ago.

---

"*It's not the barm*," said Marta Fell. "*Before you say it. Everyone says it. The Keeper said it. I took the same barm next door to Bet Arley's and she made a batch and it rose in an hour like a bride's cake.*" She looked at the bowl. "*Bet Arley's is six doors east. Mine's the last house on the west road.*"

Lex put out his hand.

He didn't touch the dough. He held his palm an inch above it. Felt the warmth of it. The life in it. The small busy world of a thousand thousand yeast-things that should have been eating and breathing and pushing the dough up toward the cloth.

And he reached.

Not with fire. With the other thing. The newest thing. The seventh Well, the one he'd learned last, the one that wasn't heat or weight or water or wind, but *room*. The empty space every new thing is made into.

*Hollow*.

---

He'd learned it at the Blightmarch. From his wife, actually. Lying in the grass beside her on a summer night with his hand on her belly, feeling their daughter kick for the first time. Feeling the room she was in. The room Wren had made in herself, without trying, without knowing, for something new to grow into.

Every casting needs a mould. Every forging needs an anvil. Every new thing needs somewhere to *be*.

He'd been reaching for it ever since. Listening. Answering. In the spring, in Ashbourne, with gold dust coming down on the green, he'd learned to make room in front of the gilt before it set. *Speaking*.

He reached for it now. Into the dough. Into the warm pale lump in the brown bowl.

And there wasn't any.

---

Not a little. None.

He could feel the yeast. Alive. Not dead, not cold, not poisoned. Alive and ready and waiting. Like a crowd at a door. Like horses at a gate.

And nowhere to go.

No room in front of them. No space to grow into. Not because something was blocking it—he'd have felt that, a wall, a lid—but because the room had *gone*. Been drawn out. The way the air goes out of a bellows when you lean on it. The way water goes out of a bucket with a hole in the bottom.

West.

He could feel it going. Even now. Even as he held his hand over the bowl. A faint, steady, endless pull. Out of the dough, out of the kitchen, out of the walls and the beams and the thyme hanging from them, out through the west window and away. Toward the edge.

Like a draught under a door.

---

He pushed.

He made room. With his hand. The way he'd made it in front of the gilt on the green. Cupped the air an inch over the dough, and *asked*, and felt the seventh Well stir in him, faint and far—three hundred miles off, a pool in a green field, frightened—and answer.

The dough moved.

Everyone saw it. Marta Fell made a small sound. The pale lump in the bowl *lifted*. Swelled. Rounded. A finger's width. Two. Like a sleeper taking a breath.

And then sank back.

Slowly. Softly. To exactly where it had been. As if the breath had gone out of it again. As if the room he'd made had been poured out of the bowl as fast as he poured it in, and gone west, through the window, into the snow.

Like pouring water on sand.

---

He took his hand away.

"*Well*," said Marta Fell. Very quietly.

Nobody said anything.

Lex stood in the warm kitchen with flour on his boots and looked at the dough. Then he looked out of the west window. At the snow. At the white road out of the village, running west. At the low grey sky over it.

He couldn't see anything. Snow. Hedges. A field. A copse of black trees.

He could feel it. Right there. Past the copse. Past the field.

Something drinking.

---

The boy's name was Davy Pruett.

He was nine. Red-haired and gap-toothed and enormously proud of his bandage. He sat on an upturned bucket in his mother's yard at the next farm along the west road and unwrapped it for them very carefully, as if unwrapping a present, and held out his hand.

Across the palm, from the base of the thumb to the edge, a cut.

Clean. Straight. Not deep. The kind of cut you'd get from the edge of a scythe if you grabbed the blade instead of the snath because you were nine and showing off.

Red. Open. A thin bright line of blood standing in it.

As if he'd done it a minute ago.

"*Nine days*," said his mother. From the door. With her arms wrapped round herself. "*Nine days, and it hasn't so much as scabbed. It doesn't bleed. It doesn't close. It doesn't go bad. I've had the Keeper's healer to it and the hedge-woman from Barrow's Knap and they both stood where you're standing and looked at it and went away.*" Her voice cracked. "*He can't hold a spoon.*"

"*I can*," said Davy Pruett. "*With the other one.*"

Wren crouched down in front of him.

She didn't say anything at first. She just looked at the cut. Then at the boy. Then she held out her own left hand. Palm down. So he could see the gold.

Davy's eyes went very round.

"*Is that real*?"

"*Real gold*," said Wren. "*Right down to the bone. I can't bend them.*" She wiggled the other two fingers. "*These ones I can.*"

"*Does it hurt*?"

"*No*."

"*Mine doesn't hurt either*," said Davy. "*That's the funny thing. It's open and it doesn't hurt.*" He looked at his palm. "*It's like it's waiting.*"

Wren didn't answer that.

She put her good hand over his. Very gently. And held it. And looked up at Lex over the boy's red head.

He knew the look. He'd seen it on her face in the yard at Harrow in the summer, with gold coming down on her drawing hand and her bow up and the arrows still flying.

*Fix it*.

---

He tried.

He knelt in the snow beside her and held his hand over the boy's palm and reached for room. And felt it. The same as the dough. The cut, waiting. The flesh on either side of it ready to knit, the way flesh is always ready to knit. Alive. Patient.

And nowhere to knit into.

He made room. He pushed. The edges of the cut drew together—Davy gasped—a hair's breadth. Pink. Closing.

And opened again. Softly. As the room drained away.

West.

---

He sat back on his heels in the snow.

"*Sorry*," he said. To the boy. To his mother. To the cut. "*I'm sorry.*"

"*That's all right*," said Davy Pruett kindly. "*The Keeper's healer said that too.*"

---

They rode west.

Lex and Wren and Elowen, on Keepers' horses, along the west road out of Westerling in the snow. Elowen's two sergeants behind. And behind them, in a cart, a load of stakes.

Ash poles. Five feet long. Pointed at one end. Painted white at the other.

"*Seraphine's idea*," said Elowen. Riding beside him. "*Three weeks ago. When the first letters came. She wrote back by return and said put a stake in the ground at every farm that's been touched. Write the date on it. Then go back every day and see where the touching's got to.*" She nodded ahead. "*So we did.*"

He saw them as they rode.

White-topped poles. In the snow. Along the road. In the fields on either side. In a long ragged line, north and south, as far as he could see, every few hundred yards. And another line, a little further west. And another. And another.

Like the rings of a tree.

"*Slow, at first*," said Elowen. "*A few hundred yards a day. Then faster.*" She didn't look at him. "*It's near a mile a day now. One day, in the big wind, nearly three. And it's still getting faster. Straight east. Along the whole edge, as far as we've been able to ride.*"

"*How far is that*?"

"*North to the Teeth*. *South to the sea cliffs at Morrow.*" She shrugged. "*Two hundred miles of it. Maybe more. My people are thin.*"

"*Seven weeks*," said Wren.

"*Nine miles*," said Elowen Dunmore. "*Give or take. It reached Westerling yesterday.*" She looked back, east, toward the village. "*Ashbourne's two hundred and ten.*"

Nobody said anything.

They rode on. West. Past the stakes. Going back in time. Each line with a date on it in black paint, in a Keeper's careful hand, and each line a little closer to the last, the further west they went, the way the rings of a tree crowd together near the heart.

---

He felt it before they'd gone a hundred yards.

Not a line. Nothing so clear. There was no wall in the snow, no change in the colour of the sky. The hedges went on. The fields went on. A crow went over, cawing.

But the snow was different.

He couldn't have said how. It was white. It was snow. It lay on the hedges and the fields and the road exactly the way snow lies on things. But it lay there the way snow lies in a painting. Not falling. Not settling. Not melting where the sun touched it, or drifting where the wind took it. Just *there*. Finished.

And the quiet.

He noticed it the way you notice a clock has stopped. Not at once. After a while. The crow had gone. The horses' hooves made no sound. Not muffled—*none*. He could see them lifting and falling in the snow and couldn't hear them land.

"*Lex*," said Wren. Very low.

"*I know*."

"*The wind's still blowing*." She had her face turned up. Her eyes half closed. The way she held it when she was listening to the air. Mistwalker. Gale Commanding. She'd felt wind the way other people feel heat since she was twenty. "*Feel it. It's blowing. West. Hard.*" She opened her eyes. "*And the snow isn't moving.*"

He looked.

The wind was blowing. He could feel it on his face, on the back of his neck, pulling at his coat. Steady. Endless. West. Pouring past him toward the edge like a river pouring into a hole.

And the snow on the hedge beside him lay perfectly still.

As if the wind were going somewhere else. Through it. Past it. Not touching it.

As if the snow had stopped noticing.

---

Elowen drew rein.

They'd come to the first line of stakes. White-topped. In the snow. Running north and south across the road and away over the fields. And on the nearest one, in black paint, yesterday's date.

"*Here*," she said. "*Yesterday.*" She nodded back, east. At the village. At the last house on the west road, with its chimney smoking. "*It'll be at Bet Arley's by tonight.*"

Lex got down.

He walked to the stake. And past it. West. Into what yesterday had been.

And stopped.

---

He could feel it now. On his skin. In his hands. In the place under his breastbone where the Wells lived.

The room going.

Out of everything. Out of the snow and the hedge and the frozen furrows under the snow and the roots under the furrows. Out of the air. Out of *him*. A faint, soft, endless pull, west, like standing in a river with the current pulling at your legs. Like the draught under a door.

It didn't hurt. That was the thing. It didn't hurt at all.

It just—*took*. Gently. A little at a time. The room in front of every moment. The space where the next thing would happen. Took it, and drew it west, and left everything exactly where it was.

Waiting.

*Kept*, he thought. *Not gold. Not stopped. Just—kept.*

*The bread didn't notice the night go by*.

---

He reached for fire.

He didn't decide to. It came up in him the way it always came, when he was frightened or angry or didn't understand something. Heat. Weight. The roar of a forge with the bellows going. Flame Commanding. He'd brought a dragon's breath out of the ground in the Greyspine. He'd burned a thousand moths out of the air over the Blightmarch with black heat.

He held up his hand. Palm out. West.

And let it go.

---

It went.

A gout of it. White-gold. A column of fire the width of a cart, roaring out of his palm and across the snow toward the west. He felt the heat of it on his face. Saw the snow go up in steam where it passed. Heard it—

He didn't hear it.

He saw it roar. He didn't hear it.

And a hundred yards out, over the field, toward the old edge, it—

*Went*.

Not out. Not quenched. It didn't hiss or smoke or gutter. It just went *thinner*. And thinner. Paler. Like a line of ink in water. Like a shout in a very big empty room. Spreading. Thinning. Drawn out west across the snow, out and out and out, toward a horizon he couldn't see, until there was nothing left of it but a faint warmth on the air.

And then not that.

---

He stood in the snow with his hand still up.

He'd poured enough fire into the world to forge a wagon-tyre. He'd felt it go.

The snow at his feet hadn't melted.

---

Wren came and stood beside him.

She didn't say anything. She took his hand. His raised one. And brought it down. And held it. Her right hand in his left.

"*Like water on sand*," he said.

"*I saw*."

"*It just drinks it*." He looked west. "*Whatever it is. Whatever's out there. It drinks it and drinks it and it's never full.*"

"*Because it's empty*," said Wren. Quietly. "*It's been empty for nine hundred years.*" She looked west too. Her eyes were very green in the white light. "*Like Mal was. Before.*"

He looked at her.

She'd said it simply. The way she said most things. And it went into him like a nail into green wood.

*Like Mal was*.

The grey country. Nine hundred years with all its room drunk away. A country the Crowned had emptied, the way they'd emptied the Well. Starving. Hungry. *Empty is hungry*. Mal had said that. At the rim. *I was empty, and empty is hungry*.

And now there was no edge between it and the world. No wall. No Veil. Nothing at all.

And it was drinking.

---

They rode on to the old edge.

It took the rest of the day. Nine miles of snow and silence and white-topped stakes, crowding closer and closer together. Past farms with no smoke in their chimneys, empty, the families gone east to cousins. Past a mill with its wheel turning in the stream and no sound coming off it at all. Past an orchard where every tree stood in the snow with its bare black branches up like hands and you could feel them, Lex could feel them, every bud on every branch ready and waiting and going nowhere.

Past the gold ones.

---

He saw the first at noon.

A man. In a field. Off to the right of the road, two hundred yards. Gold. Tall and stooped, in the clothes of a groom from nine hundred years ago, a short tunic and leggings and a hood. With a gold bridle in his hand. Walking.

East.

So slowly. Lex could see him move only if he looked away and back. A finger's width. A finger's width. Across the snow. Through the field. Toward a hedge. Toward the world.

And then another. Further off. A woman. With a gold basket on her hip.

And another. Two together. Small. Children. Holding hands.

And more. And more. As they rode west. Scattered across the snow on both sides of the road. Dozens, then hundreds. Gold figures in the white, a long way apart, all walking. All facing east. All so slow they might have been statues someone had set out across the fields in the night, for no reason, facing the sunrise.

He could hear them. Even through the silence. Even here, where his own horse's hooves made no sound.

One note.

*...home...*

---

"*They don't do anything*," said Elowen. Riding beside him. Not looking at them. She'd stopped looking at them, Lex thought, a long time ago. "*They just walk. Through fences. Through barns. A woman at Hobb's Cross woke up with one walking through her kitchen. Took him all day to get from the door to the hearth. She sat in the corner with the children and a poker and watched him the whole time.*" She shook her head. "*He didn't even look at her. Walked through the hearth and out the back wall. There's a hole in it now. Man-shaped.*"

"*Walked through the wall*?"

"*Not through*. *Into. And kept walking.*" Elowen's mouth twisted. "*They don't stop. You can't stop them. The carter at Hobb's Cross put an axe in one. It's still in him. He's two miles east of there now, still walking, with an axe in his shoulder.*"

"*Do they hurt anyone*?"

"*No*," said Elowen. "*No. Never. They don't touch anyone. They just go round you if you stand in the way. Very slowly. It takes hours. But they go round.*" She was quiet for a while. "*It's the eyes, Lex. That's what frightens people. Not the gold. The eyes. They move. They look at you. They look at you as you go past, and you know—*" She stopped.

"*Know what*?"

"*That they know*," said Elowen Dunmore. "*All of it. What's happened to them. How long it's been. Where they're going.*" She looked away. "*And that they'll never get there. Not at that pace.*"

---

They came to Nan Ashby's croft at dusk.

---

It was a low stone house in a fold of the hills, eight miles from the edge, with one tree by it and a byre. No smoke. The door shut. Snow drifted against it.

Elowen didn't stop.

"*She's not there*," she said. "*She's never there.*"

They rode on. Over the last hill. And down.

---

The field was still there.

Gold. All of it. A long slope of gold grass running down to a gold hedge, under a sky going purple with dusk. Not under snow. The snow had fallen on it and lay on it, white, and the gold showed through underneath, glowing faintly, the way coals glow under ash.

Forty sheep. Gold. Heads down. Grazing.

A hawk. Gold. Overhead. Pinned to the sky mid-stoop with its wings half-folded, twenty feet up, the snow lying in a thin white line along the top of each wing.

And among the sheep, tall and thin, mid-step, with his crook out and snow on his cap—

A boy.

---

And at the edge of the gold, on the grey ground, on a three-legged stool, wrapped in a shawl and a horse-blanket with a basket at her feet and snow on her shoulders—

A woman. Talking.

---

"*—and the byre roof's held, which I didn't think it would, after that wind, but your father built it, and he was a fool about most things but he could build a roof. And the hens are in the kitchen. I know. I know what you'd say. But it's cold, Col, it's so cold this winter, and they're only hens.*"

She didn't look round as they came down the hill.

She didn't look round when they stopped, a little way off, and got down from the horses, and stood in the snow.

She went on talking.

"*—and the smith came back. Did I tell you? The one with the handprint. He's standing behind me now with his wife and the Knight. I can hear them breathing. I expect they want to look at you.*" A pause. "*Everyone wants to look at you.*"

Lex stood very still.

Nan Ashby turned round on her stool.

---

She was older than he remembered. Much older. In the spring she'd been a hard brown woman of fifty with a basket and a sharp tongue, walking eight miles every morning to sit at the edge of the gold and bring her son his supper. She looked seventy now. Grey-faced. Hollow-cheeked. Her eyes very bright and very dry.

"*Smith*," she said.

"*Mistress Ashby*."

"*You came back*." She looked at him. Up and down. At the handprint. "*You said there was a way. In the summer. You said you'd found the room in front of it.*"

"*I did*. *Before it sets.*" His throat was tight. "*He's set.*"

"*I know he's set*. *I can see he's set.*" She turned back to the field. To the boy. Gold. Mid-step. With his eyes moving. Following her. Always following her. "*They carried me east in the summer. The Keepers. When the King came through. I bit one.*" She almost smiled. "*I came back in the autumn. When he'd gone. I've been here every day since.*"

"*It's not safe*," said Elowen. Gently. "*Nan. The grey's going past you. It went past you weeks ago. You're sitting in it.*"

"*So's he*," said Nan Ashby.

---

Lex looked at the boy.

At Col Ashby. Fourteen. Gold from his cap to his boots. Standing in a gold field at the edge of the world with his crook out, the way he'd been standing since the spring, since the first gold dust came through the first crack in the Veil and settled on him while he was walking out to his flock on a Tuesday with the wind from the west.

His eyes moved.

They moved to Lex. Held there. A long moment.

Then went back to his mother.

---

He knelt down beside her stool.

Not to try anything. There was nothing to try. He'd tried in the spring. He'd poured fire into the gold until his hands blistered and it hadn't even warmed. He'd reached for room in it and found none—not drunk away, like the bread, but *never there*. Kept. Finished. A door with nothing behind it.

He knelt down beside her because she was alone.

Wren knelt on the other side.

And the three of them sat there in the snow at the edge of the gold field in the dusk, and looked at the boy, and Nan Ashby went on talking.

---

"*—and you'll never guess what. The bread won't rise. At Westerling. Marta Fell's in a state. Her bread won't rise and the Pruett boy's hand won't close and nobody's hens are laying.*" She pulled the blanket tighter. "*I said to Marta. I said, now you know. Now you know what it's like. Waiting for something to happen that won't.*"

A long silence.

"*She didn't like that*," said Nan Ashby. "*But it's true.*"

---

They left her there. At full dark. Because she wouldn't come, and they couldn't make her.

Wren left her own scarf on the old woman's shoulders. Elowen left a lantern, lit, on the grey ground by the stool. Lex left nothing. He didn't have anything to leave.

He looked back once from the top of the hill.

A small gold light at the edge of a field of gold. And a woman on a stool beside it, talking.

And past her—past the field, past the hedge, past the last of the gold—the edge.

---

There was nothing there.

That was what he couldn't get used to. He'd seen the Veil. Twice. Grey. Hanging from nowhere. Falling to nowhere. Moving very slightly, the way a curtain moves in a room with a window open somewhere behind it. A wall across the end of the world.

It was gone.

Past the gold field, the snow went on. White. Then, without any line at all, without any seam, without anything to say *here*, it went grey. Grey-gold. Fine as flour. Lying in drifts. Going on. West. Flat. To the end of everything.

And running away into it, starting at the foot of the place where the Veil had been, broad, straight, white as old bone—

A road.

And at the end of the road, on the edge of the dark sky, very small, very far—

Towers.

Glass. Iron. Gold. Bone. And one wrapped in something brown and dry, like a bramble patch at the end of winter.

---

The wind went past him.

West. Down the hill. Over the gold field. Over the woman on the stool and her son. Over the hedge. Onto the white road. Into the grey.

Endless. Soft. Steady. Like a river pouring into a hole in the ground.

And the grey country drank it.

He could feel it drinking. From here. From the top of the hill. A great dry patient thirst, nine hundred years old, as wide as the world. Not cruel. Not angry. Not anything. Just *empty*. Taking the wind. Taking the room. Taking everything new that came within reach and drawing it in, and in, and in, and never being full.

*A mile a day*, he thought. *And faster.*

*Ashbourne's two hundred and ten*.

---

They stayed the night at Greyfall.

It was a Keepers' outpost on a crag above the old edge, stone-walled, with a tower and a gate. It had been the last outpost before the Veil for two hundred years. The Wardens had built it to watch the grey and report what moved there.

It was empty now. Elowen's people slept at Westerling.

She'd brought them for the gate.

---

He was standing in it.

Exactly where he'd stood in the summer, when the Gilded King walked out of the crack in the Veil and east across the world. Square in the middle of the gateway. Feet planted. A staff held crosswise in both hands, chest-high, barring the way. The way you'd bar a door with your own body.

Gold.

Daxian Roole. Commander of the Keepers of the Stones. Warden-Captain once. Inquisitor. The man who'd hunted Lex across Caldor when he was nineteen, and been spared, and changed sides, and led the reformed Wardens for five years with a stiff arm and a stiff back and a stiff sense of what was right.

He'd sent his Keepers east. He'd sent everyone east. Then he'd stood in the gate.

*You're not coming through this gate*.

And the King had walked up to him. And looked at him. And reached out, very gently, and touched his face.

*Somebody has to stand somewhere*, Roole had said. Elowen had told them. One of the Keepers had heard it, running. The last thing he said.

And the King had said *yes*. *Yes, they do. Forever.*

---

There was snow on his shoulders.

Elowen brushed it off. Carefully. The way you'd brush snow off an old man's coat at a door. Then she stood in front of him in the dark gateway, with a lantern in her hand, and said, quite ordinarily:

"*Evening, Commander*."

The gold face didn't change. It couldn't.

The eyes moved. To her. To the lantern. To Lex and Wren behind her.

"*Brought the smith*," said Elowen. "*And his wife. They've been to see Nan Ashby. Bread's still not rising in Westerling. The line moved a mile and a quarter today.*" She set the lantern down at his gold feet. "*I'll be back on Thursday.*"

She turned away.

Lex saw her face as she passed him. In the lantern light. Just for a moment.

He looked away.

---

He stood in front of Roole for a long time after she'd gone.

He didn't try anything. There was nothing to try.

He thought about a forest in the spring, seven years ago. A Warden-Captain on his back in the mud with Lex's hammer at his throat. Grey eyes, hard. *Do it*.

He hadn't done it.

He thought about the same man, a year later, in the ruins of Ashbourne, holding out his hand. *I was wrong. About you. About all of it. I'd like to help.*

He thought about him standing here. In the summer. Alone. With a staff.

*Somebody has to stand somewhere*.

"*You did*," said Lex. Out loud. To the gold. "*You stood.*"

The eyes moved. To him.

And held.

---

He didn't sleep much that night.

He lay on a Keepers' cot in the empty tower at Greyfall with Wren asleep beside him, her gold fingers spread on the blanket so they wouldn't press, and listened to the wind. Going west. Past the walls. Past the gate. Past Roole. Down the hill and over the edge and into the grey. All night.

Endless.

He thought about bread.

He thought about a boy's hand, open, waiting.

He thought about nine thousand gold people walking across the world a finger's width at a time toward a home that had been gone for nine hundred years.

He thought about the dead at Mal's rim, swaying, saying their names.

He thought about three Crowned, somewhere in the world, full of what Mal needed, who'd never given anything back in nine hundred years.

*We ask*, he'd said. At the rim. As if it were easy.

He lay in the dark and didn't know how.

---

In the morning, at the farm below Greyfall, an old woman met them at her gate.

She'd heard there was a smith. With a handprint. She'd been waiting for him since dawn, in her shawl, in the snow. Eighty if she was a day. Bent. Bright-eyed. With a clay pipe in her teeth and a look of great satisfaction, like someone who has a piece of news nobody else has heard.

"*You'll be wanting to know about the big gold one*," she said.

Lex stopped.

"*Came by here*," said the old woman. "*A month back. Just after the edge came down. Walking. Very slow. Very careful. Like a man walking through a house full of glass.*" She took the pipe out of her mouth. "*Stopped at my gate. Asked if he could come in for tea.*"

"*What did you say*?"

"*Said no*," said the old woman. "*Course I did. I'm not daft. I've seen the field.*" She put the pipe back. "*So he sat on the wall. Outside. In the snow. And I brought him a cup. Old one. Chipped. Didn't care if it went gold.*" A puff of smoke. "*It went gold.*"

"*What did he want*?"

"*Want*?" The old woman considered this. "*Didn't seem to want anything. Sat on my wall for an hour and drank my tea and asked about my grandmother.*"

"*Your grandmother*?"

"*Her name*. *What she was like. When she died.*" The old woman's bright eyes narrowed. "*Asked if she'd been frightened. At the end. If it hurt. If there was anyone with her.*" A pause. "*Asked about my mother, too. And my husband. And my sister that drowned. Wanted to know all of it. How they went. Who held their hands.*"

Lex didn't say anything.

"*Strange sort of king*," said the old woman. "*Sat on my wall with tears running down his face. Gold tears. They went on the snow and the snow went gold.*" She tapped the pipe on the gatepost. "*Then he got up and thanked me very kindly and asked me the way to Ashbourne.*"

---

"*Ashbourne*," said Lex.

"*Said he had a friend there*," said the old woman. "*An old woman. With one eye.*" She grinned round the pipe. Three teeth. "*Said she'd told his steward she wasn't greedy. To his face. In a storm of gold. Said he'd been thinking about it ever since.*"
