# Dawn

Nobody slept that night either.

Lex sat on the grass in front of the threshold with Lark asleep in his lap and Wren's head on his shoulder and watched the door.

It stood open. Antho stood in it. He hadn't moved since the King went through. He stood there with one hand on the frame and one on the blue wood, the way a man stands in his own doorway at night when someone he loves has gone down the road and he's watching till he can't see the lantern any more.

Behind him, the soft dark. The stars. The shapes going in.

And at the bottom, very far off, the pool. With its ring of lanterns. And an old man's dust on the rim, grey, fine, beside a boy who hadn't let go of his hand.

---

Wren's fingers were pink.

She kept looking at them. In the lantern light. In the dark. Flexing them. Two fingers and a thumb on her left hand. Pink and stiff and cold and alive. She'd make a fist, and open it, and look at it, and make a fist again.

"*They hurt*," she said. Wonderingly. Around midnight. "*Lex. They hurt.*"

"*They've been asleep*," said Lex. "*Nine months. They're waking up.*"

"*Like when you sit on your foot*."

"*Like that*."

She was quiet for a long time.

"*I'll have to learn again*," said Wren. "*Both hands. The left's forgotten. And the right's learned it wrong.*" She looked at her pink fingers. "*I'll miss trees all summer.*"

"*Yes*," said Lex.

She laughed. Very quietly. Into his shoulder.

"*Good*," said Wren Ashdown.

---

Brannoc sat a little way off. On a stone. With his hands in his lap. Looking at them. Turning them over. Gold no more. Old fingers. Scarred. Burned at the tips.

Every so often he held one up close to the nearest lantern and squinted at it. As if checking.

Tilda sat beside him. With her right hand in her left. Holding it. As if it might go somewhere.

Neither of them said anything.

---

The Ferryman sat on the step.

With his back against the frame and his oar across his knees and his lantern beside him on the grey stone. With his eyes closed. Lex didn't think he was asleep. He didn't think the Ferryman slept. He thought he was just sitting. The way an old man sits on a bench in the sun in the afternoon, not doing anything, because for the first time in his life there's nothing he has to do.

The lantern on the stone flickered. Small. Gold.

Every so often it said something. Quietly. To itself. As if taking notes.

*[...a threshold of grey stone, approximately sixteen feet...]*

*[...a door of apple wood which appears to be in flower...]*

*[...blue paint. A great many small pictures. A loaf, possibly. Or a cushion...]*

Lex didn't listen. He couldn't. He sat on the grass with his daughter asleep in his lap and looked at the door and didn't listen to Ember describe it to nobody.

---

It got cold, toward morning.

Not the grey cold. Not the glass cold. The ordinary cold of a summer night in the open, an hour before dawn, when the dew comes down and the stars—

There were stars.

---

Lex looked up.

He didn't know when they'd come. He'd been looking at the door. But they were there. Over the rise. Over the camp. Over the four hundred lanterns burning straight up in the still air. Faint. Pale. A scatter of them in the dark.

There'd been no stars in the grey country.

He looked at them for a long time.

Then he looked at the grass under his hand.

It was wet. Dew. Cold and wet and real.

And it was green.

---

He hadn't noticed. Nobody had. It had been dark. The grass on the rise had been green since they made camp, because of the lanterns, because the lanterns held it. But the grass beyond the lanterns—the grass on the long slope going down to the threshold, beyond the last ring, where nobody had put a lantern because the door was there—

He'd sat down on it at dusk. It had been grey.

It was green now.

---

"*Wren*," he said.

She lifted her head.

He didn't say anything else. He just pointed. Out. Past the lanterns. Past the last ring. To the slope going away north along the end of the road, where the seam had been. Where the grey had lain like a picture of a field.

It wasn't a picture.

It was dark, and it was hard to see. But Lex could see it. In the light of the last lanterns. Grass. Long grass. Dew on it. Moving. Very slightly. In a breath of air that came from nowhere in particular, from the east, the way the morning air comes before the sun.

And somewhere out in it—far out, beyond the reach of the lanterns, in the dark—

A bird.

---

Not a song. Not yet. A sound. A single sleepy *chk*. The kind of sound a blackbird makes in a hedge when something wakes it before it's ready.

Then nothing.

Then, a long way off, another.

---

Lex stood up.

Carefully. With Lark asleep against his chest. With his ribs. He stood up on the green slope in front of the threshold and looked east.

Back along the end of the road. Over the rise. Over the camp. Over the four hundred lanterns. To the green road they'd walked for nineteen days, with a light every mile, going away into the dark like a string of beads.

And past it.

To the sky.

---

It was going pale.

---

Not grey. Not the flat page-colour of the grey country, that had been the colour of every sky for five months. Pale. The pale of a real sky before dawn. Blue under it. Faint. And at the very bottom, at the edge of the world, where the land met the sky in the east—

A line.

Thin. Bright. The colour of the inside of a shell.

---

The camp was waking.

He heard it go back along the rise. People sitting up. People standing. People turning round. A murmur. Then quiet. Four hundred people standing in the dark on a hill at the end of the world, turned to the east, looking at a line of colour on the edge of the sky.

Nobody said anything.

The line got brighter.

---

His mother was beside him. He didn't know when she'd come. With the clay lamp in her hands. Burning. Not needed. She held it anyway.

Antho had come out of the door. He stood on the threshold. Just on it. On the grey stone. With his back to the soft dark and his face to the east, so that he was in both at once, the room and the world. Seraphine came down the slope and stood beside him, and he put his arm round her without looking.

The Ferryman opened his eyes.

---

The sun came up.

---

Lex had seen a lot of sunrises. Every day of his life, nearly. A smith gets up before the sun. He'd stood in the Forge doorway at Ashbourne ten thousand mornings with a cup of tea and watched it come up over the Vale. He'd watched it come up on the Spire roof, the morning he asked Wren. He'd watched it come up over the Pale Wastes, and the Teeth, and the sea at Gull's Reach.

He'd never seen one like this.

---

It came up over the world.

Over the green road. Over the Vale, two hundred miles off. Over Ashbourne, and Harrow, and the Teeth, and the east. Gold. Slow. Enormous. The whole rim of the sky catching fire, the way a forge catches when the bellows go.

And the light came west.

Across the land. Over the hills and fields and woods and villages. Two hundred miles of them. Grey. Still. A picture of a world.

And everywhere it touched—

---

Colour.

---

He watched it come. Everyone did. They stood on the rise in the dawn and watched the light come west across the grey country like water across a floor.

And behind it, the colour came back.

Not all at once. Not like a lamp being lit. Like a painting being finished. Like someone very patient going over a drawing with a brush, one field at a time, one hedge, one tree. Green into the grass. Brown into the plough. Gold into the barley. White into the birches, and black into the marks on their trunks, and green—the soft bright green of June—into their leaves.

He could see it moving. Toward them. Across the hills. A line of colour. Like the grey had come, months ago, a line in the grass. But the other way.

Going home.

---

The birds woke.

All at once. Everywhere. As the light reached them. As the colour reached them. The hedges on the hillsides. The woods. The fields. He heard them start, far off, to the east, a long way back along the green road—a sound like a kettle beginning to sing—and then nearer, and nearer, and then all round them.

Blackbird. Wren. Thrush. Lark.

A chiffchaff, somewhere close, in a hedge at the bottom of the rise, saying its own name over and over, as if it had been afraid it would forget.

---

Lark woke up.

On his chest. In the noise. In the light. She lifted her head off his shoulder with her hair flat on one side and her eyes screwed up against the sun, furious at being woken.

Then she heard the birds.

Her eyebrows went up.

She turned her head. Slowly. On his shoulder. Toward the east. Toward the sun. Toward the gold light coming across two hundred miles of grey country, turning it green.

And looked at it.

For a long time. Without saying anything. With her blue arm round his neck.

Then she said—not to him, not to anyone, to the sun, very seriously, as if greeting someone she'd been expecting—

"*Mmo-ning*."

---

The light reached the door.

It came up the slope. Over the green grass, already green. Up to the threshold. Over the grey stone. Over the Ferryman's feet, sitting on the step.

And onto the door.

The blue paint went bright. The apple wood went gold—not the borrowed gold of Seren's lamp, lent, but real, morning gold, the way wood goes in the first sun. The petals lit up, every one, white and pink, staying. The bell on its bracket caught the light and threw it back in a small bright star.

The hinges breathed.

Out. And back.

---

And a panel opened.

In front of Lex. In front of all of them. Every Stone. Every panel. Everywhere.

---

Antho laughed.

On the threshold. In the light. With his arm round his wife. He put his head back and laughed until he had to hold on to the frame, and Seraphine was laughing too, and crying, and writing it down at the same time with her quill going all over the page.

---

The Ferryman stood up.

---

He did it slowly. The way he did everything. Unfolding. Very tall. Grey. In the sun. He stood on the threshold step with his long oar in one hand and looked at the light.

At the sun, coming up over the world.

At the colour, coming across the land.

He'd never seen it. Lex understood that, watching him. He'd lived on the edge of everything forever. On the river. On the shore. In the grey. At the foot of beds at night, with his lantern. He'd never once stood in the open on a summer morning and watched the sun come up.

He looked at it for a long time.

Then he looked down. At the lantern on the stone at his feet. Small. Tin. With a gold flame in it.

He bent. And picked it up.

---

He came down off the step.

Across the grass. Through the dew. To Lex.

He stopped in front of him. Very tall. With the sun on his face. With the lantern held out in front of him in one long grey hand.

Lex looked at it.

At the small gold flame. Dry. Courteous. Curious. Flickering. Looking at him—he could feel it looking—the way it had looked at him in the boat. Politely. Interested. Without knowing who he was.

*[Good morning*,] said the flame. *[You're the smith. From the boat. With the hammer.]*

"*Yes*," said Lex.

*[I thought so*.] A pause. *[You were sad. I remember. I wondered why.]*

---

"*A love for a love*," said the Ferryman.

Lex looked up at him.

"*That was the price*," said the Ferryman. "*At the Veil. In the winter. To carry your father across. It wanted his love for you. All of it. And it gave it.*" He looked down at the lantern in his hand. "*A love for a love. That was fair. That was mine to take.*"

He held the lantern out. Further. Toward Lex.

"*The rest isn't*," said the Ferryman.

---

Lex didn't move.

"*It remembers everything*," said the Ferryman. "*Every book. Every Well. Every forge. It remembers you. Your face. Your voice. Every morning for seven years.*" His grey eyes were very steady. "*It just doesn't remember why it mattered.*"

"*I know*," said Lex.

"*It might never remember*," said the Ferryman. "*I can't give it back. The love. That's gone. That went where prices go.*" A pause. "*But I can give it back to you. What's left. And you can see.*"

He looked at the flame.

"*Love isn't a thing you remember*," said the oldest thing in the world. "*It's a thing you do. Every morning. It can learn again. If someone's patient.*"

---

Lex took the lantern.

His hands were shaking. The burned one and the other. He held it in both of them, against his chest, with Lark on his hip looking at it with enormous interest.

The flame flickered.

*[Oh*,] it said. Surprised. *[That's warm. Are you warm? You're very warm.]*

"*I'm a smith*," said Lex. His voice cracked. "*We run hot.*"

*[How interesting*.] A pause. *[I've read about smiths. I think I've read a great deal about smiths. I seem to know an extraordinary amount about tempering.]*

"*You do*," said Lex. "*You know more than me.*"

*[Do I*?] Pleased. *[How odd. I wonder why.]*

---

Lark put her hand out.

Toward the lantern. Toward the flame. Her blue hand. Square. Small.

"*Hot*," she said. Seriously. To the flame. As if warning it.

The flame went very still.

Then it leaned. Toward her. Through the tin. Toward the small blue hand. The way the flames in the Harrow hearth had leaned toward her the night the Veil fell, when she said *mmo-ning* to the fire and Lex had half heard something small and dry and courteous in it.

*[Hello*,] said Ember. Softly. *[Hello, small person. I know you.]*

A pause.

*[I don't know how*,] said Ember. Puzzled. *[But I do.]*

---

Lex couldn't say anything.

He stood on the green slope at the end of the road in the sunrise with his daughter on his hip and his old friend in his hands, and the birds singing all round them, and couldn't say anything at all.

The Ferryman looked at him for a moment longer.

Then he turned.

---

He didn't go back through the door.

Lex had thought he would. Back into the room. Back to the river. Back to the edge of things, where he'd always been.

He didn't.

He walked up the slope. Past Seren with her lamp. Past Brannoc on his stone. Through the camp. Through the four hundred, who stood back to let him pass, very quietly, staring. With his long oar in his hand like a walking stick and no lantern at all.

East.

Toward the sun.

---

"*Where are you going*?" called Antho. From the threshold.

The Ferryman stopped. At the top of the rise. On the green road. With the whole of the world in front of him, turning green, and the sun on his face.

He turned his hooded head.

"*There's a door now*," said the Ferryman. "*They can walk. They don't need rowing.*" He looked at the oar in his hand. As if seeing it for the first time. "*I've never seen a morning.*" A pause. "*I thought I'd go and see some.*"

"*Will you come back*?"

The Ferryman was silent for a long time.

"*Everyone does*," he said. "*In the end.*"

And walked away down the green road. Tall. Grey. Slow. An old man with a stick, going east, into the sunrise, with the birds getting up out of the hedges in front of him and the lanterns, one every mile, going pale in the morning.

They watched him until he was too small to see.

---

Lex sat down on the threshold.

On the grey stone. Where the Ferryman had sat. With his back against the frame. With the lantern in his lap and Lark beside him, poking at the blue paint to see if it was dry.

Antho sat down on the other side.

The door stood open between them. The room behind. Soft. Dark. Full of stars. The shapes going in.

And at the bottom, the pool.

---

They could see it from here. Clearly. In the morning. As if through a window. A green circle in a field. A ring of lanterns, going pale. A stone house on the slope. Three lights in the water.

And in the grass, all round the rim—

The dust was getting up.

---

Lex watched it.

Hundreds of little heaps of grey. Lying in pairs in the grass where they'd come apart in the night. Living and dead together. And as the light came down through the soft dark of the room and touched them—sunlight, through the door, into Mal—they rose.

Not drifting. Walking. Pairs of grey columns. Side by side. Close. Holding, the way dust holds. Getting up out of the grass the way people get up from a long sleep on a hillside. And turning. To the water.

And going in.

---

He saw a soldier's dust go in beside a small grey column no taller than his shoulder.

He saw a steward's dust go in with his wife's.

He saw two laundry maids go in together, hand in hand, and the dust of an old woman and a young one go with them.

He saw a boy—small, six, maybe, he could tell by the size—go in beside his brother.

---

And by the stone house, on the slope above the pool—

Two columns. One tall. One small.

Not going in. Not yet. Standing in the grass by the door of the house. Under the lantern in the window. The tall one bending. Over the small one. The way a woman bends over a cot.

And a boy beside them.

Pim. Sitting on the step of the house. With his knees drawn up. With an old man's dust on the rim of the pool behind him, and a thorn-woman kneeling beside it, and a dust-steward sitting very still in the grass at her back.

The small column turned. Toward him.

And bent. A little. The way you bend to someone to say *thank you*.

Pim didn't say anything. He never did, if he could help it.

He lifted his hand.

---

The two columns went down the slope together. Thirty yards. Through the long grass. Past the lanterns. To the rim.

They stopped there for a moment. On the edge. Where for sixty-one days a gold girl had stood on the surface of the water and the water wouldn't take her.

Then the tall one bent all the way down. Round the small one. Close. Arms. Arms that could close, now.

And they went in.

Together.

---

*[...hello, Ilda...]* said the water. Softly. Through the whole of the room. Through the door. Out onto the threshold where Lex and Antho sat in the sun. *[...hello, Maudie...]*

*[...welcome home...]*

---

Antho put his face in his hands.

Lex put his arm round his brother.

They sat there on the threshold for a long time.

---

The old man's dust went in last.

Lex saw it. After all the others. After the pairs and the brothers and the laundry maids and the bread-woman and her girl. After the sun was well up over the world and the lanterns round the pool had gone pale and the birds in the green circle were singing so loud he could hear them through the door, five hundred miles.

A heap of grey on the rim. Fine as flour. With a little silver-wire crown sunk in the water beside it, and the glass dust of a sister already gone.

The thorn-woman put her bandaged hands under it. Gently. And lifted. As much as she could. And let it go into the pool.

The dust-steward got up from the grass. And walked to the rim. And bowed. Very low. The way a steward bows when his master leaves the room.

And followed him in.

---

*[...hello, Oz...]* said Mal.

The whole of the room shivered. Like a breath. Like a sigh. Like someone who's been waiting up very late and has finally heard the door.

*[...I'm whole now...]*

*[...I'm all here...]*

*[...thank you for bringing me back...]*

---

And far away—far out in the white, past the edge, past where the edge had been, at the east gate of an empty Court of glass and iron and gold and bone—

Lex didn't see it. He heard about it later. From the man himself. At a table in the Forge at Ashbourne, in the autumn, over a mug of Brannoc's terrible ale.

A grey-haired man in a Keeper's coat, who'd stood at a gate for five months with his staff held crosswise against a king's household that never came, set his staff down against the gatepost.

And looked at it for a moment.

And walked out of the gate. Down the white road. East. Toward the door. Toward the sun.

He didn't hurry. He said, later, at the table, that he'd thought about hurrying and decided that after five months he'd earned a walk.

"*Took me eleven days*," said Roole. "*I'd forgotten what it was like. Walking somewhere you want to go.*"

---

The sun was well up.

On the threshold, Lark had decided the paint was dry and was adding to it. With a finger. Very carefully. Low down, under her own handprint. A long wobbly line, and a round thing at the end of it.

"*What's that*?" said Antho.

Lark looked at him with scorn.

"*Da*," she said. And pointed at the round thing. Then at the line. "*Ham'er.*"

Then she pointed at the lantern in Lex's lap. At the small gold flame.

"*Hot*," she said.

*[Thank you*,] said Ember. Gravely. *[I try.]*

Lex laughed.

It hurt his ribs so much he nearly fell off the step.

He didn't care.
