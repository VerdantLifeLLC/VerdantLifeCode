# Tea for Two

They buried Old Mag on a Thursday, in the churchyard at Ashbourne, under the yew, beside a thatcher called Will who had fallen off a roof at sixty and lived another twenty years out of spite.

Seren stood at the back.

She liked the back. She'd always liked it. Harrow women stood at the back of things, by the door, where they could leave if the moths came. Her mother had stood at the back of every funeral and wedding and christening in every village they ever lived in, with Seren's hand in hers, and never once sat down.

So Seren stood at the back. By the lychgate. In her grey shawl. With the snow coming down soft and slow and the whole of Ashbourne in front of her, black-coated, hatless, round the grave.

Sister Agathe read the words. Ninety-three, now, and bent like a shepherd's crook, with a voice that still carried to the back of any room she'd ever stood in. *Earth to earth*. *Dust to dust*. *Into the Lady's keeping, with a light left on.*

Tilda threw the first handful. With her good hand. She was crying so hard she couldn't see where it went.

The Pruett boy stood at the front with his bandaged hand and a ginger cat the size of a small dog clutched to his chest. The cat looked furious. The boy looked as though he'd been given the moon.

---

And at the back of the churchyard, on the far side, under the bare black elms by the wall—

The King.

---

Nobody had asked him. Nobody had told him not to come. He'd just come. Up the lane from the green, very slowly, very carefully, in the snow. And stopped at the churchyard wall. And stood there. Outside it. Behind the elms. As far from the grave as he could get and still see it.

The snow round his feet was gold.

He didn't come in. He didn't come closer. He stood by the wall with his great hands folded in front of him, holding a lopsided gold cup, and watched them lower an old woman into the ground.

Nobody looked at him.

Everybody knew he was there.

---

Seren watched him over the heads of the mourners.

She'd been watching him for a year, on and off. Since he came out of the crack in the Veil and walked east across the world. Since he followed her daughter-in-law round the high country for eighty-six days with his hand out. Since he knocked on her blue door in the autumn and sat on her floor and drank tea he couldn't drink.

Since he knelt on the gold flags beside her husband's bed and watched him die.

She'd hated him. She remembered that. She remembered it the way you remember a fever. A great hot red hate, the first night, with the gold coming up through the floor of her cottage from his knees and Lark on the rug and Arion in the bed. *Get out*. *Get out of my house*.

He hadn't got out.

He'd sat on her floor with her cup in his hands and told her about his father. And she'd listened. Because she couldn't not.

And then he'd stayed.

He'd stayed two nights. He'd stayed while Arion died. He'd knelt by the bed and not touched anything and not said anything and watched it, the whole of it, from the first slow breath to the last. And when the Ferryman came, he'd stood up and backed into the corner like a child caught somewhere it shouldn't be.

*Not you*, the Ferryman had said. *Your punishment. Not mine.*

She didn't hate him anymore.

She didn't know what she felt instead.

---

She walked home afterward. Alone. Up the Vale road in the snow, and through the birch wood, along the gold path.

It was still there. The King's footprints from the autumn. A step wide. Wandering. From the Ashbourne road up through the birches to her door. Every step gold. The snow lay on them and they showed through, glowing faintly, like coals under ash.

The children used it as a slide.

She didn't mind. She liked hearing them. Shrieking. On the gold. In the snow. Hollis Penn's three, and the Tull girl, and the miller's boys, on sacks, flying down the path between the white birch trunks with their scarves out behind them.

They'd gone home now. It was getting dark.

She walked up the gold path alone.

---

Lex was at the grave.

Not Mag's. Arion's. By the stream. Kneeling in the snow with his back to her. By the grey stone. With the twenty lanterns round him and the soft black turnip and the one red rose.

He didn't hear her come.

She stopped on the stepping stones and looked at him.

At her son. Kneeling. In his forge clothes, with snow on his shoulders. Broad-backed. Burned. Twenty-six. The baby she'd carried in a basket at the first Midwinter, while Arion fell in a drift digging them out. The boy who'd been eleven when she went into the dark, and twenty-four when she came out, with his father's eyes and a handprint on his face.

He'd come back from the coast four days ago on a dragon in the dawn with the bell ringing. He'd gone straight to Mag's. He'd knelt on her gold floor for an hour and held the room open with his bare hand so a king could hold an old woman's.

And then he'd come home.

And he hadn't said a word about what happened after.

She knew anyway. Tilda had told Wren, and Wren had told her, in a whisper, on the ramp, with her face white.

*It asked him if they'd met*.

---

She came down off the stepping stones and stood beside him.

He didn't look up.

She knelt. In the snow. Beside him. With her knees cracking—fifty-one, and thirteen years in the dark, and her knees knew it. And looked at the stone.

*ARION VEY*.

*He turned back twice*.

---

"*It was kind*," said Lex. After a long time. Very low. "*That's the thing. It was kind.*"

She waited.

"*It said, you look very tired. It said, I hope you'll get some rest.*" He was looking at the stone. "*The way you'd say it to someone at an inn. Someone you'd never see again.*" He swallowed. "*It was so polite, Mam.*"

"*It always was*."

"*It was polite to me the first day*. *In the forge. Seven years ago.*" A breath. A shake. "*Good evening, Smith. Please do not die. I have only just arrived.*" His voice cracked on it. "*It called me Smith for a month. It didn't call me Lex till—*"

He stopped.

Seren didn't say anything.

She put her hand on his back. Between his shoulders. Flat. The way she used to put it there when he was small and couldn't sleep and she'd sit by the bed and hum. And left it there.

---

"*I didn't know him*," she said.

Lex went still under her hand.

"*Your father*," said Seren Harrow. "*For a year and a half. I didn't know him.*" She looked at the stone. "*I came out of the dark past eleven hundred lanterns, and there was an old man in a chair, and I said you have a nice voice. And he said thank you. And I didn't know him.*"

"*I know, Mam*."

"*You don't*," she said. "*Not this part. You weren't there for this part.*" She took a breath. "*Every morning. Every morning for a year and a half, he said good morning. To me. Across the table. Across the room. From the chair. From the bed, at the end.*" She looked down at her hands. "*Good morning, Seren. And I said good morning back. Because it was polite. Because that's what you say to an old man you're living with, whom you like, whom you're beginning to—*"

She stopped.

"*And every morning*," she said, "*he looked at me like I was the sun coming up. And every morning I didn't know why.*"

---

Lex turned his head.

He looked at her. Grey eyes. Arion's eyes. Wet.

"*How did he*—" He stopped. Started again. "*How did he stand it?*"

"*I don't know*," said Seren. "*I asked him. At the end. When I remembered. I said, how did you stand it, all those mornings, saying good morning to a woman who didn't know you.*"

"*What did he say*?"

Seren smiled. It hurt.

"*He said, I didn't need you to know me*," she said. "*I needed you to have a good morning.*"

---

Lex looked at her for a long time.

Then he put his face in his hands.

She didn't take her hand away from his back. She knelt beside him in the snow by the grave of the man they'd both loved, with the twenty lanterns round them and the stream running black under the ice, and let him.

It was getting dark.

Across the stream, in the window of the blue-door cottage, a horn lantern glowed. Soft. Gold. Blurred. With two words in the base.

---

The King came at dusk.

---

She heard him before she saw him. Not footsteps. He made no sound in the snow. She heard the *children*. Very faint. Down at the bottom of the birch wood, where the gold path started at the Ashbourne road.

Shrieking.

Not frightened. *Delighted*. The way they shrieked on the slide.

She went to the door.

---

He was coming up the gold path.

Slowly. Carefully. Putting each great foot down in his own footprints from the autumn, exactly, so the gold didn't spread. Gold. Glowing. Taller than the birches, nearly. With the cup in his hand and his head bowed under the branches.

And behind him, at a respectful distance—ten yards, twenty—

Children.

Hollis Penn's three. The Tull girl. The miller's boys. On their sacks. Not sliding. *Following*. Walking up the gold path behind the Gilded King in a straggling line, like ducklings behind a goose, whispering, giggling, shoving each other, daring each other to get closer.

The King didn't look round.

But Seren saw his face as he came up through the last of the birches, into the yard, into the light from her door.

He was smiling.

---

He stopped at the foot of her ramp.

The children stopped too. At the edge of the trees. In a clump. Staring.

"*Mistress Harrow*," said the King.

"*You'll frighten them*," said Seren.

The King looked back over his shoulder. At the children. Who scattered like starlings. Shrieking. Then stopped, ten yards further off, and crept back.

"*They're not frightened*," said the King. "*They followed me from the green. They've followed me every day for two weeks. They ask me things.*" He looked down at her. Furnace eyes. Dimmed. Tired. "*They asked if I could make their snowmen gold. I said I'd rather not. They said why. I didn't know what to tell them.*"

"*What did you tell them*?"

"*That gold snowmen never melt*," said the King. "*And that they'd want to build a new one next winter.*" He paused. "*They seemed to think that was a good reason.*"

---

She looked at him for a long time.

Then she stepped back from the door.

"*Come in*," said Seren Harrow. "*The floor's already gold in the middle. You might as well.*"

---

He came in the way he'd come in the autumn. Bent double. Sideways through the door. Very slow. And knelt. On the flags. In the same place. Where the gold was already. A circle of it. In the middle of her floor. Where he'd knelt beside the bed and watched her husband die.

The bed was gone now. Brannoc had taken it, the week after. Carried it out to the lean-to with the boards from the windows. She hadn't asked him to. She'd come back from the grave one morning and it was gone, and the place where it had been was swept, and there was a rag rug on it.

The King looked at the rug.

He didn't say anything.

She put the kettle on.

---

She made tea.

The good kind. In the tin. Warm the pot. One spoon each and one for the pot. Water just off the boil. Not on it. Never on it. *You'll scald the leaf, Seren. It's not a horseshoe.*

She'd made it that way for thirty years without knowing why. Her hands had known. Her hands had remembered him when she didn't.

She poured two cups.

One for her. Blue. Chipped. Her own.

And one for him.

---

She'd thought about it. On the walk home from the churchyard. What to give him. He couldn't hold a cup without it going gold. He couldn't drink from a gold cup. The gold one he already had—her lopsided white one, Brannoc's wrong handle, her thumb—he carried everywhere, and couldn't put down, and couldn't drink from, because the tea in it was gold too. A gold disc. Forever.

So she took a cup off the dresser. Plain. White. Cheap. One of a set of six from the Ashbourne market. And filled it. And didn't hand it to him.

She set it on the floor. In front of him. At the edge of the gold. Just outside. On the bare flags.

And sat down in her chair by the west window. Under the horn lantern. With her own cup.

---

The King looked at the cup on the floor.

Steam rose off it. Thin. White. Curling up into the firelight.

"*I can't drink it*," he said.

"*I know*."

"*If I pick it up*—"

"*Don't pick it up*," said Seren. "*Leave it there. It's yours. You can look at it.*"

He looked at it.

The steam rose. The tea in it trembled very slightly when the fire popped. The light from the hearth lay on its surface, red-gold, moving.

The King watched it.

He watched it for a long time. Kneeling on her floor. With his own gold cup held in his great hands on his knees, and its gold disc of tea that never moved, never steamed, never trembled, never cooled.

"*It's moving*," he said. Very quietly.

"*It's tea*."

"*It's moving*," said the Gilded King. "*The steam. The top of it. When the fire moves.*" He swallowed. "*Mine doesn't.*"

"*No*," said Seren. "*Yours doesn't.*"

---

They sat.

She drank her tea. He watched his. The fire ticked. Outside, faint, the children giving up at last and going home down the gold path, their voices fading into the dark of the birch wood.

"*I held her hand*," said the King.

"*I know*. *Tilda told Wren.*"

"*Your son held the room*. *Between us. So I wouldn't keep her.*" He looked at his own hand. "*For an hour. I could feel it shaking. His hand. Under mine.*" A pause. "*He didn't let go.*"

"*He wouldn't*," said Seren. "*He never does.*"

"*She went*," said the King. "*At noon. Just like that. She said, there, see, not so bad. And breathed out.*" He was looking at the steam. "*And I held her hand while she did it. And the gold tried to take her after, when your son let go of the room. And it couldn't. Because there wasn't anything left to keep.*"

He looked up.

"*She was finished*," he said. "*Like a cake.*"

---

Seren didn't laugh.

She wanted to. It was so absurd. A gold king on her floor at dusk, saying *like a cake* with tears in his furnace eyes.

She didn't.

"*Mag said that*?"

"*Kept's the oven*," said the King. "*Finished is the cake.*" He looked down at the gold cup in his hands. "*I've been thinking about it for four days. Walking up and down the green. The children asking me why I'm sad.*" A pause. "*I told them about the cake. They didn't understand either.*"

---

"*Arion asked about you*," said Seren.

The King looked up.

"*At the end*," she said. "*The last night. When you'd gone into the corner. When the Ferryman—*" She stopped. Started again. "*He asked me to tell you something. Afterward. If you came back.*"

The King didn't move.

"*He said*," said Seren slowly, "*tell him it's all right that he stood outside the door. Tell him everyone does, the first time. Tell him I did. Two hundred years. I stood outside every door I ever came to.*" She looked at the horn lantern on the sill beside her. "*He said, tell him the second time is easier.*"

---

The King was very still.

He knelt on her floor in his circle of gold, with the white cup steaming in front of him and the gold cup still in his hands, and didn't move at all.

"*The second time*," he said.

"*That's what he said*."

"*I've only been in once*," said the Gilded King. "*Four days ago. With your son holding the room.*" He looked at his hand. "*And I couldn't have, without him. I'd still be on the step.*"

"*That counts*."

"*Does it*?"

"*Everything counts*," said Seren Harrow. "*That's the thing nobody tells you.*"

---

She sang to him.

She hadn't meant to. She'd never sung it for anyone but Arion. Not once. Not in thirty years. Not in twenty generations of Harrow women, if her mother was to be believed, had anyone sung it outside the family.

But it was dark. And the fire was low. And there was a king on her floor watching a cup of tea steam as if it were the most beautiful thing in the world.

So she sang it.

Quietly. The way her mother had sung it. The last night of summer. With the cottage half-packed.

*Gold for the eldest, who feared to grow old,*
*Glass for the vain one, who loved to be told,*
*Iron for the soldier who never once laughed,*
*Thorns for the gardener who listened—and drank.*

She stopped there. Where it broke. Where it always broke.

The King had lifted his head.

---

"*That's us*," he said.

"*That's you*."

"*Gold for the eldest*." He said it slowly. Wondering. "*Who feared to grow old.*" He looked at his hands. "*That's not right. I wasn't afraid of getting old. I was afraid of—*" He stopped.

"*Of what*?"

"*Of watching*," said the King. "*Of watching everyone else get old. And go.*" He looked at the white cup. "*I'd watched Father. Or I hadn't. I'd stood outside the door. And I thought, if I drink, I'll never have to go through that. Not for me. Not for anyone. I'll keep them all.*"

"*So the song's wrong*."

"*The song's nearly right*," said the Gilded King. "*Songs usually are.*"

He looked at the fire.

"*Glass for the vain one*," he said. "*That's Ysy. Who loved to be told.*" He almost smiled. "*She did. She loved it. She was the prettiest of us and she knew it, and she used to make us tell her. Every morning. In the nursery. At the long mirror. Am I pretty, Oz? Am I the prettiest? Tell me.*" The smile went. "*She was nineteen. At the pool.*"

"*And now*?"

"*Now she can't see herself*," said the King.

---

Seren put her cup down.

"*What*?"

"*In the mirror*," said the King. "*After the pool. We all went in together. To the long hall. To look at ourselves. Deathless.*" His voice was very quiet. "*And she wasn't there. In the glass. We were all there, arm in arm, and she was in the middle, and the glass showed the wall behind her.*"

Seren stared at him.

"*Nine hundred years*," said the King. "*She's never seen her own face. Not once. Not in any glass, any water, any polished thing. She's spent nine hundred years learning everything there is to know about glass, so she could find out where it went.*" He looked at the white cup. At the steam. At the firelight moving on the surface of the tea. "*She never found it.*"

"*Nobody knows*," said Seren slowly.

"*I know*," said the King. "*I was standing next to her. She made me promise never to tell.*" He looked up. "*I've just told you. I don't know why.*"

---

Seren sat very still.

*Glass for the vain one, who loved to be told*.

She thought of a face in a bathwater. *Hello, Mistwalker*. She thought of the Glass Queen in every window in Caldor, at night, reaching out, showing people their own faces. Their true faces. Whether they wanted to see or not.

*Why don't you cry*? she'd said to Wren. *It's a true question. I want to know.*

She thought of a woman who'd spent nine hundred years looking out of every pane in the world and never once seeing herself.

"*Oh*," said Seren Harrow. Quietly. "*Oh, the poor thing.*"

The King looked at her.

"*Nobody's ever said that*," he said. "*About Ysy.*"

"*Nobody's ever known*," said Seren.

---

She finished the song.

The last two lines. Quietly.

*Silver for the moth, who went toward the light,*
*And nothing, nothing, for the tenth, who stood in the door all night.*

---

The King closed his eyes.

"*She did*," he said.

"*Did what*?"

"*Stood in the door*," said the Gilded King. "*All night. Hes.*" He opened his eyes. "*After the pool. After she poured hers back. We went inside, the nine of us, laughing, to the long hall, to look in the mirror. And she didn't come in. She stood in the garden door. In the dark. All night.*" He swallowed. "*I asked her why. In the morning. When she was leaving. I said, why did you stand there, Hes, all night, in the cold?*"

"*What did she say*?"

"*She said, in case one of you wanted to come out*," said the King.

---

The fire popped. A log settled. Sparks went up the chimney.

On the floor, in front of the King, the white cup steamed. Less now. Cooling. The surface of the tea trembling when the log fell.

"*Nobody did*," said the King. "*Nobody came out. Not one of us. We were in the long hall, looking at ourselves, being deathless.*" He looked at his hands. "*And she stood in the door till morning. And then she left. With her crown in a bundle. And we never saw her again.*"

"*She left the door open*," said Seren.

"*She left it open*," said the Gilded King. "*Nine hundred years.*" He looked at the white cup. "*I never went through.*"

---

They sat for a long time after that.

Neither of them said anything. Seren finished her tea. The white cup on the floor stopped steaming. The King watched it stop. Watched the last thread of steam thin and thin and go out, like a candle snuffed. Watched the surface of the tea go still, cold, dark.

"*It's stopped*," he said.

"*It's gone cold*."

"*Is that the same as kept*?"

"*No*," said Seren. "*Cold tea's finished. You make a new pot.*"

The King looked at her.

Then he laughed. Quietly. The cracked bell. Not quite broken. "*You're all the same*," he said. "*All of you. The old woman. Your husband. You.*" He shook his great head. "*Everything's a cake. Everything's a pot of tea.*"

"*Most things are*," said Seren.

---

She got up to make a new pot.

And as she passed him, on the way to the hearth, she saw the gold cup.

In his hands. On his knees. Small. Lopsided. Too thick. Brannoc's wrong handle. Her own thumb-print in the rim. Gold, all of it, glowing in the firelight.

And the tea in it. A gold disc. Flat. Still. Polished.

Like a mirror.

She stopped.

The King saw her stop. Saw where she was looking. His great hands closed round the cup. Very slightly. The way a child closes its hands round something it's been told not to have.

---

"*She came last night*," he said.

Seren didn't move.

"*Ysy*," said the King. "*In the cup.*" He looked down at it. At the gold disc. "*I didn't know she could. It's gold. It's not glass. It's not water.*" His voice was very low. "*But it's still. And it's flat. And it shines. And I was sitting on the green in the dark looking into it, and she was there.*"

"*What did she want*?"

The King didn't answer.

"*What did she want*?" said Seren again.

---

"*She's found it*," said the King. "*The crown in the road. The thing Cad went down the thread for. The middle of your net. Where the smith let go.*" He swallowed. "*She's found the way to it. Through the Stones. She says the Stones are glass all the way down.*"

Seren's hands had gone cold.

"*She says it's a mirror*," said the King. "*The net. The biggest mirror in the world. Held up to everyone.*" He looked at the cup. "*She says if she held it, she could make it keep everyone. Not just my household. Everyone. Every thread. Forever. Nobody would ever have to go through the door again. Not old women. Not old men. Not husbands in beds by windows.*"

He looked up at Seren.

"*She says nobody would ever have to be finished*," said the Gilded King. "*Ever.*"

---

Seren stood in the middle of her floor with the empty pot in her hands.

"*And what does she need*," she said. Very quietly. "*To hold it.*"

The King looked at her.

He didn't say anything. He didn't need to. She saw it in his face. She saw him look past her. At the door. At the dark outside. At the stream, and the stepping stones, and the cottage on the other side of the water with the HERE lantern in its east window, and a cradle in the loft with KNIT on the rockers.

"*A Vey hand*," said Seren.

"*A small one*," said the King.

---

The pot slipped.

It didn't break. It fell on the rag rug and rolled and stopped against the leg of the table. Seren didn't pick it up. She stood over the King on his gold circle with her hands empty and her heart going.

"*She asked you*," she said.

"*Yes*."

"*To bring her the child*."

"*Yes*."

"*And what did you say*?"

---

The King looked down at the gold cup in his hands.

At the flat bright disc of tea in it. Still. Polished. Showing the firelight. Showing the beams of the ceiling. Showing his own great gold face, looking down.

Showing nothing else. Not now.

"*Nothing*," he said.

Seren stared at him.

"*I didn't say anything*," said the Gilded King. "*I sat on the green in the dark with her face in the cup, and she told me. Everyone. Forever. No more doors. And I thought of the old woman. And your husband. And Father.*" His voice cracked. "*And I didn't say yes.*"

He looked up at her. The furnace eyes wet.

"*But I didn't say no*," he said.
