# The Reflections

Lex woke because the house was too quiet.

It was a thing you learned. With a child. You learned the sounds of a sleeping house, the way a smith learns the sounds of a forge at night. The tick of the banked hearths. Brannoc's snoring through the wall. Wren's breathing beside him on the pallet, slow and deep. And under all of it, from the cot in the corner of the old tool-room, the small fierce snuffle of his daughter, who slept the way she did everything else. As if she meant it.

He lay there in the grey before dawn and listened.

Hearths. Brannoc. Wren.

Nothing from the cot.

---

He was up before he'd finished thinking it.

The cot was empty. The blanket kicked down to the end. The rag rabbit Wren's mother had sewn face-down on the floor beside it, which meant she'd climbed out over the side, which she'd been able to do for three weeks and had been told not to do every day of those three weeks.

The tool-room door was open a crack.

The long hall beyond it was dark. The banked hearths glowed down the length of it like a row of sleeping eyes. The door—*the* door, the apple wood door on its frame in the middle of the floor, with its forged hinges and no threshold and no latch—stood shut. Petals on the flags round it.

And the great doors at the end of the hall, onto the yard, were open.

A foot. Just a foot. Just wide enough for something small in a nightshirt to slip through sideways.

Lex ran.

---

He heard her before he saw her.

Laughing.

That big delighted belly-laugh she'd had since she was four months old, that came up out of her like water out of a spring and went on and on, as if she'd heard the best joke in the world and wanted everybody to know. It came across the yard in the half-dark. From the bottom. From the back. From the place where the yard wall ended and the stream ran past, brown and quick, under the alders, where Brannoc took his quench-water and Lex had washed her feet on Sunday because she liked to splash.

"*Gan*!" shouted Lark. Delighted. "*Gan! Gan! Mmo-ning!*"

---

She was on the bank.

On her knees. On the flat stone where Brannoc set his buckets. In her nightshirt, with her black hair sticking up at the back and her bare feet muddy, leaning out over the water with both hands on the edge of the stone. Looking down. Laughing.

The stream wasn't moving.

That was the first thing Lex saw. Before he'd taken it in. Before he understood. The stream was always moving. It had been moving every day of his life at the Forge, brown and quick and loud. And it was lying under the alders like a sheet of black glass. Flat. Smooth. Still.

Not grey. Not stopped. Just—still. The way a pond goes still at dawn when the wind drops. As if the water had somewhere to be and had decided, all of it, at once, to wait.

And in it, under his daughter's face—

His mother's face. Upside down. White. Looking up.

And under that. Below it. Deep. Deeper than the stream had any right to be. Coming up.

Light.

White. Cold. Clear.

---

"*LARK*."

---

He didn't remember crossing the yard.

He remembered the stone under his knees. He remembered his arms going round her. He remembered the weight of her, warm and solid and outraged, coming up off the edge of the stone into his chest. He remembered her shriek—*NO! Gan! GAN!*—and her fists beating on his collarbone.

And he remembered the hand.

Coming up out of the water. Where she'd been. Where her face had been, a heartbeat before. White. Clear. Glass. Long fingers. Reaching.

It closed.

On his wrist.

---

Cold.

Not the cold of winter. Not the cold of iron left out in frost. The cold of the long mirror. The cold of the Glass Queen's hall, that he'd never been in and knew anyway, because it was the cold he'd felt in the panel in the spring when she'd shown him himself with a crown on. The cold that *remembers*.

It went up his arm. Into his elbow. His shoulder. His chest. Like water going up a wick.

He held on to Lark.

The hand held on to him.

And for a moment—one moment, one heartbeat, kneeling on the flat stone in the half-dark with his daughter screaming in his arms—he looked down into the black water past the glass fingers round his wrist.

And saw her.

---

Ysenne.

Not in a panel. Not in a bath. Not in a cup of gold tea. Close. Under the water. A foot down. Looking up at him.

White. Clear. Beautiful in the way that a frost on a window is beautiful. A woman made of glass, with a crown of glass on her head and a face like a cut jewel, all planes and edges and light. With eyes that were nothing. Clear. Like looking through a window at a white sky.

She looked at him.

Then past him. At Lark. At the small furious face screaming against his chest.

And something went across the glass face. Something that wasn't anger. That wasn't anything Lex had a word for.

"*Not you*," said the Glass Queen. Through the water. Very softly. To him. "*I never wanted you.*"

The hand let go.

---

He fell backward off the stone.

Onto the cinders of the yard. With Lark on his chest. With the cold going out of his arm like a tide going out, fast, leaving it numb and white and stiff to the elbow.

When he looked back at the stream, it was moving.

Brown. Quick. Loud. Over the stones. Under the alders. As if it had never stopped.

---

"*She said Gan*," said Wren.

In the tool-room. Ten minutes later. With Lark in her lap, hiccuping, furious, clutching the rag rabbit. With Brannoc in the doorway in his nightshirt and his boots, holding a poker. With Antho behind him, white, and Seraphine behind Antho, whiter.

"*She said Gan*," said Wren again. "*Why would she say Gan?*"

"*She saw her*," said Lex.

He was sitting on the edge of the pallet. Holding his right arm. It was coming back. Slowly. Pins and needles. Up from the fingers. There was a mark round his wrist like a bracelet. White. Frosted. In the shape of four long fingers and a thumb.

"*She saw my mother*," said Lex. "*In the water.*"

---

His mother came at noon.

Down the river path from Harrow. On foot. Through the grey.

He saw her from the top of the Forge roof, where he'd climbed to look west. He'd been up there all morning. Looking. At the line. You could see it now, from the roof. Three miles off, where the river path went into the trees above Harrow. Not a wall. Not a cloud. Just the place where the trees on one side were trees and the trees on the other were a picture of trees.

And coming out of it, down the path—

Three women.

Holding hands.

With a ring round their feet, green, a foot wide, moving with them over the grey grass like a lantern carried through a dark room.

---

He was off the roof and down the lane and onto the river path before anyone could stop him. He met them at the edge. Where the grey stopped and the green started. A hundred yards beyond the last of the Ashbourne lanterns. Where the grass was grass on one side of a line and not on the other.

His mother stepped across it.

She looked very small. She looked very tired. Her grey hair was coming down out of its pins and her boots were muddy and her square freckled hands were bleeding in the palms, as if she'd been holding thorns.

She looked up at him.

"*Is she all right*?" said Seren Harrow.

He couldn't answer. He just put his arms round her.

"*She's all right*," said Rhosyn. Behind her. Quietly. Looking at his face. "*Look at him, Seren. She's all right.*"

His mother cried into his shirt.

---

They told it in the Forge.

All of it. The birds. The hedge. The ring. The grave. The stream stopping. The pond filling. The face in the water at dawn.

Seraphine wrote it down. Every word. With her new spectacles on her nose—not glass, horn, scraped thin, the way Lex had made the panes of his mother's lantern, because she wouldn't wear glass any more and couldn't read without something. Her quill went very fast and her other hand stayed flat on her belly the whole time.

When they'd finished, she looked at the page for a long time.

"*It's the edge*," said Seraphine.

Everybody looked at her.

"*The edge of the grey*," she said. "*The front. Where it meets running water.*" She drew a line on the page. A wavy line. And a straight one across it. "*The water comes down from real ground. From the east. It hits the grey. It stops. It can't go on. It backs up. It pools. It goes flat.*" She tapped the place where the lines crossed. "*Every stream. Every river. Everywhere the grey's crossed running water since Midwinter, there's been a mirror. At the front. Right at the edge. Moving east as the grey moves east.*"

"*And she can come through it*," said Wren.

"*She can see through it*," said Rhosyn. "*Along it. Up the water. Up the whole stream. Anywhere it runs.*" She looked at Lex. "*Your mother's pond is at the bottom of one long thread of water that runs past your yard. She looked up it. She saw your child.*"

Silence.

"*How long*," said Brannoc. Gruff. "*Till it's here. The grey.*"

Lex looked at Seraphine.

Seraphine looked at her page.

"*Two days*," she said. "*Three. If it's slow.*"

"*And Midsummer*?" said Antho.

"*Four*," said Seraphine.

---

They covered everything.

That afternoon. That night. The next day. Every surface in the Forge that could hold a face.

Brannoc drained the quench-troughs. All twenty. Tipped them out into the yard and turned them upside down and sat on the last one with his arms folded as if daring it to fill itself. Tilda went round with a bucket of soot and blacked every piece of polished iron on every anvil and every wall. Kestrel took down the hall's two windows, glass, old, bubbled, and wrapped them in sacking and put them in the coal-hole and stood on the coal-hole door.

Wren went through the tool-room and the kitchen and the loft and took away every cup, every bowl, every spoon, every pan with a bright bottom. Everything. She piled it all in a sack and gave it to Hollis Penn, who took it away up the lane in his barrow without asking why.

Lex dammed the stream.

---

He did it with Stone.

Up at the top of the yard, where the stream came round the bend from the east under the alders. He knelt on the bank and put both hands on the ground and reached. Not through the net—the net was still, frozen, a held breath—but round it. Down. Past it. To the Well under everything. The slow patient deep of the earth.

*Move*, he said. *Please. Over. A little.*

The ground moved.

It came up out of the bank. A shoulder of rock. Grey-brown. Mossed. It came up slowly, the way a sleeper turns over, and lay across the stream from bank to bank, and the water hit it and turned. North. Away from the yard. Into the old dry channel behind the smithy where the stream had run a hundred years ago, before somebody's grandfather had moved it.

The stream bed past the yard went dry. Mud. Stones. A few trout flapping. Posy Tull's little brother came down from the village with a bucket and carried them off to the mill-pond in a state of high excitement.

"*It's still running*," said Antho. Beside him. Looking north. At the new old channel. At the water going away round the back of the smithy, brown and quick, toward the green. "*It's just running somewhere else.*"

"*It's running away from her*," said Lex.

"*It's running toward the grey*," said Antho. Quietly. "*Everything does. In the end.*"

---

The grey came on the third day.

Lex stood on the green and watched it.

He'd stood on the Forge roof every morning and watched it come. Through the birch wood above Harrow. Down the valley. Across the Long Meadow, where the sheep had been, before Hollis and the Pruett boys drove them east to Fen Hollow. Over the river path. Up the hill. Toward the village. You could see it coming now. If you didn't look straight at it.

And round the village, in a ring, in the long grass—

Lanterns.

Seven hundred and twelve of them. And more. The ones given round the frame on the green, in the spring, the night Tom Pruett built the leaf. The ones the village had given since. Every lantern in Ashbourne. Every lantern anybody had. Tin and horn and glass and paper, square and round and bent, brass carriage lamps and kitchen candles in jam jars and one very old one, iron, rusted, with a crack down one pane, which Old Mag's neighbour said had been in her family since her great-grandmother's time and had never once been lit for anyone but family.

Given.

All of them.

Set out in a ring round Ashbourne. Round the green and the lanes and the mill and the Forge and the Penns' bakery and the church and the forty-one houses. In the grass. In the hedges. On gateposts and walls and the branches of trees. A long ring. A mile round. Gold.

The grey came up the hill to the first of them.

And stopped.

And went round.

---

Lex let out a breath he'd been holding since Midwinter.

Beside him, Brannoc did the same.

All round them, on the green, three hundred people did the same. A sound like wind. Like the whole village sighing. And then somebody laughed—a high shaky laugh, Davy Pruett's mother, with her new baby in a sling, holding her husband's arm—and somebody else, and then they were all laughing, or crying, or both, and Hollis Penn was handing out bread from his barrow, real bread, risen, still warm, because the ground inside the ring was real ground and the yeast didn't know there was anything wrong.

Lex didn't laugh.

He was looking west. Past the green. Past the church. Down the lane. To the bottom of the village. To the mill.

---

The stream came into Ashbourne from the east.

Under the bridge at the top of the village. Down the side of the green. Past the church. Round the back of the Forge now, in its old new channel. Down to the mill. And out again. West. Toward Harrow. Toward the grey.

It turned the mill wheel. It always had. Old Joss Miller's wheel, that had been turning since before Lex was born.

It wasn't turning now.

Because the stream ran out of the village at the bottom. Past the mill. Out through the ring of lanterns. Into the grey.

And stopped.

And backed up.

And the mill-race was filling. And the mill-pond behind it was filling. And spreading. Out over its banks. Into Joss Miller's orchard. Into the bottom of the lane. Brown and quick and live, coming down from the east, from real ground, and meeting the stillness at the bottom of the village, and pooling.

Going quiet.

Going flat.

---

"*It's inside the ring*," said Antho.

Beside him. Very quietly. Looking where he was looking.

"*Yes*," said Lex.

"*You can't dam it*," said Antho. "*Not this one. There's nowhere for it to go. You turned it once. Wherever you turn it, it ends up at the grey.*"

"*I know*."

"*It's a mirror*," said Antho. "*Inside the lanterns. Inside the village. Forty yards from the bakery.*"

"*I know*," said Lex.

They stood there on the green while the village laughed and cried and ate warm bread round them. And watched the mill-pond at the bottom of the village fill. And go smooth. And go bright.

And in it, upside down, perfect—the mill. The church tower. The lantern ring, gold.

The sky. Blue.

The only blue for two hundred miles.

---

They covered it.

What else could they do? Tom Pruett and the Penn boys and Ned Tull and half the men in the village carried sailcloth down from the old rope-walk, and planks from the timber-yard, and laid them across the pond from bank to bank. Weighted them with stones. Tied them down. Brannoc stood on the bank with his hammer and supervised, and when it was done he stood there and looked at it, a great grey sagging lid of canvas and boards over forty yards of water, and spat.

"*It'll hold*," said Brannoc.

"*It's canvas*," said Wren.

"*It'll hold*," said Brannoc.

He didn't go back up the hill. He sat down on the bank with his hammer across his knees and stayed there.

---

Two days to Midsummer.

---

It came out of the lanterns.

---

Lex had thought about the mill-pond. They all had. They'd thought about it for two days. They'd sat up in shifts on the bank beside it with lanterns and hammers. They'd checked the ropes on the sailcloth every hour. They'd kept Lark in the Forge, in the tool-room, with the door shut, with Wren beside her and Seren beside Wren and Rhosyn in the doorway.

Nobody had thought about the lanterns.

Seven hundred and twelve lanterns. And more. A mile round. Tin and horn and paper and brass. And glass.

Glass panes. Hundreds of them. Lit. Gold.

Every one of them a little mirror, at night, with the dark outside it and a flame inside.

---

The first one broke at midnight.

Lex heard it from the Forge. A small sound. A tinkle. The sound of a pane going.

Then another.

Then a scream.

---

He was out of the Forge with the hammer in his hand. Down the lane. Onto the green.

The ring was going out.

Not all of it. Not at once. Here and there. On the west side. On the south. Points of gold in the dark, in a long curve round the village, and one by one, one after another, going dark. Like someone walking round the ring snuffing candles.

And where they went dark—

The grey came in.

---

"*THE LANTERNS*," somebody was shouting. "*THE LANTERNS, SOMETHING'S BREAKING THE LANTERNS—*"

Lex ran.

West. Toward the nearest gap. Down the hill past the church, past the Pruetts', into the long grass. To the ring. To a gap in the ring three lanterns wide where the gold had gone out and the grey was coming through. Slow. Patient. A picture of grass, creeping into the real.

There was a man standing in the gap.

---

Glass.

Clear. Bright. Moving. A man made of glass, the way the things on the lantern line had been, the way Wren had told it. Tall. Broad. A farmer's shoulders. A farmer's hands. Lex knew the shape of him. Jem Tolley, who kept bees on the hill. Whose lantern this had been. Whose own face had looked back at him out of the glass pane of his own lantern every night he'd lit it in his kitchen for thirty years.

It was holding the lantern.

It looked at Lex with Jem Tolley's face, clear as ice, and smiled, and closed its glass hand.

The lantern crumpled. Tin and glass. Like paper.

The light went out.

The grey came on.

---

Lex hit it.

He didn't think. He swung. The hammer. Aurelian's hammer. Black iron, old, heavy, worn smooth at the grip. It went into the glass man's chest and the glass man shattered. All at once. Into a thousand bright pieces. Into the grass.

And the pieces got up.

Every one. A thousand small bright pieces of glass, in the grass, in the dark. Rising. Turning. Each one with a face in it. Jem Tolley's face. A thousand of them. Smiling.

And drifted toward the next lantern.

---

*[Fire won't come through the net*,] he thought. *[Not for me. Not since the still. I have to go round.]*

He went round.

Down. Past the net. Past the held breath of it, the frozen weave. To the Well. To the heat under everything. He reached for it and it came, slow, the way it had come every day since the net went still. Not a roar. A climb. Up out of the deep, hand over hand. White-gold.

Too slow.

By the time it reached his palm, the thousand pieces of glass had reached the next three lanterns, and the next three lanterns went out.

---

He saw them all down the ring.

All the way round. In the dark. Where the gold had gone out. Glass figures standing in the gaps. Dozens. Each one holding a lantern. Each one wearing the face of the person who'd given it. Who'd lit it every night in a kitchen window, in a byre, on a gatepost, and looked into the pane and seen themselves.

Every lantern in Ashbourne was a mirror.

Every lantern in Ashbourne had seen its owner's face.

And she was in all of them.

---

"*LEX*."

Kestrel. Out of the dark. Running. With her hand up in front of her and the air over it thick and wavy and dark, like the air over a road in summer. Black heat. Fire with no light.

She walked into the cloud of glass pieces over the next lantern and they melted. All at once. Into drops. Into a fine bright rain that hissed on the grass.

"*It can't see me*," panted Kestrel. "*It can't see black heat. It's made of seeing.*" She shoved past him. "*But there's hundreds, Lex. There's hundreds of them, and there's one of me.*"

Behind her, Wren. Out of the dark. On the mist. Not walking. Going. A cold grey blur between one gap and the next. And where she went, the glass figures turned. Blind. Confused. Groping at the fog.

Behind Wren, Antho. With his hands up and the dark coming off them. Starlit. Deep. Shade, Commanding, the way he'd learned it on the hill above the Keep, showing the dead their own shapes in the stars.

"*They're coming from the glass*," Antho shouted. "*The lantern panes. All of them.*"

"*I know*."

"*Lex, we can't hold the ring. We can't—if the ring goes—*"

"*I KNOW*."

---

And then the voice behind him said: "*You could, though.*"

---

Lex turned round.

There was a man on the green.

Tall. Black-haired. Broad-shouldered, a smith's shoulders, with a smith's burn-scars up both forearms and a grey-gold handprint on his left cheek. In a leather apron. With a hammer in his hand. Black iron. Old. Worn smooth at the grip.

Glass.

Clear. Bright. A man of glass with Lex's face.

And on his head—

On his head, gold. Seven points. Thin. Plain. No jewels. The crown that had been lying in the road at the Heart for nine hundred years. The net's crown. Warm. Gold. Empty.

Not empty now.

---

"*Hello*," said the glass Lex.

---

He'd seen it before. In the panel. In the spring. *YOUR DAUGHTER'S HANDS ARE VERY SQUARE*. A picture of him crowned. Standing in the Heart. Holding the net.

It had been a picture then.

It wasn't a picture now. It was standing on the green at Ashbourne in the dark with the lantern ring going out behind it, and it was looking at him with his own eyes, and smiling his own smile.

The crooked one. The one Wren said made him look about twelve.

"*You could hold it*," said the glass Lex. Pleasantly. Reasonably. In Lex's own voice. "*The ring. All of it. Every lantern. You could hold every one of them up and keep them lit and keep her out and keep the grey out and keep everybody safe.*" It held out its free hand. Glass. Square. "*You've got Lv 99. You're one off. One. And then Tier Five. And then the crown.*"

"*No*," said Lex.

"*She'd give it you*," said the glass Lex. "*She told you. In the spring. She's holding it for you. It's warm. It's empty. She doesn't even want it. She just wants—*"

"*No*."

The glass Lex sighed.

"*I knew you'd say that*," it said. "*I'm you.*"

And swung.

---

It was faster than him.

That was the first thing. It was him—his shoulders, his reach, his weight, his twenty years at the anvil—but it was faster. Because when it reached for fire, the fire came.

Straight through the net. Not round. Through. A white-gold roar out of the glass hands, out of the glass hammer, the way Lex's own fire had come to him every day of his life until the still. Instant. Enormous. Easy.

Because the net was still, and the net was hers, and it was hers.

It hit him in the chest.

He went backward across the green. Twenty feet. Into the stocks by the church, which had not been used for punishing anybody since before Lex was born and were mostly for children to climb on. They broke. He broke, a little. Something in his side. He lay in the splinters with the breath knocked out of him and the fire still burning in his apron and looked up at the stars.

There weren't any stars. The grey had come in over the west of the village through the gaps in the ring, and the sky over the church was a picture of a sky.

---

The glass Lex walked toward him.

Unhurried. Hammer in hand. Crown on its head. Through the firelight.

"*I'm what you are*," it said. "*Underneath. Isn't that what she shows everyone? I'm the bit of you that wanted it. In the panel. In the spring. Just for a heartbeat. You looked at me and you thought, I could keep them safe.*"

Lex got up on one elbow.

"*You did*," said the glass Lex. "*She saw it. She sees everything.*"

He reached. Down. Round. To the heat. It came. Slow. Hand over hand. Up out of the deep.

The glass Lex hit him again before it got there.

---

He lay on his back on the green.

Burning. Broken. With the fire in him climbing too slowly up out of the earth and the fire in the glass man's hands pouring straight down out of the net like a waterfall.

He couldn't win this. Not fire against fire. Not his slow reaching against her open tap. Not a smith against his own reflection with the whole net behind it.

He couldn't burn it.

*It's made of seeing*, Kestrel had said.

---

He thought of Brannoc.

He didn't know why. Lying there. He thought of Brannoc at midnight in the Forge, kneeling at the great hearth. With his knees cracking. Banking the fire. Raking the ash over the coals, slow, careful, the way he'd done every night for forty-five years. Not putting it out. Never putting it out. Just—covering it. Hiding the light. So it would keep till morning.

*Banked*, said Brannoc. In the dark. To the King. *Not kept. There's a difference.*

He thought of Antho. On the hill. In the starlit dark. *It's a mould. A lantern's just a dark box with a light in it.*

He thought of Kestrel's black heat. Fire with no light.

A reflection was made of seeing.

You couldn't see without light.

---

"*ANTHO*," Lex shouted.

He didn't know if his brother could hear him. Across the green. Across the dark. Over the screaming and the glass breaking and the roar of the glass Lex's fire.

"*ANTHO, PUT IT OUT. ALL OF IT. EVERY LIGHT IN THE VILLAGE.*"

Silence.

Then, from somewhere across the green, high, cracking—

"*THE RING—*"

"*NOT OUT*," Lex shouted. "*DARK. BANK IT. COVER IT. LIKE BRANNOC. LIKE THE HILL.*" He coughed. Something in his side grated. "*LET THEM BURN IN THE DARK. LET THEM NOT BE SEEN.*"

---

The glass Lex stopped.

Over him. With its hammer raised. With the fire pouring down its arm. And looked down at him.

For the first time, it didn't smile.

---

And Antho Vey, Threshold Warden, Shade Commanding, standing in the middle of the green at Ashbourne with his hands up and a thousand glass faces turning toward him, understood.

---

The dark came down like a lid.

Not out of the sky. Out of him. Out of his hands. Out of his chest. Out of the shapes of the dead he'd seen in the gaps in the stars. Deep. Soft. Total. It went out from the middle of the green in a ring, like a ripple in a pond, over the church and the bakery and the forty-one houses and the Forge and the mill and the long grass, out to the lanterns.

And over them.

Every lantern in the ring. Every one that was still lit. The dark went over them like a hand closing gently round a candle flame. Not putting it out. Holding it. Cupping it. So the light stayed inside. So it burned, and burned, gold and real and given, in a little cup of dark, and didn't throw a single ray beyond its own panes.

Lex felt it. On his skin. The ring held. The lanterns burned. The grey stopped where it was.

And nobody could see a thing.

---

The glass Lex screamed.

It was Lex's voice. Lex's own voice, coming out of a man made of glass, screaming. High. Thin. Like a wet finger on the rim of a cup.

It came apart.

Not shattering. Fading. The way a reflection fades in a window when someone inside blows out the lamp. It went thin. Clear. Clearer. Until it was just an outline of a man with a hammer and a crown, drawn in faint lines on the dark. And then not even that.

The last thing to go was the crown.

It hung in the air over Lex for a moment after the rest of it had gone. Gold. Seven points. In the dark. On nothing.

Then it went too.

---

All round the ring, the glass faces went out.

Jem Tolley. Ned Tull. Hollis Penn. Old Mag's neighbour. Davy Pruett's mother. Hundreds of them. Faces of glass, in the gaps, at the lanterns, in the long grass. Going thin. Going clear.

Gone.

---

Silence.

Total. Dark. Like the inside of a closed book.

Lex lay on his back on the green in the splinters of the stocks and breathed. In. Out. The thing in his side grating. The fire going out of his apron.

He couldn't see his own hand.

Nobody could see anything.

Somewhere, very close, Brannoc was swearing. Steadily. Quietly. With great feeling.

Somewhere further off, a child was crying.

---

Then he saw it.

The only light in Ashbourne.

---

It was small. It was gold. It was soft. It was coming down the lane from the Forge. Not from a lantern. Not from a fire. Not from anything Antho's dark had cupped and covered.

It was coming from a child.

---

He'd known. They'd all known. Since the spring. Since the night Seraphine fainted on the green and Rhosyn caught her and said *you're carrying a light*. Since before that. Since the night his daughter was born on Lantern Night with the whole of Harrow lit round her. *Carrying a light* was a Harrow idiom, Old Mag said. And Lark was a Harrow. And a Vey. And an Ashdown. And a smith.

She glowed. A little. Always had. You only saw it in the dark.

There'd never been a dark like this.

She came down the lane in her nightshirt. Barefoot. On her own. Twenty months old. Out of the Forge, where somebody had opened a door in the dark, out of somebody's arms, where she'd wriggled, the way she always wriggled. Glowing. Soft. Gold. The only light in the world.

Laughing.

"*Gan*," said Lark. Delighted. To the dark. "*Gan, Gan—*"

Going toward the mill.

---

"*NO*—"

Lex was up. He didn't know how. His side screamed. He didn't care. He ran. Into the dark. Blind. Toward the small gold light going down the lane.

Behind him, he heard his mother. Running. Rhosyn. Running.

Ahead of him, Lark. Not running. Toddling. Fast. The way she did when she'd made up her mind. Down the lane. Past the bakery. Past the Pruetts'. Toward the bottom of the village. Toward the place where Brannoc had sat for two days on the bank with his hammer, and wasn't sitting now, because Brannoc had gone up the hill to the green when the lanterns started breaking.

Toward the mill-pond.

Under its sailcloth.

---

She found the corner.

Of course she did. She'd been finding corners all her life. The corner of a blanket. The corner of a gap in the hedge. The corner of the sailcloth over the mill-pond, where one of the ropes had come loose in the fighting and the canvas had slid back a foot.

She sat down on the bank. Glowing. And pulled it back.

And leaned over.

---

The pond was black.

Flat. Smooth. Still. Forty yards of it. Under the canvas. In the dark.

And in it—

One light.

Small. Gold. Soft. Round-faced. With a crest of black hair sticking up at the back like a jackdaw's.

The only light in Ashbourne. Reflected.

---

Lex was ten yards away.

He saw it. Running. Blind. With his side screaming and his heart going. He saw his daughter's light on the bank, and her reflection in the black water, gold, perfect, upside down.

And under it.

White.

Coming up.

---

Five yards.

---

The hand came out of the water.

Not reaching. Not grabbing. Gently. The way you'd lift a sleeping child out of a cot. It came up out of the black pond and closed round the small gold body on the bank, round the nightshirt and the bare muddy feet and the fists, and lifted.

Lark looked up at it.

At the glass hand round her. At the white cold light under the water. At the face coming up through it. Glass. Clear. Beautiful in the way a frost on a window is beautiful.

Her square determined eyebrows came down.

"*No*," said Lark.

---

"*I know, little one*," said the Glass Queen.

Very softly. Under the water. Through it. In a voice like a bell under ice.

"*I know you say no*," said Ysenne. "*You said it to me in the cup. You said it to the King.*" The glass hand held her. Gently. Firmly. "*That's why I'm not asking.*"

---

Three yards.

His mother was beside him. Running. Rhosyn was ahead of him. Somehow. Barefoot. Forty years old. Faster than any of them. Throwing herself across the last of the dark toward the bank. Toward the water. Toward the glass wrist coming up out of it.

She caught it.

Both hands. Round the glass. On the bank. On her knees.

"*YSY*," screamed Rhosyn. "*YSY. DON'T. DON'T. YSY, PLEASE, LOOK AT ME—*"

---

And for one moment—

One heartbeat. On the bank of the mill-pond. In the dark. With the only light in the world held up in a glass hand over black water—

The Glass Queen stopped.

Under the water. With her face a foot down. Looking up. At her sister. At Rosie. At a mortal woman of forty with thorns in her hair and tears on her face and both hands round a glass wrist, holding on.

Something went across the cut-jewel face. Something that wasn't anything Lex had a word for.

"*I'll give her back, Rosie*," said Ysenne. Very quietly. "*After.*"

And pulled.

---

Rhosyn went into the water to the shoulders.

She didn't let go. Lex saw it. Saw her hands round the glass, white-knuckled. Saw her go in. Saw the black water close over her arms, her shoulders, her hair.

Then the glass wrist turned.

The way glass turns. Not bending. *Cutting*. Edges. Planes. It turned in Rhosyn's hands and cut them. Both palms. To the bone.

Rhosyn screamed.

And let go.

---

Lex reached the bank.

He went into the water after them. Up to his chest. Down. Under. Into the black. Reaching. Blind. For anything. A nightshirt. A foot. A small gold light.

There wasn't anything.

There wasn't any water.

There was glass.

Under his hands. Under his knees. Smooth. Hard. Cold. Flat. A sheet of it. As if the mill-pond had frozen. As if it had never been water at all. He knelt on it, chest-deep in a black that wasn't water any more, and beat on it with his fists, and it didn't crack. It didn't even ring.

Under it, a long way down, a small gold light was going away.

Down. Into the dark. Into the glass. Smaller. Smaller. Like a lantern carried away down a long road.

Gone.

---

His mother pulled him out.

He didn't remember that either. He remembered her hands, square, freckled, bleeding, on his collar. He remembered lying on the bank in the mud beside Rhosyn, who was curled over her own hands, sobbing, with blood running between her fingers onto the grass.

He remembered the dark lifting.

Slowly. Antho letting go. The lanterns coming back, one by one, round the ring, gold. The grey stopped where it had stopped. The village coming back out of the dark. Houses. The church. The green. People. Running. Shouting.

And the mill-pond.

Lit by a hundred lanterns. Flat. Grey. Hard. Not black any more. Not bright. Not anything. Forty yards of dull grey glass, from bank to bank, like a slate. Like a gravestone. Reflecting nothing at all.

---

He heard Wren before he saw her.

Not shouting. Not screaming. She came down the lane at a run and stopped at the top of the bank and didn't make any sound at all.

He looked up at her.

He couldn't say it. He opened his mouth and nothing came. He'd been the one who said *put out the lights*. He'd been the one who made the dark. He'd made the only light in Ashbourne his daughter, and sent it down the lane to a mirror, and been five yards too slow.

Wren looked at his face.

Then she looked at the mill-pond. At the grey glass. At Rhosyn's blood on the bank. At Seren, on her knees, with her hands over her mouth.

She didn't ask.

---

She took her bow off her back.

She laid it on the grass. Carefully. And her quiver. And her knives. Both of them. And unbuckled her belt and laid that down too, and kicked off her boots, and stood on the bank of the mill-pond in her shirt and her leggings and her bare feet, with her fox-red braid down her back and her gold fingers—two fingers and a thumb on her left hand—catching the lantern light.

"*Wren*," said Lex.

"*She went down the glass*," said Wren. Not a question.

"*Yes*."

"*The glass road*." Wren looked at the grey slate of the pond. "*To the Heart. To the anvil. Where Ysenne sits.*"

"*Yes*."

"*Then I'm going down the glass*," said Wren Ashdown.

---

"*You can't*," said Rhosyn. From the grass. Through her teeth. Holding her bleeding hands against her chest. "*You can't walk on glass. Nobody can. It's hers. It's all hers. Every pane. She'll see you the moment you set foot—*"

"*She won't see me*," said Wren.

She looked down at her left hand. At the two gold fingers and the gold thumb. At the three fingers on her right hand that had learned to draw a bow in the spring because the left ones couldn't feel the string any more.

"*She can only see what throws a reflection*," said Wren. "*Kestrel said. It's made of seeing.*" She looked up. "*Mist doesn't throw a reflection. Mist just—goes. Between. Through.*" A thin hard smile. "*I'm the Mistwalker. I've been walking where things can't see me since I was ten.*"

"*Wren*," said Lex again. He was getting up. He didn't know how. "*Wait. I'll come—*"

"*You can't*," said Wren. Gently. "*You're a smith, love. You're iron and fire. You'd throw a reflection like a beacon.*" She looked at him. Her green eyes. Very steady. "*And you've got a door to finish. And a crown to deal with. And a gold king in the yard. And your mam and the thorn lady bleeding on the grass.*"

She knelt down in front of him. In the mud. And took his face in her hands. The gold ones and the flesh ones.

"*I'll bring her back*," said Wren. "*Or I'll stay with her. One or the other. She's not going to be on her own down there. Not for one minute.*"

She kissed him.

Then she stood up. And turned. And walked down the bank. Onto the grey glass of the mill-pond. Barefoot.

And went.

---

Not walked. Went.

Lex saw it. Everybody on the bank saw it. One moment there was a woman standing on a sheet of grey glass in the lantern light, a red braid down her back.

The next there was a breath of cold fog. Grey. Thin. Curling. Like your breath on a winter window.

And it sank.

Into the glass. Through it. The way mist sinks into a cold hollow at evening.

And was gone.

---

Lex knelt on the bank.

In the mud. With his side grating and his hands burned and the glass Lex's crown still printed on the backs of his eyes, gold, seven points, in the dark.

He looked at the mill-pond. At the grey. At the place where his wife had gone down after his daughter.

He thought: *the door's not finished*.

He thought: *Midsummer's in two days*.

He thought: *she said I'll give her back after*. *After what*?

He knew.

He'd always known. Since the spring. Since the panel. *YOUR DAUGHTER'S HANDS ARE VERY SQUARE.*

After the small square hand touched the net.

After the net took a Root.

After the crown.

---

Behind him, on the grass, somebody was walking.

Big. Slow. Gold. The grass going gold under his feet in a trail behind him, up the lane, from the green. Holding a cup. Holding, in his other hand, very gently, the way you'd hold a moth, something small and white.

The King stopped at the top of the bank.

He looked at the mill-pond. At the grey glass. At Rhosyn on the grass, bleeding. At Seren holding her. At Lex.

"*She took the little one*," said Oszric.

"*Yes*," said Lex.

The King stood there for a long time.

Then he opened his hand. And looked at the petal on his palm. White. Pink at the edges. Not gold.

"*Then I think*," said the Gilded King, very slowly, very quietly, the way a man says something he's been practising in the dark for a long time, "*it's time I went and had a word with my sister.*"
