# The Stranger in the Forge Light

For a long moment, nobody moved.

The stranger stood at the edge of the light with his staff planted on the cobbles and the mist curling around his boots. The raven on his shoulder cocked its head and studied Lex with one bright, black, entirely too intelligent eye. In the hearth behind Lex, the fire roared and leaned and reached, every flame bent toward the doorway, as if it wanted to get a look at the newcomer too.

"I think," Lex said carefully, "you've got the wrong forge."

"I don't think so." The stranger's voice was low and pleasant, with the clipped vowels of the north. "Lex Harrow. Nineteen years old. Apprentice, and lately journeyman, to Brannoc Holt of Ashbourne. Kindled three years ago this spring as a Smith, Common grade, level cap twenty-four." The storm-grey eyes flicked down to Lex's hands, then back up. "Spirit of four."

Lex's mouth went dry. "How do you know that?"

"Because it's wrong," said the stranger. "And I've been looking for exactly that kind of wrong for a very long time."

"*Long time*," agreed the raven.

There was a heavy footstep behind Lex. A shadow fell across the doorway, blotting out half the forge light, and Brannoc Holt came up beside him with a fire poker in one enormous fist. He didn't raise it. He didn't need to. Brannoc holding a poker was a statement in itself, the way a mountain is a statement.

"Evening," Brannoc said.

"Good evening." The stranger inclined his head, unruffled. "Master Holt, I presume. You'll forgive the hour."

"Depends what you want with my boy."

"Only to talk."

"You can talk from there."

"I could," the stranger agreed. "But it's cold, and wet, and I've walked eleven miles since noon, and what I have to say is not the kind of thing either of you will want the neighbors hearing." He glanced across the green, to where Mother Orla had come to the door of the Crooked Kettle and was peering out into the mist with undisguised interest. "And I suspect your neighbors hear a great deal."

Brannoc considered this. Lex could see him weighing it: the stranger's thin frame and old age against the staff and the brass charms and the bright, hard intelligence in those eyes. Brannoc had lived in the Vale all his life, but he'd been young once, and in his youth he'd travelled, and he knew what it meant when a man wore lightning on his cloak.

"You're a sorcerer," Brannoc said.

"I am."

"Warden?"

Something flickered across the stranger's face, quick as the tail of a fish. "Not for a long time."

Brannoc grunted. He looked at Lex. Lex, who didn't know what to say, said nothing.

"You'll keep your hands where I can see them," Brannoc said at last, and stood aside.

---

His name was Elric Thorne.

He told them so as he lowered himself onto the bench by the forge, with a small sigh of relief, and stretched his long legs toward the fire. Quill the raven hopped down from his shoulder onto the workbench and began investigating Brannoc's basket of eggs with the air of a tax collector, until Slag the cat rose from his ledge with every hair on end and the two of them settled into a silent, deadly staring contest that would last the rest of the evening.

"Elric Thorne," Brannoc repeated. He'd set the poker down but he hadn't sat; he leaned against the anvil with his arms folded. "Never heard of you."

"You wouldn't have. I've spent most of my life trying very hard not to be heard of." Elric held his hands out to the coals. They were long, thin, knotted hands, the knuckles swollen with age, and across the back of the right one ran another of those pale, branching scars. "It's a skill, like any other. You get better at it with practice."

"And what is it you practice," said Brannoc, "when you're not being unheard of?"

"Storms, mostly." Elric smiled. "And research. And, lately, looking for people who don't wish to be found." His gaze moved to Lex. "Which brings me to you."

Lex had been standing by the slack tub all this while, his arms crossed over his chest, trying to look like a person who wasn't shaking. He didn't know why he was shaking. He didn't know why the fire kept leaning toward him, or why the bar of cold iron he'd picked up and put down three times now was warm to the touch, or why every time this old man looked at him, he felt as though something in his chest was turning over, like a key in a stiff lock.

"You said my Spirit was wrong," he said.

"I did."

"It's not wrong. It's just low. Some people have low Spirit."

"Nobody has a Spirit of four, Lex." Elric's voice was gentle. "Not nobody *healthy*. Not nobody *alive*. The lowest natural score ever recorded in the Spire's archives was seven, and that was a man who had been born without part of his brain. Four isn't low. Four is *impossible*. Four is what you get when someone takes a natural score and puts a lid on it."

"A lid," Lex repeated.

"A seal." Elric leaned forward, the brass charms on his cloak chiming softly. "Young man, I've come a long way to tell you something, and I'm going to tell it to you plainly, because I'm too old and too tired to dress it up. You are no ordinary blacksmith. You were never meant to be. You possess the essence of a sorcerer, a power that runs deep in your blood, deeper than you can imagine, and someone you loved very much locked it away when you were small enough to hold in one arm."

The forge was quiet. The fire crackled. Out on the green, the mist moved silently north.

"That's mad," Lex said.

"Yes, it rather is."

"I've never done anything. Not once. Not a spark. My whole life—" He stopped. He thought of the slag pockets he could hear in iron. The sparks that hadn't fallen. The fire, leaning. "My whole life," he said again, less certainly.

"Your whole life, you've been sitting on a fire with a forge-door bolted over it," said Elric. "And now the bolts are starting to give. You've noticed, haven't you? These last few days. Weeks, perhaps. Iron that's warm when it should be cold. Fire that turns to look at you. Things you can feel, or hear, that no one else can." He watched Lex's face. "The Lattice Stone on your green. It's been changing color."

Lex said nothing.

"Gold," Elric said softly. "At the edges."

"How," said Brannoc, in a voice like distant thunder, "do you know all this?"

Elric turned his head to look at the smith. For a moment the bright hard intelligence in his eyes dimmed, and he looked only old and very tired.

"Because I knew the man who made the seal," he said. "I was his friend for forty years. And his name was Arion."

---

Lex had not heard his father's name spoken aloud in eight years.

His mother had said it, sometimes, when he was very small. Never in anger. Never with tears, not where the boys could see. She would say it quietly, offhand, while she was kneading bread or mending a shirt: *Your father liked his tea black. Your father used to sing that one. Your father would have laughed at that.* As if he were a man who'd stepped out to the shops and would be back any minute. As if there were nothing strange at all about a man who walked out of his family's life when his youngest son was four and never once wrote, never once sent money, never once came home.

*He was a merchant*, she'd said, when Lex was old enough to ask. *He went east for trade. Something must have happened. Roads are dangerous.* And then she would change the subject, and her face would close like a door.

After the fire, nobody said the name at all.

"My father," Lex said now, and his voice came out flat and hard and strange to his own ears, "was a merchant. He went east. He didn't come back."

"Your father was a sorcerer of the Wardens of the Lattice," Elric said. "Tier Four. One of perhaps a dozen people living who could claim the title Warden-Architect. He was the most gifted man I ever met, the most frustrating, and the most stubborn, and he didn't come back because he couldn't. Not because he didn't want to." He paused. "He did go east, though. That part's true."

"No."

"Lex—"

"*No.*" The word came out louder than he meant it to. The fire in the hearth jumped, a sudden roaring gout of flame that made Brannoc flinch and set the chains swinging. Lex didn't notice. "You don't get to walk in here and tell me that. You don't get to tell me he was some great sorcerer who had good reasons. He *left*. He left us. He left her. Whatever he was, wherever he went, he chose that over us, and she spent eleven years pretending she wasn't waiting for him, and then she *died*, and he wasn't there, and he's never been there, and I don't—" He realized his hands had curled into fists. He made them open. "I don't care what he was."

Elric looked at him for a long moment.

"You sound like him," he said quietly. "When you're angry. Did you know that?"

Lex turned away.

There was a long silence. The fire settled slowly back down in the hearth. Quill and Slag continued their staring match. Brannoc, by the anvil, was looking at Lex with an expression Lex didn't want to see.

"Why now?" Brannoc said at last. "Fifteen years. Why come now?"

"Because the seal is failing." Elric turned back to the fire. "A seal like the one Arion put on his sons doesn't hold itself. It's not iron; it's a working, a living thing, and it needs to be fed. Arion fed it from his own life. As long as he was alive and whole, the seal would hold, quiet and invisible, and the boy would grow up thinking he was ordinary." He spread his knotted hands. "If the seal is failing, it means the life feeding it is failing too."

Lex went still.

"I don't know where Arion is," Elric said. "I've been searching for eleven years. I know he went east, to the Vhessan march, sixteen years ago. I know he came back from there changed and frightened and wouldn't tell me why. I know he disappeared not long after. And I know that for these last few months, all across Aldermere, the Lattice has been... flickering. Fraying. Like a rope with too much weight on it." He looked up at Lex. "And then a month ago I felt it. A flare of something gold, down here in the southern Vale, where there shouldn't be anything gold at all. Like a spark from under a door. And I thought: *There. That's one of his.*"

"One of his," Lex said. "You mean—"

"I mean you," said Elric. "I mean the seal on you is cracking, Lex Harrow. Which means your father is in terrible trouble, wherever he is. Which means that very soon, and I mean days, not weeks, that power of yours is going to come through whether you want it or not." He held Lex's gaze. "And I would rather be standing next to you when it does."

---

He wanted to test it.

That was how Lex found himself, an hour later, standing in front of the old cracked Lattice Stone in the middle of the night with a cut on his palm.

It was full dark now. The village had gone to bed. A few lamps still burned in windows, and the Crooked Kettle was dark and shuttered, and the mist had thickened until the green was a shallow sea of silver-grey, waist-deep, drifting slowly, steadily north. The Stone stood up out of it like a rock out of the tide. Up close, its glow was stronger than Lex had ever seen it: a soft, pulsing silver, like breath, and at its edges, unmistakable now, a faint wavering fringe of gold.

"This is ridiculous," said Brannoc, who had insisted on coming. He held a lantern in one hand and the fire poker in the other.

"Very probably," Elric agreed. He had produced a small knife from somewhere in his cloak and used it to nick the ball of Lex's thumb, quickly and neatly, before Lex had quite registered what he was doing. "Hold it out. Over the crack. That's right."

Lex held his hand over the Stone. A bead of blood welled up from the cut, fat and dark in the lantern light, and trembled on his skin.

"What happens now?"

"Now," said Elric, "we find out whether I'm a mad old fool who walked eleven miles for nothing."

The drop of blood fell.

It struck the Stone right on the crack, where lightning had split it a hundred years ago, and for one heartbeat nothing happened at all.

Then the Stone *woke*.

Light came up out of it, gold light, so sudden and so bright that Lex cried out and threw his arm across his eyes. It poured out of the crack like molten metal poured from a crucible. It ran up the face of the Stone in branching lines, in shapes, in glyphs Lex had never seen and somehow almost recognized. The mist around the Stone lit up from inside like a cloud with the sun behind it. The whole green went gold, the trees, the cottages, the chapel steeple, every window flashing like a coin, and from somewhere deep in Lex's chest came an answering heat, sudden and enormous, like the door of a furnace swinging open.

The silver panel snapped open in front of his eyes on its own.

And then, as he watched, the numbers *moved*.

Not the Might, not the Agility. The Spirit. The little silver four shivered like a candle flame in a draft. It blurred. For an instant, just an instant, something else showed through underneath it, as if the four had been painted on glass and there was a different number on the wall behind:

Gold, not silver. A two-digit number, too blurred to read.

Then it was gone, and the four was back, and the light from the Stone was sinking back down into the crack, and the green was dark again except for Brannoc's lantern and a few gold motes drifting in the mist like fireflies.

Lex realized he was on his knees in the wet grass. He didn't remember falling. His heart was going like a hammer on an anvil. His hand was throbbing, and when he looked at it, the cut on his thumb had sealed itself shut, leaving only a thin pink line.

"Well," said Elric Thorne softly, somewhere above him. "Well, well. So it's true." He let out a long breath. "Of course it is. Of course it's true. He never did anything by halves."

Something in his voice made Lex look up.

The old sorcerer was standing very still in the drifting mist, staring at the Stone. The lantern light carved deep lines in his face. And his expression, just for a moment, before he noticed Lex watching and smoothed it away, was a strange, raw tangle of things: wonder and relief and grief, and under them something darker, something tight and hungry and sad, like a man looking through a window at a feast laid out for someone else.

Then it was gone, and he was only an old man again, offering Lex his hand.

"Up you get," he said. "I think we've made our point."

Brannoc hadn't moved. He was staring at the Stone with his mouth slightly open, the lantern trembling in his fist.

"Lady of Lanterns," he whispered. "Lady of Lanterns preserve us. I felt that in my *teeth*."

"Everyone in the valley felt it," said Elric. He didn't sound pleased. He was looking north now, past the Stone, past the edge of the village, toward the dark wall of the Greyspine. "And not only the valley, I'm afraid. That was a beacon. I'd hoped it would be smaller."

"What do you mean?" Lex got to his feet. His legs felt like wet rope. "Who else felt it?"

Elric didn't answer.

Quill had flown up from the forge and was circling overhead, a black shape against the dark. Now he let out a single harsh croak, very loud in the silence, and wheeled sharply north.

And in the quiet that followed, from somewhere up in the hills above the village, there came a sound.

It wasn't a howl. A howl was a wolf sound, a dog sound, a living sound. This was thinner and higher and colder, like wind through the gap of a broken window, and it seemed to come from several places at once. It rose, and held, and broke apart into whispers.

Every hair on Lex's arms stood up.

"Elric," Brannoc said. "What was that?"

The old sorcerer had gone very still. The brass charms on his cloak, Lex saw, had begun to glow. Faint blue sparks were crawling over them like ants.

"Master Holt," Elric said quietly, "I'm going to ask you to do something, and I need you to do it quickly and without asking why. Go to every house on this green and wake whoever's inside, and tell them to bar their doors and put out their lights and stay away from the windows. Start with the ones nearest the north road."

"What—"

"*Now*, Brannoc."

Something in his voice moved the big smith faster than Lex had ever seen him move. Brannoc was gone into the mist at a lumbering run, lantern swinging, bellowing names.

Lex stood by the Stone. Up in the hills, the not-howl came again. Closer.

"Lex," said Elric. "Do you have a weapon?"

"I'm a smith," Lex said. His voice sounded far away. "I have *several*."

"Get one. The heaviest you can swing."

---

Lex ran.

He ran across the green through the waist-deep mist with his heart slamming, and into the forge, where the fire was roaring so high in the hearth that the flames were licking out over the brick lip and up the chimney breast. Slag the cat was nowhere to be seen. Lex went straight to the rack on the back wall and grabbed the first thing his hand fell on, which was Brannoc's old sledge, the big one, a twelve-pound head of black iron on a hickory haft as long as his arm. He'd swung it a thousand times. It had never felt like this. When his hands closed around the haft, the whole thing seemed to *hum*, a low, steady note, like the ploughshare earlier that day, and he felt the heat in his chest rise up to meet it.

He ran back out into the night.

Mist. Moonless dark. Doors were banging all around the green. He could hear Brannoc's voice, and somewhere a child crying, and Reeve Dunstan demanding to know what in the name of the Lady was going on. Lamps were going out one by one in windows like eyes closing.

And coming down the north road, over the crest of the hill where the lane ran out of the village and up toward the mountains, the mist was changing color.

It was black.

Not dark. Black. A slick, oily, absolute black, like spilled ink, like the bottom of a well. It came pouring over the hill and down the lane in a slow, heavy tide, swallowing the silver of the Veil as it came, and where it touched, things *went wrong*. The hedgerows on either side of the lane went grey, leaves and all, as if every bit of color had been drained out of them. The grass went grey. A wooden gate that it flowed over sagged suddenly on its hinges, its timbers gone pale and soft as old bread. And the smell came ahead of it on the wind, a smell Lex would never forget for as long as he lived: wet ash, and rot, and under them, faint and impossibly cold, the smell of nothing at all.

Things were moving inside it.

They came out of the black mist at the bottom of the lane, where it pooled against the first cottages: four of them, then six, then eight. At first he thought they were dogs. They had the shape of dogs, or of wolves. Lean, long-legged, slope-backed shapes, loping low to the ground. But they were the wrong size, too big and then too small, shifting from moment to moment like shadows thrown by a guttering candle, and they had no color at all, no fur, no flesh. They were made of the black mist, folded and packed and pressed into the shape of hunting animals, and their edges smoked and frayed and drifted away behind them as they ran.

Their eyes were white. Blank, flat white, like boiled eggs, like the eyes of the Tanners' sheep.

And they were *whispering*. All of them. A soft, wet, hissing murmur that came from everywhere and nowhere, and as they got closer Lex could make out words in it, or almost words. *Bright. Bright. There. Bright. Warm. There.*

They were looking at him.

"*Gloamhounds*," said Elric Thorne beside him, very quietly. Lex hadn't heard him come up. The old sorcerer's staff was raised, and the brass charms on his cloak were blazing now, crawling with blue-white fire, and the air around him smelled sharp and clean, like the moment before a lightning strike. "Stay behind me if you can. If you can't, keep your back to something solid, and hit them with iron. Cold iron hurts them. Not much. But it hurts."

"*What are they?*"

"Hungry," said Elric, and the first hound leaped.

---

Lex had never seen magic. Not real magic. He'd seen Old Mag light a candle with a whispered word, and Sister Agathe bless the lanterns so they burned a little brighter at service, and once, when he was small, a traveling [Illusionist] at the summer fair who'd made silver fish swim through the air over the children's heads.

This was not that.

Elric Thorne raised his staff and said a word that was not a word, and the night split open.

Lightning came down out of a clear sky. It hit the leaping gloamhound in midair with a sound like the world being torn in half, so loud that Lex felt it in his chest more than heard it, and so bright that for a long moment afterward he could see nothing but a jagged purple afterimage burned across his eyes. When his sight cleared, the hound was gone. There was a smoking black scorch mark on the cobbles where it had been, and a few wisps of dark mist curling up off it like steam off a horse, and that was all.

The other hounds didn't slow down.

They came on in a rush, seven of them, low and fast and whispering, and Elric met them. He moved like a man thirty years younger. His staff spun and cracked and spat blue fire, and every time it touched one of the hounds, the creature screamed, that thin, cold, glass-cracking scream, and came apart in rags of smoke. Lightning danced from his fingertips. It chained from hound to hound, leaping through the black mist in forks and branches, and the air filled with the smell of ozone and burning ash. One hound got its teeth into the hem of his cloak and he struck it with the butt of his staff and it burst like a soap bubble. Another came at him from the side and he turned without looking and a crackling bolt took it in the chest and flung it twenty feet back into the dark.

Lex stood frozen with the sledge in his hands and his mouth open.

Then a hound went past him.

It went wide around Elric, low to the ground, so fast it was only a smear of dark in the corner of Lex's eye, and it wasn't going for Lex at all. It was going for the Crooked Kettle. Where a door had just been flung open, and a small figure in a nightshirt had come stumbling out into the mist, rubbing his eyes, calling, "*Mam? Mam, what's happening, what's that noise—*"

Pell.

Pell had been sleeping over at the Kettle. He did that sometimes, when his da had been at the ale.

Lex didn't think. He moved.

He ran across the green with the sledge in both hands and his boots skidding on the wet cobbles, and the hound reached the boy a heartbeat before he did. It reared up, all black smoke and white eyes and a mouth that opened far, far too wide. Pell screamed. Lex didn't. He swung the sledge sideways with everything he had, the way he'd swung it ten thousand times at ten thousand glowing billets, and twelve pounds of cold black iron caught the gloamhound square in the ribs.

It was like hitting wet sand. The hammer went *into* the creature, and the hound screamed and flew sideways and crashed against the wall of the Kettle, and for a moment it came apart, a burst of black smoke spattering across the whitewash like ink.

Then it pulled itself back together.

It took a second. Lex watched it happen. The scattered smoke drew back in, curling, folding, packing itself down into the shape of a hound again, and the white eyes opened, and it turned toward him.

"*Get inside*," Lex said to Pell, not looking at him. "Get inside and bar the door. Go. *Go!*"

He heard the boy scramble backward, the door slam.

The hound came at him.

He swung again. It ducked. It was fast, so much faster than him, and it went under the hammer and its teeth closed on his left forearm.

He'd been burned before. Every smith had. He'd burned his hands and his arms and once, memorably, the back of his neck, and he knew what it felt like when hot iron touched skin.

This was the opposite.

The cold went into him like a nail. It went through the skin and the muscle and into the bone and up his arm and into his shoulder and down into his chest, and everywhere it went, things *stopped*. His arm went numb. His fingers opened on their own and the sledge thudded into the mist at his feet. The cold reached his heart and his heart stuttered. He couldn't breathe. His vision was going grey at the edges, grey like the hedgerows, grey like the grass, all the color draining out of the world, and somewhere very far away he could hear the hound's whisper, close now, right against his ear, wet and pleased and hungry: *Bright. Bright. Warm. Mine.*

A red panel opened in front of his eyes.

He was going to die.

He knew it with total clarity, the way he'd known about the slag pocket in the horseshoe that morning. He was going to die on the village green, in the mist, with a monster made of smoke hanging off his arm, and Brannoc would find him in the morning grey and cold and white-eyed like the Tanners' sheep, and that would be that. Lex Harrow. Smith. Common grade. Level nine. Cap twenty-four.

*No*, he thought.

It wasn't brave. It wasn't defiant. It was simpler than that. It was the same stubbornness that had kept him at the anvil every morning for eight years. The same stubbornness that had made him refuse, at four years old, to give up an iron horseshoe nail he'd found in the road. *No. Mine. I'm keeping it.*

*No. Not like this. Not cold.*

And deep inside him, under the cold, under the grey, behind a door he'd been walking past his whole life, something heard him.

The door didn't open.

It *broke*.

---

It started in his chest, in the place right under his breastbone where the cold had stopped his heart, and it was heat. Not warmth. *Heat*. The heat of the forge at full blast, of iron at white glow, of the sun on a summer anvil. It came roaring up out of some place he hadn't known was there, and the cold didn't so much retreat in front of it as simply *cease to be*, the way frost ceases to be when you hold a torch to it. His heart slammed back into motion, once, twice, hard as a hammer. Color flooded back into the world. His left arm, his numb, dead left arm, filled with a burning so fierce he cried out, and the gloamhound clamped on it *screamed*.

It let go. It reeled back. Smoke was pouring off its jaws where they'd touched him, black smoke turning grey, turning white, and Lex looked down at his arm and saw that his skin was *glowing*. Faintly. Gold. Like iron just beginning to take the heat. The bite wounds in it were sizzling shut.

The world went still.

Not stopped. Still. The hound hung in the air, reeling backward, frozen in place like a fly in amber. The mist had stopped moving. A drop of water hanging from the eave of the Crooked Kettle had stopped halfway through its fall and hung there, catching the light. There was no sound at all except his own heartbeat, slow and enormous.

And in front of his eyes, the silver panel of his status *cracked*.

He watched it happen. A fine line ran across the silver text like a crack running across ice. Then another. Then a hundred, a spiderweb of them, spreading outward from the center. Light shone through the cracks. Gold light. Warm and deep, the color of embers in a banked fire. And then the silver shattered completely, fell away in glittering pieces that dissolved before they hit the ground, and behind it, waiting, as if it had been waiting all along, was something else.

Heat poured off it in waves that set the mist steaming. The hickory haft should have charred in his grip; it didn't. The heat ran from his chest down his arm and into the iron like water down a channel, and the iron drank it and asked for more, and he knew, without anyone telling him, without understanding how he knew, exactly what to do.

*[Ember Strike,]* said the voice, quiet and approving. *[You already know how. You've been doing it for years. You just never had anything to put into it.]*

The hound leaped.

It was the most natural thing in the world. It was the swing he'd made a hundred thousand times, from the shoulder, from the hips, with the whole weight of his body behind it, with the heat in his chest pouring out through his arms and into the iron at the exact moment of impact. *Chang.* The hammer met the hound in midair. One moment it was there, black and white-eyed and hungry. The next it was a burst of gold sparks drifting down through the mist, winking out one by one as they fell.

Elric Thorne sat down next to him, slowly, with a small groan, and laid his staff across his knees.
