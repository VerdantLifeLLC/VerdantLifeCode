# The Road to Thistledown

The wolves found him on the second morning.

He'd been climbing for a day and a half by then, up out of the foothills and into the true mountains, where the road shrank to a track and the track shrank to a goat path and the goat path finally gave up altogether and became a vague suggestion of flattened grass between boulders. The air had turned thin and sharp. The pines had thinned out and gone stunted and twisted, crouching low against the rocks as if they were afraid of the wind. Twice he'd crossed snowfields, old dirty snow from the winter, crusted and grey and pocked with the tracks of things he didn't recognize. Once he'd stopped at the edge of a gully and looked down and seen, far below, a patch of grass gone the color of ash, and the thin black thread of something that might have been smoke and might not, curling up out of a crack in the rock.

He'd gone around that gully. A long way around.

*[Wise,]* Ember had said.

The wolves came out of the morning mist in a tight grey wedge, five of them, loping silently across the slope toward him. They were big, bigger than any wolf he'd seen in the Vale, with thick shaggy coats the color of granite and pale yellow eyes. A small silver tag floated over the lead wolf's head as it came, the way tags floated over anything the Lattice considered a threat.

The lead wolf leaped.

He moved without thinking, the way he'd move to catch a falling tool. He sidestepped, quicker than he'd ever moved before, and the wolf's jaws snapped shut on air where his throat had been. He brought Anvilsong around in a short, flat arc and felt the heat go down his arms into the iron, *Ember Strike*, and the hammer caught the wolf on the shoulder with a flash of gold and a smell of singed fur. The wolf yelped and tumbled away across the rocks.

It didn't die. Ordinary beasts were tougher than gloamhounds, it turned out. Gloamhounds were made of mist and hunger, and the fire went straight through them. Wolves were made of muscle and bone and stubbornness, and the fire had to fight its way in.

The other four came at him all at once.

He fought. He didn't know how else to describe it afterward. He had no training, none at all; Brannoc had taught him to swing a hammer at iron, not at things that dodged. But he had strength, and the new quickness in his legs, and the hammer was so perfectly balanced that it seemed to know where it wanted to go. He kept his back to the boulder so they couldn't get behind him. He swung low to keep them off his legs. When one darted in, he struck at it, and when it darted out, he let it go and turned to the next.

And under it all, steady as a heartbeat, Ember's voice.

*[Left. Low. Good. The one on the right is circling. Watch its shoulders, not its eyes; wolves telegraph with the shoulders. Now. Good. You're spending too much mana. Not every swing needs fire; save it for when you'll connect. Breathe. There's one trying to get above you on the rock—]*

He spun, and swung upward, and the hammer caught a wolf in midair as it leaped down from the top of the boulder. Gold fire. A yelp cut short. The wolf hit the ground and didn't get up.

He knew that. He was a village boy; he'd seen animals put down before. He'd held a lantern for Farmer Tobbin once when the old plough-horse broke its leg in a ditch. He lifted the hammer.

The wolf watched him.

He brought the hammer down, quick and clean, without fire. It was over in an instant.

He leveled again by evening, and once more the next morning.

The mountains were full of things that wanted to kill him, just as Elric had said. Rock-lizards the size of dogs that lay perfectly still on sun-warmed ledges, the color of the stone, and lunged when you got too close. A thing like a huge shaggy goat with horns like a ram's and a temper like a bull's that charged him across a snowfield and nearly sent him over a cliff. A swarm of small grey bats that came boiling out of a crack in the rock at dusk, shrieking, and that he finally drove off by swinging Anvilsong in a wide blazing circle over his head until the air around him shimmered with heat.

That had been a new thing. He hadn't known he could do it until he did. He'd been swinging the hammer, desperate, and the heat had come pouring off the iron in waves, not in a single strike but in a continuous rolling pulse, and the bats had veered away from it like smoke from a fan.

But he'd only gotten to level eight because of the bear.

It had happened late on the second day.

He'd been following a stream up a narrow valley, looking for a place to cross, when he smelled it: wet ash, and rot, and under them, faint and cold, the smell of nothing. The Gloam smell. He'd stopped dead on the bank with every hair on his arms standing up.

*[Lex,]* Ember had said quietly. *[Up the slope. To your left. Don't move suddenly.]*

He'd looked.

At first he'd thought it was a boulder. A huge grey-brown mound of a thing, half-hidden among the stunted pines thirty paces up the hillside. Then it had lifted its head.

It was a bear. Or it had been a bear once. It was a cave bear, the great shaggy kind that lived high in the Greyspine and that the trappers at Thistledown spoke of with careful respect, twice the size of a plough-horse and covered in a thick coat of brown fur. But the fur was wrong. Great patches of it had gone grey, the dead lifeless grey of the hedgerows on the north road, and where the grey patches were, the flesh underneath seemed to have shrunk, pulled tight over the bones. One of its eyes was small and dark and rimmed with red, a beast's eye. The other was flat, blank, boiled-egg white.

And it was whispering.

Low, wet, almost too faint to hear. Not words. Just a hiss, like breath going in and out of a cracked bellows, rising and falling.

The tag that floated over its head wasn't silver. It was silver with black bleeding into the edges, like ink spreading on wet paper.

---

He was up late in Thistledown's little smithy, which the old miner who ran it had grudgingly let him use for a silver penny, working on Wren's bow by the light of the forge.

It was delicate work, much harder than iron. He couldn't hammer a rune into wood. He had to *coax* it, warming the yew gently with Heat Shaping until it was soft and supple, then pressing the rune in with the tip of a heated awl, a fraction at a time, so slowly and carefully that his eyes ached and his hand cramped. Twice he felt it start to go wrong, felt the wood begin to scorch, and had to stop and let it cool and start again.

But by midnight the crack was gone. In its place, set into the grain of the yew where the crack had been, was a tiny mark like a knot of thread, glowing faintly gold for a moment before it faded.

He turned toward the back of the smithy, toward the little door that let out onto the woodpile and the palisade wall behind it.

Wren was standing in it.

She had her pack on her back and her coat buttoned to the chin. She looked at him, and at her bow in his hands, and at the gold mark glowing faintly in the yew.

"Took you long enough," she said.

"How did you—"

"I've been sitting on the woodpile for an hour waiting for you to finish. It's freezing out here." She held out her hand, and he gave her the bow, and she ran her thumb along the mended limb. Her eyebrows went up. "Huh. That's good work, Hammer."

From the yard came the voice of the hatchet-faced Warden: "Search the buildings. All of them."

"There's a gap in the palisade behind the woodpile," Wren said briskly, slinging the bow over her shoulder. "Kids use it to sneak out and go skating on the tarn. It's tight. You'll have to leave the hammer for a minute and pass it through after." She turned. "Come on."

"Wren," he said. "If they catch you helping me—"

"Then they catch me." She looked back at him over her shoulder, and her green eyes were bright in the forge light. "You paid me in full, Hammer. Nobody pays me in full. I'm not losing a customer like that to a bunch of grey-cloaks." She jerked her head at the door. "Besides. I want to see what you are."

She slipped out into the dark.

Lex looked once more through the front door of the smithy at the torch-lit yard, at the grey cloaks, at the tall pale-eyed man turning slowly, scanning, his gaze sliding across the buildings like a blade across a whetstone.

Just for a moment, that pale gaze passed over the smithy door. And stopped.

Lex didn't wait to see what came next. He ducked out the back door into the freezing night, and followed Wren Ashdown into the dark.
