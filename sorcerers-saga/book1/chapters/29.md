# A Ray of Light

The Pale Wastes were white.

Not snow-white. Not cloud-white. A dead, flat, glaring white that went on in every direction to the edge of the world and stopped there, and the sky above it was so pale it was almost white too, so that on the worst days there was no horizon at all. Only white below and white above and the four of them walking through the middle of it like ants across a sheet of paper.

Salt. All of it. A great dry lakebed of salt, Seraphine said, from some sea that had dried up long before the Sundering. Crusted and cracked into a million hexagons, like the floor of a giant's kitchen. It crunched under their boots. It got into everything: their clothes, their packs, their hair, the corners of their eyes. It cracked their lips. It burned their skin by day and froze it by night, because the Pale Wastes were bitterly hot when the sun was up and bitterly cold when it went down, and there was nothing in between.

There was no water.

They'd filled every skin and bottle they had at the last stream on the edge of the fens. Wren had made them drink until they were sick. It lasted nine days. On the tenth day, Wren started rationing. On the twelfth, she stopped drinking her own share and started giving it to Seraphine when she thought no one was looking. On the fourteenth, Lex noticed, and started giving his to Wren, and Wren noticed, and they had a furious whispered argument in the middle of the white nothing while Antho and Seraphine pretended not to hear.

On the fifteenth day, the water ran out.

---

Ashka found the spring.

She'd been ranging out ahead of them every morning, high and wide, gliding on the hot air rising off the salt, her storm-blue scales glittering against the white sky. She was bigger now. Much bigger. The size of a pony, almost, though still lean and light and long-limbed. The lightning in the storm on the northern moor had done something to her that hadn't stopped; she grew a little every week. Her silver wing-frame had stretched and stretched, the runes Lex had struck into it letting it grow with her, and the torn membrane inside it had almost healed.

On the morning of the sixteenth day, she came back from the east screaming.

Not a frightened scream. An excited one. She swooped down out of the white sky and landed in front of Antho in a spray of salt crystals, and pushed her long narrow head against his chest, and sent him pictures so bright and so loud that Lex felt them through the Bloodbond like a shout.

*Water. Water. Little water. Under the white. Smells wet. Come. COME.*

It was barely a spring. A crack in the salt crust, four miles east, with a little seep of brackish water pooling at the bottom, so salty it made Lex's eyes water to drink it. But it was water. They lay on their bellies around it and drank in turns, slowly, a mouthful at a time, the way Wren told them to. And then they filled the skins, a little at a time, as the seep refilled, and it took all day.

"Two more days," said Wren that night. She was sitting by their tiny fire of dried salt-brush, looking at the skins. "Maybe three. If we're careful."

"How far to the Stillwater?" said Antho.

Seraphine had the old map open on her knees. She'd been studying it every night by firelight, squinting through her cracked spectacles. Her burns had begun to heal, slowly, leaving shiny pink scars down the left side of her face and neck. She could walk now, with a stick, limping.

"I don't know," she said quietly. "The map says *the Stillwater lies at the heart of the white waste*. That's all. It doesn't say where the heart is." She looked up. "We could be a day away. We could be a month."

Nobody said anything.

---

Lex heard the hum on the seventeenth day.

He didn't know what it was at first. He thought it was his own blood, ringing in his ears from the heat and the thirst. They'd been walking since dawn, and the sun was a white hammer on the white anvil of the salt, and his head was pounding.

Then he realized it wasn't in his ears. It was in his *feet*.

He stopped walking.

"Lex?" Wren turned. Her face was burned red and peeling, her lips cracked and bloody. "What is it?"

"Shh."

He stood very still on the cracked white salt. He closed his eyes.

It was there. Faint. So faint. Coming up through the soles of his boots, through the salt crust, from somewhere deep below. A hum. A vibration. Like the hum he used to feel in iron when he was hammering it, the hum that told him where the slag pockets were. Like the hum of the fire in the room under the water at Qel'Varran.

Not the same. Different. Lower. Slower. *Heavier*.

*Stone*, he thought, and didn't know why he thought it.

He knelt. He laid his palm flat on the salt.

The hum got louder.

It wasn't coming from directly below. It was coming from his left. Northeast. It was stronger in that direction, like a sound you can hear better if you turn your head. And under it, faint, very faint, something else. A different note. Higher. Cooler. *Moving*. Like a stream running underground.

*Water.*

He opened his eyes. Wren was crouched beside him, staring at him.

"What is it?" she said. "What are you doing?"

"I can hear it," said Lex. His voice was hoarse. "The ground. Under the salt. There's rock down there. And it's—*humming*. And under the rock, there's something moving." He swallowed. "Water. There's water. That way."

He pointed northeast.

Wren looked at where he was pointing. At the flat dead white, going on to the edge of the world. Then back at him.

"You can *hear* it," she said slowly.

"I can hear it."

"Your class is locked. Your Root's locked. You can't do anything. You said so."

"I know." He looked down at his palm, flat on the salt. "This isn't the class. It's like the fire. In the room under the water. It's not the Lattice." He looked up at her. "It's *under* it."

Wren stared at him for a long moment.

Then she stood up, and shouldered her pack, and turned northeast.

"Then let's go," she said.

---

They walked northeast for a day and a night and half of another day.

Lex walked in front now. Every hour, he'd stop, and kneel, and lay his palm on the salt, and close his eyes, and listen. And every hour, the hum was a little louder. A little clearer. The heavy slow note of the stone, and the cool moving note of the water under it, and something else, something he couldn't name, a third note, warmer, steadier, like a fire burning in a hearth very far away.

He didn't know what he was doing. He just did it. The way he'd known which way to turn his wrist over a flawed horseshoe, on the morning Elric came. The way his hand found the hammer in the dark.

On the morning of the nineteenth day, the water ran out again.

On the evening of the nineteenth day, Seraphine fell and didn't get up.

She just went down, quietly, in the middle of the white salt, her stick clattering away from her. Antho was beside her in an instant. He knelt and gathered her up and held her, and her face was grey under the shiny pink scars, and her lips were cracked and white.

"I'm fine," she whispered. "I'm fine. I just need—a minute—"

"You're not fine." Antho's voice was raw. "*Sera*, you're not fine."

"I'm *clinical*," said Seraphine faintly, and tried to smile.

Antho made a sound that wasn't a laugh.

Lex saw the black creeping into the edges of his brother's eyes. Saw the shadows beginning to rise off him, slow, curling, out of control.

"*Antho*." He went and knelt beside them. "*Antho, look at me.*"

"*She's dying, Lex*."

"She's not dying. She's thirsty. We're all thirsty." Lex put his hand on his brother's shoulder. "The water's close. I can hear it. It's close."

"*How close?*"

Lex didn't answer. He didn't know. The hum was loud now, louder than it had ever been, but he didn't know how far it was. A mile. Ten. A hundred.

He knelt on the salt and laid both his palms flat on it, and closed his eyes, and *listened*.

*Please*, he thought. Not in Root. Not in words. *Please. Where are you? Help us. Please.*

And something answered.

Not the stone. Not the water. The third note. The warm one. The fire in the hearth very far away.

It got *louder*.

It came toward him. Through the stone, through the salt, through the cold dry air of the evening, like the warmth of a hearth when someone opens the door. And it wasn't coming from underground. It was coming from *in front* of him. From the east. From a little way off across the flat white salt.

He opened his eyes.

And there was a man walking toward them out of the sunset.

---

He was small.

That was the first thing Lex noticed. Very small, smaller than Seraphine, shorter even than Wren. Brown and wrinkled as a walnut, with a bald brown head and a wispy white beard that came down to his waist. He was barefoot on the salt. He wore a robe of undyed wool, very old, patched and darned a hundred times, belted at the waist with a length of rope. He carried a long staff of pale wood, gnarled and smooth with handling, and over one shoulder he had slung a goatskin bag that sloshed when he walked.

And he was laughing.

Not loudly. Softly, to himself, as he came across the white salt toward them. As if something had just occurred to him that was very funny, and he was enjoying it privately.

Ashka saw him and went absolutely still. Then she did something Lex had never seen her do for anyone but Antho. She lowered her long blue head to the salt, and spread her wings flat on either side of her, and *bowed*.

The little man stopped in front of her. He looked down at her with his head tilted on one side.

"Well, well," he said. His voice was high and cracked and very cheerful, like an old door hinge that someone had oiled. "A storm-drake. Haven't seen one of you in a very long time. Two hundred years? Three?" He reached out one gnarled brown hand and touched the top of her head, very gently. Ashka shivered all over and made a sound like a purring cat the size of a horse. "Hello, little sister. You've been hurt. Someone mended you. Someone with good hands." He looked at the silver frame on her wing, and his eyebrows went up. "*Very* good hands."

He turned and looked at the four of them.

At Lex, kneeling on the salt with his palms flat. At Wren, standing beside him with her bow half-raised and her face wary. At Antho, crouched with Seraphine in his arms, his eyes flickering black.

His gaze lingered on Antho for a long moment. Something in his wrinkled face went very still and very serious. Then it moved on, to Seraphine, and softened.

"Thirsty," he said. "Very thirsty. Silly children, walking out into the white with no water. Didn't anyone tell you there's no water in the white?" He unslung the goatskin bag from his shoulder and tossed it to Wren, who caught it out of the air by reflex. "Drink. Slowly. Give the sick one the first of it."

Wren stared at the bag in her hands. It was heavy. Full. It sloshed.

"Who are you?" she said.

The little man looked at her. Then at Lex. And his eyes, Lex saw, were the strangest eyes he'd ever seen. Not grey, not brown, not any one color. They seemed to change, as he looked at you. Brown like earth, then blue like water, then gold like fire, then grey like a storm. Shifting. Never still.

"I'm the one you've been looking for," he said. "Or one of them, anyway. I expect you've been looking for a lot of people." He grinned. Half his teeth were missing. "I'm Malachi."

---

Lex used True Sight without thinking. He couldn't, of course. His Root was locked. He reached for it out of habit and found only the iron door.

But Wren could Inspect. Every Lattice-bound person could Inspect, a little, the ordinary way. And Lex saw her eyes go unfocused as she looked at the little man, and then saw her face change.

"There's nothing," she said slowly. "There's no panel. There's nothing there."

Antho had looked too. His black-grey eyes were very wide.

"It says *no record*," he whispered. "The Lattice doesn't—it doesn't *know* him."

"Of course it doesn't." Malachi sat down cross-legged on the salt, as if he'd decided they were going to be there a while. "I never let it. I was there when it was made, you know. When my brother wove it. I stood beside him on the night of the Weaving and watched him do it." He shrugged. "I told him I didn't want to be in it. He was cross, but he respected it. He made a little hole in the net, just my size, and let me stay outside." He grinned again. "Very kind of him. He wasn't usually kind."

"Your *brother*," said Seraphine faintly, from Antho's arms. She'd taken a mouthful of water from the goatskin and was looking at the little man over the rim of it with her cracked spectacles askew. "*Malachi, brother of Aurelian.*"

"That's what they call me in the margins, is it?" Malachi looked delighted. "*Brother of Aurelian.* As if that's the most important thing about me." He shook his head. "He was the oldest. I was the youngest. There were five of us in between. He was always the clever one. I was always the one who laughed too much." He was quiet for a moment, and the laughter went out of his strange shifting eyes. "I miss him. Even now. Even after everything."

Lex stared at him.

"You're nine hundred years old," he said.

"Nine hundred and forty-one, actually. Nobody ever counts the forty-one." Malachi leaned on his staff. "The Wells keep you, if you listen to them long enough. Not forever. Nothing's forever. But a long time." He looked at Lex. "You've started listening. Haven't you. I heard you. Calling out across the white." His eyes went gold. "*Please. Where are you? Help us.* Very loud. Very rude. Like someone shouting in a library." He grinned. "I came to see who was making all the noise."

"You *heard* that?"

"The whole white waste heard it. The stone heard it. The water heard it. The fire in my hearth jumped right up out of the grate." Malachi tilted his head. "You've got a very loud voice, for someone who doesn't know how to use it yet."

---

He took them to the Stillwater.

It wasn't far. That was the absurd thing. A mile, perhaps, across the white salt to the east. They'd been walking toward it for three days without knowing. And then the salt simply *dipped*. A shallow bowl in the white plain, so gentle you'd never notice it until you were standing at the edge. And in the bottom of the bowl, there was green.

A valley. A small one. A hidden one. Sunk down below the level of the salt, so that you couldn't see it until you were almost on top of it. And it was *green*. Grass and trees and moss and ferns, so bright and so lush after nineteen days of white that Lex's eyes stung. There were fruit trees, heavy with apples and plums. There was a little stream, running down the side of the bowl in a series of tiny waterfalls. There were goats grazing on the slopes, white and brown, with bells around their necks that clanked softly.

And in the center of the valley, a pool.

It was small. Round. No bigger than the millpond at Ashbourne. And it was perfectly still. As still as Mirrormere. It held the sky the way the great lake had held it: every cloud, every streak of sunset, perfectly reflected. A little round mirror laid in the green bottom of the valley, holding a little round piece of the sky.

Beside it, there was a hut. Round, of grey stone, with a turf roof. Smoke rising from its chimney.

"Home," said Malachi cheerfully. "Not much. But I've had nine hundred years to get it how I like it."

He led them down into the valley.

---

Inside the hut, there was a fire.

Lex knew it the moment he ducked through the low doorway. It was burning in a round hearth of white stone in the center of the single room, and there was no wood in it, no coal, no fuel. Just fire, dancing on bare stone. The twin of the fire in the room under the water at Qel'Varran.

And it leaned toward him.

Harder than the one at Qel'Varran. Much harder. The flames bent toward him so sharply they almost lay flat, like grass in a wind. Like a dog straining at a leash toward its master.

Malachi stopped in the middle of the room and looked at the fire. Then at Lex.

"Oh," he said softly. "Oh, *my*."

"What?" said Lex.

"It knows you." Malachi's shifting eyes had gone gold, steady gold, and were fixed on Lex's face. "It *likes* you. That fire's been burning in that hearth for nine hundred years, and it's never once bowed to anyone but me." He looked at the fire again. It was still leaning. "And it's not bowing to *me*, now."

Nobody said anything.

Malachi sat down slowly on a three-legged stool by the hearth. He looked at Lex for a long time.

"Sit down," he said at last. "All of you. You're very tired and very thirsty and one of you is very sick. And I have a pot of goat stew and a great deal of tea and more questions than I've had in nine hundred years." He reached out one gnarled hand toward the fire, and the flames, reluctantly, the way a dog reluctantly leaves its master to come to someone else, bent toward him and let him warm his fingers. "And then I'll tell you what you are. And what's wrong with you. And whether I can fix it."

---

He fixed Seraphine first.

He did it after the stew, which was the best thing Lex had ever eaten in his life, and the tea, which tasted of honey and mint and something green and sharp that Lex didn't recognize. He made Seraphine lie down on a pallet by the fire. He knelt beside her. He looked at her burns: the shiny pink scars down the left side of her face and neck, and the worse ones under her robe, on her chest, where Elric's gold lightning had struck.

"Root-fire," he said. "Stolen Root. Burned with a Hollow-graft." He clicked his tongue. "Nasty. Very nasty. It's still in there, a little. Still burning, slowly. That's why you're not healing."

"Can you—" Antho started.

"Hush." Malachi didn't look at him. He held out one hand toward the door of the hut, toward the little round pool outside. And he *listened*. Lex could see him do it. His whole body went still. His shifting eyes went blue, deep blue, like water.

And water came through the door.

Not poured. *Flowed*. A thin stream of it, rising out of the pool and running through the air, across the grass, through the door, into the hut, like a silver ribbon unspooling. It came to Malachi's outstretched hand and curled around his fingers like a cat. Then he laid his hand on Seraphine's chest, over the worst of the burns, and the water went *into* her.

Seraphine gasped.

Lex saw it happen. Saw the water sink into the burned skin like rain into dry earth. Saw the angry red go pink, and the pink go pale, and the shiny scar tissue soften and smooth. Not all the way. Not perfect. But better. Much better. And something came *out* of her, too, as the water went in. A thin wisp of something gold and black, smoking, writhing, like a splinter drawn out of a wound. It hung in the air above her for a moment, hissing. Then Malachi flicked his fingers, and the little fire in the hearth leaped up and took it, and it was gone.

Seraphine lay on the pallet, breathing. Long, deep, easy breaths. Her face was still scarred, but the grey was gone from it. She opened her eyes and looked up at the little man.

"It doesn't hurt," she whispered. "For the first time since Caldris. It doesn't hurt."

"It'll scar," said Malachi. "I'm sorry. I'm good, but I'm not that good." He patted her hand. "The water can only give back what the body remembers. Your body remembers being burned now. It'll always remember a little." He smiled. "But you'll live. And you'll heal. And you'll be able to read your books without wincing every time you turn a page."

Antho had his hand over his mouth. His shoulders were shaking.

"*Thank you*," he said. It came out cracked to pieces. "*Thank you, thank you*—"

Malachi looked at him. And his smile went away.

"Don't thank me yet, deep one," he said quietly. "I haven't looked at you."

---

He looked at all of them, that night. One by one.

He looked at Wren first. He sat in front of her by the fire and stared at her for a long time with his shifting eyes, until she squirmed and went red and said *what?* And then he laughed.

"Wind," he said. "You've been listening to the wind your whole life. Up on that mountain of yours. You hear it change before a storm. You smell it shift before an avalanche. You know which way the air's going to move before it moves." He tilted his head. "You thought that was just being a good tracker. Didn't you."

Wren stared at him. "It *is* just being a good tracker."

"It's Gale," said Malachi. "One of the Wells. You've been half-listening to it since you were a little girl. Never knew what it was." He grinned. "And the Lattice capped you at twenty-four, did it? Silly thing. The Lattice doesn't know anything about the wind."

Wren had gone very still.

He looked at Seraphine. Not for long. "Clever," he said. "Very clever. Too clever, maybe. You think in words. The Wells don't speak in words." He patted her knee. "You'll be useful anyway. Someone has to write it all down."

He looked at Lex.

He looked for a very long time.

"Your wheel's jammed," he said at last.

"My what?"

"Your wheel. Your Lattice wheel." Malachi leaned on his staff. "Let me tell you what the Lattice is, smith. It's a waterwheel. A great big clever waterwheel that my brother built over the river of the world. The river is the Wells. All six of them. Flame and Stone and Tide and Gale and Radiance and Shade. They've been flowing since before there was anything to flow past. And Aurelian built a wheel over them, and the wheel turns, and it grinds, and it gives everyone a little sack of flour. Classes and levels and skills. Very useful. Very tidy." He shrugged. "And you've all been living your whole lives thinking the flour comes from the wheel."

Nobody said anything.

"It doesn't," said Malachi. "It comes from the river. The wheel just *turns* it." He looked at Lex. "And someone has jammed your wheel. Locked it. With stolen Root. Very nasty. Very clever. You can't turn it anymore." His eyes went gold. "But they didn't dam the river, smith. They *couldn't*. Nobody can. The river doesn't belong to anybody."

"So I can—"

"So you can stop mistaking the wheel for the river," said Malachi. "And learn to *swim*."

He looked at Lex a moment longer. Then something in his face softened.

"You've already started," he said. "You heard the fire at Qel'Varran. You heard the stone in the salt. You heard the water under it. You called out across the white, and *I* heard you." He shook his head slowly. "Nine hundred years, nobody's ever done that. Not without being taught. Not without a Listener sitting beside them for years, teaching them to be quiet." He grinned. "You're very loud. That's going to be the hard part. Teaching you to be quiet."

Lex didn't know what to say.

And then Malachi turned to Antho.

---

The little man's face changed.

The laughter went out of it. The cheerfulness. The grin. He looked at Antho, sitting by the fire with Seraphine's hand in his and Ashka curled at his feet, and his shifting eyes went dark. Grey. The grey of a storm cloud. The grey of a grave.

"Oh, child," he said softly. "Oh, deep one. What have they done to you."

Antho didn't move. His eyes were flickering black at the edges.

At dawn, he sat them down at the edge of the still pool, in the cool grey light, with the goats' bells clanking softly on the slopes above.

"Sit," he said. "Close your eyes. Be quiet."

They sat. They closed their eyes.

"Quieter," said Malachi.

Lex sat on the damp grass by the pool with his eyes shut and tried to be quiet. It was harder than it sounded. His mind wouldn't stop. It ran in circles, the way a dog runs in circles before it lies down. *Father in the crystal. Elric on the stair. Garrick at the gate. Brannoc. Ember. The locked door.* Round and round and round.

"Quieter," said Malachi. "You're shouting, smith. I can hear you from here."

He tried.

An hour passed. Two. The sun came up over the rim of the valley and warmed his face. His legs went numb. His back ached. His mind kept running.

*Ember.*

He thought of the voice. The dry calm courteous voice that had lived in the back of his head for three months. That had called him *Smith* and *Lex* and *little smith*. That had sung his mother's lullaby off-key in a Founders' waystation in the ice.

That was gone. Shut away. Behind the iron door.

*I miss you,* he thought. Not to anyone. Just thought it.

And in the silence after the thought, for the first time all morning, his mind stopped running.

It just stopped. Like a dog finally lying down. Like a hammer finally put down at the end of a long day. Everything went still.

And in the stillness, he heard it.

Not a voice. Not words. A sound. Very faint. Very far away. Like a fire burning in a hearth on the other side of a wall. Warm. Patient. Crackling softly.

*I am Flame*, it seemed to say. *I'm still here. I was always here.*

*Who are you?*

Lex opened his eyes.

The little fire in Malachi's hut, a hundred paces away up the slope, was leaning out through the open doorway. Leaning so far that its flames were licking out over the threshold onto the grass. Toward him.

Beside him on the bank, Malachi was looking at him. His shifting eyes had gone gold.

"*There*," said the little man softly. "There you are."
