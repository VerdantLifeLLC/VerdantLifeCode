# Prologue: The Seal

The storm came down off the Greyspine in the second hour after midnight, and Arion Vey knew that he had run out of time.

He stood at the window of the cottage with two fingers resting on the sill, tapping, tapping, a rhythm older than the house. Beyond the glass the Veil had gone the color of pewter. Lightning walked the ridgelines on long white legs. Somewhere down the valley a dog was barking at nothing, the way dogs did when the air was wrong, and the thatch above him hissed with rain.

"They're asleep," Seren said behind him. "Both of them. Finally."

He didn't turn around. If he turned around he would see her face, and if he saw her face he would not do this, and if he did not do this, they would all die. So he watched the lightning and tapped the sill, and said, "Light the lantern, love."

He heard the scrape of the striker, the soft cough of the wick. Gold light found the window and laid his own reflection over the storm: a tired man of middle years, silver at the temples, with a face that people forgot as soon as they looked away from it. He had worked hard at that face. Faces that were forgotten lived longer.

"Arion." Her voice was very steady. Seren's voice was always steadiest when she was most afraid. "Tell me again why it has to be tonight."

"Because they found the Vhessa records." He made himself say it plainly. "Because Kell has a list of every Warden who signed out the Founders' codices in the last ten years, and my name is on it, and by tomorrow someone at the Spire will start asking why the Warden-Architect spent three months in the eastern march before half of it turned black." He breathed. "Because the thing that came through the breach has learned my scent. And because if any one of them comes looking for me, they will find the boys. And the boys burn brighter than I do."

"They're children."

"They're mine," he said. "That's the whole problem."

He turned then. He couldn't help it.

Seren Harrow stood by the hearth with the lantern in one hand. She was small and fierce and freckled, her hair tied back with a strip of blue cloth, her sleeves still rolled from the washing. There was flour on her cheek. There was always flour on her cheek; he had stopped telling her years ago, because he liked it there. She was looking at him the way she had looked at him the first time, across a market square in Saltreach, as if she could see straight through the forgettable face to the thing underneath and had decided, against all sense, that she liked it.

"Show me," she said.

---

The boys slept in the loft, under the eaves, in a nest of quilts that smelled of woodsmoke and lavender. Arion climbed the ladder with the lantern, and Seren came after.

Anthoren was nine, all knees and elbows, sprawled on his back with one arm flung over his eyes. Even asleep he looked like he was in the middle of an argument. He had Seren's freckles and Arion's long hands and a way of staring at things until they gave up their secrets: the clock in the mill, the hinge of a door, the night sky. Last week he had asked his father why stars didn't fall, and when Arion had told him, he'd asked a second question, and a third, and by the seventh Arion had found himself sketching the motion of the heavens on the kitchen table in flour while Seren laughed at them both.

Lexander was four. He slept curled into his brother's side like a puppy, one fist wound tight in Antho's nightshirt. His hair was a riot of dark curls. He had a scab on his knee from falling off the woodpile, and a small iron horseshoe nail clutched in his other hand, which he had found in the road three days ago and had refused to put down since. "Mine," he'd said, every time anyone reached for it. "Mine. I'm keeping it."

Arion knelt on the boards between them, and for a long moment he only looked.

Then he set the lantern down, and closed his eyes, and *looked* in the other way.

The world fell away. In its place stood the Lattice.

It was everywhere, always. Most people only ever saw the little window of it that belonged to them, a few silver lines of text when they called for it. *Name. Class. Level.* A small, tidy piece of the great machine, polished for their use. But Arion Vey had been born with the blood that saw the whole thing, and the whole thing was vast. It hung over the world like a net of light, threads beyond counting, every thread humming with mana, every knot a life. It ran through the walls of the cottage and the rain outside and the sleeping valley, out across the mountains and the sea, and the ten thousand threads that made a person were laced into it at a thousand points, so that it was impossible to say where the person ended and the Lattice began.

Most people's threads were silver.

His sons' threads were gold.

Not all of them. Not yet. The boys hadn't been Kindled; the Lattice hadn't fully taken hold of them, and wouldn't until each turned sixteen and laid his hand upon a Stone. But the gold was there, under the skin of the world, the way a vein of ore lay under rock. Antho's burned in a slow, dark, deep color, like honey held up to a candle. Lex's was bright and restless and hot, and it flickered at the edges, as if it were already trying to get out.

*Root*, the gold said, to anyone who knew how to read it. *Here is Root. Here is the blood of the one who made all this.*

Anyone who knew how to read it. The Wardens. The thing in the east.

And the other one. The one Arion did not let himself think about, even here, even now.

"It's so bright," Seren whispered beside him. She didn't have the blood, but she was a Listener, and Listeners could feel what they could not see. Her hand had found his shoulder. "I can feel it from here. Like standing near a forge."

"Yes."

"Will it hurt them?"

He didn't answer at once. He reached into his coat and drew out the things he had prepared: a silver needle, a small pot of ink ground from the ash of a Founders' cedar, a strip of linen. His hands were perfectly steady. His hands were always steady. It was the rest of him that shook.

"The sealing won't hurt," he said. "They'll sleep through it. It will feel like a dream about being cold."

"And after?"

"After, the Lattice will look at them and see ordinary boys. Common blood. Common classes when they're Kindled. Nothing anyone would hunt." He threaded the needle. "Nothing anyone would *use*."

"And if it breaks?"

That was the question. He had asked it of himself every night for a month.

"It won't break," he said. "Not while I'm alive to hold it."

"Arion."

"It's a good seal, love." He dipped the needle. "It's the best work I've ever done."

---

He started with Antho, because Antho was older and the gold in him was deeper, harder to cover. He drew back the boy's nightshirt and laid his palm flat over his heart, and felt it beating there, quick and fierce.

*Seal*, he thought, in the language under language, the glyph-speech of the Founders that the scholars called Wellscript. *Conceal. Let the gold be grey. Let the deep thing sleep.*

The needle moved. It didn't pierce the skin; it touched the threads beneath, the gold ones, and where it touched, Arion's own Authority flowed out of him like blood from a cut. He felt it go, a cold draining, the way warmth leaves a body in snow. The ash-ink hung in the air above the boy's chest, a lattice of fine black lines, and then sank into him and vanished.

Antho stirred. His face creased. He mumbled something.

"Shh," Seren whispered, stroking his hair. "Shh, sweetheart."

Antho's eyes opened a crack. They were unfocused, heavy with sleep. They found his father's face in the lantern light.

"Papa?" he said thickly. "It's cold."

"I know. Go back to sleep."

"Are you doing magic?"

"A little."

Antho considered this with the grave seriousness of the very sleepy. His gaze drifted to his brother, still curled against his side. "Will it hurt him?" he asked.

Arion's hand stopped.

"Only if it breaks," he said quietly. And then, because the boy was still looking at him, because he had always believed in telling his children the truth even when the truth was too large for them: "If it ever breaks, Antho, you look after him. Can you do that? You're his big brother. You look after him."

"'Kay," Antho mumbled. His eyes were already closing. "I always do."

The last line of the glyph sank into him. Arion felt the seal settle like a lid onto a pot, and the deep honey-gold of his eldest son dimmed, and dimmed, and went grey.

He let out a breath he hadn't known he was holding. Then he turned to his younger son.

---

Lex was harder.

Antho's power had been deep and still. You could lay a lid on still water. Lex's was a fire, and the moment Arion's palm touched his small chest the fire *noticed*. It leapt up toward the needle like a cat after string. The threads under the boy's skin went from gold to white-gold to a color that had no name, and the heat of it came up through Arion's hand so sharply that he hissed.

"Arion?"

"He's fighting it." He set his jaw. "He's four years old and he's fighting it."

"Of course he is." Seren made a sound that was almost a laugh and almost a sob. "He's ours."

Arion bent closer. *Seal*, he thought. *Conceal. Let the gold be grey.* The fire threw the glyph back at him. *Please*, he thought, which was not Wellscript, which was not anything. *Please, little one. Let me hide you. Let me keep you safe.*

The fire flickered. Considered.

And then, in the place under language, something in his son *looked at him*.

It wasn't thought, not yet; Lex was too young for thought of that kind. It was something more basic. The sense of a hand being held out in the dark. *Who are you?*

*I'm your father*, Arion thought back. *I'm going away for a while. I don't want to. I'm sorry.*

The fire didn't understand the words. But it seemed to understand the sorrow, because slowly, slowly, it let itself be covered.

He worked for a long time. The needle moved and moved. The Authority poured out of him, far more than he had planned for, and the lantern burned lower, and the storm walked off down the valley grumbling to itself. Once Seren had to hold his wrist to keep it steady. Once he had to stop and press the heels of his hands against his eyes until the dizziness passed. The seal on Antho had been a lid. The seal on Lex had to be a forge-door: iron, banded, locked, with the fire still roaring behind it.

And because it had to be locked, it had to have a keeper.

He had known that for a month too. He had not told Seren.

He reached inside himself, past the Authority, past the threads, to the place where *he* was: the shape of his own mind, the knot of memory and habit and love and stubbornness that made Arion Vey into Arion Vey. And he took a piece of it. Not a large piece. A sliver, the width of a fingernail paring. He took his patience and his courtesy and his terrible jokes about smelting, and his memory of the Founders' codes, and the part of him that had once sat up all night with a feverish infant singing nonsense songs about blacksmiths. He took the part of him that was a father.

He pressed it into the seal like a maker's mark into hot iron.

*Watch him*, he told it. *Wake when the seal wakes. Guide him. Tell him what I couldn't.*

The sliver didn't answer. It wasn't awake yet. It might never be. But it settled into the lock, gold among the grey, and went still.

When Arion finally sat back on his heels, the threads of his younger son were grey as old pewter, and the only sign of what lay underneath was a faint warmth, like a hearthstone the morning after a fire.

Lex had not woken once. He slept on, fist still tight in his brother's nightshirt, the horseshoe nail still clutched in the other hand.

Arion bent and kissed his forehead. Then Antho's.

"There," he said. His voice came out hoarse. "There. Done."

---

At the bottom of the ladder, Seren put the lantern on the table and turned to him, and he saw that she had been crying the whole time, silently, the way she did everything difficult.

"How long?" she asked.

"I don't know." He could at least give her that. "Until the breaches are closed. Until the Order forgets my name. Until it's safe."

"That isn't an answer."

"It's the only one I have."

"And what do I tell them?"

He'd thought about this too. "Tell them I was a merchant," he said. "Tell them I went east for trade and didn't come back. Let them be angry with me. It's easier to be angry at a man who left than to grieve a man who's dead, and it's safer than the truth." He swallowed. "Use your name. Harrow. Not Vey. Never Vey. Raise them as Harrows. Let them grow up ordinary."

"They'll never be ordinary."

"Let them think they are. For as long as they can."

She came to him then and put her flour-smudged hand against his cheek and looked at him for a very long time, and he let her, and did not look away.

"You're a fool, Arion Vey," she said at last. "You're the cleverest fool I ever met."

"I know."

"Come back."

"I'll try."

"*Come back*," she said again, fiercely, and kissed him, and then pushed him toward the door before either of them could change their minds.

---

He walked out into the wet grey morning. The rain had thinned to a fine mist, and the Veil lay thick along the valley floor, silver-white, softening the hedgerows and the stone walls and the roofs of Ashbourne below. Somewhere a cockerel was making a fool of itself. In the village square, the old Lattice Stone stood in its ring of mossy cobbles, faintly glowing, the way all the Stones glowed at dawn: a crack running through its face, the result of some long-forgotten lightning strike.

He didn't look back at the cottage. He had promised himself he wouldn't. He looked once, which he had not promised himself about, and saw the lantern burning in the window, small and gold against the grey.

*A light left on for the lost.*

Then he pulled his hood up, and let his face become forgettable, and walked into the mist.

Behind him, deep in the threads of the world, where no one in the valley could see, a line of silver text wrote itself into the great ledger of the Lattice and was filed away among ten thousand million others:

:::system

          LEXANDER HARROW (4)

Status: CONCEALED

:::

And beneath it, smaller, in a color that was not silver at all but the deep gold of embers banked for the night, something else. Something the Lattice did not file, because the Lattice did not know it was there.

:::root
GUARDIAN CONSTRUCT — DORMANT
Bound to: LEXANDER
Waiting.
:::

Fifteen years would pass before it woke.
