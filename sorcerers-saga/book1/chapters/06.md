# The Teeth of the Mountain

The gap in the palisade was barely wide enough for a child, and Lex was not a child.

He went through it sideways, sucking in his breath, with the logs scraping his chest and back and Wren hissing at him from the other side to *hurry up, Hammer, lean left, no, your other left*. He got stuck once, halfway, and for one awful moment he was sure he'd be found there in the morning by a squad of Wardens, wedged in a fence like a cork in a bottle. Then something gave, possibly his coat, possibly a rib, and he popped out onto the frozen hillside behind Thistledown and sat down hard in the snow.

"Hammer," Wren whispered. She was crouched beside the gap, holding out her hands.

He reached back through the gap for Anvilsong where he'd left it leaning against the logs. Then he stopped, and thought about it, and held out his hand instead.

*Homeward*, he thought.

The hammer slid through the gap on its own, scraping over the snow like a dog wriggling under a gate, and leaped the last few feet into his palm.

Wren stared at it.

"Huh," she said.

"Don't."

"I didn't say anything."

"You were going to."

"I was going to say *huh*," said Wren. "I said it. I'm done." She stood up and brushed the snow off her knees. "Come on. They'll have Fenn on our trail by morning."

"Who's Fenn?"

"The little woman with the squint. She was in the yard with the grey-cloaks. She's a Tracker too." Wren slung her bow over her shoulder and started up the slope, moving fast and low, her boots barely seeming to touch the snow. "I've seen her work. She's good. Not as good as me. But good."

---

They climbed through the night.

There was a moon, a fat lopsided one, three days off full, and it turned the snowfields to silver and the shadows to ink. Wren led. She went up the mountain the way water goes down it, choosing every step without seeming to think about it, finding footholds in rock that looked smooth as glass to Lex, traversing slopes he'd never have dared alone. When the snow was crusted, she walked on top of it. When it was soft, she found the places where the wind had packed it hard. Twice she stopped dead and held up a hand, and they waited in silence while something large moved across the slope above them in the dark, and then went on.

Lex followed, and sweated, and slipped, and tried very hard not to fall off the mountain.

*[She's very good,]* Ember observed, sometime after midnight.

*I noticed.*

*[Her Climbing skill is Expert. That's rare for a Common class. You don't get to Expert in anything without years of practice and a great deal of natural talent.]* A pause. *[She's capped at twenty-four.]*

*I know.*

*[I'm only saying.]*

He knew what Ember was saying. He'd been thinking about it since he'd seen her status in the Last Hearth. *Climbing — Expert 1. Level 19. Cap 24.* Five more levels, and then nothing. A girl who climbed mountains like water flowing uphill, and in a year or two the Lattice would tell her she'd climbed as high as she was allowed.

He thought of Brannoc, staring at his hands. *Nineteen years.*

The heat in his chest stirred.

They stopped to rest in the lee of a big boulder a little before dawn. Wren pulled a strip of dried meat out of her pack and tore it in half and handed him the bigger piece without asking. They sat side by side with their backs against the cold rock, chewing, watching the sky over the eastern peaks begin to pale.

"So," Wren said. "Are you going to tell me?"

"Tell you what?"

"What you are. Why the Wardens are chasing you up a mountain in the middle of the night. Why your eyes go gold." She chewed. "Why you want to go to the Hollowmaw, when every person in Thistledown would cut off their own foot before they went near it."

Lex didn't answer right away. He looked out over the mountains, ridge after ridge going grey and then blue in the growing light.

"My father was a sorcerer," he said at last. "I didn't know that until a week ago. I thought he was a merchant who left us. Turns out he was something else, and when he left, he put a seal on me. On my power. So nobody would find me." He turned Anvilsong over in his lap, looking at the runes. "The seal broke. Now people are finding me."

Wren was quiet for a moment. "And the Hollowmaw?"

"My brother's there."

He felt her go still beside him.

"Your brother," she said slowly.

"His seal broke eight years ago. Badly. He—" Lex stopped. Started again. "Our mother died. Our house burned down. He ran away up here. And now everyone calls him the mad warlock of the Greyspine."

The silence stretched for a long time. The sky over the eastern peaks had gone from grey to pale gold.

"So you're the warlock's brother," Wren said at last.

"Yes."

"And you're going up there to find him."

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Because my father's dying somewhere. And the only way to reach him is if both of us go together." Lex laughed shortly. It didn't sound much like a laugh. "That's what I'm told, anyway."

Wren chewed her dried meat. She didn't say anything for so long that he started to wonder if she was working out how to get her money back and leave him on the mountain.

"My da was killed by a rock-wyrm," she said finally. "Three winters ago. Up on the north face, past the Teeth. He was running a trapline and it came out of a crevasse and took him. I found what was left a week later." She tore off another piece of meat. "Mam was down the mines by then, so it was me and my little brothers on the ridge. Tam and Robbie. They're eleven and nine. I've been running Da's lines ever since."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry. It's the mountain. The mountain kills people." She shrugged. "What I'm saying is, I don't have a da either. Not one that's coming back. If someone told me I could find mine, if I'd just go and knock on a mad warlock's door..." She was quiet for a moment. "I'd probably do it. I'd be scared out of my mind. But I'd do it."

Lex looked at her.

She didn't look back. She was looking at the sunrise, her freckled face lit gold, her jaw set.

"Thank you," he said.

"Don't thank me. You paid me." She stood and brushed off her hands. "Come on, Hammer. The Teeth are an hour up. And Fenn will be on the trail by now."

---

The Teeth were well named.

They were a ridge of black rock running east and west across the top of the world, jagged and broken, each spire of stone sharp as a fang and streaked with ice. Snow lay deep in the gaps between them. And on the eastern side, where the Sawback Pass should have been, there was only a great raw scar where half the mountain had come down: a slope of shattered ice and boulders and snow, forty feet deep, stretching for half a mile.

"Told you," said Wren.

The entrance to the Frostvault was at the base of the westernmost Tooth, where the ridge met the slope. It didn't look like much. A crack in the rock, tall and narrow, black inside. Old ice hung from the lip of it like teeth. But as they came close, Lex felt something change in the air: a kind of pressure, a humming, like standing near a big bell after it's been struck. The silver Veil that hung thin over everything up here was thicker around the crack, drawn toward it, swirling slowly into the black.

And when he stepped through the crack, a panel opened in front of his eyes.

"Delve exits open only on completion or death," Lex read aloud. "That's cheerful."

"It means you can't go back." Wren had stepped in beside him. He could see from the slight unfocus of her eyes that she was reading her own panel. "Once you're in, you're in. Only way out is through."

"You didn't mention that."

"You didn't ask." She glanced sideways at him. "Also, you're level eight. Recommended's twelve. I didn't mention that either."

"I'm aware."

"I'm nineteen. So we average out." She paused. "Sort of."

*[You should form a fellowship,]* said Ember. *[It'll let you share experience, and see each other's health. In a Delve, that matters.]*

"How do I—"

*[Just want it. And ask.]*

Lex looked at Wren. He thought *fellowship*, deliberately, the way he did with True Sight, and felt something shift in the Lattice around them, like a door being held open.

"Do you want to—" he started, and then stopped, because he didn't know the words, and Wren was already looking at him with her eyebrows raised.

"Is that a fellowship invitation?" she said. "Just popped up. From *Lex*, it says." Her eyes narrowed. "It doesn't say your class."

*[I've hidden it,]* said Ember. *[Your class shows as —. People will see your name and your level, and nothing else. It seemed wiser.]*

"Do you want to accept it?" Lex asked.

Wren looked at him for a long moment.

"I haven't been in a fellowship since my da," she said.

She accepted.

"All right, Hammer," Wren said. She unslung her bow and nocked an arrow. "Stay behind me on the first floor. It's mites, mostly. They come in swarms."

"Mites?"

"You'll see."

---

He saw.

The first floor of the Frostvault was a cave, but not like any cave he'd imagined. It was ice. All of it. Walls of blue-white ice, floor of black ice, ceiling hung with icicles thick as tree trunks. Light came from somewhere inside the ice itself, a cold blue glow, as if the whole cave were a lantern made of frozen water. Their breath smoked. Their boots squeaked on the floor. The air was so cold it burned the inside of Lex's nose.

The mites came out of the walls.

They were small, no bigger than a fist, glassy and many-legged, like spiders made of ice. And there were *hundreds* of them. They came pouring out of cracks in the walls with a sound like sleet on a window, a skittering clicking flood, and the silver tag that floated over them read simply:

"Wren," said Lex.

"I know."

"There's two hundred."

"I *know*." She was already shooting. Her arrows went into the flood with sharp little cracks, and every one burst a mite into glittering shards. But there were so many. "Hammer, if you've got anything that does *a lot* of fire all at once, now would be the time."

He did. He thought he did.

He planted his feet, and lifted Anvilsong over his head in both hands, and pulled the heat up out of his chest the way he'd pulled it out of the coals by the campfire, the way he'd driven off the bats. Not a strike this time. A *wave*. He pushed it into the hammer and the hammer drank it and blazed orange, then gold, and then he swung it down and around in a great flat circle at waist height, and let go.

The heat went out from him like the blast from an opened furnace door.

It rolled across the ice in a shimmering ring, and where it touched, the mites didn't so much die as *melt*. The front ranks of them sagged and slumped and dissolved into puddles, and the puddles steamed, and the steam rose in a great white cloud that filled the cave. The ones behind kept coming. He swung again. And again. The floor turned to slush under his boots. Water was pouring down the walls. Somewhere a huge icicle cracked and fell and shattered like a dropped chandelier.

Then it was over, and the cave was full of fog and the sound of dripping, and his mana bar was a third of what it had been.

---

The second floor was worse.

It was a mine. Or had been, once, long before the Lattice took the place and made it a Delve. There were old timber props in the ice, frozen solid, and rusted rails running along the floor, and here and there the shape of a pickaxe or a lantern locked in the walls like a fly in amber. The tunnels branched and twisted and doubled back. Wren knew the way. She'd marked it the last time she came through with small scratches at the junctions, a habit her father had taught her.

And there were dead men in the ice.

Lex saw the first one at a bend in the tunnel, standing upright inside the wall, perfectly preserved: a miner, middle-aged, bearded, in a leather apron and a felt cap, his eyes open, his mouth open, frozen in the middle of a shout. Lex stopped and stared at him.

"Don't look at them," Wren said quietly, without turning round. "Just keep walking."

"Who were they?"

"The miners who worked here. Before the Delve. A hundred years ago, maybe more. The mana knot opened up underneath them, and the Lattice made the mine into a Delve, and they were still in it." She kept walking. "Da said the Lattice doesn't care what's in the way when it makes a Delve. It just makes it."

The second miner was further on. The third was a little further.

The fourth one turned its head to watch them pass.

*[Ice is brittle,]* said Ember. *[Heat it and it softens. Heat it fast and it cracks. Hit it hard while it's cracking and it shatters. You know this. You've watched it happen in your slack tub a thousand times.]*

Wren stopped. She'd seen it too. She stood very still in the cold blue tunnel, looking at something only she could see, and Lex watched her face change. It wasn't joy, exactly. It was something more complicated.

"Twenty," she said quietly. "Took me a year to get from eighteen to nineteen." She looked at him. "Four left."

He knew what she meant. Four levels left before the Ceiling.

He didn't say anything. There wasn't anything to say.

---

He found the hidden room by accident.

They were halfway across the second floor, picking their way along a narrow ledge above a frozen shaft, when his Forge Sense *rang*. Not loud. A faint, hollow note, like knocking on a wall that turns out to have a cupboard behind it. He stopped. He put his hand on the ice beside him.

"What?" said Wren.

"There's something behind this."

"There's ice behind that. And then more ice. And then a mountain."

"No. It's hollow." He pressed his palm flat. He could feel it, or hear it, or both: a space behind the ice, a shape, a *made* shape. Square corners. Straight edges. Something built. "There's a room."

*[He's right,]* said Ember, sounding interested. *[I can feel it too. It's warded. Very old wards, very faded.]* A pause. *[Founders' work, I think.]*

He used Heat Shaping. Slowly, carefully, he pushed the heat into the ice, and the ice went soft and slumped and ran down in streams of meltwater, and behind it, there was a door.

It was stone. Grey, smooth, perfectly square, set flush into the living rock of the mountain. There was no handle. In the center of it was a carving: a hand, open, palm up, and above the palm, a single flame.

Lex stared at it. He knew that shape. He didn't know how he knew it. But his own hand came up, on its own, and he found himself looking at Anvilsong, at the fourth rune on the hammer's head, the one that neither he nor Ember could read. The one that had risen up out of him at the end of the forging.

An open hand, held out in the dark.

"That's the same mark," Wren said, looking over his shoulder. "That's the mark on your hammer."

He put his hand on the door.

---

The room behind it was small, perhaps ten feet square, and completely dry. There was no ice in it at all. The walls were smooth grey stone, and they were covered from floor to ceiling in writing.

Not ordinary writing. Wellscript. The language under language, the glyph-speech of the Founders. Lines and lines of it, carved into the stone with a precision that made Lex's smith's eye ache with envy. And he could read it. Not all of it. Not even most of it. But words jumped out at him from the walls like faces in a crowd, half-recognized.

*Root.* *Anchor.* *Hollow.* *Seal.* *Blood.*

And a name, repeated over and over, carved larger than the rest. *Vey.*

*[It's a waystation,]* Ember said softly. *[The Founders built them all over the mountains, nine hundred years ago, when they were weaving the Lattice. Places to rest. Places to record.]* A pause. *[I can read more of it than you can. Do you want me to tell you what it says?]*

*Yes.*

Ember was silent for a long moment.

*[Mostly it's technical,]* it said at last. *[Measurements of mana flow. Notes on the knot beneath the mountain. But there's a passage here, near the door, that's different. It's a sort of... dedication.]* A pause. *[It says: "Let those of the blood who come after find rest here. The Lattice is a lantern we have hung over the dark. Keep it lit. And if the dark comes calling, remember: a lantern is lit for the ones behind."]*

Lex went cold all over.

*A lantern's lit for the ones behind.*

"That's my mother's song," he said aloud.

Wren looked at him. "What?"

"The last line. That's from a song my mother used to sing to us. When we were small. *Little light, little light, burning low...*" He stopped. His throat had closed up. "It's a lullaby. Everyone in the Vale knows it. It's about the Lady of Lanterns. It's—it's just a song."

*[Little light, little light, burning low,]* said Ember, very quietly, in his head. *[Keep the door for the ones who go.]*

Lex went absolutely still.

*[If the dark comes calling, don't you mind,]* Ember went on, and it wasn't speaking anymore, not exactly. It was almost humming. Almost singing. A low, tuneless, slightly off-key murmur, like a man singing to himself while he worked. *[A lantern's lit for the ones behind.]*

It stopped.

The third floor was a single cavern, vast and round and dark, and in the middle of it, the guardian was waiting.

They felt it before they saw it. The cold. Not the ordinary cold of the Delve, which was bad enough, but a deeper, heavier cold that pressed down on them like a weight as they came out of the tunnel onto the cavern floor. Blue-white, glassy, glowing faintly from within, with a heart of darker blue pulsing slowly in the center of its chest like a lantern behind frosted glass. Frost poured off it in waves. It took everything they had.

She nodded.

He went in one last time. He didn't swing. He ran straight at the Rimewarden, under its swinging fist, and slapped his bare palm flat against the cracked ice of its chest, right over the heart. And he pushed. Every last drop of heat he had, every last scrap of mana, all at once.

The ice around the heart *exploded*.

It burst outward in a blast of steam and shards, and Lex was thrown backward across the cavern for the third time, and as he flew, he saw the heart, the dark blue pulsing heart, suddenly naked in the middle of a smoking crater in the Rimewarden's chest.

The Rimewarden stopped.

Lex lay on his back on the cold black ice and looked up at the ceiling of the cavern and did not move.

They climbed out of the Frostvault at dusk.

The exit was on the far side of the Teeth, a crack in the rock just like the entrance, and it let them out onto a high windswept shoulder of the mountain with the whole of the eastern Greyspine spread out before them. Snow, and black rock, and cloud, and the sun going down behind them in a sky the color of a forge.

And there, a mile away across a deep valley, in the face of the highest mountain Lex had ever seen, a cleft.

It was huge. A great black split in the mountainside, a hundred feet tall, as if some giant had driven an axe into the rock and wrenched it out again. Ice hung from its lip. Snow lay heaped around its base. And deep inside it, so deep it was hard to be sure it was there at all, a light was flickering. Not firelight. Something green and cold, and something else, darker than dark, that seemed to pulse in time with the green, like a heartbeat.

As they watched, a sound came echoing across the valley from it. A long, low, wavering note. Not a howl. Something stranger. Almost like music. Almost like a flute.

"The Hollowmaw," said Wren quietly.

Lex looked at it for a long time.

"That's as far as I go," Wren said. "That was the deal."

Across the valley, the strange flute-like note came again, rising and falling, and broke off.

Lex shifted Anvilsong on his shoulder and pulled the Frostwalker's Mantle tighter around him. It was warm. He hadn't expected it to be so warm.

"Probably," he said. And he started down the slope toward the Hollowmaw.

He didn't look back. He'd promised himself he wouldn't.

So he didn't see Wren Ashdown stand there on the mountainside for a long time after he'd gone, watching him go, her jaw set and her hands clenched into fists at her sides. And he didn't see her, after a while, swear softly under her breath, and unsling her bow, and start down the slope after him.
