# What Was Left on the Anvil

By the time the sky over the Greyspine had begun to pale, half of Ashbourne was crowded into Brannoc's forge, and the other half was standing outside it in the mist, trying to see in.

Lex sat on the upturned bucket Pell usually claimed, with a blanket around his shoulders that he didn't need and a mug of Mother Orla's spiced cider in his hands that he hadn't touched. He was warm. He was so warm. The heat in his chest hadn't gone back to sleep after the fight; it had settled down to a steady glow, like coals banked under ash, and he could feel it in his fingertips and the soles of his feet and the backs of his eyes. When people came close to him, they flinched a little, as if they'd stepped too near a stove.

People kept coming close anyway.

"He saved my Pell," Mother Orla was telling anyone who would listen, and several who wouldn't. She'd been crying on and off for an hour and showed no sign of stopping. "I saw it. I saw it from the window. That thing had its mouth open like a barn door and Lex just *walloped* it, walloped it right into my wall, and there's a stain there now, I don't know how I'm going to get it out—"

"I saw him *glowing*," said one of the Dunstan twins.

"He blew one up," said the other. "It went *pfoom*."

"It went *pfoom*," the first agreed, with deep satisfaction.

"Lex," said Pell, for perhaps the fortieth time. The boy had attached himself to Lex's side like a burr to a sheep, and his eyes were enormous. "Lex. Lex, are you a sorcerer? Are you a real one? Can you do it again? Can you show me? Lex—"

"Pell," said Brannoc, from the anvil, "let the lad breathe."

Not everyone was pleased.

Reeve Dunstan stood near the door with his arms folded tight across his narrow chest, and he was looking at Lex the way he might have looked at a dog that had started talking. Behind him, a knot of older villagers muttered among themselves. Lex caught words. *Wildcaster.* *Unlatticed.* *His brother.* That last one more than once. *Same blood. Same as his brother. You remember what happened then.*

Old Mag the herbwoman, ninety and half-blind, sat on the bench by the forge with her stick across her knees and said nothing at all. But whenever Lex looked at her, he found her one good eye already on him, bright and thoughtful and not at all surprised.

And Elric Thorne sat at the end of the workbench with his long legs stretched out and his eyes closed, as if he were asleep, while Quill the raven perched on his shoulder and kept watch over the room with the satisfied air of a bird who'd been right about something.

*[You're very popular,]* said the voice in Lex's head.

Lex didn't answer. He'd discovered, in the last hour, that if he spoke to the voice aloud, people stared. He'd also discovered that if he simply *thought* at it, very deliberately, it heard him.

*Are you going to stay?* he thought.

*[In your head? I'm afraid so. I'm bound to the seal, and the seal is bound to you. Where you go, I go.]* A pause. *[I'll try to be a considerate tenant.]*

*What are you?*

*[That's a much harder question than it sounds.]* The voice was quiet for a moment, as if thinking. *[I can tell you what I know. I'm a guardian construct: a working, placed inside your seal, meant to wake when the seal broke and guide you through what came after. I have knowledge: of the Lattice, of its workings, of things like Root and Authority. I have a purpose. Beyond that...]* Another pause. *[Beyond that, my memory is divided into partitions, and most of them are locked. I don't know who made me. I don't know when. I know how to talk, and I know a great deal about metallurgy, which is odd, and I appear to have opinions about the correct way to brew tea. That's about the extent of it.]*

*That's not very much.*

*[No. I was hoping you'd be less observant.]*

Despite everything, Lex felt the corner of his mouth twitch.

*Do you have a name?*

*[Guardian Construct, technically.]*

*I'm not calling you Guardian Construct.*

*[I'd rather hoped you wouldn't.]*

Lex looked into the forge, where the fire had settled at last to a low, steady orange glow. He thought of the gold text breaking through the silver, the color of coals banked for the night.

*Ember,* he thought. *I'm going to call you Ember.*

There was a long silence in his head.

*[Ember,]* the voice repeated at last, slowly, as if trying the word for size. And then, in a tone Lex couldn't quite read, something that might almost have been emotion, if a working could feel such a thing: *[Yes. Yes, I think that will do very well. Thank you, Lex.]*

It was the first time it had used his name.

---

It took another hour for Brannoc to clear the forge.

He did it the way Brannoc did everything: patiently, kindly and with total immovability. He thanked Mother Orla for the cider and pressed her hand. He told the Dunstan twins that if they wanted to see anything else go *pfoom* they could come back after they'd done their chores, and then he'd see what he could arrange. He told Pell, gently, that Lex needed sleep, and that a good friend would go home and let him have it. And when Reeve Dunstan opened his mouth to say something about the Order and the law and what happened to people who harbored wildcasters, Brannoc simply stood up from the anvil, all six and a half feet of him, and looked down at the Reeve without saying anything at all, until the Reeve closed his mouth again and went away.

Old Mag was the last to leave. She hobbled to the door on her stick, and paused in it, and turned her head to look back at Lex with her one good eye.

"Your mother knew," she said.

Lex went still.

"Not about the fire. Not about your brother. She didn't see that coming, poor thing, or she'd never have let him near that Stone." Old Mag's voice was thin and dry as old paper. "But she knew what you were. Both of you. She came to me once, the year after your father left, and asked me for herbs to help a child sleep through a fever that wasn't a fever. Said you'd been running hot." She sniffed. "I told her there wasn't a herb in the Vale for what ailed you. She said she knew. She said she just needed something to *do*."

"Mag," Lex said, "did she ever tell you what my father was?"

The old woman considered him for a long moment.

"She told me he was a good man who made a hard choice," she said. "And that she'd have made the same one." She turned and hobbled out into the grey morning. Over her shoulder, without looking back, she added, "She also said you'd be a menace once you woke up. She was right about that too."

The door swung shut behind her.

---

Then it was just the three of them, and Slag the cat, and Quill the raven, and the voice in Lex's head.

Brannoc put a kettle on the coals, because Brannoc's response to every crisis was to put a kettle on, and came and sat down heavily on the bench across from Lex. Elric opened his eyes. Nobody said anything for a while.

"Right," said Brannoc at last. "I'd like to know what I've been raising for eight years. If it's not too much trouble."

Elric smiled faintly. "That depends how much you know about the Lattice, Master Holt."

"I know it's there. I know it gives you a class. I know it tells you when you've gone up a level and when you've hit your cap." Brannoc's beard twitched. "I hit mine nineteen years ago. I know a lot about that part."

"Most people know about that much. It's all the Wardens want most people to know." Elric shifted on the bench, wincing; he'd strained something in the fight, Lex thought, though he hadn't complained. "Let me tell you a little more. Stop me if it sounds like heresy. It probably is."

He held up one knotted hand.

"Nine hundred years ago, give or take, the world almost ended. You'll have heard stories of the Sundering, the war of the sorcerer-kings, when magic was wild and anyone with enough of it could do anything they liked. Most of the stories are wrong about the details, but they're right about the scale. Mountains were moved. Seas were boiled. Whole kingdoms burned in a night. And at the end of it, a handful of the greatest mages left alive got together and decided it could never happen again." He turned his hand palm-up. "So they built the Lattice."

"Built it," said Brannoc. "I was always told the Lady made it. That it was her gift."

"The Wardens encourage that belief. It's tidier." Elric shrugged. "But no. People made it. Very great people, and very frightened ones. They wove a net of power over the whole world, a working so vast it's still running nine hundred years later, and every person born under it is caught in it. It *rations* magic. Gives every man and woman a shape, a class, a set of skills, a ladder of levels, and lets them climb as far as the ladder goes and no farther. No more sorcerer-kings. No more wild power. Everything ordered. Everything in its place."

"The Ceiling," said Brannoc quietly.

"The Ceiling." Elric nodded. "Though I'll tell you a secret, Master Holt, and you can decide for yourself what you think of it. The Founders didn't build a Ceiling. Not like the one you know. Their Lattice let anyone climb as high as their talent would take them. Slowly, carefully, with trials and tests at every tier, but *anyone*." His mouth thinned. "The Ceiling came later. The Wardens added it. Three hundred years ago, give or take. Common classes stop at twenty-four, and to climb past that you need a Warden's Sanction, and a Warden's Sanction costs a great deal of money and a great deal of the right kind of birth." He looked at the big smith steadily. "You were never meant to stop at twenty-four, Brannoc. Nobody was. You were *stopped*."

The kettle began to sing on the coals. Nobody moved to take it off.

Brannoc's face had gone very still. He was staring at his own hands, the great scarred hands that had been working iron for forty years.

"Nineteen years," he said at last, very quietly.

"I'm sorry," said Elric. And he sounded it.

Lex looked at Brannoc's bowed head and felt something in his chest, the heat, the banked fire, flare suddenly hot. He didn't know what it was. Anger, maybe. A great, slow, smoldering anger that had nowhere to go yet.

He made himself breathe. The heat settled.

"And the Wardens," he said. "They're the ones who keep the Lattice?"

"Keep it. Maintain it. Police it. Profit from it." Elric took the kettle off the coals at last, and poured three mugs of something that smelled like bark and burnt honey. "They have a great tower in Highcrest called the Spire, and a great Stone at the top of it, and from that Stone, the senior Wardens can see a little further into the Lattice than the rest of us. Not far. But far enough to set Sanctions, and to grant and revoke certain permissions, and to see when something happens that shouldn't." He handed Lex a mug. "Like a flare of gold light from a village Stone in the middle of the night."

"You said gold was Root," Lex said. "What does that mean?"

"Ah." Elric sat back. "That's the interesting part."

*[It certainly is,]* said Ember.

"The Lattice has layers," said Elric. "Most people only ever see the top one. The silver. Their own status, their own skills. The Wardens see a little deeper, into the second layer, where the permissions are kept. But under that, at the very bottom, there's a third layer. The foundation. The Founders called it the Root. It's where the Lattice's rules are actually written, and the only people who can touch it are people of the Founders' blood." He looked at Lex over the rim of his mug. "One Founder in particular. The greatest of them. The one who designed the whole thing. And his line has been very, very thin for a very long time."

"My father," Lex said.

"Your father was born with Root in his blood. So were you. So was your brother." Elric set down his mug. "That's what a seal is for, Lex. Not to take your power away. You can't take Root out of someone; it's in the bones. A seal *hides* it. Paints it over. Makes the Lattice see a Smith where there's a—" He paused. "What *did* it give you, in the end? I saw the fire. I didn't see the class."

Lex hesitated. Then he called up his status.

He read it out loud, slowly. Brannoc's eyebrows climbed with every line. When he got to the class name, Elric made a small sound.

"Emberwright," the old man repeated. "Unique."

"Is that bad?"

"It's *unheard of*." Elric leaned forward, his storm-grey eyes very bright. "Unique classes appear perhaps once in a generation. There hasn't been one recorded in the Spire's archives in sixty years. And I've never heard of that one at all." He let out a breath. "No cap. Of course. Of course he'd hide a Unique under a Common. It's exactly the sort of joke Arion would make."

*[It's a good class,]* said Ember, sounding faintly defensive, as if Lex's class were something it had made itself and was being criticized. *[It's a very good class. It suits you.]*

"What are free points?" Brannoc asked.

"Every time you gain a level," Elric said, "the Lattice gives you a few points to put where you like. Common classes get three. Rare get four. Unique classes..."

"Six," said Lex, reading.

Elric closed his eyes briefly. "Of course they do."

*[You should spend them,]* said Ember. *[You've been fighting at the strength of a level-nine smith with a level-four sorcerer's mana pool. It's lopsided. Every time you swing that hammer, your body is working harder than it was built for.]*

*What do you suggest?*

*[Vitality, first. You were badly hurt last night, and the gloam-cold went deeper than you think. More health will help you heal. Then Agility, because you are, if you'll forgive me, rather slow. Then Spirit, because Spirit is your weapon.]* A pause. *[But it's your body. You know it better than I do.]*

Lex thought about it. He thought about the gloamhound ducking under his swing. He thought about the cold in his bones.

He put five points into Vitality, five into Agility, four into Spirit, two into Might and two into Resolve, and felt each one go into him like a swallow of something hot: a stretch and a settling in his muscles, a sharpening in his eyes, a deepening of the fire in his chest.

He flexed his hands. They felt the same and not the same, like a familiar tool newly sharpened.

"Better?" Elric asked.

"Different."

"It will feel strange for a few days. The body takes time to grow into its numbers." Elric's gaze sharpened. "Now. You said Root. Prerogatives. True Sight?"

*[A Root power,]* Ember explained, as Lex hesitated. *[The first and simplest. It lets you see what the Lattice sees, about anyone. Their true status, including the parts they'd rather hide. It costs Authority, which you don't have much of, and which comes back slowly. One point to use it.]* A pause. *[I'd recommend trying it. Carefully.]*

Lex looked at Elric.

He didn't mean to. Not really. He just looked, the way you'd look at anyone across a table, and he thought *True Sight* in the deliberate way he'd learned to talk to Ember, and he felt something behind his eyes go *hot*.

The world flickered gold.

And floating in the air over Elric Thorne's grey head, there was suddenly a panel. Gold, like his own. Lines of text unrolling.

*Denied three times*, Lex thought. He didn't know why that one line caught at him. It was the only one in the panel that didn't seem to be about power. It seemed to be about something else.

"Don't," said Elric sharply.

The gold flickered and went out. Lex blinked. The old sorcerer was looking at him with an expression that was half amusement and half something much less comfortable.

"Your eyes," Elric said. "They go gold when you do that. Like coins in a fire. Anyone who knows what to look for will know exactly what you're doing." He picked up his mug again, a little too deliberately. "And it's rude, Lex. Reading a man's status without asking. It's like reading his letters."

"Sorry," Lex said. And then, because he couldn't help himself: "Who denied you Tier Four?"

Elric's hand stopped halfway to his mouth.

"The Spire did," he said after a moment. "Twice." He drank. "And once, someone else."

He didn't say who. And Lex, looking at the old man's lined face in the firelight, didn't ask.

---

They talked until the light through the door had gone from grey to pale gold.

Elric told them what he could. That the black mist was called the Gloam, and that it had first appeared sixteen years ago in the eastern march of Vhessa, and that it came from *somewhere else*, from cracks in the Lattice itself, the way water comes up through cracks in a cellar floor. That it had been spreading ever since, slowly, quietly, the Wardens hushing up every report. That whole villages in the east had gone silent. That the Order called it a *blight*, a natural thing, a disease of the land, and burned whatever it touched, and arrested anyone who said otherwise.

That Arion Vey had been among the first Wardens sent to investigate it. And that he'd come back from the east with a face like a man who'd seen his own grave, and had refused to say what he'd found, and had vanished not long after.

"He knew something," Elric said. "Something about the Gloam, and the Lattice, and why it was breaking. He wouldn't tell me what. He said it was safer if I didn't know." His mouth twisted. "He always did think he knew best."

"And now he's dying," Lex said.

"Now his seal is failing. Which means he's in trouble. Dying, maybe. Or trapped, or hurt, or giving everything he has to something else." Elric looked at him steadily. "I don't know which. I want to find out. And I can't do it without you."

"Why not?"

"Because I've been looking for eleven years and I've found nothing. Because wherever he's gone, he's gone there by Root, and Root paths can only be walked by Root blood." Elric paused. "And because, if your seal fails completely, if he dies and the last of it gives way, it won't crack, Lex. It'll *shatter*. Everything that's been held back for fifteen years will come through at once. And without someone to guide you through it, it might do to you what it did to your brother."

The forge went very quiet.

"What do you mean," Lex said slowly, "what it did to my brother?"

Elric looked at him for a long moment. Then he looked at Brannoc, and something passed between them that Lex couldn't read.

"Not tonight," Elric said quietly. "You've had enough for one night. On the road. I'll tell you everything I know about Anthoren on the road."

"*Tell me now.*"

The fire in the hearth jumped.

"Lex," said Brannoc gently.

Lex looked at him. At the old smith's tired, kind, soot-streaked face. And he made himself breathe, and the fire settled.

"On the road," he said. "Fine." He stood up. His legs had stopped shaking. The heat in his chest had settled to a steady, comfortable burn, like a well-fed hearth. "So I'm leaving, then."

"You have to," said Elric. "The Wardens will have seen that flare. The nearest garrison is at Millbridge, two days' ride south. They'll send someone. Probably someone unpleasant." He rose too, leaning heavily on his staff. "We should be gone by noon."

"Noon," Lex repeated. He looked around the forge. The anvil. The racks of tools. The slack tub and the bellows and the brick ledge where Slag was curled asleep. Eight years of his life. "Fine," he said again.

And then, because there was something he needed to do first, and he'd only just realized what it was: "But not without a hammer."

---

He felt it unseal the moment he set his hand on the anvil.

It wasn't dramatic. There was no burst of gold light, no shattering of silver text. Just a small, warm *click* somewhere behind his eyes, like a key turning in a well-oiled lock, and a line of gold writing at the edge of his vision.

And suddenly he could see them.

The runes. They were everywhere. In the grain of the iron on the rack. In the flicker of the coals. In the air itself, when the heat-shimmer rose off the hearth: faint shapes, half-formed, the way you sometimes see faces in the bark of a tree. Shapes he'd seen a thousand times and never noticed. Shapes he almost understood.

*[Wellscript,]* said Ember softly. *[The language the Lattice is written in. The language under language. Every made thing has a little of it in it, if it was made well. Most smiths never see it. You've been seeing it for years without knowing what it was. That's how you hear the flaws.]*

*Can I write it?*

*[With a hammer? Yes. That's what an Emberwright does.]* A pause. *[I can show you a few. Simple ones. You'll learn more as you go.]*

Lex went to the rack at the back of the forge, the one where Brannoc kept his best stock, the good Saltreach bar-iron that came up the river once a year and cost more than the village earned in a season. He took the heaviest bar. Then he stopped. Then he reached into the pocket of his trousers and took out the thing he always carried there.

It was a horseshoe nail. Old, black, slightly bent, worn smooth by fifteen years of a boy's fingers. He'd found it in the road outside the old cottage when he was four, the day after his father left, and he'd refused to let it go. He'd had it in his pocket the night of the fire. It was the only thing he still owned from the life before.

*[That nail,]* said Ember, very quietly.

*What about it?*

There was a long silence.

*[I don't know,]* Ember said at last, and it sounded troubled. *[I remember it. I think. Something about it. It's in one of the locked partitions.]* A pause. *[Put it in the hammer, Lex. I think it wants to be there.]*

He didn't ask how a nail could want anything. He laid it on the bar and folded the bar over it, and put the whole thing into the fire.

---

Brannoc struck for him.

That was how it was done, in the old way, for big work: one smith holding the piece and shaping it with a hand hammer, the other swinging the sledge where the first one pointed. The striker and the smith. They hadn't worked like that in years. Not since Lex was fifteen, and his arms had gotten strong enough to swing the sledge himself.

Brannoc didn't say anything. He just picked up the big sledge, the one Lex had used the night before, its head still faintly scorched, and took his place on the other side of the anvil, and waited.

They worked.

Lex pulled the folded bar from the fire when it reached the color of a sunrise. He laid it on the anvil and touched it with his hand hammer, *tink*, and Brannoc's sledge came down where he'd touched, *CHANG*, and the iron spread and lengthened under the blow. *Tink. CHANG. Tink. CHANG.* The old rhythm. Lex could do it in his sleep. His body knew it the way it knew breathing.

But this time, every time his hand hammer touched the iron, he put something into it.

He didn't know how to describe it, even to himself. It was like the Ember Strike, but smaller and more careful. A thread of heat, a thread of *intent*, flowing out of his chest and down his arm and through the hammer into the metal. And as it went in, he *thought* the shape Ember was showing him: a rune like a flame folded over itself, a rune like a closed fist, a rune like a road curving home. *Ardent. Steadfast. Homeward.* The iron took them. He could feel it taking them. Each one sank into the metal like a drop of ink into water and spread through it and settled, and the iron's color changed, very slightly, deepening from orange toward a rich red-gold that didn't belong to any heat he'd ever seen.

Brannoc noticed. Lex saw him notice. The old smith's eyes went wide, and then narrow, and then, very slowly, something in his face that Lex had never seen there before began to shine.

He didn't stop swinging.

They worked through the morning. They drew out the head and punched the eye and drifted it clean. They shaped the faces: one flat and broad for striking, one tapered to a wedge for splitting, the way Brannoc had taught him, the way you'd make a cross-peen hammer for the forge, only bigger, heavier, a hammer for a man to swing with both hands. Lex cut a haft from the seasoned ash in the rafters and fitted it and wedged it, and then he heated the head one last time and laid his palm flat against the iron.

It was hot enough to take the skin off a normal man's hand. It didn't burn him. It hummed.

He put one more rune into it. He didn't know where it came from; Ember hadn't shown it to him. It just rose up from somewhere deep inside him and went down his arm into the metal, a shape like an open hand held out in the dark. And as it sank into the iron, the whole hammer *rang*, a long, clear, singing note that went up into the rafters and set every chain and tool in the forge trembling, and went on, and on, fading slowly, like a bell at the end of a song.

Brannoc set down the sledge.

Lex quenched the hammer in the slack tub. The water didn't scream this time. It sang too, a low hiss that rose into something almost like a voice, and then it was quiet.

He lifted the hammer out. It was dark iron, almost black, with a faint red-gold sheen in its depths, like embers seen through smoked glass. It was heavy and perfectly balanced. When he held it, it felt like part of his arm.

"Anvilsong," Lex said aloud. He didn't know where the name came from either. It was just there, written on the inside of his head, the way the last rune had been.

*[Anvilsong,]* Ember agreed quietly. *[That fourth rune. I can't read it.]* A long pause. *[I don't think it's one of mine.]*

Brannoc was looking at the hammer. He hadn't said a word in four hours. Now he reached out, slowly, as if afraid it might bite him, and laid one huge scarred finger against the iron of the head.

"It's warm," he said.

"It'll stay warm," said Lex. He knew that without knowing how he knew it. "As long as I'm alive."

Brannoc took his hand away. He stood there by the anvil for a long moment with his head down, and Lex saw his shoulders shake, once, and realized with a lurch that the old smith was crying.

"Brannoc—"

"Forty years," Brannoc said hoarsely. "Forty years I've been at this anvil. I've made ten thousand horseshoes. I've made ploughshares and hinges and kettles and nails, so many nails, and I was proud of every one, and I'd have told you I was the best smith in the southern Vale and meant it." He wiped his face with the back of his wrist, smearing soot. "And I never knew it could *sing*."

Lex didn't know what to say. So he set the hammer down on the anvil and went around it and put his arms around the old man, the way he hadn't since he was eleven years old and crying in the wet grass outside a burning house. Brannoc's huge arms closed around him, and held on.

"She asked me to look after you," Brannoc said into his hair, very quietly. "Your mother. Once. Years before. She came by the forge with you on her hip and she said, *Brannoc, if anything ever happens to me, you look after my boys*. I laughed. I said what's going to happen to you, Seren? And she just looked at me." He was quiet for a moment. "I only managed the one of you. I'm sorry."

"You did fine," Lex said. His throat was very tight. "You did fine, Brannoc. You were—" He stopped. Tried again. "My father left. You stayed. That's all a father has to do. You *stayed*."

Brannoc made a sound like a laugh with something broken in the middle of it, and held on harder.

---

They left at noon.

The whole village turned out to watch them go. Lex hadn't expected that. He'd expected to slip away quietly, the way his brother had, alone up the north road with nobody watching. Instead there were people lining the green: Mother Orla with a basket of bread and cheese she pressed into his arms, crying again; the Dunstan twins, solemnly demanding one more *pfoom* and being solemnly refused; Farmer Tobbin, who shook his hand and said gruffly that he'd find someone else to finish the ploughshare. Even Reeve Dunstan was there, standing at the back with his arms folded, and when Lex passed him the Reeve looked away and muttered something that might have been *good luck* and might have been *good riddance*.

Pell walked beside him all the way to the edge of the green.

"I could come," the boy said. "I could carry things. I'm good at carrying things. I'm strong. I carried a whole sack of flour once, all the way from the mill—"

"Pell," said Lex.

"I'd be useful. I would."

"I know you would." Lex stopped, at the edge of the cobbles, where the north road began. He put down the basket and crouched so that he was looking the boy in the eye. "That's why I need you to stay. Somebody has to look after Brannoc. Somebody has to look after the village. If those things come back—"

"I can't fight them. I'm not a sorcerer."

"Neither was I. Until last night." Lex held his gaze. "Nine days, Pell. Your Kindling's in nine days. And whatever the Stone says, whatever it tells you you are, I want you to remember something for me. Can you do that?"

Pell nodded, wide-eyed.

"It's not the end," said Lex. "Whatever it says. It's not the end of what you can be. It's just the beginning of what they'll *let* you be. And that's not the same thing." He stood and ruffled the boy's straw-colored hair. "Remember that."

He picked up the basket and turned to the road.

Brannoc was waiting there, at the very edge of the village, where the cobbles gave way to packed earth. He'd changed out of his apron into a clean shirt, which he did perhaps twice a year. He had a pack in one hand, stuffed full, and a purse in the other, and he held them both out to Lex without a word.

"I can't take your money."

"It's not mine. It's yours. Your wages. Eight years of 'em." Brannoc pushed the purse into his hand. "I've been putting a bit by. For when you'd want your own forge."

Lex looked at the purse. It was heavy. Much heavier than eight years of an apprentice's wages had any right to be.

"Brannoc—"

"Take it, lad. Take it and go find him." Brannoc's voice was rough. "Go find your father. And when you've found him, you look him in the eye, and you tell him from me that his son's the best smith I ever trained, and that if he wants to argue about it, he can come down to Ashbourne and argue with *me*."

Lex laughed. It came out wet. "I will."

"And come back."

"I will. I promise." Lex hefted Anvilsong onto his shoulder. The hammer was warm against his neck. "Hammer-oath."

Brannoc's face did something complicated. Then he put out his huge hand, and Lex clasped it, smith to smith, the way it had been done in the Vale for a thousand years. *What's struck on the anvil holds.*

"Hammer-oath," said Brannoc.

Then he let go, and stepped back, and Lex turned and walked up the north road, out of Ashbourne, with Elric Thorne beside him and the raven on Elric's shoulder and a voice in his head and the warm weight of the hammer across his back.

He didn't look back. He'd promised himself he wouldn't.

He looked once. He hadn't promised himself about that. Brannoc was still standing at the edge of the cobbles, enormous and alone, with one hand raised. Behind him the village lay small and grey in its valley, the mist thinning in the noon light, the cracked old Stone glinting on the green.

In the window of the forge, someone had lit a lantern. In the middle of the day.

*A light left on for the lost.*

Lex turned his face to the mountains and walked on.

---

They'd gone perhaps a mile, climbing steadily, when Quill gave a sharp croak and launched himself off Elric's shoulder, circling up and back toward the south.

Elric stopped. He turned. Lex turned with him.

From up here, on the first rise of the foothills, you could see the whole of the Vale laid out below them: the patchwork fields, the silver thread of the river, the cluster of grey roofs that was Ashbourne. And beyond it, the south road, winding away toward Millbridge through the green hills.

There were riders on the road.

A dozen of them, maybe more. They were too far away to make out faces. But Lex could see the grey of their cloaks, all alike, and the glint of steel at their hips, and the banner the lead rider carried, snapping in the wind: a silver net on a field of grey. A lattice.

They were riding hard. Toward Ashbourne.

"That was fast," said Elric quietly. "That was very fast. Millbridge is two days' ride." He narrowed his eyes. "Unless they weren't coming from Millbridge. Unless they were already on their way."

"Already on their way? Why would they—"

"I don't know." Elric turned back to the mountains. His face was grim. "But I think we should walk faster."

*[I concur,]* said Ember.

Lex looked down at the village one last time, at the riders closing on it, at the tiny glint of a lantern in the forge window. The heat in his chest flared, hard and bright.

Then he shifted Anvilsong on his shoulder, and turned his back on everything he'd ever known, and walked faster.
