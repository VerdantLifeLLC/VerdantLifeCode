# Oathkeeper

They smelled the village before they saw it.

It was the third day out from the Oracle's gorge. The moor had begun to give way to farmland, poor thin farmland, stone-walled fields of oats and barley gone ragged and untended, a few sheep wandering loose with nobody to mind them. The Road ran on through it, straight and heaved and mossy. And on the morning wind, coming down off a low ridge to the east, there was smoke.

Not woodsmoke. Lex knew woodsmoke; he'd lived his whole life in it. This was thicker and greasier and sourer, and under it, faint but unmistakable, was the smell he'd learned to dread. Wet ash. Rot. Nothing.

"Gloam," said Antho quietly. He'd stopped in the middle of the Road, his face gone pale. Ashka, on his shoulder, was hissing softly, sparks crawling along her spines. "And fire. A lot of fire."

Wren was already moving. She went up the side of the ridge at a crouching run, quick and silent as a fox, and flattened herself among the heather at the top. Lex saw her go very still. Then she turned her head and waved them up, sharply, urgently.

They went up after her and lay down beside her in the heather, and looked.

There had been a village in the valley below. There wasn't anymore.

It had been a small place. Twenty houses, perhaps, around a green with a well in the middle and a little stone chapel at one end, like a hundred other villages in Caldor. Now most of the houses were burning. Not burning brightly, the way a house burns when it's set alight. Burning *dully*, sullenly, with a thick black smoke that rolled low across the ground instead of rising. And the smoke was mixed with something else. Darker. Oilier. Moving in a way smoke shouldn't move.

The Gloam lay over the whole valley like a stain.

It wasn't the thin threads of the Hollowmaw, or the creeping tide that had come down the north road at Ashbourne. It was thick, and it was everywhere. It filled the low places between the houses. It had soaked into the fields, turning the oats grey. It hung in the air like fog. And moving through it, between the burning houses, were shapes.

Gloamhounds. A dozen of them, maybe more. Loping silently through the smoke, their white eyes glinting.

And something else. Something bigger. Lex couldn't see it clearly through the smoke; only a vast dark hump of a shape, moving slowly across the far side of the green, and the sound of it: a heavy, dragging, grinding sound, like a sledge full of stones being pulled over cobbles.

"There," said Wren, very quietly, pointing.

At the far end of the village, the little stone chapel was still standing. Its door was shut. Its windows were dark. And in front of its door, on the steps, a single figure was standing with a sword in his hand.

He was a big man, broad and heavy-shouldered, in battered armor that might once have been fine and was now dented and scratched and patched with leather. His head was bare. His hair was grey. And he was fighting.

As they watched, three gloamhounds came at him at once out of the smoke. He met them on the chapel steps. His sword moved in a short, brutal, economical arc, and the first hound came apart in rags of black smoke. He took the second on his armored forearm and threw it back down the steps. The third got its teeth into his leg, and he grunted and brought the pommel of his sword down on its head, and it burst, and he staggered.

He didn't fall. He planted his feet again on the chapel steps. Behind him, the door stayed shut.

"There are people in there," said Antho. "In the chapel. I can feel them. Lots of them." He closed his eyes. "Thirty. Forty. Frightened. Children."

"He's holding the door," said Wren.

Lex watched the grey-haired man on the steps. Watched the next wave of hounds come at him out of the smoke. Watched him meet them, and stagger, and hold.

His sword, Lex noticed, was broken. It had snapped about a third of the way down the blade, leaving only a jagged stump of steel. He was fighting with half a sword.

"Right," said Lex, and stood up.

---

He didn't think about it. Afterward, he realized he hadn't thought about it at all. He simply stood up out of the heather and ran down the ridge into the smoke with Anvilsong in his hands, and somewhere behind him he heard Wren swear and Antho call his name, and then they were running too.

The first gloamhound saw him coming and turned. Lex didn't slow down. He hit it with an Ember Strike at a dead run and it exploded into gold sparks, and he ran through the sparks without stopping. The second came at him from the side. He threw a Slag Shot at it without breaking stride, a bolt from his belt heated cherry-red and flung with the breath of the forge, and it struck the hound in the chest and the hound burned.

He'd spent his points that morning. Finally. Ember had been nagging him for three days. Twenty-four points, from four levels he'd forgotten about, and he'd put them where it felt right without thinking too hard, and he felt them now as he ran. His legs were springs. His lungs were bellows. The hammer in his hands weighed nothing at all.

Lex held it out across the anvil, hilt first.

Garrick Dunmore stood on the other side of the anvil with the dead smith's sledge still in his hands, and looked at the sword, and didn't move.

"It's yours," said Lex. "It was always yours. I just made it remember."

Slowly, Garrick set down the sledge. Slowly, he reached out and took the hilt. His scarred hand closed around the old worn leather, and Lex saw him feel it: the balance, the weight, the cold that came off the blade. Saw him feel the runes, maybe, the way Lex felt Anvilsong's runes humming in his own grip.

The old knight turned the sword over in the light. He looked at the blue-white sheen of the blade, and the frost forming and melting along its edge. He looked at the silver pommel, his father's pommel, his grandfather's.

Then he did something Lex didn't expect.

He went down on one knee in the ashes of the burned smithy, with the sword held point-down before him, both hands on the cross-guard, and bowed his grey head.

"Garrick—" Lex started.

"Let me," said Garrick quietly. "Let me do this properly. I haven't in six years." He lifted his head and looked up at Lex, and his dark eyes were wet. "I swore my first oath on this sword when I was nineteen. Your age. I swore to protect the Lattice, and serve the Wardens, and keep their law." His mouth twisted. "I broke that oath, in the end. Or they broke it for me. I've never been sure which."

He closed his eyes.

"So I'll swear a new one," he said. "Not to the Lattice. Not to the Wardens. To you." His voice was steady and deep, and it filled the ruined smithy. "To you and your brother. And to the girl with the bow. And to the people in that chapel, and every village like this one between here and the Blightmarch." He opened his eyes. "I swear to stand on the steps. Wherever the steps are. For as long as I can stand."

The sword in his hands *rang*.

A long, clear, singing note, like Anvilsong at the end of its forging, rising up through the burned rafters and out into the morning. The runes along the blade flared gold, all three at once, and the frost on the steel thickened and sparkled like a field of snow under the rising sun.
