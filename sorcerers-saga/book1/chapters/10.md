# The Lithic Oracle

"Again," said Antho.

Lex threw the bolt.

It was one of the Wardens' crossbow bolts, a short, heavy iron-headed dart he'd salvaged from the slope below the Hollowmaw. He'd brought a bundle of forty of them, wrapped in oilcloth at the bottom of his pack, for no particular reason except that it had seemed a waste to leave good iron lying in the snow. Now he held one between his fingers and pushed heat into the iron head until it glowed cherry-red, and then he *threw* it, not with his arm, or not only, but with Heat Shaping, pushing the hot air behind it the way a bellows pushes air into a fire.

It flew. Fast. Faster than he could have thrown it by hand. It streaked across the clearing in a thin line of red light and struck the dead pine at the far side with a hiss and a thunk, and buried itself to the fletching in the trunk, and smoke curled up from the wound.

"Better," said Antho.

"It hit the tree."

"It hit the tree a hand's width below where you were aiming. Last time it was two." His brother was sitting cross-legged on a mossy rock at the edge of the clearing, Ashka curled in his lap and a staff of black, wind-twisted pine across his knees, cut on their second day out of the mountains, watching with the critical air of a schoolmaster. "You're still thinking about your arm. Stop thinking about your arm. Your arm isn't what's throwing it."

"Then what is?"

"You are. The heat is. The arm's just habit." Antho held up one long scarred hand and, without seeming to do anything at all, a small knot of shadow formed in his palm, the size of a plum, dark and dense and utterly silent. He flicked his fingers. The shadow shot across the clearing, faster than Lex's bolt, and struck the dead pine a finger's width from the bolt's fletching, and the bark where it hit simply *vanished*, leaving a neat round hole that went in half an inch and stopped.

"Show-off," said Lex.

"Eight years in a cave," said Antho mildly. "I had a lot of time to practice."

They'd been on the Road for nine days. The Greyspine was a grey wall far behind them to the south, and the country they walked through now was moorland, wide and rolling and empty, covered in heather and bracken and great grey boulders that sat on the hilltops like sleeping beasts. The Road ran straight across it, north, heaved and broken and mossy, marked every few miles by a waystone with the open hand and the flame. They'd seen no one. No villages, no farms, no travelers. Only sheep, once, on a far hillside, and a hawk hanging in the wind, and in the distance, every evening, the mist rising off the low places in long silver skeins.

Antho had started teaching Lex on the second day out from the mountains. It had begun almost by accident. Lex had been trying to light the fire with Heat Shaping, the way he'd done in the foothills, and had set the bracken around it alight instead, and Antho had stamped it out and then sat down and said, with the air of a man who'd been holding his tongue for some time: *You do realize you're doing that completely wrong.*

He hadn't realized. Nobody had ever taught him. Ember had told him what his skills did, and guided him in fights, and talked him through his points. But Ember was a construct of knowledge, not of practice. It knew what a thing was; it didn't always know what a thing *felt* like, from the inside.

Antho knew. Antho had spent eight years alone in a cave with nothing but a power he didn't understand and a voice that wanted to eat him, and he'd learned, by trial and error and sheer stubbornness, how to make the power do what he wanted. He knew how it felt when it went right. He knew, much better, how it felt when it went wrong.

"Your fire and my dark aren't the same," he'd said that first evening, sitting by the half-burned bracken. "But they're not as different as you'd think. They both come from the same place. The Root, Ember calls it." He'd shaped a little knot of shadow in his palm and let it dissolve. "The trick is the same. You don't push it. You *let* it. Like water finding a crack."

"That's not what Ember says."

"What does Ember say?"

"*Heat is a material. Shape it.*"

Antho had thought about that. "Fine," he'd said. "Both. Shape it, and let it. You shape the channel. Then you let it flow."

It had made no sense at all. And then, three days later, halfway through a fight with a pack of moorland wolves, it had suddenly made perfect sense, and Lex had stopped *pushing* his Ember Strikes and started *letting* them, and the fire had come down his arm into the hammer like a river let out of a sluice, twice as hot and half as tiring.

They reached the Lithic Oracle on the eleventh day.

Wren saw it first. She'd been scouting ahead, as she did every morning, ranging out along the Road a mile or two in front of them and then circling back, and she came back that morning at a run, her face flushed and strange.

"There's a gorge," she said. "Up ahead. The Road goes into it. And there's something in it." She hesitated. "Statues."

"Statues?"

"Lots of statues." Wren's jaw tightened. "People. Stone people. All down the sides of the gorge." She paused. "Some of them are wearing armor. Some of them are wearing *clothes*. Like real clothes. With buttons."

Lex and Antho looked at each other.

"The Oracle," said Antho quietly. "The first shrine. The book said a guardian. It didn't say what kind."

*[A stone guardian who asks questions,]* said Ember. *[Lithic means stone. An oracle answers questions, or asks them. I'd guess the statues are the people who gave the wrong answers.]*

"Ember thinks the statues are people who gave the wrong answers," Lex said.

Wren stared at him. "*Gave the wrong answers*. To what?"

"To whatever she asks."

"*She*?"

Lex didn't know why he'd said *she*. It had just come out.

---

The gorge was a deep cleft in the moor, cut by a river long since dried up, its walls sheer grey stone a hundred feet high. The Road ran straight into it and down along its floor, the heaved grey slabs gleaming wet with the mist that pooled between the walls. And on either side of the Road, all along the floor of the gorge and up onto ledges in the rock walls, there were statues.

Wren had been right. They were people.

Lex walked slowly between them with Anvilsong on his shoulder, and his skin crawled. They were grey stone, all of them, the same grey as the gorge walls. But they weren't carved. They couldn't have been carved. No sculptor alive could have made stone look like this: every fold of a cloak, every crease of a sleeve, every strand of hair. A woman in a traveling dress with her hand raised to her mouth, her stone eyes wide. A man in old-fashioned armor, his sword half-drawn, his face twisted in fury. A boy, no older than fifteen, sitting on a rock with his chin in his hands as if he'd been thinking hard about something when the stone took him. A Warden in a grey cloak, his lattice-pin carved in perfect detail on his chest, his mouth open in the middle of a word.

Some of them were very old. Worn by weather, softened by moss, their faces rubbed almost smooth. Some of them were not old at all.

"How many?" Wren whispered.

Antho was counting. He always counted. "Two hundred and twelve," he said quietly. "That I can see." He stopped beside the boy on the rock and looked down at him for a long moment. "They're not dead," he said. "Not exactly."

"What do you mean?"

"Grave-Speech. It's one of my skills. I can hear the dead, sometimes. If they want to be heard." He touched the stone boy's shoulder, very lightly. "These aren't dead. They're just—*still*. Waiting." He took his hand away. "It's very quiet inside them."

Ashka, on his shoulder, made an unhappy little trilling sound and pressed against his neck.

At the end of the gorge, the walls drew close together, and the Road ran up a broad flight of grey stone steps, and at the top of the steps, sitting on a great flat plinth of rock with the dead river's dry falls rising behind her, was the Oracle.

---

She was enormous.

She was the size of a small house, lying on the plinth the way a cat lies on a hearth, with her great forepaws stretched out in front of her and her hind legs tucked beneath. She had the body of a lion, grey stone, every muscle carved in perfect smooth relief. She had wings, folded along her back, the wings of a great eagle, each feather a separate blade of stone. And she had the face of a woman.

It was an old woman's face, lined and serene, with heavy-lidded eyes and a long straight nose and a mouth that was very nearly smiling. Her hair, stone too, was bound up in a heavy coil on the back of her head. She was the most beautiful thing Lex had ever seen, and the most frightening, and as they came up the steps toward her, her stone eyes opened.

They were silver. Not stone. Living silver, like the light of the Lattice, swirling slowly, like mist in a bowl.

"Ah," said the Oracle.

Her voice was the sound of millstones grinding, slow and deep and heavy, and yet somehow, underneath it, there was something almost warm. Something almost amused.

"Visitors," she said. "How lovely. It has been eleven years since anyone came to me who was not a fool or a thief." Her silver eyes moved over them, one by one: Wren with her bow, Antho with the dragon on his shoulder, Lex with his hammer. When they reached Lex, they stopped, and something moved in them. A swirl in the silver.

"Oh," said the Oracle softly. "Oh, *now*. Look at you."

Lex didn't know what to say. So he said, "Hello."

The Oracle laughed. It was like a small landslide, distant and rumbling.

"Hello," she said. "Hello, child of the blood. Hello, little bright one, little hammer-hand. I wondered when one of you would come." Her gaze moved to Antho. "And you. The deep one. Oh, what a dark you carry. What a heavy, hungry dark." She tilted her great stone head. "You are very brave to carry it so far from its door."

Antho's hand tightened on his staff. He didn't say anything.

"And you," said the Oracle to Wren. Her silver eyes narrowed slightly. "You are not of the blood. What are you doing in such company, little climber?"

Wren lifted her chin. "Keeping them alive."

The Oracle laughed again. "Oh, I *like* you," she said. "You may stay."

---

"You have come for the song," said the Oracle, when she had finished laughing. "The first of the three. I can feel the stone you carry." Her silver gaze dropped to Antho's chest, where the lodestone hung on its cord beneath his shirt. "It is hungry, that little stone. It has been waiting a long time to sing."

"Yes," said Lex. "We've come for the song."

"Then you know the price."

"We answer your questions."

"You answer them *truly*." The Oracle's great stone paws flexed on the plinth, a grinding of rock on rock. "Not cleverly. Not correctly. *Truly*. There is a difference, and every soul in that gorge behind you learned it too late." Her voice dropped. "I will ask three. One for each of you. If you answer truly, you will have your song. If you answer falsely..." She let the silence finish the sentence.

Wren swallowed audibly.

"What if we don't want to answer?" said Antho.

"Then you may turn around and walk back down my steps, and go home, and never find the one you are looking for." The Oracle's eyes swirled. "That is always an option. Very few take it."

The three of them looked at each other. Ashka chirped nervously.

"I'll go first," said Lex.

"No," said the Oracle. "You will go first, little hammer-hand, because I will choose you first. That is not the same thing." Her heavy-lidded eyes settled on him, and he felt the weight of them like a hand on his chest. "But since you were brave enough to offer, I will be kind, and ask you something you know."

She was quiet for a moment. The mist drifted between the gorge walls.

"*I am given by the hammer*," said the Oracle, "*and stolen by the fire. I am lost in the quench, if the quench is rushed. Old smiths keep me in their arms. Young smiths think they have me. What am I?*"

---

Lex stood very still.

*[Careful,]* said Ember, very quietly. *[She said truly, not correctly. There may be more than one answer.]*

He thought about it. He thought about the forge. *Given by the hammer.* Every blow on hot iron did something to it. Packed the grain tighter. Made it harder, tougher. *Stolen by the fire.* Put iron back in the fire too long, too hot, and it lost all that. Went soft again. *Lost in the quench, if the quench is rushed.* Plunge hot steel into cold water too fast, too carelessly, and it would crack, or warp, or go brittle as glass.

*Mind the quench.*

"Temper," he said.

The Oracle's silver eyes swirled.

"*Which* temper?" she said softly.

And Lex understood. He understood what she was really asking. Because there were two. There was the temper of steel, the hardness and toughness and springiness that a smith worked into a blade with hammer and fire and water. And there was the other kind. The temper of a man. *Old smiths keep me in their arms.* Brannoc, steady as a wall, never once raising his voice in eight years. *Young smiths think they have me.* Lex, nineteen, standing in the forge shouting at a stranger about his father, the fire in the hearth leaping up a foot high.

"Both," he said.

The Oracle looked at him for a long, long moment.

Then she smiled. A real smile, slow and deep, the stone lines of her face rearranging themselves like hills shifting in a dream.

"Both," she agreed. "Truly answered, little hammer-hand. You will keep yours in your arms one day. Not yet. But one day."
