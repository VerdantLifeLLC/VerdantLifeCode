# The Six Wells

"The first stage is Listening," said Malachi. "You've done that. Very loudly. Congratulations." He was sitting cross-legged on a flat rock at the edge of the still pool, peeling an apple with a little bone knife, the peel coming off in a single long curl. "The second stage is Answering. That's harder."

"What's the difference?" said Lex.

"Listening is hearing the Well. Answering is the Well hearing *you*." Malachi held up the curl of apple peel and examined it critically. "You've heard Flame. Flame knows you're there. It leans toward you. It likes you, the way a cat likes a warm lap." He popped the peel into his mouth. "But it hasn't *answered* you yet. It hasn't done anything you asked. Has it?"

Lex thought about it. The fire in the hut had leaned out of the doorway toward him. The fire at Qel'Varran had leaned. But neither had *done* anything.

"No," he said.

"No." Malachi bit into the apple. "Because you haven't asked properly. You've been asking the way you asked the Lattice. *Ember Strike. Heat Shaping. Fire, do this.*" He chewed. "Like a man giving orders to a servant. The Lattice likes that. It's built for that. It's a wheel. You push it, it turns." He swallowed. "Flame isn't a wheel. Flame is a river. You don't give orders to a river."

"Then what do you do?"

Malachi grinned his gap-toothed grin.

"You tell it the truth," he said.

---

It was the hardest thing Lex had ever done.

Malachi built a fire for him every morning in a ring of stones by the pool. An ordinary fire, of dead apple-wood. He sat Lex down in front of it and told him to make it rise. Not with the Lattice. Not with Heat Shaping or Ember Strike or anything he'd learned in the last three months. With Flame. By *asking*.

The first day, nothing happened.

Lex sat in front of the fire and thought at it. *Rise. Please rise. Burn higher.* He thought it a hundred times. A thousand. The fire burned on, ordinary, crackling, indifferent. It didn't lean toward him the way the eternal fire in the hut did. It didn't do anything at all.

"You're giving orders again," said Malachi from his rock. "Politely, I'll grant you. *Please*. Very nice manners. Your mother raised you well." He bit into another apple. "Still orders."

"Then what do I *say*?"

"Not *say*. *Tell*." Malachi chewed. "The truth. What's true about you, right now, sitting there. Fire doesn't care what you want. Fire cares what you *are*."

The second day, nothing happened.

The third day, Lex lost his temper. He'd been sitting in front of the fire since dawn, and it was afternoon, and his legs were numb and his back was aching and his mind was running in circles again, and the fire was just *sitting* there. Burning. Ignoring him. The way the Lattice Stone in Highcrest had ignored the girl in the brown dress. The way the Anvil of Ascent had said *not granted*. The way Elric had looked at him at the foot of the cellar stair and said *I'm sorry, Lex*, and taken everything.

And something in his chest went up like a forge with the bellows going full.

*I'm ANGRY*, he thought at the fire. *I'm so angry I can't breathe. He took everything. He took Father and he took my class and he took Ember and I couldn't stop him. I couldn't do ANYTHING.*

The fire *exploded*.

It went up in a column ten feet high, a roaring orange pillar that blew the stones of the ring outward in a scatter and scorched the grass in a circle around it and singed Lex's eyebrows clean off. He threw himself backward. The column hung there for a moment, howling, and then collapsed in a shower of sparks, and the fire was gone. Just ash. Just smoking ash.

Lex lay on his back on the scorched grass, staring up at the sky.

Malachi was laughing.

He was laughing so hard he'd fallen off his rock. He lay on the grass by the pool with his little brown body shaking and tears running down into his white beard, wheezing.

"*There!*" he gasped. "*There it is! That's Answering!*"

"I didn't mean to *do* that!"

"Of course you didn't! You never do, the first time!" Malachi sat up, wiping his eyes. "You told it the truth. You told it *I'm angry*. And it believed you. And it answered. With anger." He grinned. "Fire's honest, smith. It gives back what you give it. Anger gets anger. Grief gets grief. Love gets love." He got up and brushed the grass off his robe. "Now you know. The rest is just learning what you want to tell it."

---

It came faster after that.

Not easily. Never easily. But faster.

He learned that when he was angry, the fire was wild. Big and loud and hungry and impossible to control. It wanted to eat everything around it, and it didn't care whether that was a log or a tree or the hem of his coat.

He learned that when he was afraid, the fire was small. It shrank down into the coals and hid, the way he'd hidden in the loft above the forge after the cottage burned.

He learned that when he was sad, the fire was gentle. Low and warm and slow, like a hearth on a winter night. It didn't do much. But what it did, it did carefully.

And on the eighth day, he learned what happened when he told it about Brannoc.

He hadn't planned to. He'd been sitting in front of the morning fire, trying to make it rise without anger, and failing, and his mind had wandered. To the forge in Ashbourne. To the old smith standing at the edge of the cobbles with his hand raised. To the lantern in the forge window in the middle of the day. To the hammer-oath. *What's struck on the anvil holds.*

*I promised him I'd come back,* he thought. Not at the fire. Just thought it. *I miss him. He's the best man I know. He gave me everything and he never asked for anything back. I want to go home and tell him he was right. That the iron can sing. That I found my brother. That I'm sorry I left him alone.*

The fire rose.

Not in a column. Not in an explosion. It just *rose*. Gently. Steadily. The flames lifting up out of the coals like a man standing up from a chair. Gold, not orange. Warm, not hot. They rose until they were as tall as Lex sitting down, and they held there, burning steady and clear and bright, without wavering, without smoke.

And they leaned toward him. And curled around his outstretched hand like the water had curled around Malachi's fingers. And didn't burn.

Lex stared at it.

Malachi had stopped peeling his apple. He was sitting very still on his rock, looking at the fire with his shifting eyes gone steady gold.

"*Love*," he said softly. "You told it about someone you love."

"Brannoc. The smith who raised me."

"Ah." Malachi nodded slowly. "That's the one. That's the truth it likes best, smith. Fire's a hearth before it's a weapon. Always was." He set down his apple. "Now you're Answering."

---

Antho learned in the dark.

Malachi took him down into a cave under the hut on the fourth day. A small round chamber, carved out of the rock under the valley, reached by a ladder through a trapdoor in the hut's floor. It was completely dark. Not dim. Not shadowy. *Dark*. The kind of dark where you can't see your hand in front of your face, where your eyes make up shapes and colors because there's nothing for them to see.

Lex went down with them the first time, because Antho asked him to. He stood at the bottom of the ladder in the absolute black and felt his brother beside him, shaking.

"I don't like this," said Antho. His voice was very tight. "I don't like the dark. Not anymore. Not since—" He stopped. "It's *louder* in the dark."

His Clarity had risen since they came to the Stillwater. Forty, forty-five, fifty, as the days went by, as Malachi fed them and made them sleep and sat with Antho by the eternal fire in the evenings and told him stories. Not the Keeper's Lantern's floor of fifteen. Not the Hollowmaw's eighty. But better. Much better.

Now, in the dark, it was dropping again.

"Of course it's louder," said Malachi's voice out of the blackness. Cheerful. Unconcerned. "The thief likes the dark. It's been telling you the dark is its house for eight years. Of course it's going to shout when you walk into it." A pause. "Sit down, deep one."

Antho sat. Lex heard him: the scrape of cloth on stone, a shaky breath.

"Now," said Malachi. "Listen."

"*To what?*" Antho's voice cracked. "*All I can hear is it. It's so loud. It's saying—it's saying—*"

"I know what it's saying. It always says the same thing. *Hungry. Warm. Let me in.*" Malachi's voice was very calm. "That's not what I want you to listen to. Listen *under* it."

"*There's nothing under it.*"

"There's always something under it." A pause. "What's under the noise of a crowd, deep one? When you're standing in a market square and everyone's shouting?"

Silence. Then, very faintly, Antho: "I don't know. I haven't been in a market in eight years."

"The ground," said Malachi. "The ground's under it. The ground's always there, quiet, holding everyone up, whether they notice or not." A pause. "What's under the noise of a storm?"

"The sky?"

"The sky. Quiet. Still. Holding the storm the way a cup holds water." Another pause. "So. What's under the noise of the thief, deep one? In the dark? What's holding *it* up?"

Antho didn't answer for a long time.

Lex stood in the absolute black at the foot of the ladder and listened to his brother breathe. Short, sharp, frightened breaths. Then slower. Then slower still.

He felt it through the Bloodbond before he heard anything. A change. In the cold deep pool that was his brother. The surface of it had been churning, whipped up by the thing at the bottom, the way a pond churns in a gale. And now, very slowly, something under the surface was *settling*. Not the thief. Something else. Something deeper than the thief. Older. Bigger. So big that the thief was only a small dark fish in it.

"*Oh*," whispered Antho.

"There," said Malachi softly.

"*It's—it's so big*." Antho's voice was full of wonder. "*It's so big and so quiet. It's like—like the sky at night. Between the stars. It's not hungry. It's not anything. It's just—*there. Holding everything.*" His voice cracked. "*Holding me.*"

"That's Shade," said Malachi. "That's the Well. That's the dark that was there before the thief moved in." His voice was very gentle. "It's been under you the whole time, deep one. For eight years. Under the noise. Holding you up. You just couldn't hear it over the shouting."

Wren learned on the edge of a cliff.

There was a place on the rim of the valley where the green bowl of the Stillwater met the white salt plain above, and the edge was steep: a sheer drop of forty feet down a face of grey rock to the meadow below. Malachi took her there on the sixth day, with Lex and Antho trailing behind, curious.

"Stand on the edge," he said.

Wren stood on the edge. She didn't even hesitate. She'd been standing on the edges of cliffs her whole life.

"Now close your eyes," said Malachi. "And tell me which way the wind's going to blow."

Wren closed her eyes. She stood very still on the rim of the cliff, her fox-red braid stirring on her shoulder, her face lifted. Lex watched her. He'd seen her do this a hundred times on the road. Stop on a ridge, lift her face, close her eyes. *Weather Sense*, he'd thought, from her panel. *Journeyman 2*.

"From the west," said Wren. "In a moment. Gusting. Then it'll swing round north." She opened her eyes. "Why?"

"How do you know?"

"I just know. I always know. It's—" She frowned. "It's like hearing someone coming up the stairs before they open the door. The air sort of—*leans*. Before it moves."

"*Leans*," said Malachi. "Yes. Like fire leaning toward the smith." He grinned. "You've been listening to Gale your whole life, little climber. You just thought it was a skill in your panel." He stepped back from the edge. "Now. When the west wind comes. Step off."

Wren turned and stared at him.

"*Step off*? Off the *cliff*?"

"Off the cliff."

"It's forty feet!"

"It's forty feet to the ground, yes. If you fall." Malachi leaned on his staff. "I'm not asking you to fall. I'm asking you to step."

"Malachi—" Lex started forward.

"Hush, smith. She knows what she's doing. She just doesn't know she knows." Malachi didn't take his eyes off Wren. "When the wind comes, climber. Don't fall into it. *Step onto it.* Like a stair. Like a rock. Like a branch. Like you've stepped onto a thousand handholds on a thousand mountains." His shifting eyes went pale. Pale grey. Pale as the sky. "It's there. It's always been there. You just never asked it to hold you."

Wren stood on the rim of the cliff and stared at him.

Then she turned around and faced the drop.

Lex couldn't breathe.

The wind came. From the west, gusting, exactly as she'd said. Lex felt it hit his face. Saw it stir the grass on the rim, saw it lift Wren's braid off her shoulder.

And Wren stepped off the cliff.

She didn't fall.

She *stood* on the air. For one heartbeat. Two. Her boot planted on nothing, on the empty wind forty feet above the meadow, her arms spread wide for balance, her face white with shock. The wind rushed past her, lifting her braid straight up, billowing her coat. And held her.

Then it swung north, exactly as she'd said it would, and she fell.

Not hard. Not fast. She *drifted*. Down. Slowly. Like a leaf. Like a feather. Spinning gently in the air, her arms still spread, forty feet down to the soft green meadow below, and landed on her feet in the grass as lightly as a cat jumping off a wall.

She stood there in the meadow, looking up at them. Her mouth was open.

Then she started to laugh.

She laughed and laughed, standing alone in the meadow at the foot of the cliff with her arms still spread, and Lex could hear it all the way up on the rim. A wild, delighted, disbelieving laugh, like a child's.

Seraphine didn't hear any of the Wells.

She tried. Every morning, she sat by the still pool with the others and closed her eyes and listened. Malachi sat beside her and talked to her, gently, patiently. *Under the noise. What's under the noise.* She tried for days.

On the tenth day, she opened her eyes and said, very calmly, "I don't hear anything."

Malachi looked at her.

"I hear words," said Seraphine. "That's all I've ever heard. Words, and the spaces between them. I hear what people say and what they don't say. I hear what the books say and what the footnotes say." She took off her spectacles and polished them on her sleeve. "I don't hear fire, or stone, or wind. I've tried. I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry," said Malachi. "Not everyone hears the Wells. Not even in the old days. Most people didn't." He shrugged. "They still lived perfectly good lives. They baked bread and built houses and wrote books." He smiled at her. "Someone has to write it all down, clever one."

Seraphine looked at him for a long moment. Then she put her spectacles back on and nodded.

"Then I'll write it all down," she said.

She did. Every evening, by the eternal fire in the hut, she sat with the grey Listeners' Codex open on her knees and a stack of paper Malachi had found somewhere, and copied it out. Every word. And then she wrote down what Malachi said. Every lesson. Every story. Every joke, even the bad ones. Her neat cramped handwriting filled page after page after page.

"For whoever comes after," she said, when Lex asked. "So they won't have to find a nine-hundred-year-old man in the middle of a salt desert to learn how to listen."

---

The stories came in the evenings.

Malachi told them by the eternal fire, while the goats bleated outside and the stars came out over the rim of the valley. Stories about the time before the Lattice. About the Listeners. About the six of them, his brothers and sisters, and Aurelian, the oldest, the cleverest, the one who was always right.

"He was a smith," Malachi said one night. "Did you know that? The best smith who ever lived. He could make anything. Swords that sang. Shields that remembered every blow. A plough that turned the earth so gently the worms didn't notice." He smiled into the fire. "When I was small, he made me a little bell. Silver. No bigger than my thumb. It rang whenever I was sad, so he'd know to come and find me."

"What happened to it?" said Lex.

Malachi was quiet for a long moment.

"I threw it in the sea," he said at last. "The night after the Weaving. When he'd put himself into the net and I couldn't reach him anymore." He shook his head. "It rang and rang. All the way down."

Nobody said anything.

"He was afraid," said Malachi. "That's what nobody understands. They think he built the Lattice because he was wise. Because he saw what needed to be done and did it." He stared into the fire. "He built it because he was *afraid*. Afraid of the hunger. Afraid of what people did with power. Afraid of himself. Of what *he* might do, with all that strength, if nobody stopped him." His voice was very quiet. "So he built a cage. For the hunger, and for everyone else, and for himself. And then he got inside it and locked the door."

"And the hunger?" said Antho softly.

"Is still in the cage. With him." Malachi's shifting eyes went dark. "Nine hundred years. In the dark. Holding it. Holding it so close, so long, that I don't know anymore where he ends and it begins." He looked at Antho. "*Whoever holds it, it will try to become.* I wrote that, you know. In the margin of a book in Qel'Varran. The night after the Weaving. I was very drunk. And very sad." He smiled faintly. "And very right."

---

On the eleventh day, Lex asked about the lock.

He'd been putting it off. Afraid of the answer. But he'd been Answering Flame for three days now, steady and gold and warm, and Stone had started to hum back at him when he laid his hands on the rocks of the valley, low and slow and heavy, and he couldn't put it off any longer.

"Elric locked my class," he said. "With stolen Root. And when he did, Ember went quiet. The voice. The construct. My father's piece." He swallowed. "I can't reach it. I've tried every day. I've tried with the Wells. I can feel something, behind the lock. Warm. Faint. Like a fire behind a door. But I can't get to it."

Malachi looked at him for a long time.

"Your little voice," he said. "Your father's piece. It lives in your seal. Yes?"

"Yes."

"And your seal is part of your Root. And your Root is the lowest part of the wheel. The axle, if you like." Malachi turned his apple over in his gnarled hands. "When the thief locked your class, he locked the whole wheel. Axle and all. Your little voice is shut inside it, the way a man's shut inside a mill when someone bars the door."

"So how do I get him out?"

"You unjam the wheel."

"*How*?"

Malachi set down the apple.

"With the river," he said. "Same as anything." His shifting eyes went gold. "The thief locked your wheel with Root. Root is the strongest thing in the Lattice. Nothing in the Lattice can break it." He leaned forward. "But Root isn't the strongest thing in the *world*, smith. It's just the strongest thing in the wheel. The river's stronger. The river's always stronger. The river was here before the wheel, and it'll be here after." He tapped Lex on the chest, right over his heart. "You're a smith. You've got Flame answering you. You've got Stone humming at you. What do smiths do with things that are stuck?"

Lex stared at him.

"Heat them," he said slowly. "Until they move."

"And then?"

"Hit them. Until they're the shape you need."

Malachi grinned his gap-toothed grin.

"Then go and find an anvil," he said.

---

There was an anvil in the valley.

Lex hadn't known. He'd been there eleven days, and he'd walked every inch of the green bowl, and he hadn't seen it. But Malachi led him that evening to the far side of the still pool, where the slope of the valley met the rim of white salt, and pulled aside a curtain of hanging ivy, and behind it there was a cave. A shallow one. Barely more than an overhang. And in it, there was a forge.

Old. Ancient. A hearth of white stone, like the one in the hut, but bigger. A chimney carved up through the rock to the surface. And beside the hearth, an anvil. Black iron. Not white stone like the Anvil of Ascent. Iron. Real iron. Pitted and scarred and worn smooth on its face by long-ago hands.

There was a hammer lying on it.

Small. A hand-hammer. The kind you use for fine work. Its head was black iron, like the anvil, and its haft was some pale wood Lex didn't recognize, worn dark and smooth by a palm that had held it for years. And on its head, worn almost smooth, there was a mark.

An open hand. And above the palm, a flame.

Lex stood very still.

"His," said Malachi quietly. "Aurelian's. He made it when he was young. Before the Weaving. Before any of it." He looked at the little hammer for a long moment. "He gave it to me, the night before. *Keep it for me, Mal. I won't need it where I'm going.*" His voice was rough. "I brought it here. Built the forge for it. Nine hundred years, it's been lying on that anvil." He looked at Lex. "Waiting for someone of the blood with the gift of the forge."

Lex couldn't speak.

"The hearth's cold," said Malachi. "It's been cold for nine hundred years. I could never light it. It's not my fire." He stepped back. "See if it's yours."

---

Lex knelt in front of the cold white hearth.

He didn't reach for the Lattice. There was nothing there to reach for. He didn't reach for his Root. It was locked behind the iron door.

He reached for Flame.

He closed his eyes and listened, the way Malachi had taught him. Under the noise. Under the running thoughts and the grief and the fear. Down to the quiet place where the fire was. Not his fire. Not the Lattice's fire. *Fire*. The fire that had been in the world since before anything.

*I'm here,* he told it. *I'm a smith. I'm my father's son and my mother's son and Brannoc's apprentice. I'm Antho's brother. I'm Wren's—* He stopped. Started again. *I love them. All of them. I want to make something that will help them. I want to get my fire back so I can protect them. I want to find Ember. I want to save my father. I want to stop the thing that's hurting everyone.*

*That's the truth,* he told it. *That's all of it.*

He opened his eyes.

The hearth was burning.

Gold. Steady. Clear. Rising up out of the bare white stone without fuel or spark, the way the eternal fire in the hut burned, the way Seren's lamp burned on the rock above the Hollowmaw. It lit the little cave with warm gold light. It lit the black iron anvil, and the little hammer lying on it with the open hand and the flame on its head.

And it leaned toward him.

Behind him, he heard Malachi let out a long, slow breath.

"Nine hundred years," the little man whispered. "Nine hundred years, and it's burning again."

Lex stood up. He went to the anvil. He looked down at the little hammer.

Then he picked it up.

It fit his hand like it had been made for it. Like Anvilsong fit his hand. The pale wood of the haft was warm, and worn smooth in exactly the places his fingers fell.

He turned around. Malachi was standing in the mouth of the cave with his staff, and his shifting eyes had gone a color Lex had never seen in them before. Wet. Very bright.

"Tomorrow," said Lex. "At dawn. I'm going to forge."

"Forge what?"

Lex looked down at the little hammer in his hand. At the open hand on its head.

"Myself," he said.
