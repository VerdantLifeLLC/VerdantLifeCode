# The Gathering of the Unbound

Antho came home at dawn.

Lex was waiting on the north road, at the edge of the cobbles, where Brannoc had stood with his hand raised on the morning Lex left. He'd been there since before first light. Wren had brought him a blanket and a mug of Mother Orla's spiced cider and then gone back to bed, because, she said, there was no point both of them freezing to death waiting for a warlock.

He saw them first as a speck against the grey sky over the Greyspine. Then a shape. Then a dragon, blue-grey and shimmering, coming down out of the mountains in long slow spirals, her great wings barely moving, riding the cold morning air.

She landed on the north road in a gust of wind that sent the grey dust swirling. And sat there, folding her wings, her golden eyes half-closed with exhaustion.

Antho was on her neck. Slumped forward. His face buried in her crest.

"*Antho*."

His brother lifted his head.

He looked terrible. Grey-faced, hollow-eyed, his cheeks sunk, his lips cracked and bloody, as if he'd been sitting in the dark for three days without food or water or sleep. Which he had. His hands, where they gripped Ashka's crest, were shaking. The Keeper's Lantern at his belt was burning very low.

But his eyes were grey. Clear, still grey. And when they found Lex standing on the road, something moved in them that Lex had never seen there before.

Peace.

"Hello, Lex," said Antho. His voice was a whisper. "I closed it."

"I know. I saw."

"I *closed* it." Antho slid off Ashka's neck. His legs buckled when he hit the ground, and Lex caught him. "Lady of Lanterns. I closed it. The door. The one in the Hollowmaw." He was laughing, very weakly, against Lex's shoulder. "Four years, I sat on it. And all I had to do was—"

"What? What did you do?"

Antho didn't answer right away. He stood there leaning on his brother on the grey north road, breathing, while the sun came up over the mountains behind him.

"I listened," he said at last.

---

He told them later, in the forge, by the fire, with a bowl of Mother Orla's stew in his hands that he couldn't stop staring at, as if he'd forgotten what food was.

The cavern had been worse than he'd ever seen it. The ward-nails pulled out of the rock and scattered. *Pulled*, not broken; someone had come and taken them, he thought. One of Morvane's creatures. The green fire gone. And the breach—

"It wasn't a crack anymore," Antho said. "It was a *door*. As tall as the cavern. Wide open. And the Gloam was just—pouring out. Like a river out of a broken dam. And on the other side of it—" He stopped. His hands tightened on the bowl. "I could see it, Lex. For the first time. The other side. The Hollow."

Nobody spoke.

"It's dark," said Antho. "That's all. Just dark. Not like night. Not like a cave. Like—*before*. Before anything. Before light was a thing that could happen." He swallowed. "And it goes on forever. And in it, very far down, something was moving. Something huge. Coming toward the door." His voice dropped to a whisper. "It knew I was there. It knew me. It's always known me."

"What did you do?" said Seraphine. She'd come in the night before with the Fenmoor column, two hundred people walking into Ashbourne's green by lantern-light, footsore and grey-faced and staring at the white Stone. She was sitting beside Antho now, very close, with her scarred hand on his arm.

"I sat down," said Antho. "In front of the door. Like Malachi taught us. And I listened."

"To the thing?"

"No. Under it." Antho looked into the fire. "Malachi said the thing in the Hollow isn't the dark. It's a thief living in the dark. And Shade—the real dark, the Well—is under it. Holding everything. Holding *it*." He was quiet for a moment. "So I listened for Shade. Under the noise. Under the hunger. And I found it. It was there. Holding the door frame, almost. Like the dark was the house and the door was just a hole someone had knocked in the wall."

"And then?"

"And then I asked it to close." Antho's mouth twisted. "Not *told*. Asked. Like you taught me to ask Flame, Lex. With the truth." He turned the bowl in his hands. "I told it I was tired. I told it I'd been holding this door for four years and I couldn't hold it anymore. I told it there were people down in the Vale who'd go grey if it stayed open. I told it about you. About Mam." His voice cracked. "I told it I was afraid."

The forge was very quiet. Brannoc had stopped working at the anvil. Wren had stopped mending her bowstring. Pell, sitting in the corner on his old upturned bucket, had his mouth open.

"And it started to close," said Antho. "Slowly. Like a wound closing. The dark—the *real* dark—just sort of... knitting. From the edges. And the thing on the other side felt it, and it *pushed*. It came up toward the door so fast. So hard. I could feel it. Like a wave coming up a beach." His hands were shaking. "And I thought: *I can't. I'm not strong enough. It's going to get through.*"

He stopped.

"And then I heard it," he said. "The singing."

Lex went very still.

"*Little light, little light*," whispered Antho. "From the other side. From somewhere in the dark. Not far. Close to the door. A woman's voice." He looked up at Lex, and his grey eyes were swimming. "And the thing stopped pushing. Just for a moment. Like it did that night. In the kitchen. When she sang into it and it didn't know what a song was."

"*Antho*—"

"So I sang back," said Antho. "I sang the next line. *Keep the door for the ones who go.* And she sang the one after that. And I sang the one after. Back and forth. Through the door. Like we used to, in the cottage, when I was small and couldn't sleep and she'd sing a line and I'd sing a line." He was crying now. He didn't seem to notice. "And while we sang, the door closed. From both sides. Lex, I swear by everything, I felt it. Someone was pushing on the other side. Pushing it shut. With me."

Nobody said anything for a long time.

"It could have been the thing," said Antho at last, very quietly. "Mocking me. Using her voice. It's done that before." He wiped his face. "It could have been."

"Do you think it was?" said Lex.

Antho looked at him across the fire.

"No," he said.

They came to Ashbourne over the next ten days.

Lex hadn't sent for them. Not most of them. He'd sent Quill, who had turned up on the morning after the Unbinding, perched on the forge's chimney as if he'd never left, looking thin and battered and enormously pleased with himself. But most of them came on their own.

They'd seen the World Notices. All three of them. *A Reckoning has been executed. A Ceiling has been broken.* And then, nine days later, a third one. Smaller. Stranger. Shown, Seraphine said afterward, only to those whose panels had ever carried a Ceiling, which was nearly everyone.

And people had read it, and stood very still in their fields and forges and mines and kitchens, and then picked up whatever they had and started walking.

---

Teodra Hale came first. On the fourth day. Riding in from the east at the head of sixty Ashen Banners, which was twenty more than she'd had before Caldris, because some of the scattered Wardens and knights and freed prisoners had found her in the hills and joined. She swung down from her horse on Ashbourne's green and looked at the white Stone, and the lanterns in every window, and the people walking around with their new class names written all over their faces.

"Huh," she said. "You weren't lying."

And behind her, on a borrowed horse, wrapped in bandages from head to foot, with his left arm in a sling and his face a patchwork of half-healed cuts and his grey hair singed short, there was a knight.

Lex didn't believe it at first. He stood in the doorway of the forge with a half-finished blade in his tongs and stared.

The knight looked across the green at him. And smiled. Crooked. Warm.

"*Lad*," said Garrick Dunmore.

Lex dropped the tongs.

---

"Teodra's people dragged me out," Garrick said later. He was sitting on the bench by the forge fire, where Elric had sat on the first night, with a mug of Mother Orla's cider in his good hand and Lex and Wren and Antho crowded around him. "I held the gate as long as I could. Long enough, Teodra says. Long enough for most of them to get out the sally port." He stared into the fire. "And then a Hollow-knight put a sword through my arm and another one hit me on the head with something heavy, and I went down."

"And they didn't—"

"They were going to. I was lying in the gateway with three of them standing over me and Oathkeeper in the dirt where I'd dropped it." Garrick's mouth twisted. "And then Teodra came back. With twenty of her worst cut-throats. Back into the burning fortress. For one old knight." He shook his head. "I've told her she's mad. She says she knows."

"*Mad*," said Teodra Hale from the doorway, where she was leaning with her arms folded. "I prefer *sentimental*."

"Same thing, in a sellsword," said Garrick.

He looked at Lex.

"I didn't die," he said quietly. "I swore to stand on the steps for as long as I could stand, and I stood, and I fell, and I didn't die." He set down the mug. "So I suppose I'm still standing."

Lex couldn't speak. So he didn't. He just put his arms around the old knight, carefully, around the bandages and the sling, and held on.

---

Seraphine's column had grown on the road.

She'd started from Fenmoor with two hundred. She walked into Ashbourne with five hundred. Every grey village they'd passed through on the way west, she'd stopped. Gathered the people. Found the ones who'd been taken by the Quiet only recently. And with Ilsa, the new Healer, beside her, and the young Stormcaller from Fenmoor who'd been a Laborer, and the old Herbalist who'd been a Farmhand, they'd done what they could. They couldn't break Ceilings. They didn't have Root. But they could pull the Gloam out of the newly-taken, slowly, painfully, with Ilsa's healing and the Stormcaller's clean lightning. And they could tell people about Ashbourne.

"I didn't do anything," Seraphine said, when Lex tried to thank her. "I just read the maps and told everyone where to walk."

"She organized five hundred people across a hundred miles of Blightmarch," said Ilsa, standing beside her, "with no food and no weapons and Gloamed things in every ditch." The girl's thin face was fierce. "She didn't sleep for six days. She carried a little boy on her back for two of them when his feet gave out." She looked at Seraphine. "She did *everything*."

Seraphine went red to the tips of her ears and pushed her cracked spectacles up her nose.

Antho was looking at her. Lex saw the look. He'd never seen his brother look at anyone like that.

---

And on the seventh day, the Wardens came.

Lex saw them first from the forge doorway. A column of grey cloaks coming up the south road from Millbridge. Two hundred of them, maybe more. Marching in step. And at the front, on a big grey horse, a tall lean man with a face like a hatchet.

Lex's heart stopped.

The column came up the road to the edge of the green and halted. The grey cloaks stood in their ranks, very still, very quiet. People on the green were backing away. Teodra Hale had her greatsword half-drawn. Garrick was on his feet with Oathkeeper in his good hand.

The tall man on the grey horse swung down. He walked across the green alone, toward the forge. Toward Lex.

He stopped in front of him.

"Harrow," said Daxian Roole.

"Captain."

"Not *Captain*." Roole's hatchet face did something that might have been a smile. "Not anymore. I was stripped of rank. Court-martialed. Sentenced to hang for treason." He paused. "That was three weeks ago."

"What happened?"

"I told the truth at my trial," said Roole simply. "In front of the whole High Council. I told them about the Hollowmaw. About the breach. About your brother holding it. About Vane. About the High Warden ordering me to falsify my report." He shrugged. "Kell sentenced me to hang anyway. It was a very short trial."

"And?"

"And then the World Notices started." Roole's pale blue eyes were steady. "*A Ceiling has been broken.* And then *every Ceiling at the Stone of Ashbourne*." He looked past Lex, at the white Stone in the middle of the green, glowing in the morning light. "Every Warden in the Spire saw it. Every one. Even the ones who'd been there forty years. Even the ones who'd set the flags on the Stones themselves." He paused. "And some of them started asking questions. Out loud. In the refectory. In the corridors." His mouth twitched. "And some of them remembered a captain in the cells who'd stood up at his trial and said the same things."

"They broke you out."

"Two hundred and eleven of them." Roole glanced over his shoulder at the silent grey column at the edge of the green. "And they brought someone with them."

He stepped aside.

And behind him, coming across the green with a cudgel in her one hand and a scowl on her broad red face and her grey hair cropped short as a soldier's, there was the largest woman Lex had ever seen.

"Smith," said Hedda Vorne. "You owe me a hundred crowns of beeswax."

"That anvil's *warm*," she said slowly.

"It remembers," said Lex.

The war council met in the Crooked Kettle on the tenth night.

Mother Orla had cleared the tables and spread out Seraphine's map on the biggest one and lit every lamp she had. They crowded around it: Lex and Antho, Wren and Seraphine, Garrick with his arm in its sling and Elowen beside him, Teodra Hale, Daxian Roole, Dame Brand, Hedda Vorne, and Brannoc, who had insisted on coming even though he said he didn't know anything about wars, because he knew about Lex.

And Lucius Vane.

He sat at the far end of the table, apart from everyone, with his white hair and his slate eyes and a blanket around his shoulders. He'd barely spoken since the green. He ate when food was put in front of him. He slept when he was told to sleep. Roole had taken custody of him, and Roole kept him close.

It took them the rest of the night to plan it.

Three fronts.

Teodra Hale and Daxian Roole would take the main force, all of it, every Unbound and Warden and sellsword who could hold a weapon, east to the Blightmarch border. To the Thornhollow Pass, where the old Vhessan road crossed into Caldor. Where Morvane's army was massing. They would hold the pass. Not to win. To *hold*. To be loud and bright and stubborn, to draw every Gloamed eye in the east toward them.

"Like a lantern in a window," said Teodra, with a grim smile. "Moths can't help themselves."

Elowen Dunmore and her knights would ride south and west, to the coast. To Saltreach. Vane had said there was a second force there: three thousand Gloamed coming up the coast road from the drowned villages of the south. If they reached Saltreach, the free cities would fall. Elowen would stop them. Or slow them.

"With fifty knights," said Elowen. "And whatever the Saltreach militia can scrape together." She looked at her father. "I've had worse odds."

"No, you haven't," said Garrick.

"I'm your daughter. I'll invent some."

And the third front was Duskhold.

Lex. Antho. Wren. Seraphine, because someone had to read the wards on the crystal, and nobody alive could read them better. And Garrick.

"No," said Lex, when Garrick said it. "Your arm. You can barely lift your sword."

"I can lift it." Garrick looked at him steadily. "I held the gate at Caldris with three holes in me. I'll hold whatever needs holding at Duskhold." He put his good hand on Oathkeeper's hilt. "I swore an oath on this sword, lad. To stand on the steps. Wherever the steps are." He looked at the map, at the black mark in the grey country. "Those are the steps. You know they are."

Lex looked at him for a long time.

Then he looked at Elowen. The young captain was sitting very still beside her father. Her face was pale. But she didn't say anything. She just put her hand on his.

"All right," said Lex.

He found Brannoc in the forge, afterward, in the grey hour before dawn.

The big smith was at the anvil. Hedda was beside him, on a stool, with the *Book of Making* open on her knee, reading aloud. Not the Wellscript; she couldn't read that. Seraphine's translation, the copy she'd made in the Stillwater. *Ardent: the rune of held heat. The upstroke curves back like a flame folding over itself.* And Brannoc, with his tongue between his teeth and sweat running down his bald head, was striking it.

Into a sword blade. Plain steel. For the army.

The hammer came down. *Chang.* And something gold went into the steel. Faint. Wavering. Barely there. But there.

Brannoc stared at it.

"Did I—" he started.

"You did," said Hedda. She leaned over to look. "That's *Ardent*. Badly struck. The upstroke's crooked. It'll hold heat for about a week before it fades." She sat back. "But it's *there*."

Brannoc looked at the sword. Then up at Lex, standing in the doorway.

"Lad," he said. His voice was rough. "Lad, I did a rune."

"I saw."

"My first rune. Forty years at this anvil. *Forty years*." He laughed, a big shaky boom of a laugh. "And it's crooked."

"So was my first one," said Lex.

He came into the forge. He picked up a hammer from the rack, a hand-hammer, the one he'd used for eight years before Anvilsong. He took his place on the other side of the anvil. Across from Brannoc. The way they'd stood on the morning he left.

"Show me," said Brannoc. "Show me how to make it straight."

They worked until the sun came up. Striker and smith. Smith and striker. Hedda reading. Brannoc striking. Lex guiding. Sword after sword. Spearhead after spearhead. Arrowheads for Wren and every archer in the Unbound. Each one with a rune struck into it. Brannoc's crooked ones and Lex's straight ones, side by side on the rack.

When the sun came up over the mountains, there were forty blades on the rack. And every one of them was warm.

I've watched you grow. I've watched you forgive. I've watched you break Ceilings and close breaches and fall in love.]* A pause. *[I'm not his anymore, Lex. Not really. I'm yours. If he'll let me be.]*

Lex sat in the sun by the white Stone and didn't say anything for a while.

*Then you'll stay,* he thought. *I'll ask him. And if he says no—*

*[He won't,]* said Ember. *[I know him. I* am *him, a little. He won't.]* A pause, and something in the voice that might have been a smile. *[He always did let his children keep the things they found in the road.]*

---

Antho kissed Seraphine that evening.

Lex didn't see it. Wren did. She came and found him in the forge afterward, grinning so widely her face looked like it might split.

"*By the Stone*," she said. "In front of *everyone*. She was telling him about the ward-glyphs on the crystal, Vane's description of them, and he just—" She made a gesture. "Mid-sentence. She was in the *middle of a sentence*, Lex."

"What did she do?"

"Finished the sentence." Wren was laughing. "*Afterward*. She finished the sentence and then she pushed her spectacles up her nose and said *that was very unscientific* and then she kissed *him*."

Lex laughed until he couldn't breathe.

---

They left at dawn on the eleventh day.

The army went east first. A thousand strong, more or less. Fifteen hundred. Unbound farmers with Brannoc's crooked-runed swords. Wardens with their silver pins torn off. Sellswords and knights and Fenmoor folk and Ashbourne folk. Teodra Hale at the front on her big horse, roaring orders. Roole beside her, quiet and grim. Hedda in a cart, with a portable anvil and the *Book of Making* and her cudgel. Brannoc beside her, driving, with forty warm blades in the back.

Elowen rode south and west with her fifty knights, toward the coast and Saltreach. She stopped at the edge of the village and looked back at her father, standing on the green with his arm in its sling. She didn't say anything. She just raised her hand.

Garrick raised his.

And Lex and Antho and Wren and Seraphine and Garrick stood on Ashbourne's green beside the white Stone, with a storm-dragon lying in the grass beside them, and watched them all go.

Pell came running out of the forge as they were mounting. He had a bow on his back, a new one, Wren's old one, which she'd given him that morning, the one Lex had mended in Thistledown with the rune *Knit*. And a quiver of Brannoc's arrows. And a look on his face that Lex recognized, because he'd seen it in the mirror once, three months ago.

"I'm going with the army," Pell said. "With Teodra. As a scout. A Ranger." His face was very pale and very determined. "Brannoc said I could. If I stayed at the back."

"Pell—"

"You said it's not the end. What the Stone says. You said it's just what they'll let you be." Pell's chin went up. "Well, they let me be a Ranger now. So I'm going to *be* one."

Lex looked at him for a long time.

Then he reached out and ruffled the boy's straw-colored hair.

"Stay at the back," he said.

"I will."

"I mean it, Pell."

"I *will*." Pell grinned, the eager spaniel grin, and turned and ran after the army.

---

Old Mag was the last.

She came hobbling across the green on her stick as Lex climbed onto Ashka's back behind Antho. She stopped beside the dragon and looked up at him with her one good eye.

"Your mother," she said.

Lex looked down at her.

"When she came to me," said Old Mag. "The year after your father left. For the herbs. For the fever that wasn't a fever." She sniffed. "I told you she said you'd be a menace when you woke up."

"I remember."

"She said something else too." The old woman's eye was very sharp. "I didn't tell you. Didn't think you were ready." She leaned on her stick. "She said: *If they ever go looking for him, Mag, tell them he's not the only one who's lost. Tell them to leave a light on for both of us.*"

Lex went very still. On Ashka's neck in front of him, Antho had turned his head.

"I didn't know what she meant," said Old Mag. "Not then. I thought it was just grief. Talking." Her eye moved from Lex to Antho and back. "I'm not so sure now."

She turned and hobbled away across the green toward the forge, where a lantern was burning in the window in the middle of the day.

Lex and Antho looked at each other.

Neither of them said anything.

Then Ashka spread her great storm-colored wings, and leaped, and caught the wind, and climbed. Up over the white Stone and the lantern-lit windows and the grey fields of the Vale. Up past the Greyspine, where a tiny gold light still burned on a rock above a closed door. Up into the high cold river of wind that ran east across the world.

Wren stepped off the green into the air beside them and followed.

East. Toward the grey country. Toward the bruise on the horizon. Toward a black fortress with no doors, and a crystal at the edge of a chasm, and a man inside it who was holding on with nothing at all.
