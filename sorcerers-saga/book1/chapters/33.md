# The Unbinding

Ashka flew for two days without stopping.

She carried Lex and Antho on her back, one behind the other, Antho on her neck with his hands buried in her crest and Lex behind him with his arms around his brother's waist and Anvilsong strapped across his back. She flew west over the grey Blightmarch and then over the green hills of Caldor beyond it, high and fast, her great storm-colored wings beating in a steady rhythm that Lex felt through his whole body like a heartbeat. She didn't stop to eat. She didn't stop to rest. When night came, she flew through it, by starlight. When she grew tired, she found the wind, the high cold river of wind that ran west across Aldermere above the clouds, and rode it.

Wren flew beside them.

She couldn't keep up all the time. Her Windstep lasted hours now, not minutes, but not days. When she tired, she'd drop down onto Ashka's back behind Lex, and he'd hold her while she slept, a few hours, against his chest, with the wind howling past. And then she'd wake, and step off into the air again.

Seraphine had stayed with the column from Fenmoor. Two hundred people, walking west. Someone had to lead them, she said. Someone who could read maps and wards and knew which roads the Wardens used. And Ilsa, the new Healer, had stood beside her with her thin chin raised and said *I'll help*. Lex had looked at the two of them, the burned scholar with her cracked spectacles and the starved girl with her shovel-blistered hands, and thought they were the bravest people he'd ever seen.

"Go," Seraphine had said. She'd been looking at Antho. Not at Lex. "Go home. We'll be right behind you."

Antho had looked at her for a long time. Then he'd done something Lex had never seen him do. He'd stepped forward and kissed her on the forehead, quick and clumsy, and then turned away and climbed onto Ashka's back and not looked at anyone.

Seraphine had stood in the road with her hand on her forehead and her face bright red, and Wren had laughed so hard she'd had to sit down.

---

They came over the northern arm of the Greyspine on the evening of the second day, and saw the Vale.

It was grey.

All of it. The whole long green valley Lex had been born in. Grey. The fields, grey. The hedgerows, grey. The river, a sluggish grey ribbon winding through grey meadows. The chestnut woods on the lower slopes, grey skeletons. And over all of it, lying thick in every hollow and dip, rolling slow and heavy down every gully from the mountains, the Gloam.

It came from the north. From the mountains. Lex could see it. A great black river of it, pouring down out of the high Greyspine like smoke from a chimney, down the gullies, down the slopes, spreading out over the Vale like ink spilled across a page. Its source was high above them, in the face of the highest mountain, where a great black cleft split the rock.

The Hollowmaw.

"*No*," whispered Antho.

The Gloam was pouring out of the cleft. Not a trickle. Not threads, curling up against an invisible wall. A *flood*. Black and endless, as if the mountain itself were bleeding.

The ward had broken. The ring of charcoal stones. The twelve ward-nails. Ninety-four percent. All gone.

And above the cleft, on the dark face of the mountain, so small it was almost nothing, there was a light.

Gold. Steady. Burning.

Seren's lamp.

Still burning. On its rock above the Hollowmaw, facing out over the world. Through the flood, through the Gloam, through the grey. The only light in all the darkened mountains.

*Burns without fuel while the maker lives.*

Antho made a sound. It wasn't a word.

---

They landed on a ridge above the Vale, in the last of the light. Ashka was shaking with exhaustion. She folded her great wings and lay down in the grey heather and closed her golden eyes.

Antho slid off her neck and stood on the ridge, looking up at the Hollowmaw. At the black river pouring out of it. At the tiny gold light above it.

"I have to go up there," he said.

Lex had known he would say it. He'd known since the peddler on the road. He'd known since he saw the flood.

"Ashbourne," he said. "Brannoc. Everyone—"

"I know." Antho didn't look at him. "I know, Lex. Go. Go to Ashbourne. Save them." His jaw was tight. "But if I don't go up there and shut that door, there won't *be* an Ashbourne. Or a Thistledown. Or a Vale. It'll just keep coming. Forever. Until the whole south is grey." He finally turned his head. His grey eyes were very steady. The Keeper's Lantern glowed at his belt. "I keep the door, Lex. That's what I am. I said it on the silver road. I meant it."

"You'll be alone."

"I've been alone with it before."

"Not like this. Not with it this big."

"No," Antho agreed. "Not like this." Something moved at the corner of his mouth. "But I've got a Legendary lantern now. And Shade. And a dragon." He looked at Ashka, sprawled exhausted in the heather. "Once she's rested."

Lex stared at his brother.

He thought about the Hollowmaw. The cave full of lanterns. The breach in the cavern floor. Four years of his brother sitting on top of it, alone, holding it shut with charcoal glyphs and stubbornness, while the thing at the bottom of it whispered in his head every waking minute.

*Distance weakens the pull. The further he goes, the less purchase it has.*

"It'll be stronger," Lex said. "Close to it. The thing in you. The link."

"I know."

"Your Clarity—"

"Is ninety-two." Antho put his hand on the lantern. "Ninety-two, Lex. It's never been ninety-two." He took a breath. "And I'm not going up there to sit on it this time. I'm not going to *hold* it." His grey eyes went dark. Not black. The soft deep dark of Shade. The sky between the stars. "I'm going to *close* it."

"Can you?"

"I don't know," said Antho. "Malachi thought I might. If I listened hard enough." He looked up at the mountain. At the tiny gold light. "And I've got something to listen *for*, now."

Lex looked at the lamp. Small and gold and steady. A light left on for the lost.

"Go," he said.

---

Antho went at dawn.

Ashka had slept the night through. She woke in the grey morning and stretched and shook her great wings and looked at Antho, and Antho climbed onto her neck without a word. He looked down at Lex.

"Don't die," he said.

"You either."

"I'll try." His brother's mouth twisted. "If I'm not back in three days—"

"You'll be back."

"If I'm not." Antho looked at him steadily. "Don't come after me. Don't. Do you understand? If I'm not back, it means I couldn't close it, and the thing's got me. And if you come up there, it'll get you too." His voice was very quiet. "Promise me."

Lex didn't answer.

"*Promise me*, Lex."

"I promise," Lex lied.

Antho looked at him for a long moment. Then he laughed, very softly.

"You're a terrible liar," he said. "You always were. Even when you were four."

Ashka spread her wings and leaped off the ridge, and caught the wind, and climbed. Up and up, toward the high grey peaks, toward the black river pouring out of the cleft in the mountain, toward the tiny gold light burning above it. Lex watched them until they were a speck against the sky. Then until they were nothing.

Wren put her hand in his.

"Come on, Hammer," she said quietly. "Let's go home."

---

They came down into Ashbourne at noon.

There was no sun. The Gloam lay so thick over the Vale that the light was grey and flat and dim, like the light at the bottom of a pond. The road down from the foothills was grey dust. The fields on either side were grey. A grey crow sat motionless on a grey fence post and didn't move as they passed, and Lex saw that its eyes were white.

He'd walked this road a thousand times. Up to the high pastures with Brannoc to fetch the summer sheep. Down from the woods with a load of charcoal on his back. Up toward the mountains, three months ago, with Elric Thorne beside him and a hammer on his shoulder.

He didn't recognize it.

Then they came over the last rise, and he saw the village.

The roofs were grey. The walls were grey. The chestnut trees on the green were grey skeletons. The mill wheel on the river was still. The chapel steeple, which had been white, was grey. The Crooked Kettle was grey.

And on the green, there were people. Standing in rows.

The Quiet.

He knew them. Every one. Even from here, even grey, even motionless, he knew them. Farmer Tobbin, standing at the end of a row with his big farmer's hands hanging at his sides. Sister Agathe from the chapel. The Dunstan twins, side by side, the same height, the same grey faces, the same white eyes. Reeve Dunstan behind them. Gaffer Wim. The Tanners. Half the village. More than half. Standing in silent rows on the grey green, facing inward.

Facing the Stone.

The old cracked Lattice Stone, in the middle of the green, where Lex had been Kindled three years ago. Where Elric had cut his thumb and the gold had poured out of the crack and lit the whole valley. It was black now. Not grey. *Black*. The crack down its face was black, and black veins ran out from the crack across the stone like cracks across ice. Like the veins under Morvane's skin.

And standing beside it, with one hand resting on the black Stone, there was a man.

Tall. Very thin. All length and no width. In a long black coat buttoned to the throat, though the coat wasn't black anymore. It was grey. And the man's face wasn't pale anymore. It was *white*. Chalk-white. Bone-white. With black veins running under the skin. His slicked black hair had gone white too, and hung loose around his narrow skull.

And around his left forearm, from wrist to elbow, he wore a chain. Not silver-black anymore. Just black. Dripping. Like the overseer's whip at Fenmoor.

"*Vane*," whispered Wren.

That night, Ashbourne lit every lantern it had.

Every one. In every window of every house. The chapel's lanterns, all of them, blazing on the steps. The tavern's. The mill's. The little clay lamps from the lantern service, one for every family, set out on every sill. Brannoc lit the one in the forge window again, the one he'd lit on the morning Lex left. Mother Orla lit a dozen on the tables outside the Crooked Kettle and opened every barrel she had.

The village sat on the green in the warm gold light and ate and drank and wept and laughed, and stared at their panels, and told each other their new class names over and over, as if afraid they'd forget them. *Hearthkeeper. Stonemason. Shepherd-Warden. Wisewoman. Ranger.* The Gloam was still in the Vale. Still thick in the hollows outside the village, still pouring down from the mountains. The grey hadn't gone out of the fields or the trees. But on the green at Ashbourne, around the white Stone, it was gone. Burned away. And the lanterns held it back.

Lex sat on the edge of the green with his back against the wall of the forge and Wren beside him, and watched them.

"You did that," said Wren quietly.

"We did that."

"You did that." She leaned her head on his shoulder. "I just blew."

He laughed. It hurt; everything hurt; he had nothing left. Not a point of Authority. Barely a drop of mana. He felt like he'd hammered iron for three days and nights without stopping.

He looked up at the mountains.

At the Greyspine, dark against the night sky. At the highest peak. At the black cleft in its face, where the Gloam was still pouring out, a black river in the dark. At the tiny gold light above it.

Still burning.

*While the maker lives.*

And then, as he watched, something changed.

The black river pouring out of the cleft *faltered*.

He sat up. Wren sat up beside him.

The Gloam pouring down the mountain thinned. Wavered. Like a stream when someone upstream drops a stone in it. It slowed. It *stopped*.

And from the cleft of the Hollowmaw, high on the dark face of the mountain, came a light.

Not gold. Not Lex's light. Something else. Deep and soft and dark and somehow bright at the same time, like the night sky when the stars are out. Violet-black. Shimmering. It came out of the cleft in a slow steady pulse, like a heartbeat. Once. Twice. Three times.

And then the cleft was dark. And the black river on the mountain was only a black river, flowing downhill, its source cut off. Draining away. Growing thinner. Thinner.

And above the cleft, the tiny gold light burned on.

Lex stared at it.

*[Lex,]* said Ember. Its voice was shaking. *[Lex, that was Shade. That was the Well. And the breach—]*

*I know.*

Wren had her hand over her mouth.

All across the green, people were looking up at the mountain. At the place where the black river had been pouring out for a week, and now wasn't.

And Lex felt it. Through the Bloodbond. Faint and far away, so faint he almost didn't feel it. But there.

The cold deep pool of his brother. Very still. Very tired. And very, very quiet.

Not the bad quiet. Not the Quiet of the Gloam. The other kind. The kind that comes when something that has been screaming for eight years finally, finally stops.

*I keep the door,* said his brother's voice, in his head, faint as a whisper from the bottom of a well. *I kept it, Lex. I closed it. I'm coming home.*
