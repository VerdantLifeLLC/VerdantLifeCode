# The Lodestone Wakes

The lodestone woke on the fourth day north of the river.

They were crossing high moorland by then, a great rolling emptiness of heather and bog-cotton and grey stone under a vast grey sky, with the northern arm of the Greyspine rising ahead of them like a wall at the end of the world. No roads. No villages. No people. Only the wind, which never stopped, and the curlews, which cried and cried over the bogs, and the cold.

Antho stopped in the middle of a sheep track, very suddenly, and put his hand to his chest.

"It's moving," he said.

Lex turned. His brother had drawn the lodestone out on its old black cord and was holding it up in the grey light. It was glowing. Not the slow soft pulse it had held since the Barrow Throne: grey and green and blue twined together, beating like a sleeping heart. This was brighter. Stronger. And it wasn't hanging straight down from the cord. It was *pulling*. Leaning out sideways, away from Antho's chest, at an angle no stone on a string should hang, as if something far away had hold of it and was drawing it gently in.

North and east. Toward the mountains.

"It's pointing," said Seraphine softly. She'd come up beside him and was staring at it through her spectacles. "Like a compass needle. *I will always find my way home.*" She looked at Antho. "It's found the way."

Antho turned slowly on the spot. The lodestone turned with him, always leaning the same way, northeast, toward a notch in the mountains where two great grey peaks leaned together like old men sharing a secret.

"How far?" said Garrick.

Nobody knew.

*[Close,]* said Ember. *[It wasn't pulling yesterday. It's pulling today. That means we've crossed into range of whatever it's looking for.]* A pause. *[Ten days. Perhaps less.]*

Lex told them.

Nobody said anything for a moment. They all stood on the windy moor, looking at the little glowing stone pulling at its cord, pointing at the notch in the mountains.

Ten days. After weeks. After months. After fifteen years.

"Right," said Antho, very quietly. He let the stone fall back against his chest. It kept pulling, leaning out against his shirt like a dog on a leash. "Right. Let's go."

---

They walked. The mountains grew closer. The lodestone pulled harder.

And the silence among them grew.

It had started on the barge. Or maybe before that. Maybe in the Barrow-King's hall, with the dead king's white eyes moving slowly over all their faces. But it had grown on the long days north. Like frost spreading across a window, slow and quiet, until you looked up one morning and couldn't see through the glass.

Seraphine walked alone, mostly. Or with Antho. She didn't talk much anymore. She'd lost half her books in the Old Drains, and her notebook with three hundred and forty-six names written in it, and her spare spectacles, and something else, something harder to name. She walked with her shoulders hunched and her eyes on the ground and her half-empty pack clutched to her chest, and when anyone spoke to her, she flinched.

Antho stayed close to her. He didn't say anything about the token. He didn't need to. He walked beside her every day, and sat beside her every night, and when Elric was near, the shadows around his feet stirred and coiled like cats with their hackles up.

Garrick was carefully neutral. He was kind to Seraphine, the way he was kind to everyone. He was polite to Elric. He watched them both with his steady dark eyes and said nothing.

Wren watched Elric. All the time. Lex saw her doing it. When the old sorcerer went off into the dark in the evenings, Wren's eyes followed him. When he came back, she watched his boots, his hands, his face. She didn't say anything either.

And Elric was the same as ever. Kind. Patient. Dry. He taught Lex in the evenings, circles and structures and the careful folding of power, and Lex learned. He told stories around the fire, about Arion when he was young, about the Spire in the old days, about battles and storms and cities by the sea. He made them laugh, sometimes, even now.

And he watched Seraphine. Out of the corner of his eye. Very carefully.

Like a man watching a coin on the ground.

---

On the sixth night, Lex went to find her.

She was sitting on a rock a little way from the fire, alone, with her arms wrapped around her knees, looking north toward the dark mass of the mountains. Antho had gone to fetch water. Ashka was curled asleep in Seraphine's lap; the little dragon had taken to sleeping there lately, when Antho wasn't around.

Seraphine looked up as Lex came near. He saw her face go tight. Saw her brace.

"You want to ask me about the token," she said.

"Yes."

"I've told you. It isn't mine." Her voice was flat and tired. "I don't know how it got in my pack. I've never seen it before. I never had one." She looked back at the mountains. "You don't have to believe me. Nobody has to believe me. I wouldn't believe me."

Lex sat down on the rock beside her.

"Can I look?" he said.

She turned her head. "Look?"

"True Sight. At you. Properly. All the way down." He hesitated. "I don't know if it'll show anything. I don't know if it can see the kind of thing Elric's worried about. But I'd like to look. If you'll let me."

Seraphine stared at him for a long moment. Her dark eyes behind her spectacles were very wide.

"You're *asking*," she said.

"Elric says it's rude not to."

Something went across Seraphine's face that Lex couldn't read. Then she let out a breath, and straightened her shoulders, and lifted her chin.

"Look," she said. "Look as deep as you like."

He looked.

The gold rose behind his eyes, and the world flickered, and over her head a panel unrolled. He let True Sight go deeper. Deeper than he'd ever pushed it on anyone. Layer beneath layer, the way he'd looked into the Lattice Stone in Highcrest, the way he'd looked into his brother's breach-link in the Hollowmaw.

Lex didn't say anything.

Across the camp, on her rock in the dark, Seraphine sat with Ashka in her lap and looked north at the mountains and didn't turn around.

---

The storm came on the eighth day.

They saw it coming from a long way off: a wall of black cloud rolling across the moor from the west, lit from within by flickers of lightning, trailing grey veils of rain beneath it. The wind picked up until it was screaming across the heather, flattening it, tearing at their cloaks. The temperature dropped like a stone.

"Shelter!" Garrick shouted over the wind. "We need shelter!"

There wasn't any. There was nothing on the moor but heather and bog and stone. Wren found a hollow in the lee of a big granite boulder, a shallow scoop in the ground barely big enough for all of them, and they crowded into it with their cloaks over their heads as the first drops hit like thrown pebbles.

And then the storm was on them.

Lex had seen storms in the Vale. Summer thunderstorms that rolled down off the Greyspine and rattled the shutters and flooded the millrace. This was nothing like that. This was the sky falling. The rain came down in sheets, in walls, so hard it bounced back up off the ground and soaked them from below. The wind howled like a living thing. And the lightning—

The lightning was everywhere. Fork after fork of it, splitting the black sky from horizon to horizon, striking the moor all around them so close that Lex felt the hair on his arms stand up and tasted metal in his mouth. The thunder came on top of it, instantly, deafening, a sound like the world's biggest hammer striking the world's biggest anvil, over and over and over.

Elric was standing up.

He'd come out of the hollow and was standing on the open moor with his staff raised and his grey cloak whipping around him and the brass storm-charms on it blazing blue-white. His face was lifted to the sky. His eyes were closed. And the lightning was *coming to him*. Not striking him. Arcing down out of the black clouds and curving at the last instant, bending toward his staff, wrapping around it in crackling coils, and then fading, absorbed, swallowed.

*[He's drinking it,]* said Ember, startled. *[The storm. He's a Stormcaller. He's taking the power out of it.]*

*Why?*

*[I don't know. Because he can? Because it feels good?]* A pause. *[Because he's storing it for something.]*

And then Ashka went out.

Lex felt her go. She'd been curled in Antho's coat, pressed against his chest, shivering. And then suddenly she wasn't. She'd squirmed out and climbed up onto the top of the boulder, into the full fury of the wind and the rain, and was standing there with her wings spread, both of them, the whole one and the torn one in its silver frame, her head thrown back, crying out into the storm.

"*Ashka!*" Antho lunged after her. "*Ashka, get down! Come back!*"

She didn't come back.

The lightning came down.

It struck the top of the boulder. Struck *her*. A great white fork of it, out of the black sky, straight down onto the little blue-grey dragon with her wings spread, so bright that for a long moment Lex couldn't see anything at all.

Antho screamed.

When Lex's sight came back, Ashka was still standing on the boulder.

She was glowing. Every scale on her body was lit from within, blue-white, crackling, like a lantern full of lightning. Sparks were pouring off her spines in a continuous stream. Her golden eyes were blazing. And her wings—

Her wings were bigger.

Not much. But Lex saw it. They'd *grown*. The whole wing and the torn one both. The silver frame on the torn wing had stretched to fit, the way the runes Lex had struck into it let it stretch, and the membrane inside it was glowing, and the torn edges were—

The torn edges were *knitting*. Slowly. Lit from within by the lightning she'd swallowed. Not healed. Not whole. But closer. A little closer.

Ashka threw back her head and screamed into the storm. Not a chirp. Not a trill. A real scream, high and wild and fierce, the cry of a hawk, the cry of something that belonged in the sky.

And she leaped off the boulder, and spread her wings, and *flew*.

Not a glide. A flight. She beat her wings, both of them, the whole one and the silver-framed one, and she rose. Up off the boulder. Up into the wind. Up into the black screaming sky, into the rain and the lightning, a small blue-white shape blazing like a star, spiraling up and up and up until she was lost in the clouds.

Antho stood at the foot of the boulder with the rain streaming down his face, staring up at the place where she'd vanished.

"She's flying," he said. "She's *flying*."

The lightning flashed. And in the flash, high above them, Lex saw her. Turning in the storm. Wheeling. Riding the wind like a leaf, like a hawk, like she'd been born to it. Which she had.

Then she came down.

She came out of the clouds in a long spiraling dive, wings folded, falling like a stone, and at the last instant spread them wide and swooped low over the moor, so low her belly brushed the heather, and up again, and around, and landed on Antho's shoulder with a thump that nearly knocked him over.

She was bigger. Lex could see it now. Not cat-sized anymore. The size of a small dog. Her scales were still crackling with leftover lightning. Her golden eyes were blazing. And she was making a sound in her throat that Lex had never heard before, a deep rumbling purr, like a cat the size of a horse.

Antho was laughing. And crying. Both at once. He had his arms around her and his face pressed against her storm-wet scales, and he was saying something over and over that Lex couldn't hear over the wind.

Ashka turned her head. She looked at Lex across the hollow, through the rain. And a picture came into his head, sharp and bright and overwhelming. *Sky. Storm. Lightning in her belly like warm food. Antho's face, small and far below. FLYING.*

*Same blood,* she sent. *Your silver. Your wing-cage. Same blood. Thank you. FLYING.*

---

The storm passed in the night.

In the morning, the moor was washed clean and glittering, every blade of heather hung with drops of water, the sky a pale, scrubbed, delicate blue. They climbed out of the hollow soaked to the skin and stiff with cold. Garrick built a fire with wet heather and swore at it for half an hour until Lex took pity on him and lit it with a thought.

Ashka spent the whole morning in the air. She'd go up in long spirals, higher and higher until she was only a speck, and then come down in screaming dives, and pull up just over their heads, and go up again. Every time she landed on Antho's shoulder, she was a little more tired, and a little more pleased with herself, and a little bigger. Antho couldn't stop smiling.

And Lex sat by the fire next to Wren, drying his boots, and didn't say anything for a long time.

"You're thinking," said Wren. "I can see it. You get a little line between your eyebrows."

"I don't have a line."

"You do. Right there." She poked it. "What is it?"

He looked at the fire.

"Ashka," he said slowly. "She was hurt. For four years. The Wardens shot her out of the sky and tore her wing, and she couldn't fly. She'd try, and fall, and try again, and fall." He was quiet for a moment. "And then someone made her a frame. And the storm came. And she flew."

Wren was watching him.

"It's like the Ceiling," said Lex. "Isn't it. Someone tore her wing so she couldn't fly. Someone set a Ceiling so people couldn't climb. And she was always *meant* to fly. That's what she *is*. She just needed someone to—" He stopped.

"Make her a frame," said Wren quietly.

"Yes."

They sat in silence for a while. Across the moor, Ashka came screaming down out of the sky in another dive, and Antho laughed out loud.

"There's a Prerogative," said Lex. "In my panel. Locked. Ember told me about it in the Thornwood. After you hit twenty-four." He didn't look at her. "It's called Unbind. It breaks a Ceiling. Permanently. Lets someone climb past twenty-four. Re-rolls their class to something better."

Wren didn't say anything.

"I can't use it yet," said Lex. "I'm not strong enough. My Authority's too low. I don't know when I'll be strong enough." He turned and looked at her. "But when I am—"

"Don't."

He stopped.

"Don't make promises for me, Hammer." Wren was looking at the fire, not at him. Her jaw was tight. "I've heard a lot of promises. *Next year the mine will pay better.* *Next spring we'll get the roof fixed.* *Your da will be home by supper.*" Her voice was very steady. "I don't want promises. I've got enough of them. They're heavy to carry."

"Wren—"

"If you want to make a promise," she said, "make it for *you*. Not for me. You want to break the Ceiling? Fine. Break it. For Brannoc. For that girl in Highcrest. For everyone." She turned and looked at him, and her green eyes were very bright. "But don't do it for me. I don't want to be the reason. I don't want to be the thing you're carrying."

Lex looked at her for a long time.

Then he reached out, slowly, and took her hand. Her small, hard, calloused climber's hand. She didn't pull it away.

"All right," he said. "Not for you." He held on. "But *with* you. Is that all right?"

Wren looked down at their hands. Something moved at the corner of her mouth.

"That's all right," she said quietly.

They sat by the fire with their hands together, not saying anything, watching Ashka wheel and scream in the clean blue sky, until Garrick came back from the stream with the waterskins and coughed very loudly and very pointedly from twenty paces off, and Wren snatched her hand back and went the color of her hair, and Garrick grinned like a wolf.

They came over the last ridge on the evening of the tenth day.

The notch between the two grey peaks was a pass, high and narrow and cold, floored with old snow. They'd climbed it all afternoon, the lodestone pulling so hard now that it was humming audibly, a thin high singing note like a wet finger on the rim of a glass. Antho walked in front with the stone in his fist and his face set and Ashka gliding in slow circles overhead.

And at the top of the pass, the world opened up below them.

A valley. Long and narrow and deep, walled on every side by mountains, their peaks white with snow and gold with the last of the sunset. The valley floor was a single sheet of water. A lake. Long and narrow and shaped like a teardrop, filling the valley from wall to wall.

It was perfectly still.

Not calm. *Still*. Lex had seen calm water before: the millpond at Ashbourne on a windless morning, the black pools of the Drowned Barrows. This was different. There was not a ripple on it. Not a breath. Not a flicker. It lay in the bottom of the valley like a great slab of polished glass, and it held the sky.

All of it. Every cloud, every streak of gold and rose and violet from the setting sun, every first faint star. The mountains on every side, upside down, every peak and ridge and snowfield, as sharp and clear as the real ones above. There was no line between the water and the air. No horizon. You couldn't tell where the world ended and its reflection began. It was as if the valley held two skies, one above and one below, and the travelers on the ridge stood between them, suspended, floating in a great bowl of light.

And in the center of the lake, so far away it was only a faint shape in the dusk, there was nothing.

Just water. Just sky. Just reflection.

No island. No temple. Nothing.

"Mirrormere," whispered Seraphine. "The waters that hold the sky."

The lodestone in Antho's fist had stopped humming. It had gone silent. It hung on its cord, perfectly still, pointing straight down at the lake. At the center of the lake. At the place where there was nothing at all.

"*Only those of the blood can find it*," said Elric quietly behind them. "Everyone else who's looked for it has found nothing but an empty lake." He leaned on his staff. His face, in the last of the sunset, was very strange. "I've stood on this ridge before, you know. Three times. Over the years. And every time, that's all I saw." He was quiet for a moment. "An empty lake."

Lex looked at the lake. At the perfect stillness. At the two skies, and the nothing in the middle.

Then he looked at his brother.

Antho was looking back at him. His grey eyes were very wide. The lodestone was glowing in his fist, grey and green and blue.

*Two of the blood.*

"Together?" said Antho quietly.

"Together," said Lex.
