---

The moon came up as he crossed the valley floor, fat and white, a night off full. It lit the snow so bright he could have read by it. It lit the black rock of the mountain face. And it lit, as he came closer, the thing that was wrong with the Hollowmaw.

The Gloam was there.

Not much of it. Not the tide of oily black that had come pouring down the north road into Ashbourne. Just threads. Thin dark wisps of it, curling out of the great cleft like smoke from a smoldering fire, drifting down the mountainside and pooling in the hollows of the snow. Where they pooled, the snow went grey. A stunted pine at the base of the cliff had gone grey to the tips of its needles, and stood there in the moonlight like a tree made of ash.

But the threads didn't go far. That was the strange thing. They came out of the cleft, and drifted, and pooled, and then, at a certain distance from the mouth of the Hollowmaw, maybe fifty paces out, they *stopped*. As if they'd hit a wall. ---

The cleft was a cave, and the cave went deep.

It narrowed quickly from the great split in the mountainside to a passage barely wide enough for two men side by side, floored with rough rock and walled with black stone that glistened with frost. The moonlight faded behind him. The green light ahead grew stronger. And as he went deeper, he began to see things.

Charcoal marks on the walls. More of the barred-door glyphs, and others, dozens of others, scrawled across the rock in overlapping layers, some fresh and dark, some faded almost to nothing. Wards on wards on wards. Some of them were crossed out. Some had been written over three or four times, as if the writer had tried and failed and tried again.

And lanterns.

The first one hung from a spike driven into the wall at the first bend of the passage. An old tin lantern, dented and rusted, its glass cracked. Unlit. Then another, a few paces on. Then three together, hanging from a single spike like fruit from a branch. As he went deeper, there were more and more of them. Dozens of them. Scores. Hanging from spikes and pegs and outcrops of rock, from ropes strung across the ceiling, crowded together in the dark.

None of them were lit.

"*Little light, little light*," Antho whispered. "She sang it right into the dark. And I felt the thing stop. Just for a second. Like it was listening. Like it didn't know what that was, a song, a person singing into the dark without being afraid. And she looked back at me over her shoulder, and she said—" His voice broke completely. He pressed his face into Ashka's spines for a moment, and when he lifted it again, it was wet. "She said, *Look after your brother, Antho. I'll keep the door.*"

"No," Lex said.

"And she walked into it."

"*No*—"

"She walked into the dark. Still singing. I could hear her for a long time. Getting fainter. And the hole closed behind her. Like a door slamming. From the *other side*." Antho's hands were shaking. "And when it closed, everything it had been holding back came out at once. All the heat. All the fire that it had frozen. The kitchen just *went*. Everything. The whole house. That's why it burned so hot. That's why they never found her." He looked at Lex. "Because she wasn't there to find. She was on the other side of the door. She closed it on herself to keep the thing from getting to you."

Lex couldn't speak.

"She told me to look after you," said Antho. "And I couldn't. I couldn't even look after myself. The thing was still in me, still talking, still pushing, and I knew if I stayed it would get out again. It would go into you. Or into Brannoc, or Orla, or anyone. So I ran." He laughed, harsh and broken. "I told myself I was keeping you safe. Keeping the door, like she said. Maybe I was. Or maybe I was just a coward who couldn't stand to look at you." He shook his head. "I've never been able to work out which."

The cave was silent. Somewhere deep in it, the green light flickered and the dark pulse moved, slow as a sleeping heart.

Lex sat on the cold rock floor and looked at his brother.

Eight years. Eight years of hating him. Eight years of lying awake in the loft above the forge and remembering those black eyes in the smoke, and building the hate up, brick by brick, wall by wall, until it was a house he lived in. A house with no doors and no windows, where he didn't have to look at anything else. *Murderer.* *He went mad and burned his own mother.* *He walked away and he didn't look back.*

And all that time his brother had been up here in the dark, alone, with a monster in his head. Collecting lanterns.

"I've hated you," Lex said. His voice came out very small. "For eight years. I've hated you every day."

"I know," said Antho quietly. "I've hated me longer."

Lex looked at him. At the gaunt face and the wild beard and the grey, grey eyes.

Then he got up off the rock floor, and crossed the three feet of cave between them, and sat down next to his brother against the wall. Close enough that their shoulders touched.

Antho went rigid. Ashka chirped.

Lex didn't say anything. He didn't know what to say. He just sat there with his shoulder against his brother's shoulder, the way they'd slept in the loft under the eaves when he was small, Antho's warm back against his. And after a long moment, he felt Antho's shoulder, very slowly, begin to relax.

They sat in the dark together, surrounded by unlit lanterns, and neither of them said anything for a long time.
