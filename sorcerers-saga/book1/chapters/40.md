# Legacy of the Sorcerers

The first Festival of Sorcerers was held in Ashbourne, on the green, a year to the day after the Unbinding.

Nobody planned it. That was the thing Lex remembered best, afterward. Nobody sat down in a room and decided there would be a festival. Nobody sent out invitations. Nobody built a stage.

People just came.

They came up the south road from Millbridge in carts and on foot. They came down the north road from Thistledown, where the Last Hearth had a new sign and a new keeper and a waiting list for its rooms. They came over the Greyspine on the high passes, from the healing Blightmarch, from Fenmoor and the grey villages that were growing green again. They came from Highcrest, from Saltreach, from the Fells and the moors and the fens. Farmers and miners and smiths and millers and tavern-keepers. Wardens with no pins. Sellswords. Knights.

Unbound, most of them. People who had stood in front of a Stone in the last year and felt something break, and looked at their panels and seen a number with no end.

They came to Ashbourne because that was where it had started. The first Stone. The one with the crack down its face that glowed gold now, all the time, day and night. They came to stand in front of it and look at it. To touch it, some of them. To lay their palms flat on its face and close their eyes.

And on the evening of the anniversary, as the sun went down behind the Greyspine, they lit lanterns.

Every one they had. In every window. On every sill. Hung from the chestnut trees on the green. Set out in rows along the edge of the cobbles. Carried in hands. Thousands of them. Tin and brass and clay and paper and glass, in every size and shape, until the whole village of Ashbourne glowed gold in the blue dusk like a coal in a banked fire.

*A light left on for the lost.*

The Lantern Service, they'd always called it, on the green at Ashbourne. Every autumn. A little clay lamp in every window, for the ones who hadn't come home.

Now they called it something else.

Lex heard a child say it first. A little girl, five or six, sitting on her father's shoulders at the edge of the green with a paper lantern in her hands, staring at the glowing Stone. *Papa, is this the Sorcerers' Festival? The one with the brothers?*

And her father had looked up at her, and at the Stone, and at the forge across the green with its doors thrown open and twelve fires burning inside, and said: *Yes, love. This is the Festival of Sorcerers.*

And it stuck.

---

The forge had grown.

It wasn't Brannoc's forge anymore. Not only. It had swallowed the house next door, and the barn behind it, and the field behind the barn. There were twelve anvils now, in a long open hall with a roof of timber and slate and walls that could be thrown open in the summer to let the heat out. Twelve hearths. Twelve sets of bellows. And at every anvil, from dawn to dusk, someone working.

Students. That was what they were called, though most of them were older than Lex. Unbound smiths from every corner of Caldor who'd heard there was a place in the southern Vale where you could learn to strike runes. Where a capped smith who'd been level twenty-four for nineteen years would stand beside you at the anvil and show you the upstroke of *Ardent*, and tell you it was crooked, and show you again.

The Ashbourne Forge. Nobody called it a school.

Brannoc was its master. He hadn't wanted to be. He'd said so, loudly and often, in the first months. *I can barely read, lad. I've been striking runes for half a year. I don't know enough to teach anyone anything.* And then a young smith from Millbridge had come to the door, a girl of seventeen, Kindled the spring before as a *Smith*, Common, and Unbound at the Millbridge Stone in the autumn. And she'd stood in the doorway staring at the fire, and wouldn't leave until he let her pump the bellows.

Brannoc had looked at her for a long time. Then he'd handed her the bellows handle without a word.

He was level thirty-one now. *Runesmith*, Rare. He could strike *Ardent* and *Steadfast* and *Knit* and *True*, and they were almost straight. He still hummed hymns while he worked, badly. He still cooked eggs on the anvil.

Hedda Vorne ran the reading room. A small stone building beside the forge, with shelves on every wall and Seraphine's copy of the *Book of Making* chained to a lectern in the middle. Every student who came through the forge spent a month in Hedda's reading room before they were allowed near an anvil. Learning the runes. Their shapes. Their names. What they did. Hedda taught it with her one hand and her cudgel and a voice like gravel in a bucket, and her students were terrified of her and adored her, and she pretended not to notice either.

And Lex taught everything else.

How to listen to the iron. How to hear the flaws in it. How to shape the channel, and then let the fire flow. How to tell Flame the truth.

That last one was the hardest. Most of them couldn't. Most Unbound couldn't hear the Wells at all, any more than Seraphine could. They could strike runes, with the Lattice's help, the way Brannoc did. They could make good things. Warm things. But they couldn't reach down under the wheel to the river.

A few could. One in thirty, maybe. One in fifty. The girl from Millbridge was one. Lex had watched her sit in front of a cold hearth for nine days, the way he'd sat in front of Malachi's fire by the still pool, and on the tenth day the fire had leaned toward her.

She'd burst into tears.

---

Wren flew in from the north an hour after sunset, as the lanterns were all being lit.

Lex was standing in the doorway of the forge. He saw her coming. A small dark shape against the blue dusk sky over the Greyspine, getting bigger. Not on a dragon. On the wind. Arms spread, braid streaming, riding the cold mountain air down into the Vale.

She landed on the green in front of the forge in a gust that sent a hundred paper lanterns swinging. People scattered, laughing. A few of them cheered.

"*Show-off*," said Lex.

"*Always*," said Wren. She was windburned and grinning. "You should see the passes. There's snow on the Teeth already. And the Frostvault's got a new guardian. Bigger than the Rimewarden. Ugly." She pushed her braid off her shoulder. "I didn't fight it. I just looked. I'll take Pell up next week."

"You're going to take Pell into a Delve?"

"He's level twenty-two. He's ready." She looked at him. "*You* took *me* into a Delve at level nineteen."

"You took *me*."

"Details." She came up the step and stood in front of him. "Hello, Hammer."

"Hello."

She kissed him. In the doorway of the forge, in front of the whole festival. Someone wolf-whistled. Lex suspected it was Pell.

She'd evolved in the spring. In the Greyspine, on the high snowfields above the Hollowmaw, alone, in a blizzard. She'd come back three days later and walked into the forge and stood in the middle of the floor until Lex looked up from the anvil, and then said, very calmly, *Mistwalker. Epic.* And then burst into tears, and laughed, and kissed him, and gone to sleep for a day and a half.

The mirror on the silver road. The one that had gone dark instead of shattering. Waiting.

It didn't have to wait anymore.

---

His father arrived at midnight. Of course he did.

Lex had known he would come. Arion Vey had come to Ashbourne every month for a year, on the first of the month, without fail. Sometimes on a horse. Sometimes in a carriage, when the Queen insisted. Once, memorably, on Ashka, when Antho had flown down from the Hollowmaw to fetch him and the two of them had arrived together in a gust of wind that took the roof off the Crooked Kettle's privy.

He came every month, and he spent the day in the forge with Lex and Brannoc, and he spent the night in the Crooked Kettle with Brannoc, arguing.

They argued about everything. About runes. About whether *Ardent* was better struck with a light hammer or a heavy one. About the correct way to brew tea. About Lex. Mostly about Lex. Brannoc would say *the lad's the best smith I ever trained* and Arion would say *I agree entirely* and Brannoc would say *then why are you arguing with me* and Arion would say *I'm not arguing, I'm agreeing loudly* and they would both laugh until Mother Orla threw them out.

Lex didn't understand it. He'd stopped trying.

*[They like each other,]* Ember had said, after the third month. *[Very much. They just don't know how to say so. So they argue.]*

*That's ridiculous.*

*[It's very common among men of a certain age.]* A pause. *[Your father did the same with Elric, for forty years. Argued about everything. Loved him the whole time.]*

His father came down the north road at midnight, on foot, in a long dark coat, with his hands clasped behind his back. He stopped at the edge of the green and looked at it. At the lanterns. At the glowing Stone. At the forge with its twelve fires.

His face, in the gold light, was very still.

Lex went out to meet him.

"You're late," he said.

"*I'm always late*," said Arion. "*Your mother used to say I'd be late to my own funeral.*" He was looking at the lanterns. "*This is—*" He stopped. "*This is the Lantern Service. The one she used to bring you to. Every autumn.*"

"They're calling it something else now."

"*I heard.*" Something moved in his father's lean face. "*The Festival of Sorcerers.*" He shook his head slowly. "*The Queen wants to make it a royal holiday. All across Caldor. Every year. She asked my advice.*"

"What did you say?"

"*I said she should ask you.*"

Lex looked at him.

"*It's your festival*," said Arion quietly. "*Yours and your brother's. And Brannoc's, and Wren's, and Garrick's, and every Unbound in Aldermere.*" He looked at the glowing Stone in the middle of the green. "*I just broke the world. You mended it.*"

"We mended it," said Lex. "Three of us. At Duskhold."

"*You let me kneel in the middle*," said his father. "*That's not the same thing.*"

---

They walked around the green together, through the lanterns and the crowds.

Arion Vey was advisor to the Queen now. He hadn't wanted to be that, either. He'd said, loudly and often, in the first months, that he was the last person in Aldermere who should be advising anyone on anything, since the last time he'd decided he knew best, he'd torn a hole in the world. And Queen Lysandra had looked at him across the council table in the castle at Highcrest and said: *Which is exactly why I want you. You know what it costs to get it wrong.*

So he advised. He sat at the council table beside Daxian Roole, who was Warden-Commander now of an Order that didn't call itself the Wardens of the Lattice anymore. It called itself the Keepers. And the first thing Roole had done, on the first day, was send every Keeper in Caldor to every Stone in Caldor with orders to remove every flag. Every *WEIGHT* and every *DENY*. Every rigged Kindling. Three hundred years of them.

It had taken six months.

And the Queen had decreed that any Stone a Vey could reach would be Unbound. Every Ceiling. Every one. Lex and Antho had spent the year traveling to them, one at a time, on Ashka's back. Ashbourne. Fenmoor. Thistledown. Millbridge. Coldwater. Highcrest itself, the Lower Town Stone where the girl in the brown dress had been made a Laborer. A hundred Stones. Two hundred.

There were thousands. It would take years.

But the Queen had said: *Then it takes years. We've had three hundred years of the other way.*

"*She's very good*," said Arion, as they walked. "*The Queen. Better than I expected. Better than anyone expected.*" He was quiet for a moment. "*She reminds me of your mother, a little. The way she looks at you. As if she can see straight through to the thing underneath, and has decided, against all sense, that she likes it.*"

Lex didn't say anything.

They'd reached the Stone. The old cracked Stone in the middle of the green, glowing gold. Children were sitting around its base with their lanterns, staring up at it. One little boy was pressing his palm against its face, very solemnly, his eyes shut tight.

Arion stopped. He looked at the Stone for a long time.

"*She was Kindled here*," he said quietly. "*Your mother. Forty years ago. Washerwoman, Common.*" His voice was very soft. "*She told me about it once. In Saltreach. She said she cried for a week. She said she'd wanted to be a singer.*"

Lex turned his head.

"*It's in the record now*," said Arion. "*You Unbound her. With the dead. Three thousand one hundred and eighty of them.*" He reached out and laid his palm on the Stone, beside the little boy's. "*I looked. After. With the Root. I looked at her thread.*"

"What does it say?"

His father was quiet for a long time.

"*Listener*," he said at last. "*Rare. Level twenty-four. And then no cap.*" His mouth twisted. "*She was always a Listener. She just never had the class for it. The Lattice never knew.*"

He took his hand off the Stone.

"*And her thread*," he said, so quietly Lex almost didn't hear it, "*doesn't end.*"

Lex went very still.

"*The dead have threads that end*," said Arion. "*In the record. A last date. A last place. Like a line drawn under a column of numbers.*" He was looking at the Stone. Not at Lex. "*Hers doesn't end. It just—goes somewhere the Lattice can't follow.*"

"Father—"

"*I said I'd tell you when I was sure*." Arion turned and looked at him. His grey eyes, in the lantern-light, were very bright. "*I'm not sure, Lexander. Not yet.*" He swallowed. "*But I'm closer than I was.*"

---

Antho came at the darkest hour of the night.

Lex heard Ashka first. The great beat of her wings, overhead, in the dark. Then the roar of wind as she came down. And then a gasp from the whole festival, thousands of people looking up at once, as a vast blue-grey dragon dropped out of the night sky and landed on the green beside the glowing Stone, so lightly that not a single lantern was knocked over.

She was bigger again. Lex could swear it. She seemed to grow every time he saw her. The size of a barn now, maybe, with wings that could have shaded half the green. The silver frame on her left wing was so fine against the vast membrane that you could only see it when the light caught it, a thread of spider-silk on a sail.

Antho slid off her neck.

He looked different. A year in the Hollowmaw. A year of reading and listening and teaching. His black hair was cut short, and his beard trimmed close, and he wore a long dark coat that might once have been a Warden's but had no pin. The Keeper's Lantern hung at his belt, glowing steady. And his eyes, when he turned them on the crowd, were grey. Clear grey. With the soft deep dark of Shade underneath, like the sky behind the stars.

There were people behind him on Ashka's back. Three of them. Young. Wide-eyed. Clinging to the dragon's crest with white knuckles.

"Students," said Antho, when Lex came across the green to meet him. He looked faintly embarrassed. "From the Lantern Keep. They wanted to see the festival." He nodded at the three of them, climbing shakily down from Ashka's back. "Two of them hear Shade. One hears Tide, I think. Maybe." His mouth twisted. "It's hard to teach. Malachi made it look easy."

"Malachi had nine hundred years."

"Malachi," said Antho, "sent me a letter last month."

Lex stared at him.

"By goat," said Antho. "A goat walked into the Hollowmaw with a letter tied round its neck. All the way from the Stillwater. Across the Wastes." He shook his head. "I don't know how. I don't want to know how."

"What did it say?"

Antho reached into his coat and pulled out a scrap of paper. He handed it to Lex.

It was written in a spidery, cheerful, slanting hand.

*Deep one—*

*Well done. All of you. I heard the World Notice. The first wound closed. I danced around the pool. The goats thought I'd gone mad.*

---

Seraphine had come with them.

She'd been on Ashka's back too, Lex realized, behind the students. Royal Archivist of Caldor, in a plain grey dress with ink on her fingers and new spectacles on her nose, uncracked. The scars down the left side of her face had faded to pale silver lines, like frost on a window. She climbed down from the dragon and came across the green and put her hand in Antho's without looking at him, the way Wren did with Lex.

"We've been reading," she said. "In the Lantern Keep. The bronze cases from Qel'Varran. The ones Antho brought out." She pushed her spectacles up her nose. "There's a lot about Aurelian. Things that aren't in any other records. Things the Order destroyed everywhere else."

"Like what?" said Lex.

Seraphine looked at Antho. Antho looked at the ground.

"Like the fact that he's not just *in* the net," said Seraphine quietly. "He *is* the net. The root of it. His mind. His will. Holding it together. Holding the hunger down." She was quiet for a moment. "And the Listeners believed, the ones who wrote the bronze foils, that he'd been holding it so long he couldn't tell the difference anymore. Between himself and it."

*Whoever holds it, it will try to become.*

"And there's something else," said Antho. He looked up. "Something about the Lattice. About how it works. How it's *powered*." His grey eyes were very dark. "We don't understand it yet. We need more time. More records."

"But?"

Antho looked at Seraphine. She nodded slightly.

"But there's a word," said Antho. "In the oldest foils. Over and over. A word for what the Lattice takes from everyone who's in it. A little bit. From every spell. Every skill. Every level." He paused. "*Tithe*."

---

Lex didn't sleep that night.

He sat on the step of the forge, as the lanterns burned down and the festival went quiet and people curled up to sleep on the green under the stars. Wren asleep with her head in his lap. Ember quiet in the back of his head. His father asleep in the Crooked Kettle, having argued with Brannoc until Mother Orla threw them both out at the third hour. Antho and Seraphine asleep against Ashka's vast warm flank, with the three students from the Lantern Keep curled up nearby.

He sat on the step and looked at the Stone. Glowing gold in the middle of the green.

*[You're thinking,]* said Ember. *[You have the little line between your eyebrows.]*

*Wren says that.*

*[Wren is right about most things.]* A pause. *[What is it?]*

*Aurelian*, Lex thought. *Mam. The Tithe. Elric.* He looked up at the stars. *It's not over. Is it.*

*[No,]* said Ember quietly. *[I don't think it is.]*

*We mended the first wound. We freed Father. We Unbound two hundred Stones.* He looked down at his hands. At the faint gold glow that came off them, always, steady, like a hearth that would never go out. *And there are still thousands of Stones. And breaches we haven't found. And Elric, out there somewhere, with nine points of Father's Authority. And Kell, gone. And something waking up at the bottom of the net.*

*[Lex,]* said Ember, after a while. *[Do you remember what Brannoc said? On the morning you left?]*

*He said a lot of things.*

*[He said there's always an unless.]* A pause. *[You just have to find it.]*

*[Yes,]* said Ember. *[And you've found every one so far.]* A pause, and something in the gold voice that was very nearly a smile. *[Little smith.]*

---

Very far to the east, beyond the healing Blightmarch, beyond the place where Duskhold had stood and was now only a ring of black crumbling stone around a patch of floor where nothing grew, beyond the end of every map in Seraphine's collection, there was a land the old texts called the Sundered Lands.

Nobody went there. Nobody had gone there in nine hundred years. It was where the war of the sorcerer-kings had been fought hardest, and where the world had nearly broken, and where the Veil grew thin and strange and the ground did not always stay where you left it.

An old man was walking through it.

He walked slowly. He had no staff. He leaned on nothing. His grey cloak hung off him like a sack, and the brass storm-charms on it were dull and tarnished, and some of them had fallen off. He had walked a very long way. He had not slept much. He had not eaten much. He had not spoken to anyone in a year.

He had nine points of stolen Authority, held very close, deep inside him, like a coal swallowed whole.

And he had a voice.

It had started a month ago. Faint, at first. Like a whisper heard through a wall. In the place under the air, where the stolen gold burned. He'd thought at first that it was the thief. The hunger. The thing that had filled Corvin Morvane and spoken through Anthoren Vey and wanted *him* so badly, Antho had said, more than it wanted most people.

It wasn't the thief.

It was a man's voice. Old. Very old. Patient, and clever, and kind. The kindest voice he'd ever heard. And it knew his name.

*Elric Thorne*, it said, in the dark, in the Sundered Lands, under a sky with too many stars in it. *Elric. You've stood beside the blood your whole life. Beside Arion. Beside his sons. Always beside. Always second.*

The old man stopped walking.

*I know what that's like*, said the voice. *I had brothers too. Six of them. I was always the one they stood beside.* A pause. *Would you like to stand above it, instead? Just once?*

The old man stood very still in the strange dark land.

*There's something here*, said the voice gently. *Something I made, a long time ago, and broke, and hid. A key. In three pieces.* A pause. *The first piece is very close. Under your feet, almost. In the ruin on the hill.*

The old man looked up.

There was a hill. He hadn't noticed it before. Low and round and bare, with a ruin on its crown. Grey stone, very old, the walls half-fallen. And carved over its broken doorway, worn almost smooth by nine hundred years of wind, a symbol.

An open hand. And above the palm, not a flame.

A keyhole.

The old man looked at it for a very long time.

He thought about a smith's apprentice standing over him in a black corridor, holding his staff. *I'm not going to kill you, Elric. But I'm not going to forgive you either.* He thought about a young man burning a black mark off his palm with fire, so he wouldn't have to wear a leash. He thought about an old friend in a crystal, with grey eyes, asking *was it worth it?*

*Not yet*, he'd answered.

He climbed the hill.

At the Festival of Sorcerers in Ashbourne, in the darkest hour before dawn, three people with Root in their blood woke at the same moment.

Lex, on the step of the forge, with Wren's head in his lap.

Antho, against Ashka's flank, with Seraphine asleep beside him.

And Arion Vey, in a narrow bed in an upstairs room of the Crooked Kettle, with a yellowed letter from his wife folded against his chest.

They woke because a panel had opened in front of their eyes.

Not silver. Not gold. Black. Edged with something that was neither, a color none of them had ever seen in the Lattice before. And on it, a single line.

On a flat rock high on the dark face of the Greyspine, above a cleft that blazed with a hundred lanterns, a little clay lamp burned on, gold and steady, facing out over the world.

And very far down, under the net, under the Wells, under everything, in the dark before the first word, someone was singing.

*Little light, little light, burning low.*

*Keep the door for the ones who go.*

*If the dark comes calling, don't you mind.*

*A lantern's lit for the ones behind.*
