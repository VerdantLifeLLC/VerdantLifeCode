# The Leafbound Archive

The house was made of a living tree.

That was the first thing Lex understood as he came up the silver path behind Wren. It wasn't a house built *in* a tree, or *of* wood. It was a tree, one of the great black thorns of the forest, that had been persuaded long ago to grow into the shape of a house. Its trunk had split near the ground into a dozen great limbs that curved up and out and back together again, like the ribs of a basket, and between the limbs, smaller branches had been woven into walls, and the walls had been coaxed to leaf. Silver leaves, thick and glossy, overlapping like shingles. There were windows where the branches parted, round ones, with something thin and clear stretched across them like the inside of an eggshell. There was a door, an arch of living wood twined with ivy, with a little round step of stone in front of it.

And in the door, exactly as Garrick had said, there was a young woman with a crossbow.

She was thin, and not very tall, with a pale narrow face and a long nose and a great untidy mass of dark brown hair that had been pinned up at some point and was now mostly coming down. She wore the remains of a long grey robe, the kind of robe Lex had seen on the Wardens at Thistledown, but with the silver lattice-pin torn off the breast and the hem cut short and patched with leather. She had spectacles on: little round ones, with wire rims, perched on the end of her long nose. And behind the spectacles, her eyes were enormous and dark and absolutely terrified.

The crossbow was pointed at Wren's chest. It was shaking so badly that Lex wasn't sure it would hit anything if she fired it.

"Stay where you are," said the young woman. Her voice was high and thin and very precise, like a schoolteacher's. "I mean it. I know how to use this."

"No you don't," said Wren.

"I—I do. I've read the manual."

"You've got the bolt in upside down."

The young woman looked down at the crossbow. Her face went from white to red. She looked back up.

"It's—it's a *deterrent*," she said.

Wren held up her empty hands. "We're not here to hurt you. The Thorn Mother sent us. She said you read the old script."

The young woman's eyes flicked past Wren. To Garrick, with his grey hair and his scarred face and the frost-edged sword at his hip. To Lex, with the hammer on his shoulder. To Antho—

And her whole face changed.

Lex saw it happen. Saw recognition go through her like a lightning strike. Her mouth fell open. The crossbow jerked up, away from Wren, and swung toward his brother.

"*You*," she breathed. "You're—you're *him*. The Ashbourne warlock. Anthoren Harrow. I've seen your file. I've *read your file*. Eleven Wardens. Four retrieval teams. *Corrupted unique, breach-linked, unstable*—"

"Hello," said Antho mildly.

"*Stay back!*"

Ashka, on Antho's shoulder, sneezed.

It was a small sneeze, but she was a storm-drake, and a small sneeze from a storm-drake came out as a sharp crackling burst of blue sparks, and one of the sparks drifted across the gap between them on the evening air and landed on the end of the young woman's long nose.

She yelped. She dropped the crossbow. It hit the stone step and went off with a *thwack*, and the bolt, upside down, shot sideways into the leafy wall of the house and stuck there quivering, and the young woman stumbled backward over her own robe and sat down hard in the doorway with her spectacles askew.

There was a long silence.

"Well," said Garrick. "That's one way to do it."

---

Her name was Seraphine Ilvane, and she had been, until two winters ago, a junior archivist in the Spire of the Wardens of the Lattice.

She told them this sitting at a long table in the main room of the leaf-house, with a cup of something hot clutched in both hands and a fresh red mark on the end of her nose where Ashka's spark had landed. She still didn't let Antho out of her sight. Every time he moved, her eyes flicked to him. But she'd stopped shaking, mostly, and she'd let them in, and made them all something to drink from a pot of dried leaves that smelled of mint and honey, and now she was talking very fast, as if she hadn't had anyone to talk to in a long time and was afraid they'd leave before she'd said everything.

The room was extraordinary. It was round, with a high domed ceiling where the living branches met and twined together overhead. The walls were shelves: shelves grown out of the living wood itself, curving, uneven, following the grain of the branches. And every shelf was full of books.

Old books. Ancient books. Books bound in leather gone black with age and books bound in wood and books that were just bundles of parchment tied with cord. Scrolls in cases of carved bone. Tablets of grey stone with Wellscript cut into them. Hundreds of them, thousands, crowding every shelf from floor to ceiling, spilling onto the floor in stacks, piled on the table among cups and candle-stubs and inkpots and sheets of paper covered in tiny, cramped, neat handwriting.

"The Leafbound Archive," said Seraphine, gesturing around her with her cup. "One of the original Founders' libraries. There were seven, once. This is the only one left. The others were destroyed, or lost, or—" Her mouth tightened. "Or taken into the Spire's sealed vaults, where nobody's allowed to read them."

"And you found it," said Lex.

"I was *looking* for it. I'd read about it. In a footnote. In a commentary on a commentary on a translation of a Founders' codex that nobody had opened in a hundred and fifty years." Seraphine pushed her spectacles up her nose. "Nobody reads footnotes. That's the great secret of archives. Everything important is in the footnotes."

*[I like her,]* said Ember.

"I was a Lattice Scribe," Seraphine went on. "That's my class. Rare grade. It means I can read the Lattice a little more deeply than most people. Not as deeply as a senior Warden. But I can read *records*. Old Lattice records, the kind the Spire keeps in its archives. Kindling ledgers, sanction rolls, incident reports." She looked down into her cup. "That's what I did for six years. Read old records and copied them and filed them. It was very boring. I loved it."

"Then why did you run?" said Garrick.

Seraphine was quiet for a moment.

"Because I read something I wasn't supposed to read," she said.

---

"The Kindling ledgers," she said. "Every Kindling ever held, at every Stone in Aldermere, is recorded in the Lattice. Who was Kindled. Where. When. What class they received. What grade." She turned her cup around in her hands. "The Spire keeps copies. Hundreds of years of them. I was assigned to cross-reference the ledgers from the last century, for a census. Very boring work. Nobody else wanted it."

"And?" said Wren.

"And the numbers were wrong." Seraphine looked up. Behind her spectacles, her dark eyes were very bright. "I don't mean a few errors. I mean *systematically* wrong. If you take every Kindling in Caldor in the last hundred years and sort them by family, noble against common, the distribution of grades is impossible. Commoners get Common and Uncommon classes. Nobles get Rare and Epic. Not mostly. *Always*. Ninety-eight percent of Rare-grade classes in the last century went to children of noble houses or Warden families." She set down her cup. "That's not blood. Blood doesn't work like that. Talent doesn't work like that. That's someone *choosing*."

The room was very quiet.

"The Wardens rig it," said Garrick slowly.

"The Wardens rig it. There's a flag in the Lattice. A permission. Senior Wardens can set it on a child before their Kindling. It doesn't guarantee a good class, but it *weights* it, like loaded dice." Seraphine's mouth twisted. "Noble houses pay for the flag. It's very expensive. It's been going on for three hundred years."

Lex thought of the Ashbourne green. Of the morning of his own Kindling, his heart pounding, half the village watching. Of Pell, fifteen, asking hopefully *do you think I'll get something good?*

Of Wren, standing in the clearing at the heart of the Thornwood, looking at a panel only she could see.

He felt the heat in his chest go up like a forge with the bellows going.

"Three hundred years," Wren said. Her voice was very flat. "Every kid in every village in Caldor. For three hundred years."

"Yes."

"And nobody ever noticed?"

"People noticed." Seraphine looked at her. "People always notice. They say *born to the Ceiling*. They say *some folk are dreamers and some are doers*. They say *the Lattice knows best*." She shrugged her thin shoulders. "They just didn't have the ledgers."

"And you did," said Lex.

"And I did." Seraphine took off her spectacles and rubbed her eyes. "I didn't know what to do. I was twenty-seven. I'd spent my whole life in the Spire. I believed in it. I believed the Lattice was the Lady's gift and the Wardens were its stewards and everything was ordered and fair." She put her spectacles back on. "So I did the stupid thing. I wrote a report. I took it to my supervisor."

"What happened?" said Antho.

It was the first thing he'd said since they sat down. Seraphine's eyes jumped to him. For a moment she looked frightened again. Then something else came into her face. Something almost defiant.

"He thanked me," she said. "He said it was very thorough work. He said he'd pass it up to the High Warden personally. And that night, someone came to my room in the Spire with a knife." She lifted her chin. "I wasn't there. I'd gone to the archives to check a reference. I always go to the archives at night; it's quieter. When I came back, my door had been forced and my bed had been cut to ribbons."

"*Lady*," breathed Wren.

"I took what I could carry and ran. Some codices. My notes. A copy of the ledgers." Seraphine gestured at the stacks of paper on the table. "And I came looking for the one place in Aldermere where I thought nobody would ever follow me." She looked around at the shelves of living wood, the silver leaves rustling at the round windows. "It took me three months to find it. The Thorn Mother nearly ate me. And then I spoke to her in Wellscript, and she stopped." Seraphine smiled faintly. "She was so surprised. Nobody had spoken to her in the old script in four hundred years. We've been friends ever since. Sort of. In the way that one can be friends with a nine-hundred-year-old memory-eating tree."

---

"You came for something," Seraphine said later, when Garrick had gone outside to see to the perimeter and Wren had gone with him, and only Lex and Antho remained at the long table with her. "The Thorn Mother wouldn't have sent you otherwise. She doesn't send people to me. She keeps me secret." She looked between the two of them. "Two of you. Brothers. I can see it. You have the same jaw." Her eyes narrowed. "And you're both on the Spire's wanted rolls. Five hundred crowns each. I heard the notice read in Thornhollow market a month ago, when I went out for flour." She paused. "*Lex and Anthoren Harrow. Wildcasters.* What are you?"

Lex looked at Antho. Antho looked back. Then, slowly, without a word, Antho drew the lodestone out of his collar on its old black cord, and laid it on the table between them.

It was glowing. Faintly. Grey and green, twined together, pulsing slowly like a heartbeat.

Seraphine went absolutely still.

She stared at the stone. She leaned closer to it, very slowly, the way you'd lean toward a sleeping animal. Her spectacles slid down her nose and she didn't push them back. Her lips moved. She was reading, Lex realized. Reading the tiny spiral of Wellscript that covered the stone, which he could only see with True Sight, and which she apparently could see without it.

"*Lodestone of Return*," she whispered. "*Bound to*—" She stopped. Her face went white. "Bound to *Arion Vey*."

She looked up at them.

"Arion Vey," she said. "Warden-Architect Arion Vey. He's in the rolls. He's one of the last entries before the Vhessa incident. And then he just—stops. No death record. No discharge. Nothing. As if he walked off the edge of the world." Her eyes went from Lex to Antho and back. "*Vey*. You're not Harrows. You're *Veys*."

"Apparently," said Antho.

"Do you know what that *means*?"

"Not really," said Lex. "We were hoping you might."

Seraphine stared at them for a long moment. Then she pushed her chair back from the table, got up, and walked to one of the shelves. She ran her finger along the spines of the books there, muttering under her breath, then pulled one out: an enormous book, bound in black leather gone grey with age, so heavy she had to carry it in both arms. She brought it back to the table and set it down with a thump that made the cups jump.

She opened it.

The pages were vellum, thick and yellowed and covered in Wellscript. And in the middle of the first page, drawn in faded gold ink, was a symbol.

An open hand, palm up. And above the palm, a single flame.

"*The hand that holds the light out for others*," Seraphine read aloud. "The mark of the House of Vey. The mark of the first Founder. The one who designed the Lattice." She looked up at them. "His name was Aurelian. Aurelian Vey."

---

She talked for a long time. Lex listened, and Antho listened, and Ember listened, and once or twice Ember spoke up in Lex's head to add something or correct something, and Lex repeated it, and Seraphine stopped and stared at him and said *how do you know that?* and he said *I have a voice in my head* and she said *of course you do* and went on.

Nine hundred years ago, the world had almost ended. The war of the sorcerer-kings, the Sundering. Seraphine told it the way Elric had told it, but with more names and more dates and more terrible details. Mountains moved. Seas boiled. And at the end of it, out of the ruin, came seven great mages. The Founders. And the greatest of them was Aurelian Vey.

"He was a Listener," Seraphine said. "That's what they called the old mages. Before the Lattice. They didn't have classes or levels. They *listened* to the world. To the elements. Fire and stone and water and wind. They heard what the world wanted to do, and they asked it nicely, and sometimes it did it." She turned a page. "Aurelian was the greatest Listener who ever lived. And he looked at what the sorcerer-kings had done, and he decided that the world couldn't be trusted to listen to anyone anymore. So he built the Lattice."

"To ration magic," said Lex. "So no one could get too powerful."

"That's what the histories say." Seraphine hesitated. "That's what *most* of the histories say."

"What do the rest say?"

Seraphine turned another page. Then another. She found what she was looking for and turned the book around so they could see it, though they couldn't read it.

"This is Aurelian's own account," she said. "Or a copy of it. The oldest copy that exists. And it says that the Lattice had *two* purposes." She traced a line of Wellscript with her finger. "*To bind the will of men, and to bind the hunger beneath.*"

Antho went very still.

"*The hunger beneath*," he repeated quietly.

"I don't know what it means," said Seraphine. "I've spent two years trying to work it out. There are references all through the Founders' records. *The hunger*. *The Hollow*. *The thing that woke in the Sundering*. But they never say what it is. Only that the Lattice is a *lid* as well as a *net*. That it holds something down." She looked up. "Something that's been trying to get out ever since."

Lex looked at his brother.

Antho was staring down at the open book, at the faded gold hand and flame, at the line of Wellscript under Seraphine's finger. His face was very pale. His hands, resting on the table, had curled slowly into fists.

"I know what it is," he said.

Seraphine stared at him.

"I've been listening to it," said Antho, "for eight years."

---

It was a long time before anyone spoke again.

Seraphine sat with her hand still resting on the open book, staring at Antho. Antho sat with his fists on the table, staring at nothing. Ashka had crept down off his shoulder and into his lap and was pressed against his stomach, chirping very softly.

"Breach-linked," Seraphine whispered at last. "That's what your file said. *Breach-linked, unstable*. I thought it was jargon. I thought it meant your power was unstable." She swallowed. "It means you're *tied* to it. To the hunger. To the thing under the lid."

"Yes."

"And it talks to you."

"It never stops."

Seraphine looked at him for a long moment. And Lex saw the fear go out of her face, slowly, the way frost goes out of a window when the sun comes up. In its place came something else. Something he couldn't name. Pity, maybe. Or wonder. Or something more complicated than either.

"Eight years," she said quietly. "Alone with that."

"Seven and a half, really," said Antho. "The first six months, I mostly screamed."

Seraphine laughed. It burst out of her, startled and shocked and slightly hysterical, and she clapped a hand over her mouth. "I'm sorry. I'm so sorry. That isn't funny."

"It's a little funny," said Antho. Something moved at the corner of his mouth. "You have to laugh. Or you'd just keep screaming."

They looked at each other across the table. And Lex, watching them, had the odd feeling that something had just happened that he didn't quite understand.

*[Hm,]* said Ember thoughtfully.

*What?*

*[Nothing. Just an observation.]* A pause. *[Your brother hasn't looked at anyone like that since we met him. Not even you.]*

---

"Avaloria," said Lex. "That's where we're going. That's where our father is."

They'd moved on from the hunger. None of them wanted to talk about it anymore, Antho least of all. Seraphine had made more tea. Garrick and Wren had come back in from the perimeter, Wren very carefully not looking at Lex, Lex very carefully not looking at Wren, and Garrick looking at both of them with an expression of deep and silent amusement. Now they were all sitting around the long table in the round room, with candles lit against the dark and the silver leaves rustling at the windows.

"Avaloria." Seraphine had gone very still again. "You're sure?"

"His letter said so. The way to him runs through Avaloria." Lex nodded at the lodestone. "And that's what calls the Way. Three songs. We've got two."

"The Temple of Avaloria," said Seraphine slowly. She got up again and went to the shelves and came back with another book, smaller this time, bound in green leather. She opened it to a page with a drawing on it: an island, in the middle of a lake, with a building on it. Columns and domes and towers, impossibly delicate, drawn in faded silver ink. "One of the Seven Temples. The greatest of them. The Founders built it as the Anchor."

"The Anchor?"

"The place where the Lattice is tied to the world. Every net needs an anchor. A place where the threads are knotted fast, or the whole thing would drift." She traced the drawing with one finger. "The Spire has one of the Seven, the Great Stone at the top. That's how the senior Wardens see into the Lattice. But Avaloria is older. Deeper. Avaloria is where the Root is." She looked up at Lex. "The records say only those of the Founder's blood can enter it. *Vey* blood. Everyone else who's looked for it has found nothing but an empty lake."

"Two of the blood," said Antho. "The Way opens to two of the blood."

"And your father went there." Seraphine looked from one to the other. "Sixteen years ago. After the Vhessa Breach." She bit her lip. "I've seen the requisition record. It was one of the things I copied, before I ran. Warden-Architect Arion Vey signed out the *Avaloria Codex* from the sealed vault, three days after he came back from the east. He never returned it. Two months later, he vanished from the rolls."

"What's the Vhessa Breach?" said Garrick.

Seraphine looked at him. Then she reached into the stacks of paper on the table, sorted through them with quick, practiced fingers, and pulled out a single sheet. She handed it to Garrick.

It was a map. A map of Aldermere, drawn in the Spire's precise style, with the kingdoms marked in faint grey lines. And scattered across it, in red ink, there were marks. Little red crosses. Lex counted them from where he sat. Twenty. Twenty-five.

Most of them were in the east, in Vhessa, clustered thick around a place on the eastern border that someone had marked with a much larger cross and the word *ORIGIN*. But there were others. Scattered across Caldor. Up in the north. Down in the south. One, Lex saw with a jolt, high in the Greyspine, exactly where the Hollowmaw would be.

"Breaches," said Seraphine quietly. "That's the Spire's private map. The one they don't show anyone. Every known breach in the Lattice, every place the Gloam has come through." She tapped the large cross in the east. "That's Vhessa. The first one. The biggest. It opened sixteen years ago. And every single one since has opened *after* it. They're spreading. Like cracks spreading out from a hammer-blow on glass."

Garrick was staring at the map. His scarred face had gone grey.

"I fought in Vhessa," he said quietly. "Twenty years ago. In the border wars. There were villages there. Towns. A whole province." He traced the area around the origin cross with one thick finger. "There's nothing there now?"

"There's the Blightmarch," said Seraphine. "That's what the Spire calls it. The whole eastern province, gone grey. Nobody goes in. Nobody comes out." She paused. "Except—"

"Except?"

Seraphine hesitated. Then she reached into her papers again and pulled out a letter. Old, creased, the seal broken.

"Two Wardens went east to investigate the breach when it first opened," she said. "Two of the most senior in the Order. One was Warden-Architect Arion Vey." She looked at Lex and Antho. "The other was Warden-Commander Morvane."

She unfolded the letter.

"This is from Commander Morvane's aide," she said. "Written to a friend in the Spire archives, about ten years ago. My predecessor kept it. I don't know why. I found it in his desk after he retired." She began to read, her voice very quiet: "*The Commander has not slept in three months. He does not eat. He does not seem to need to. He is very pale now, paler than any man should be, and when he walks through the camp, the men fall silent, and some of them do not speak again for days. He has begun gathering some of them around him. He calls them the Quiet. They stand very still and watch him and say nothing, and their eyes have gone pale like his. Yesterday he told me that he has seen the truth of the world, and that it is beautiful, and that it is silence. I do not think he is himself anymore. I do not know who he is. I am afraid.*"

She lowered the letter.

"The aide was reported dead two weeks later," she said. "Gloam exposure. And Commander Morvane was given command of the eastern containment, and has held it ever since. He never comes to the Spire. He sends reports. They're always very calm. Very orderly." Her voice dropped. "And the Blightmarch keeps getting bigger."

*[Quiet,]* said Ember softly. *[The Gloamed miller. That's what it kept saying. "Quiet. Be quiet. All be quiet."]*

Lex's skin had gone cold all over.

"He's in it," he said. "Isn't he. Morvane. He went into the breach with our father, and something happened, and he came out—*full* of it. Like the miller. Like the bear. Only all the way."

"I don't know," said Seraphine. "I don't know anything for certain. I just have papers." She looked down at the letter in her hands. "But I think whatever your father found in Vhessa, Commander Morvane found it too. And I think it found him."

---

Lex found the drawing an hour later.

He'd been wandering along the shelves while the others talked. He couldn't read most of the books. But he could see the Wellscript on their spines, the way he saw runes in iron, and every so often a word would jump out at him. *Root. Anchor. Seal. Blood.* And then, on a low shelf near the floor, half-hidden behind a stack of scrolls, there was a slim book bound in grey leather, with the open hand and the flame stamped on its spine in faded gold.

He took it out. He opened it.

It was full of drawings. Diagrams. Not of the Lattice, or of Temples, or of breaches. Of *tools*. Hammers and tongs and anvils and chisels, drawn with loving, careful precision, every one of them covered in runes. And on the facing pages, in tiny Wellscript, notes. He couldn't read the notes. But he could read the runes on the tools. *Ardent. Steadfast. Homeward. Bind.* The same runes he'd struck into Anvilsong and Oathkeeper and the ward-nails. And on the last page, a hammer. A big two-handed hammer, like Anvilsong, with a flat striking face and a tapered wedge. And on its head, four runes.

Three he knew. The fourth was an open hand, held out in the dark.

He brought it to Seraphine.

She took it from him and looked at the cover, and her eyes went wide. She turned the pages very slowly. When she got to the last one, the hammer, she stopped.

"Where did you find this?"

"On the bottom shelf. Over there."

"I've never seen it before. I've catalogued every book in this archive. Twice." She turned it over in her hands. "It's a smith's book. A Founder's smith's book. *The Book of Making*, it says. *As written by the hand of*—" She stopped. Looked up. "*As written by the hand of Aurelian Vey, in the third year of the Weaving, for those of his blood who come after and have the gift of the forge.*"

Lex stared at her.

"He was a smith," Seraphine whispered. "Aurelian. The first Founder. He wasn't only a Listener. He was a *maker*. That's how he wove the Lattice. He didn't cast it. He *forged* it." She turned to the last page again. To the hammer with the four runes. She touched the fourth one, the open hand. "This is the mark of the House of Vey. Aurelian put it on his own hammer. The hammer he used to make the Lattice." She looked up at Lex. "How did you know this rune?"

Lex didn't answer. He swung Anvilsong down off his shoulder and laid it on the table beside the book, head up.

Four runes, glowing faintly red-gold in the dark iron.

Three he'd been taught. The fourth had come up out of him, unbidden, at the very end of the forging, from somewhere deeper than memory.

Seraphine looked at the hammer. At the book. At the hammer again.

"*Emberwright*," she said slowly. "That's your class, isn't it. I heard you say it. Sorcery through making." She shook her head. "There hasn't been a Vey with the forge-gift in—I don't know. Centuries. The book says it's rare. That it only comes once in a great while in the line." She touched the fourth rune on Anvilsong, very gently, as if afraid it might burn her. "You put the family mark into your first real work without knowing it was there. Your blood remembered what you didn't."

Lex didn't know what to say.

*[Lex,]* said Ember, very quietly. *[Keep that book. Whatever else you do. Keep it.]*

*Why?*

*[Because it's yours. It was always meant to be yours.]* A pause. *[And because I can read the notes. All of them. And some of them are about things you're going to need to know.]*

---

"I'm coming with you," said Seraphine.

It was very late. The candles had burned down to stubs. Garrick was asleep in a chair by the door with Oathkeeper across his knees. Wren had curled up on a heap of cushions in the corner and was either asleep or pretending to be. Antho was sitting at the end of the long table, idly turning the pages of the green book with the drawing of Avaloria in it, with Ashka asleep on his shoulder.

Lex looked at Seraphine. "It's dangerous."

"I know it's dangerous. There's a price on my head larger than yours." She pushed her spectacles up her nose. "I've spent two years hiding in a tree reading about the Lattice. I've read everything here. Some of it three times. And there are still things I don't understand. Things I can't understand from books." She looked at the lodestone, glowing faintly on the table. "If you're really going to Avaloria, if you're really going to walk into the Anchor of the Lattice, I want to be there. I want to *see* it." She paused. "And you'll need someone who can read the Wellscript. I'm the best reader alive outside the Spire. Possibly inside it, too."

"Ember can read it," said Lex.

"Ember can read it to *you*. In your head. And then you have to repeat it, slowly, and get half the words wrong." Seraphine raised an eyebrow. "I've been listening all evening. You pronounced *Anchorage* as *anchor rag*."

*[She's not wrong,]* said Ember.

Lex sighed. "Fine."

"Besides," said Seraphine. She glanced down the table at Antho, and then away again quickly. "Someone needs to study your brother's condition properly. Scientifically. Breach-linking has never been documented in a living subject. It's unprecedented. If I could take proper notes, measure his Clarity over time, correlate it with—"

"I'm right here," said Antho, without looking up from the book.

Seraphine went pink. "I know you're right here. I'm speaking *about* you, not *around* you. There's a difference."

"Is there."

"Yes. One is rude and the other is—" She stopped. "Clinical."

Antho turned a page. Something moved at the corner of his mouth.

"Clinical," he said. "That's a new one. I've been called a monster, a murderer, a madman, and the Ashbourne warlock. I've never been called *clinical*."

"You're not clinical. Your *case* is clinical."

"Ah," said Antho. "My case."

Seraphine opened her mouth. Shut it again. Turned even pinker.

Lex looked from one to the other. Then he looked away and very carefully didn't smile.

*[Hm,]* said Ember again.

*Stop it.*

*[I'm only observing.]*

---

The knock came just before dawn.

Lex was the only one awake. He'd been sitting at the long table for hours with Aurelian's *Book of Making* open in front of him, turning the pages slowly, looking at the drawings of hammers and tongs and anvils covered in runes he didn't know yet, while Ember read the notes to him in a low murmur. *[This one is for tempering. This one is a warding-mark for doors. This one—oh. This one's clever. This one lets a blade cut light.]* He'd lost track of time.

The knock came again. Three sharp raps on the living-wood door.

Garrick was on his feet in an instant, Oathkeeper drawn, frost blooming along the blade. Wren rolled off her cushions with an arrow already on the string. Antho's eyes opened, grey and alert, and the shadows stirred around his hands. Seraphine sat bolt upright in her chair with her spectacles askew, clutching the crossbow she'd fallen asleep holding.

"Who is it?" Lex called.

"*An old man,*" said a voice outside, "*who has walked a very long way, and whose raven has been sulking for two days because nobody gave him any of the ptarmigan.*"

Lex let out a breath.

He went to the door and opened it.

Elric Thorne stood on the little round stone step, leaning on his lightning-scarred staff, his grey cloak dark with dew, the brass storm-charms on it glinting faintly in the grey light. Quill sat on his shoulder, looking deeply aggrieved. The old sorcerer's face was lined and tired, and there was mud on his boots up to the knee, and a new cut along one cheekbone. But his storm-grey eyes were bright and sharp as ever, and when he saw Lex, they crinkled at the corners.

"Hello, Lex," he said. "You look well. Better than well." His gaze moved past Lex into the room. To Antho, sitting at the end of the table with Ashka on his shoulder and shadows still curling around his hands. And Elric's face went very still. "And you found your brother."

"I found my brother."

"*Well*." Elric let out a long breath. "Well, well. I'm very glad." He looked at Antho for a long moment. "Hello, Anthoren. I don't expect you remember me. I came up to the Hollowmaw once, a long time ago. You didn't come out."

"I remember," said Antho quietly. He didn't get up. "It wasn't a good night."

"No. I didn't think it was." Elric's gaze moved on. To Garrick, with his frost-edged sword. To Wren, with her bow. And then to Seraphine, sitting very straight in her chair with the crossbow in her lap.

And something in Elric's face changed.

It was very quick. Lex almost missed it. The faintest tightening around the eyes. The faintest narrowing. Like a man who'd seen a coin on the ground and was trying to decide whether it was real.

"Seraphine Ilvane," Elric said softly.

Seraphine went white. "You—you know me?"

"Everyone at the Spire knows you, girl. Or knows your name." Elric came into the room, slowly, leaning on his staff. He didn't take his eyes off her. "The archivist who stole from the sealed vaults and vanished. The High Warden has put a price on your head of two thousand crowns." He smiled faintly. It didn't reach his eyes. "Four times what she's offering for these two. You must have taken something very valuable."

"I took the truth," said Seraphine. Her voice shook, but she held his gaze.

"Did you." Elric looked at her a moment longer. Then he turned away, and the smile came back, warmer this time, and he lowered himself into a chair at the table with a small groan. "Well. I'm sure you'll tell me all about it. But first, I'd very much like a cup of whatever that is, and something to eat, and for someone to tell me what in the Lady's name happened to Lex's face. It looks like someone hit it with a sword."

"Someone did," said Lex.

"Of course they did." Elric sighed. "Of course they did. Sit down, all of you. I have news. And very little of it is good."

Lex sat down. But as he did, he glanced at Seraphine, and saw that she was still staring at Elric, her face white, her hands tight on the crossbow in her lap.

And he glanced at Elric, and saw that the old sorcerer, while he smiled and accepted a cup of tea from Garrick and scratched Quill under the beak, was watching her too. Out of the corner of his eye. Very carefully.

Like a man watching a coin on the ground.
