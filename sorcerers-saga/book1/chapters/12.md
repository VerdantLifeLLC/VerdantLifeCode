# The Thornwood

The forest rose out of the farmland like a wall.

There was no gradual thickening of trees, no scattered copses and hedgerows growing denser until they became woods. One moment the Road was running between stone-walled fields of ragged barley under a pale grey sky. The next it ran straight into a line of trees so tall and so dark and so close together that they looked like the front rank of an army.

They were thorn trees. That was the first thing Lex noticed. Not oak or ash or pine. Thorns. Huge ones, impossibly huge, their trunks as wide as a cottage, their bark black and ridged and glistening as if wet. They had no leaves on their lower branches, only thorns: thorns as long as a man's forearm, curved like claws, black and wicked, bristling out from every limb in all directions. Higher up, where the light reached, there were leaves. Small and narrow and silver. Thousands upon thousands of them, rustling in a wind that Lex couldn't feel down on the Road, so that the whole forest seemed to whisper.

The Road went in under the trees and vanished into darkness.

"Well," said Garrick. "That's cheerful."

They stood at the edge of the forest for a long moment. Nobody moved. Ashka had climbed down off Antho's shoulder into the front of his coat and was peering out, her golden eyes very wide. Quill had flown up to the top of the nearest waystone and was sitting there with his feathers fluffed, refusing to come down.

"Then we tie ourselves together," she said. "Like on a glacier. If one of us falls, the others hold."

---

The Road went on under the trees.

It was dim in the Thornwood, but not dark. Light came down from far above, filtered through the silver leaves, grey-green and shifting, like light seen from the bottom of a river. The black trunks rose on either side of the Road like the pillars of some vast cathedral. The thorns bristled out over the Road from both sides, so low in places that they had to duck, and the paving slabs beneath their feet were the only thing in the world that was flat and straight and grey and certain.

And it was quiet. That was the worst of it. No birds. No insects. No wind, down here, though the leaves far above never stopped rustling. Only the whisper of the leaves, and the soft scuff of their boots on the stone, and the creak of Wren's rope tying them all together: Wren in front, then Lex, then Antho, then Garrick at the back.

The first hour passed. Lex felt the Resolve check go through him like a cold wind, a faint pressure at the back of his skull, like a hand trying to open a door. It didn't open. He walked on.

*[You're fine,]* said Ember. *[Your Resolve is high. Very high, for your level. You put a lot of points into it.]*

The second hour. The check came again. He felt it press harder this time. And when it was gone, there was a small strange gap in his mind. A place where something had been and wasn't. He frowned, walking. He felt for it, the way you feel with your tongue for a tooth that's been pulled.

It was the name of Brannoc's cat.

He knew the cat. He could see it: scrawny and grey, sleeping on the warm brick ledge by the forge, staring down Quill across the workbench. He could see its face. He just couldn't remember what it was called.

He stopped walking.

"Hammer?" Wren had felt the rope go taut. She turned.

"The cat," Lex said. "Brannoc's cat. I can't remember its name."

Wren's face went still. "I can't remember my horse," she said quietly. "Da's horse. The grey mare we had when I was small. I used to ride her up to the high pastures every summer. I loved her more than anything." She swallowed. "I can see her. I just can't remember what we called her."

"It's taking the small things first," said Antho, behind them. "Things you won't miss." He laughed thinly. "It's polite, at least."

"What about you?" Lex said.

His brother was quiet for a moment.

"I don't know," Antho said. "That's the problem. How would I know what I've forgotten?"

Nobody had an answer to that.

They walked on.

---

In the third hour, the forest began to show them things.

Lex saw it first out of the corner of his eye. A light, off among the trees, to the left of the Road. Warm and gold, like a lamp in a window. He turned his head to look and it was gone. Just black trunks and black thorns and grey-green dimness.

A little later, it came again. Closer.

And this time, when he turned, it didn't vanish.

There was a cottage among the trees.

It shouldn't have been there. It couldn't have been there. There was no clearing, no path, no break in the endless black trunks. But there it was, thirty paces off the Road, sitting in a little pool of warm late-afternoon light: a small stone cottage with a thatched roof and a woodpile against one wall and a door painted blue. Smoke rising from the chimney. A lantern burning in the kitchen window.

Lex knew it. He'd know it anywhere. It was the cottage he'd been born in. The cottage that had burned.

And the door was opening.

A man came out of it. A tall man in a long dark coat, with a pack on his back and a traveling staff in his hand. He paused on the step and turned his head and looked back into the house, and Lex saw his face. Silver at the temples. Grey eyes. A face that was hard to remember, even while you were looking at it, as if it slid off your attention like water off oiled leather.

His father.

He'd never been able to picture his father's face. Not since he was small. He'd tried, over and over, lying awake in the loft above the forge. But all he'd ever been able to summon was a vague shape, a blur, a dark coat and a voice. And now here it was, sharp and clear, thirty paces away. Real. Close enough to touch.

His father stepped off the cottage step. He pulled up his hood. And as he did, he looked across the thirty paces of black trunks and grey-green light, and his eyes met Lex's.

"Lex," he said.

His voice was exactly the way Lex had always imagined it. Quiet and deep and a little rough. He felt his feet stop on the Road. Felt Wren's rope go taut ahead of him.

"Lex," his father said again. "I'm sorry. I have to go."

"*Why?*" The word came out of Lex like something torn. He hadn't meant to say anything. "Why did you go? Why didn't you come back? Why did you *leave us*?"

His father looked at him for a long moment. Then he smiled. It was a sad smile, and tired, and gentle.

"Because you weren't worth staying for," he said.

Lex's heart stopped.

"Any of you," his father went on, in the same quiet, gentle voice. "Not your mother. Not your brother. Not you. You were a burden. A chain around my leg. I had important things to do, and you were in the way. So I hid you, and I left you, and I never looked back." He held out his hand across the thirty paces of forest. "But you can come with me now, if you want. You can come and see what was more important than you. Just step off the Road, Lex. Just one step. And you'll understand."

Lex's foot moved.

He didn't tell it to. It just moved. Off the edge of the grey paving slab, toward the soft black earth beneath the trees. Toward the cottage. Toward his father, holding out his hand.

It came last, and it came hardest.
