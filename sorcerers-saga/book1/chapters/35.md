# War on Many Fronts

**I. The Pass**

Pell had never seen so many of anything in his life.

He was lying on his belly on a ledge of grey rock high above the Thornhollow Pass, with Wren's old bow in his hands and an arrow on the string and his heart going like a hammer on an anvil, and he was looking east. Down the long grey valley where the old Vhessan road ran out of the Blightmarch and into Caldor. And the valley was full.

Not of people. Of grey.

It filled the road and spilled over onto the grey slopes on either side. It filled the whole valley from wall to wall, as far back as Pell could see, a slow grey tide moving west. Gloamhounds loping at its edges, low and fast. Mistwraiths drifting above it like smoke. Tall stretched grey Gloamed men in ranks, marching, their white eyes fixed ahead. Black-armored Hollow-knights among them, swords dripping darkness. And things Pell had no names for. Things that had been bears, or horses, or oxen, or *people*, once, and weren't anymore.

And it was silent. That was the worst part. Twenty thousand of them, coming up the valley, and not a sound. Not a footstep. Not a breath. Just the grey, moving, like fog rolling in off a cold sea.

Pell's panel was open at the edge of his vision. He'd been looking at it every few minutes since the Stone. He couldn't stop.

Level seven. A week ago he'd been a level-one Miller. Now he was a level-seven Ranger, with a bow and a quiver of Brannoc's crooked-runed arrows and a promise to stay at the back.

He wasn't at the back. He was on a ledge three hundred feet above the pass, because Teodra Hale had looked at him and said *you're a Ranger, aren't you? Then go and range. Get up high and tell me what you see.*

He saw the end of the world, coming up the valley.

He wriggled back off the ledge and scrambled down the goat path to the pass, where the army was waiting.

---

They'd built a wall.

Not a real one. There hadn't been time for a real one. They'd been at the pass for two days, and in two days, fifteen hundred people had dragged every boulder and log and cart and barrel they could find into a long rough barricade across the narrowest point of the Thornhollow, where the road ran between two great shoulders of grey rock. It was chest-high in places. Waist-high in others. A dragon could have stepped over it.

But behind it, there were fifteen hundred people. And every one of them had a weapon that was warm.

Pell ran along the back of the barricade to where Teodra Hale was standing on a cart with her greatsword over her shoulder and her bandaged head bare to the wind.

"*Twenty thousand*," he gasped. "*It's all of them. The whole valley. They'll be here by noon.*"

Teodra looked down at him. Her broad scarred face didn't change.

"Thank you, Ranger," she said. "Go and tell Captain Roole. Then go and find yourself a place on the wall." She paused. "Near the back."

Pell ran.

---

Daxian Roole was at the center of the barricade with his two hundred Wardens, their grey cloaks a long line among the motley of farmers and sellswords and knights. He listened to Pell's report in silence. Then he nodded.

"Thank you," he said. And then, looking past Pell: "Vane."

Pell turned. The Inquisitor-General was standing a little way behind the line, alone, with his white hair blowing in the wind and his long coat buttoned to his throat. He'd asked to come. Roole had let him. Pell didn't understand why. Nobody did. The man had turned half of Ashbourne into grey statues.

But he'd also, Pell had seen it, stood at the edge of the green on the night of the Unbinding and watched the Quiet wake up, and wept.

"I'm here," said Vane quietly.

"You said you had something," said Roole. "On the road. You said you'd tell me when it mattered."

Vane looked east. Down the valley. At the grey tide coming.

"The Severance chains," he said. "My chains. They cut the Lattice. Silver threads. That's what they were made for." He lifted his left arm. The chain was wrapped around it again. Not black now. Not dripping. Silver-black, like oil on water, the glyphs on its links glowing faintly. *Sever. Sever. Sever.* "But they cut *threads*. Any thread. That's what Wellscript is: not *silver*, *thread*." He looked at Roole. "When the Pale Warden had me, I could feel his threads. Running out of him. Into every Gloamed in his army. Like a puppeteer's strings."

Roole went very still.

"I think," said Vane, "I could cut them."

"All of them?"

"No. Not all. I'm only one man with one chain." Vane's slate eyes were very steady. "But some. A few. Enough, maybe, to make a hole."

Roole looked at him for a long moment.

"It'll cost you," he said.

"Everything I did on that green," said Vane quietly, "cost *them*." He looked away. "Let me pay something back, Captain."

---

The grey came up the pass at noon.

Pell was on the wall. Not at the back. He'd tried. He'd gone to the back, like he promised. And then he'd seen the line of Ashbourne folk at the barricade's left end, Farmer Tobbin and the Dunstan twins and Sister Agathe and Gaffer Wim and the Tanners, with their crooked-runed swords and their pitchforks and their brand-new class names and their faces white with terror, and he'd gone and stood with them. Because they were his.

The grey came up the valley like a wave coming up a beach. Slow. Silent. Unstoppable.

And Teodra Hale stood on her cart in the middle of the barricade and raised her greatsword and roared.

It was the loudest sound Pell had ever heard. It went up off the grey rocks and echoed down the valley, and the grey tide flinched. Actually flinched. A ripple went through it, like wind across a field of barley.

And fifteen hundred people roared with her.

Pell roared. He didn't know he was going to. It just came out of him. And beside him, Farmer Tobbin roared, and the Dunstan twins roared, and Sister Agathe, who had never raised her voice above a whisper in all the years Pell had known her, roared like a lion.

And the grey came on.

---

Pell remembered the next hours only in pieces.

Brannoc's arrows. That was the first piece. He nocked one, drew, loosed, the way Wren had shown him in the forge yard. And it struck a gloamhound in the chest, and the crooked rune on the head flared gold, *Ardent*, and the hound *burned*. Not much. Not like Lex's fire. A little gout of gold, and the hound screamed and staggered and came apart. He stared at it. Then he nocked another.

The barricade. Gloamed men climbing it, grey hands reaching. Farmer Tobbin swinging a crooked-runed sword in great clumsy arcs like a man scything barley, and every grey thing it touched flinching back with smoke pouring off it. Sister Agathe, who'd become a *Lanternkeeper* at the Unbinding, standing on a barrel with her chapel lantern held high over her head, its light washing across the wall, and the Gloam shrinking from it like frost from a fire.

The Dunstan twins, side by side. Both *Shieldbearers* now. Uncommon. Holding a gap in the barricade with two great wooden doors they'd torn off the chapel and carried all the way from Ashbourne. The grey breaking against them like water against a rock.

Hedda Vorne's cart, behind the line, with a portable anvil bolted to the bed and a fire roaring in a brazier, and Brannoc Holt standing at it in his leather apron, and a line of people coming back from the wall with broken swords and bent spears. And Brannoc taking them, and heating them, and striking them, *chang*, *chang*, *chang*, with Hedda beside him on a stool reading runes out of the *Book of Making*. And handing them back. Warm.

Roole's Wardens, in the center, in their grey cloaks, fighting like a single creature. Silver light on their blades. Inquisition-light. Disciplined, steady, holding.

And Teodra Hale. Everywhere. On the cart. On the wall. In the gap. Her greatsword moving in great sweeping arcs, her voice never stopping, roaring orders, roaring curses, roaring jokes. Where she went, the line held.

But it bent.

It bent, and bent, and bent. The grey didn't stop. It didn't tire. It didn't flinch for long. It came on and on and on, and every time a farmer fell or a Warden fell or a sellsword fell, there was one less warm blade on the wall, and the grey came a little further.

By mid-afternoon, Pell's quiver was empty.

By late afternoon, the barricade's left end was gone. Tobbin was down, a grey bite on his shoulder, being dragged back by the twins. Sister Agathe's lantern was guttering.

And Pell saw Vane walk out in front of the wall.

---

He did it alone. Through the gap where the left end had been. Out onto the grey road. Into the grey tide.

Nobody stopped him. Pell didn't think anyone saw him go except Pell, and Roole, who turned his head and watched and didn't move.

The Inquisitor-General walked out into the middle of the pass, with the grey all around him. Gloamhounds circling. Gloamed men turning their white eyes toward him. And he lifted his left arm, and the silver-black chain uncoiled from it.

It didn't strike. It *spread*.

It went out from him in every direction at once, longer than any chain could be, links upon links upon links, glowing, singing. *Sever. Sever. Sever.* It went through the grey tide like a net through water. And where it passed, the Gloamed things *stopped*.

Pell saw it. Saw a stretched grey man, mid-stride, freeze. Saw the chain pass through him. Saw something *snap*, a thread, invisible, a black string running from the grey man's back up into the sky toward the east. Saw the grey man stagger, and look around, confused, and then, slowly, sit down in the middle of the road. White eyes blinking.

Saw it happen again. And again. A hundred times. Two hundred. The chain spreading and spreading, the glyphs blazing, cutting thread after thread after thread.

*Puppet-strings*, Vane had said.

A hole opened in the grey tide around him. A great round hole, a hundred paces across, where the Gloamed things stood still or sat down or wandered aimlessly, cut loose from their master.

Vane stood in the middle of it. His chain was still spreading. His face was white. Whiter than it had been even when he was Gloam-taken. Blood was running from his nose. From his eyes.

"*Vane!*" Roole roared. "*Enough! Come back!*"

Vane didn't come back.

The chain spread further. Two hundred paces. Three. The hole grew. And Pell could see, even from the wall, that the chain was coming *out* of Vane. Not unspooling off his arm. Coming out of *him*. Out of his chest. Out of his life.

He'd said it would cost him.

The grey tide pulled back from the hole. Not much. A little. Like a wave that's hit a rock and doesn't know what to do.

And Lucius Vane, Grand Inquisitor of the Spire, who had turned half of Ashbourne into grey statues and hunted two brothers across half of Aldermere, stood in the middle of the Thornhollow Pass with a thousand Gloamed things sitting confused in the road around him, and looked back at the wall.

At Roole. At the Ashbourne folk. At Pell.

He smiled. A small, tired, strange smile.

And the chain went out of him completely, all at once, a great flash of silver-black light that rolled across the valley like a wave. And when it faded, he was lying in the road.

Very still.

The grey tide didn't come back into the hole. Not for an hour. Not for two.

By then, the sun was going down. And the grey, for the first time all day, *stopped*. Pulled back. Out of the pass. Into the valley. To wait for morning.

The wall had held.

**II. The Causeway**

Elowen Dunmore was not her father.

She knew it. She'd known it her whole life. Garrick Dunmore was a mountain. Broad and slow and patient, a man who stood in doorways. She was faster. Sharper. She'd made captain at twenty-two because she could read a battlefield like a book and move a company across it like a hand across a page. She didn't stand in doorways. She went *through* them.

But there was only one way into Saltreach from the south. A causeway. Two miles long and thirty feet wide, a raised road of fitted stone running across the salt marshes from the coast road to the city gates. And three thousand Gloamed coming up it.

And she had fifty knights.

And the Saltreach militia, which was four hundred dockworkers with boathooks, and a dozen sea-mages from the harbor guild who'd never fought anything but a storm.

"*Hold the causeway*," she told them, from the back of her horse, at the city end of it, at dusk, with the grey tide coming up out of the marsh mist two miles away. "That's all. Just hold it. They can't come through the marsh. It'll swallow them. The causeway's the only way. Hold it, and the city stands."

"For how long?" said the harbor-master, a squat bald woman with a voice like a foghorn.

Elowen looked at the grey coming up the causeway. Three thousand. Silent.

She thought of her father. Standing on the chapel steps for three days, with half a sword. Standing at the gate at Caldris, with three holes in him.

*I'm standing on the steps.*

"For as long as we can stand," she said.

---

They stood for a night and a day.

Elowen remembered it the way her father would have, afterward: not as a battle, but as a *doorway*. Thirty feet of stone, and herself in it. Her fifty knights in a wedge across the causeway, shields locked, swords out. The dockworkers behind them with their boathooks, hauling Gloamed things off the edges of the causeway into the marsh, where the black water took them. The sea-mages on the city walls behind, calling the tide.

That was the thing she remembered best. The sea-mages, a dozen terrified harbor-guild wizards who'd never fought anything but a storm, standing on the walls of Saltreach in the dark with their arms raised. Calling the sea. And the sea *coming*. In the middle of the night, against the moon, against the season, the tide came in. Over the marshes. Up the sides of the causeway. Higher. Higher. Until the grey things on the causeway were wading, waist-deep, chest-deep, in black salt water.

And the Gloam *hated* the sea.

Elowen didn't know why. Nobody did. Maybe because the sea was older than anything. Maybe because it moved, always, never still, never quiet. But where the salt water touched the Gloamed, they flinched and slowed and stumbled, and the black in them thinned. And her knights, standing on the high crown of the causeway with the tide lapping at their boots, cut them down.

But there were three thousand.

By dawn, she had thirty knights. By noon, twenty. The dockworkers had lost a hundred. The tide was going out. The sea-mages were collapsing on the walls, one by one, drained.

And the grey kept coming.

Elowen stood in the center of the wedge with her sword in her hand and blood running down her face from a cut on her scalp and her shield arm numb to the shoulder. She'd stopped counting how many she'd killed. She'd stopped thinking about anything but the next one, and the next, and the next.

And at noon, a sail appeared on the sea.

Then another. Then a dozen. Coming in from the west, out of the haze, fast, white sails full of a wind that came from nowhere. Saltreach ships. The merchant fleet. And at the prow of the lead ship, a figure, standing very straight, with a blue cloak streaming from her shoulders and a circlet of silver on her brow.

Elowen stared.

The ships came in to the marshes on the last of the tide, as close to the causeway as they could get. And over their sides poured men. Sailors. Marines. Knights. *More* knights, in blue cloaks with the silver crown on their shields, the Royal Guard, five hundred of them, wading through the shallows toward the causeway with their swords out.

And the woman at the prow of the lead ship lifted her arm, and pointed at the grey on the causeway, and her voice carried across the marsh as clear as a bell.

"*For Caldor. For every Ceiling. For every Stone.*"

The Royal Guard hit the grey tide from the flank like a hammer hitting hot iron.

Elowen stood in the middle of the causeway and watched it happen and couldn't move.

"*Captain*," gasped Sir Aldous beside her. He was leaning on his sword, bloody to the elbows. "*Captain, is that—*"

"Yes," said Elowen. Her voice was very strange. "Yes. That's the Princess."

The grey broke.

He felt them the way you feel thunder before you see the lightning. In his chest. In his panel. In the Lattice itself, which he could feel now, all the time, faint and vast, like the hum of a great forge in another room. He felt it shudder when Vane's chain spread through the Thornhollow Pass. He felt it shudder again when the sea came in over the Saltreach marshes. Every Gloamed thread cut, every grey thing driven back, sent a little tremor through the net.

And he felt it *thin*.
