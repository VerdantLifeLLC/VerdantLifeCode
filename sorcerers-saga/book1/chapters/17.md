# Highcrest

Highcrest rose out of the morning mist like a ship out of fog.

Lex had heard about it his whole life. Everyone in the Vale had. The capital of Caldor, the seat of kings, the city on the crag. He'd imagined it a hundred times, lying awake in the loft above the forge. None of his imagining had come close.

It was built on a mountain. Not on top of one: *on* it, all over it, climbing up its sides in terraces and tiers, street above street, roof above roof, the way ivy climbs a wall. At the bottom, where the mountain's skirts spread out into the river valley, the buildings were low and crowded and dark, timber and thatch and mud brick, smoke rising from a thousand chimneys into the grey sky. Higher up they grew taller, and paler, and finer: stone houses with slate roofs, then mansions with gardens, then great halls with columns and domes. And at the very top, on the summit of the crag, two things stood above everything else.

One was a castle. Grey and massive and many-towered, flying a blue banner with a silver crown.

The other was a tower. White. Impossibly tall, impossibly slender, rising from the summit beside the castle like a needle stuck into the sky. Taller than the castle. Taller than anything Lex had ever seen or imagined. And at the very top of it, so high that it seemed to be touching the clouds, there was a light. Silver-blue. Pulsing slowly. Like a heartbeat.

"The Spire," said Seraphine quietly. She'd stopped on the road beside him. Her face was pale. "And the Great Stone."

The Veil lay thick around the city. Thicker than Lex had ever seen it, even in the deepest hollows of the Vale. It swirled around the base of the white tower in great slow silver spirals, drawn toward it like water toward a drain. The whole city glimmered faintly through it, as if seen through gauze.

"Six years," said Garrick softly, on his other side. He was looking up at the castle, at the blue banner. His scarred face was unreadable. "Six years."

"Hoods up," said Elric. "Everyone. And remember: we're a merchant's family from the Fells, come to sell wool at the autumn market. I'm the grandfather. Lex, you're my grandson. Antho, you're his cousin. Wren, you're his betrothed."

Wren choked.

"Seraphine, you're a hired clerk. Garrick, you're our guard. Keep your sword wrapped." Elric looked at Antho. "And keep that dragon *in your pack*."

Ashka, poking her head out of the top of Antho's satchel, hissed.

"She doesn't like the pack," said Antho.

"She'll like a Warden's crossbow bolt even less." Elric pulled his own hood up over his grey hair. "Come on. And for the Lady's sake, nobody use any magic until we're off the street."

---

They went in through a gate in the lower city that wasn't really a gate. It was a gap in an old wall behind a tannery, where the stink was so overpowering that no Warden ever came near it, and an old woman with no teeth sat on a stool and took a copper penny from each of them without looking up. Beyond it, the Lower Town closed around them like a fist.

Lex had never seen so many people in one place. The streets were narrow and crooked and steep, climbing up the side of the mountain in switchbacks and stairways, and every one of them was packed. Porters with loads on their backs. Women with baskets. Children everywhere, barefoot, darting between legs. Beggars on corners. Hawkers shouting. Carts jammed in alleyways too narrow for them, their drivers swearing. The noise was tremendous. The smell was worse: smoke and sewage and cooking and sweat and animals, all mixed together into a thick warm fug that hung in the air like the Veil.

And everywhere, on every wall, on every door, nailed to every post, there were notices.

Lex saw the first one on the wall beside the tannery gate and stopped dead. Wren ran into the back of him.

It was printed on cheap grey paper, already peeling at the edges. Across the top, the silver lattice of the Wardens. And below it, two faces. Badly drawn. One was a young man with curly hair and a heavy jaw. The other was a gaunt bearded man with wild eyes. Neither of them looked much like Lex or Antho. But the names underneath were very clear.

"They spelled my name right," said Antho. "That's something."

"Keep walking," said Elric through his teeth.

They kept walking. Up the crooked stairs, through the jostling crowds, past notice after notice after notice. Lex's face, Antho's face, and once, on the door of a tavern, Seraphine's: a thin, narrow, spectacled face with *SERAPHINE ILVANE — THIEF — 2,000 CROWNS* printed beneath it in large letters. Seraphine pulled her hood lower and walked faster.

And there were Wardens. Not many. Pairs of them, in grey cloaks, standing on corners and at the tops of stairways, watching the crowds with cold eyes. Each time they passed a pair, Lex felt his heart stop, and kept his head down, and kept walking. Each time, nothing happened.

"Nearly there," muttered Elric. "Nearly there."

---

Elric's friend kept a chandlery on a crooked lane halfway up the Lower Town, in a narrow building of blackened timber that leaned against its neighbors like a drunk against a wall. A painted sign over the door showed a candle and a bell. Inside, it was dim and cramped and smelled of tallow and beeswax and lamp oil. And behind the counter, polishing a brass lantern with one hand, stood the largest woman Lex had ever seen.

She was taller than Garrick. Broader than Brannoc. Her grey hair was cropped short as a soldier's, and her face was broad and red and seamed, and her left sleeve was pinned up empty at the elbow. She looked up as they came in, and her small sharp eyes went over all of them, one by one, and stopped on Elric.

"Thorne," she said. Her voice was like gravel in a bucket.

"Hedda."

"You said a few days. That was three weeks ago."

"I was delayed."

"You're always delayed." Hedda set down the lantern. Her eyes moved to Lex, then to Antho, then to Seraphine. They narrowed. "These are the ones on the notices."

"Yes."

"You brought the Ashbourne warlock into my *shop*."

"Yes."

"And the archivist thief."

"Yes."

Hedda Vorne looked at Elric Thorne for a long moment. Then she let out a long gusty breath through her nose, like a bellows.

"Back room," she said. "Now. Before someone sees you." She lifted the hinged section of the counter. "And if that's a dragon I see in the boy's pack, it stays off my shelves. I've got a hundred crowns of beeswax up there."

---

The back room was a forge.

Lex stopped in the doorway and stared. He couldn't help it. After weeks of heating iron with his bare hands on flat rocks in the wilderness, it was like walking into a cathedral. A real forge, a big brick hearth with a hood and a chimney. A real anvil, a good one, heavy and square and worn smooth on top by years of use. Racks of tools on the walls: tongs and hammers and chisels and punches and swages. A slack tub. A grinding wheel. Bins of charcoal and coal. Bar-stock stacked in the corner, good iron and steel, more than he'd ever seen in one place outside Brannoc's best Saltreach shipment.

"Lady of Lanterns," he breathed.

Hedda had come in behind him. She was looking at him with an odd expression.

"You're the smith," she said. It wasn't a question.

"Yes."

"Thorne said there was a smith." She looked at the hammer on his shoulder. Then, slowly, she reached out with her one hand and touched Anvilsong's head. She held her fingers there for a long moment. Her eyebrows went up. "It's *warm*."

"It stays warm."

"Runes," said Hedda. She ran her thumb over the four marks on the hammer's head. "Real ones. Not paint. Not etching." She looked up at him. "I was a Warden armorer for twenty-two years, boy. I made blades for knights and Inquisitors. I put Lattice-enchantments on steel with scrolls and ink and a year's training for every one." She took her hand away. "I never once made anything that was *warm*."

"Hedda was the best armorer the Spire ever had," said Elric from the doorway. "Until a Gloamed knight took her arm off, eight years ago, in the first clearing of the eastern march. The Order pensioned her off with half pay and a thank-you note."

"A very nice note," said Hedda drily. "Good paper." She looked at Lex. "You can use the forge. While you're here. I don't use it much anymore." She glanced at her empty sleeve. "Hard to hold the tongs and the hammer at the same time."

"Thank you," said Lex. He meant it more than he could say.

Hedda shrugged her massive shoulders. "Make something worth looking at," she said. "That's all the thanks I want."

---

He made a great many things worth looking at.

They stayed in Hedda's back rooms for four days, hidden, while Elric went out each morning and came back each evening with news and supplies, and Lex worked. He worked from first light until late into the night. Hedda's forge roared and her anvil rang, and the sound of it was covered by the roar and ring of a hundred other forges in the Lower Town, so nobody noticed one more.

He made arrows for Wren. Fifty of them, with good steel heads he forged himself, and on every head, the rune *Knit* so they wouldn't break, and on ten of them, using a sliver of the Rimewarden's heart, the cold rune from the *Book of Making* that would freeze whatever they struck. He made her a new hunting knife, too, with a blade like a leaf and a handle wrapped in leather, and the rune *True* struck into the steel near the hilt, so it would always find the place she aimed it.

He made a new prod for Seraphine's crossbow, steel instead of horn, and a new trigger mechanism that wouldn't let her load the bolt upside down. She looked at it for a long time when he gave it to her, and then said, very primly, *that is a very thoughtful modification*, and went red to the ears.

He mended Garrick's armor. Every dent, every patch, every strip of leather and length of string. He reforged the worst of the plates and riveted them back in place and struck *Bulwark* into the breastplate where it would sit over the old knight's heart. Garrick stood in the forge while Lex fitted it to him, and moved his arms, and twisted, and stamped, and then looked down at himself and said quietly, *my father had armor like this. When I was a boy. I used to polish it for him.*

And he made two things for his brother.

The first was a lantern.

He'd been thinking about it since the Hollowmaw. Since the hundreds of unlit lanterns hanging in the dark. Since he'd seen Antho's face light up with Seren's lamp burning in his hands. He'd found the rune he needed in the *Book of Making*, on the third night, with Ember reading the notes aloud to him by candlelight. *[Hush,]* Ember had said. *[A rune of silence. For bells that ring too loud, or dogs that bark too much, or—]* A pause. *[Or voices that won't stop. Lex. Look at this.]*

He made it out of black iron, small enough to hang from a belt: a lantern-shaped cage, with a little glass chimney he begged off Hedda's shelves. Inside, where the wick should go, he struck *Ardent*, so it would burn without fuel. And on the iron frame, all around it, he struck *Hush*. Over and over. Twelve times. One for every ward-nail in the Hollowmaw.

He gave it to Antho on the fourth evening, in the back room, by the light of the forge.

Antho took it and turned it over in his long scarred hands. He looked at the little flame burning inside the glass. He looked at the runes on the frame.

"What does it do?" he said.

"Light it," said Lex. "Hang it on your belt. And tell me what you hear."

Antho looked at him. Then he hooked the lantern onto his belt, and stood very still, with his eyes closed.

Lex watched the red panel only he could see.

Antho didn't say anything. He stood in the forge-light with his hand on the little iron lantern and his eyes very bright, and after a moment he turned away and went and stood in the corner with his back to the room, and his shoulders were shaking.

Seraphine got up from the table where she'd been reading and went and stood beside him. She didn't say anything either. She just put her hand on his arm.

The second thing was for Ashka.

It took him two whole days. It was the most delicate work he'd ever done. Far more delicate than Wren's bow, or Seren's lamp. He used silver wire, thin as thread, that Hedda had in a drawer, and he worked it on the very tip of the anvil with the smallest hammer in the forge, so small it looked like a toy in his hand. He shaped it into a frame. A lattice of fine silver strands, light as a cobweb, curved to follow the line of the little dragon's torn wing. And on every joint of the frame, he struck a rune so small he had to use a jeweler's lens Hedda lent him to see it. *Knit*, to hold it together. *Steadfast*, so it wouldn't break. And *Homeward*, so that whatever happened, it would always come back to the place it belonged.

Ashka sat on the workbench and watched him the whole time, her golden eyes enormous, her head tilting first one way and then the other.

When he fitted it to her wing, she held perfectly still. He slid it over the torn membrane and the twisted bones, gently, gently, and fastened it at the shoulder with a tiny silver clasp. The frame settled against the wing like a second skeleton, supporting it, holding the torn edges in place.

Ashka spread her wings.

Both of them. The whole one, and the torn one in its silver cage. For the first time since Lex had known her, they opened together, wide and blue-grey and shimmering in the forge-light. She looked at the torn wing. She flexed it. The silver frame flexed with it, perfectly, as if it had grown there.

She made a sound Lex had never heard her make. Not a chirp. Not a trill. A long, high, wavering *cry*, like a hawk's. And she leaped off the workbench.

She didn't fall. She *glided*. Across the forge, wings spread, her silver-framed wing catching the air, her whip of a tail streaming out behind. She didn't fly, not really. She couldn't flap the torn wing hard enough for that. But she glided all the way across the room and landed on Antho's shoulder with a thump, and stood there with her wings still spread, crying out again and again, sparks crackling all down her spine like a little storm.

Antho was staring at her. Then at Lex.

"She's never—" he started. "Not since the bolt. Not once in four years. She'd try, and she'd fall, and she'd—" He stopped. His voice was gone.

Ashka folded her wings. She turned her narrow head and looked across the forge at Lex with her golden eyes. And then a picture came into his head, sent from her, sharp and bright and overwhelming. *Sky. Wind. Up, up, up. Antho on the ground, small, far below. Lightning. FREE.*

*Same blood,* she sent. *Same blood. Thank you. Thank you. Same blood.*

On the second day, Wren went to Fennel Street.

Lex went with her. Elric didn't like it. Elric said it was too dangerous, too exposed, that the Wardens would be watching the physics' houses for exactly this reason. Wren looked at him without expression and said, *I'm going*, and went. And Lex went after her, with his hood up and Anvilsong wrapped in sacking over his shoulder like a sack of tools.

The physic's house was a narrow stone building in a street of narrow stone buildings, a few tiers up from Hedda's lane, where the Lower Town began to give way to something cleaner and quieter. There was a brass plate on the door. MASTER ODO FENWICK, PHYSIC. And on the step, sitting in the thin autumn sunshine with a small boy on either side of her, there was a woman.

She was thin. Very thin, with a grey shawl around her shoulders and grey threaded through her red hair. She was coughing, a dry harsh cough that shook her whole body. But when it passed, she lifted her head, and her face was Wren's face, twenty-five years older, freckled and sharp and fierce.

Wren stopped in the middle of the street.

The woman on the step looked up. She saw Wren. And her whole face changed.

"*Wrennie*," she said.

Wren ran.

Lex stood in the street and watched her go. He watched her fall to her knees on the step and throw her arms around her mother, and the two small boys pile on top of them both, shouting, *Wren! Wren! Wren's back!* He watched her mother hold her and rock her and cough and laugh and cry all at once. He watched Wren, who never cried, who'd stood in the clearing at the heart of the Thornwood and looked at the Ceiling without a tear, press her face into her mother's shoulder and sob like a child.

He stayed where he was. It wasn't his to be part of.

After a while, Wren's mother looked up over her daughter's head. Her eyes found Lex, standing in the street with his hood up and a sack of tools on his shoulder. She looked at him for a long moment.

"So," she said. Her voice was rough. "You must be the Hammer."

Lex felt his face go hot. "Yes, ma'am."

"Come here, then. Let me look at you."

On the third day, Lex saw the Kindlings.

He hadn't meant to. He'd gone out with Elric in the morning, hooded, to help carry supplies back from a market in the middle tiers, and they'd come up a stairway into a little square, and there was a crowd.

In the middle of the square was a Lattice Stone. Not like the cracked old Stone on the green at Ashbourne. This one was tall, taller than a man, white and polished and carved with the faces of Founders, glowing silver in the morning light. And in front of it stood a girl.

She was perhaps sixteen. Thin and small and dark-haired, in a patched brown dress, with a face like a bird's: sharp and quick and bright. Her mother stood behind her in the crowd, twisting her hands in her apron. A Warden in a grey robe stood beside the Stone, a bored-looking man with a ledger.

"Kindling," said Elric quietly. "Every Lower Town Stone holds them on the first of the month. For everyone who's turned sixteen." He tugged Lex's sleeve. "Come on. We shouldn't stand here."

Lex didn't move.

The girl put her hand on the Stone. The silver glow flared, briefly. And Lex saw her face change. Saw the brightness go out of it. Saw it go carefully, deliberately blank, the way Wren's had gone blank in the Thornwood.

"*Laborer*," the bored Warden read from the girl's panel, and wrote it in his ledger. "Common. Next."

The girl turned away from the Stone. Her mother caught her in her arms. The girl didn't cry. She just stood there with her face blank, staring at nothing.

And Lex, before he could stop himself, used True Sight.

Not on the girl. On the *Stone*.

The world flickered gold. And the Lattice Stone in the middle of the square *opened* to him, the way his own panel had opened, layer beneath layer. He saw the silver threads running into it from the great net of the Lattice. He saw the Kindling record of the girl, just written, *Laborer, Common*. And beneath it, deeper, in a layer most people couldn't see, he saw something else.

A flag. Attached to the Stone itself. A small, neat, grey line of text, like a note in a ledger margin.

The Wardens came at midnight.

Lex was asleep. They all were, except Hedda, who sat up in the front of the shop most nights with a lamp and a cudgel across her knees, because she said she was old and didn't need much sleep and it was her shop. *Hedda Vorne. By order of the Spire, stand aside.*

Lex was on his feet with Anvilsong in his hands before he was fully awake. Around him, the others were scrambling up. Garrick, Oathkeeper already drawn. Wren, an arrow on the string. "How many?" said Garrick.
