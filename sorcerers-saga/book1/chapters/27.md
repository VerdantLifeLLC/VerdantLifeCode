# The Drowned Archive

They went in through a window.

There was no door. Or if there was, it was somewhere far below the black water of the fen, under six hundred years of silt and reeds. The only way in was one of the round windows in the dome's flank, a broken one, its glass long gone, just above the waterline. Wren found it. She waded out along the edge of the dome waist-deep in the cold black water, testing every step with her pole, and came back with her lips blue and her eyes bright.

"There's a floor inside," she said, teeth chattering. "Under the window. Dry. Mostly."

They passed Seraphine through first, on her litter, Lex lifting her from the water and Wren pulling her through from inside. Then Antho, slowly, shaking. Then Ashka, who flew in through the window with a disgusted sneeze of sparks, having refused to get her feet wet. Then Lex, last, hauling himself up over the stone sill with Anvilsong on his back.

He dropped down inside and stood up and looked around.

They were on a gallery. A narrow stone walkway running around the inside of the dome, high up, just below the windows. Below it, the dome fell away into darkness, and at the bottom of the darkness, far below, Lex could see the faint gleam of water. Still and black. Filling the lower part of the dome like water in a bowl.

And all around the walls, from the gallery down to the water and below it, there were shelves.

Stone shelves. Carved into the curved wall of the dome itself, ring after ring of them, spiraling down into the dark. And on the shelves, books. Thousands of them. Rotted and swollen and black with damp, most of them. Lumps of pulp that had once been books. But here and there, on the higher shelves, ones that had been warded, or cased, or simply lucky, were still whole.

"*Qel'Varran*," whispered Seraphine from her litter. Her voice was full of wonder and grief. "Oh. Oh, look at it. Look at all of it. Six hundred years underwater." She reached out a burned hand toward the nearest shelf, toward a black lump that might once have been a codex. "All that knowledge. Gone."

"Not all of it," said Antho quietly. He was standing at the edge of the gallery, looking down into the dark. "Something's down there. I can feel it."

Wren and Antho saw the panel. Lex didn't.

He knew it was there. He could see their faces go still and unfocused as they read it. But nothing appeared at the edge of his own vision. No silver. No gold. Nothing. The Lattice didn't know he was in its Delve. The Lattice didn't know he was anywhere.

"Thirty to thirty-eight," said Wren. She looked at him. "I'm twenty-four. Capped. Antho's thirty-eight with his Clarity in the gutter. Seraphine can't walk." She paused. "And you're—"

Wren looked at him. At the hammer on his back. At his grey eyes.

"Stubborn," she said.

---

The gallery spiraled down.

It ran around the inside of the dome in a long slow corkscrew, between the shelves, down toward the black water at the bottom. They carried Seraphine's litter between them, Lex and Antho, and Wren went ahead with her bow, and Ashka glided in slow circles through the dark air of the dome, her scales giving off a faint blue glow that was the only light they had.

The first guardian came out of the shelves.

Lex didn't see it at first. It looked like a book. A big one, bound in black leather, sitting on a shelf at the edge of the gallery. And then it unfolded. It opened like a book opening, and kept opening, pages spreading out like wings, like fins, and inside it was not paper but *ink*. Black liquid ink, swirling, rising up out of the open pages in a column, taking shape.

Letters. It was made of letters. Thousands of them, black and wet and swirling, words and glyphs and fragments of Wellscript, all churning together into a shape like a man made of smoke. Its face was a blur of overlapping words. Its hands were long dripping strokes of a pen.

Lex looked at the Inkwraith. At the swirling black letters. At the dripping strokes of its hands.

*Ink*, he thought. *It's ink.*

He knew ink. Not the way Seraphine knew it. Not the words. The *stuff*. Brannoc bought it every spring from the tinker who came through Ashbourne, for the Reeve's ledgers and the chapel's prayer-books, and Lex had watched the tinker make it in his cart. Oak galls crushed and soaked. Iron salts. Gum. Water.

*Iron gall ink. With iron in it.*

And what did every smith know about iron and water?

He didn't have Heat Shaping. He didn't have Ember Strike. He didn't have anything the Lattice had given him.

But he had a pack. And in the pack, wrapped in oilcloth, he had a flint and a steel, and a twist of dry tinder, and a little clay pot of lamp oil that Wren had bought in a village on the road north. Because he was a smith, and smiths always carry fire.

He knelt on the gallery floor. He struck the steel against the flint. Sparks. Ordinary orange sparks. They fell on the tinder. It smoldered. He blew on it, the way he'd blown on ten thousand forge-fires. It caught.

He poured the oil over a wad of rag from his pack, and lit the rag, and threw it.

It struck the Inkwraith full in its churning chest.

And the ink *boiled*.

Not burned. Ink doesn't burn well. But it boils. It boils and it dries and it cracks and it flakes, and the water goes out of it in a hiss of steam, and what's left is just a crust. A dry brown crust of iron salts and oak galls, crumbling.

The Inkwraith shrieked. A thin, papery, rustling shriek, like ten thousand pages being torn at once. It writhed in Antho's shadows. Steam poured off it. Its swirl of letters went from black to brown to grey, and slowed, and stiffened, and *cracked*.

It fell apart. A shower of dry brown flakes pattering down onto the gallery floor like dead leaves.

Antho let go of the shadows. He sat down hard on the stone, gasping, his eyes flickering black and grey.

Wren blinked. She looked down at the arrow in her hand.

"Oh," she said. "Oh, right. That's an arrow." She looked up at Lex. Her face was white. "I forgot what an arrow *was*. For a second. I forgot."

"It eats words," said Seraphine faintly from her litter. "The Inkwraiths. It's in the commentaries. The Founders made them to guard the archives. They eat *meaning*. Anyone who isn't supposed to be here, they make them forget why." She was staring at the dry flakes on the floor. "How did you do that?"

Lex looked down at the flint and steel in his hand.

"It's fire," he said. "Just fire. Ordinary fire." He turned the steel over. "You don't need the Lattice to make fire. Smiths have been making fire since before there was a Lattice. Since before there was anything." He put the flint and steel back in his pack. "I forgot that. For a while."

---

There were more Inkwraiths. Many more. They came out of the shelves as the gallery spiraled down, one at a time, then in twos and threes. Lex burned them all. Ordinary fire. Oil-soaked rags and burning torches made of shelf-wood and the last of the lamp oil.

It was slow. It was clumsy. It was nothing like an Ember Strike. Twice an Inkwraith got its dripping hand on him before he could burn it, and he lost things. The name of the inn in Saltreach where Garrick had told them he used to drink. The color of his mother's eyes. He'd find them again later, in pieces, the way you find dropped coins in grass. But in the moment, they were gone, and there was only a blank smear where they'd been.

But he burned them. One after another. A smith with a torch.

And he wasn't nothing.

---

At the bottom of the dome, the gallery ran out into the water.

It just stopped. The stone walkway went down into the black water and vanished, still spiraling, the shelves going down with it into the dark. The water was perfectly still. Perfectly black. It filled the whole bottom of the dome like ink in an inkwell.

"*Down there*," said Antho. He was standing at the edge, looking into the water. "Whatever I felt. It's down there."

"We can't go down there," said Wren. "It's water. We'd drown."

"Not all of it is water." Seraphine had pushed herself up on her litter. She was squinting through her cracked spectacles at the curved wall of the dome, where the shelves went down into the dark. "Look. There. Just below the surface."

Lex looked.

There was an arch. Set into the wall, below the gallery, just under the water. A round stone arch with Wellscript carved around it. And through the arch, faintly, so faintly he thought he might be imagining it, a glow.

Gold.

"Wards," said Seraphine. "Founders' wards. On the arch. They're old, but they're holding. They're keeping the water *out*." She looked at Lex. "There's a room behind that arch. A dry room. Under the water." She swallowed. "That's where they'd have kept the most important things."

Lex looked at the black water. At the faint gold glow beneath it.

"How far down?" he said.

"Ten feet," said Wren. She'd knelt at the edge and was peering into the water. "Maybe twelve. Then the arch. You'd have to swim down and through." She looked up at him. "In the dark. In the cold."

"I can swim."

"Can you swim twelve feet straight down in freezing black water and find an arch you can barely see and get through it before you run out of breath?"

Lex looked at her.

"Probably not," he said. "But I'm going to try."

---

It was the coldest thing he'd ever felt.

Colder than the Frostvault. Colder than the gloamhound's bite. The black water closed over his head and the cold went into him like a thousand needles, and for a moment he couldn't move, couldn't think, couldn't do anything but hang there in the dark with his heart hammering.

Then he kicked.

Down. Into the dark. Following the curve of the wall with one hand, the shelves going past under his fingers, slimy and cold. He couldn't see anything. He couldn't feel his feet. His lungs were burning. *Ten feet. Twelve.* The gold glow was there, ahead, faint, wavering. He kicked toward it.

His hand found stone. Carved stone. The arch.

He pulled himself through it.

And came up, gasping, into air.

He was in a room. Small. Round. Dry. The water ended at the arch behind him in a flat black wall, as if someone had sliced it off with a knife. The Founders' wards. Holding for six hundred years.

He crawled out onto a stone floor. Coughing. Shaking. Soaked. And looked up.

The room was lit. By a fire.

It was burning in the middle of the floor, in a small round hearth of white stone. A fire. Real fire. Orange and gold and red, dancing, crackling softly. And there was no wood in the hearth. No coal. No oil. No fuel at all. Just the fire, burning on the bare white stone, the way Seren's lamp burned on the rock above the Hollowmaw.

Except that Lex hadn't made this one.

Nobody had made this one. Not for six hundred years. Not for nine hundred.

He knelt beside it. He held out his hands, shaking with cold.

And the fire *leaned* toward him.

The way the forge-fire had leaned in Ashbourne, the evening Elric came. The way it had always leaned, his whole life, when he walked past the hearth. Toward him. Like a flower toward a window. Like a dog toward its master.

He went very still.

He hadn't called it. He *couldn't* call it. Elric had locked his class, locked his Root, locked everything. When he reached for the fire inside him, there was only a door, iron and gold and black, bolted shut.

But this wasn't the fire inside him. This was fire. Just fire. The fire that had been in the world since before the Lattice. Since before the Founders. Since before anything.

And it knew him.

He didn't know how he knew that. But he did. He knelt by the hearth with his hands held out and felt the warmth soak into his frozen fingers, and felt something else, too, very faint, like a whisper in a language he almost understood. Not words. Something under words. A sort of *recognition*. The feeling of being looked at by something very old and very patient that had been waiting a long time for someone to come and kneel by its hearth.

*Who are you?* he thought. Not in Root. Not in the language under language. Just thought it.

The fire crackled.

And, faint as a breath, something answered. Not a voice. A *feeling*. Warmth, and patience, and something almost like amusement. As if the fire had been asked a very simple question by a very small child.

*I am Flame*, it seemed to say. *Who are* you*?*

---

He didn't know how long he knelt there. Long enough for his clothes to start steaming. Long enough for the shaking to stop.

Then he looked around the room.

There were shelves on the walls here too. But these weren't carved from the stone. They were bronze. And on them, not books, but cases. Bronze cases, sealed, with Wellscript engraved on their lids. A dozen of them.

And on a low stone table beside the eternal fire, a single book.

It was small. Bound in something that wasn't leather, something grey and supple like the skin of a seal. There was no title on its cover. Only a symbol. Not the open hand and the flame. Something else.

Six circles, arranged in a ring. And in the center of the ring, a seventh, empty.

He picked it up.

---

He brought it back up through the black water wrapped in oilcloth, and two of the bronze cases with it, tied to his back with Wren's climbing rope, which she'd thrown down to him. It took three trips. On the last one, he nearly didn't make it; his legs cramped in the cold and he sank, and the dark closed over him, and he thought very clearly *this is a stupid way to die*. And then something grabbed him by the collar and hauled him up, and he broke the surface choking, and it was Antho, soaked to the neck, his eyes black-and-grey and furious.

"*You idiot*," his brother said. "*You stupid, stupid—*"

"I know."

"*I can't lose you too. Do you understand? I can't. Not you too.*"

"I know, Antho." Lex coughed up black water. "I know."

Antho dragged him out onto the gallery and held onto him, shaking, and didn't let go for a long time.

---

Seraphine read the book that night.

They'd made a camp on the gallery, high up, near the broken window, with a fire Lex had built from shelf-wood and lit with flint and steel. Seraphine sat propped against the wall with the grey book open on her knees and her cracked spectacles on her nose, and read by the firelight. Her lips moved. Her burned fingers turned the pages, slowly, carefully, as if afraid they'd crumble. They didn't. Whatever the book was made of, it had lasted nine hundred years without a mark.

The others waited.

It was a long time before she looked up. When she did, her face was very strange.

"It's the Listeners' Codex," she said. "The real one. The one the Order spent three hundred years trying to destroy." She touched the page. "It's not about the Lattice at all. It was written *before* the Lattice. By the last of the Listeners, the ones who refused to take part in the Weaving." She swallowed. "It's about the Wells."

"The Wells?" said Wren.

"The six sources of power. The old magic. The magic under the house." Seraphine turned the book so they could see the symbol on the first page: six circles in a ring. "Flame. Stone. Tide. Gale. Radiance. Shade." She touched each circle in turn. "The Listeners didn't have classes or levels. They *listened* to the Wells. Learned to hear them. And the Wells answered." She looked at Lex. "Like the fire. In the room under the water. Did it—"

"It leaned toward me," said Lex. "It knew me." He swallowed. "I asked who it was. And it said *I am Flame*. Not in words. But it said it."

Seraphine stared at him.

"You heard it," she whispered. "With your class locked. With your Root suppressed. You *heard* the Well."

"Is that—is that what it was?"

"That's what the Codex says. *The first stage of Listening is to hear. The second is to be heard. The third is to speak. The fourth is to command. The fifth is to become.*" She turned a page. "And it says—" She stopped. Read it again. "It says the Wells aren't part of the Lattice. They're *under* it. The Lattice draws on them, the way a mill-wheel draws on a river. But the river doesn't belong to the wheel." She looked up. "Elric locked the wheel, Lex. He didn't lock the river."

Nobody said anything for a long moment.

"Can you learn it?" said Antho quietly. "Listening. From the book?"

Seraphine shook her head slowly.

"No. Not from a book. The Codex says so. Over and over. *Listening cannot be learned from words. It must be learned from one who listens.*" She turned to the last page. "And then it says this." She read it aloud, her voice very soft. "*When the net is woven and the Wells are bound beneath it, and the last of us is hunted, let one remain. Let the youngest of the Listeners, who would not take the net, go into the white waste, to the Stillwater, and keep the old fire burning. And let any who would learn to listen, after the Weaving, seek him there.*"

"*Him*," said Wren.

"There's a name," said Seraphine. She traced the line of old script with one burned finger. "*Malachi.*"

The fire crackled.

"Nine hundred years ago," said Antho slowly. "The youngest of the Listeners. Nine hundred years."

"I know."

"He'd be dead. He'd have to be dead."

"The Barrow-King wasn't dead," said Wren. "Not exactly. The Thorn Mother wasn't. The Oracle wasn't." She looked at the fire. "Nothing in this whole stupid business has been as dead as it ought to be."

Seraphine was still looking at the page. Her face had gone very still.

"There's more," she said quietly. "A note. In the margin. Different hand. Much later. Faded." She squinted through her cracked lens. "It says—" She stopped.

"What?" said Lex.

Seraphine looked up at him. Then at Antho.

"It says, *Malachi, brother of Aurelian, who said to him on the night of the Weaving: you do not cage a storm by becoming the bars.*"

---

Antho found the other thing, later, in one of the bronze cases.

He'd opened it while the others slept. Lex woke in the grey hour before dawn and saw his brother sitting by the fire with the case open on his knees and a sheet of thin bronze foil in his hands, covered in tiny engraved Wellscript. He was staring at it.

"What is it?" said Lex quietly.

Antho didn't look up.

"It's about the Hollow," he said. "And the thing in it. The hunger." His voice was very flat. "The Listeners wrote about it. Before the Weaving. When it first woke, in the Sundering." He turned the foil over. "They didn't call it *the hunger*. They had another name for it."

"What?"

Antho was silent for a long moment.

"*The Unmaker*," he said.

The fire crackled. Ashka stirred in her sleep, and crooned, and settled.

"It says it doesn't want to eat things," said Antho. "Not really. Not the way animals eat. It says it wants to *unmake* them. To take them back to before they were. Before they had names. Before they had shapes." His hands were shaking slightly. "That's why the Gloamed go quiet. That's why the Inkwraiths eat words. That's why Morvane wants everyone to be silent." He looked up at Lex. His eyes were grey, but very dark. "It wants everything to go back to nothing. To before. To the quiet before the first word."

Lex didn't say anything.

"And there's a thing at the bottom," said Antho. "A note. Like the one in the Codex. Different hand." He held the foil up to the firelight. "*It cannot be killed. It can only be held. And whoever holds it, it will try to become.*"

He lowered the foil.

"*Whoever holds it, it will try to become*," he repeated quietly. "That's what's been happening to me. For eight years. That's what happened to Morvane." He looked into the fire. "That's what the Lattice has been doing. For nine hundred years. Holding it."

He put the foil back in the bronze case, very carefully, and closed the lid.

"I wonder what it's been trying to become," he said.

---

They left Qel'Varran the next morning.

Lex carried the grey Codex wrapped in oilcloth against his chest. Seraphine had made him promise not to let it get wet. Antho carried the bronze case. And as they waded back out through the black water toward the edge of the fen, Lex turned and looked back at the half-drowned dome one last time.

Deep under the water, in a dry round room behind a warded arch, a fire was burning on bare white stone. As it had for nine hundred years. As it would, he thought, for nine hundred more.

He couldn't feel it from here. He reached for it, the way he'd reached for it in the room, and found nothing but cold.

But he remembered the way it had leaned toward him.

*I am Flame. Who are you?*

"The Pale Wastes," said Seraphine from her litter, behind him. She had the old map open on her knees. Her finger was resting on a great blank space far to the north and east, beyond the fens, beyond the hills, marked only with a few faint lines and a single word in old script. "The white waste. Three weeks. Maybe more. Across nothing."

"Is there water?" said Wren.

"The Codex says there's a place called the Stillwater. In the middle." Seraphine paused. "It doesn't say anything about water between here and there."

"Wonderful," said Wren.

Lex turned his face north.

He didn't have a class. He didn't have his fire. He didn't have Ember. He had a hammer, and a book he couldn't read, and a flint and a steel, and three people and a dragon who were still, impossibly, following him.

And somewhere out in the white waste, if a nine-hundred-year-old margin note could be believed, there was a man who knew how to listen.

"Then let's go and find out," he said.
