# Two Kinds of Dark

Antho didn't move. That was the first thing Lex noticed. Anyone else, hearing a bowstring creak behind their head in the dark, would have flinched, or spun, or thrown up their hands. Antho just sat very still against the wall of the passage, and closed his eyes, and let out a slow, tired breath.

"Hello," he said, without turning around. "You must be the one who's been following him since the Frostvault."

Behind them, Wren's voice didn't waver. "How did you know that?"

"Ashka told me." The little dragon in his lap chirped. "She smelled you an hour ago. A girl, she said. Pine sap and wet leather and fear." Antho opened his eyes. "Mostly fear. Though you're hiding it well."

"I'm not afraid," said Wren.

"You've got an arrow pointed at the mad warlock of the Greyspine. If you weren't afraid, you'd be stupid. And I don't think you're stupid." Antho tilted his head slightly, still not turning. "Lex. Is she a friend of yours?"

Lex looked over his shoulder. Wren was standing at the bend of the passage, perhaps ten paces back, a dark shape against the faint grey of the moonlit mouth of the cave. Her bow was at full draw. The arrowhead, one of the blue Rimebite arrows from the Frostvault, was steaming faintly in the cold air, and it was pointed with perfect steadiness at the back of his brother's skull.

"Yes," Lex said. "She's a friend."

"Then would you ask her to put that away? I've had enough arrows in this cave for one lifetime." Antho's hand moved slowly over Ashka's torn wing. "So has she."

"Wren," said Lex. "It's all right. He's my brother."

Wren didn't lower the bow. "I saw what he did. I was at the bend. I saw the shadows. I saw his *eyes*."

"I know."

"He nearly killed you."

"*It* nearly killed me," said Lex. "Not him." He held her gaze through the dark. "It's all right. I promise."

For a long moment, nothing. Wren's arms didn't shake. Her face, what he could see of it, was white and set. Then, very slowly, she let the bowstring down, and lowered the bow, though she didn't put the arrow back in the quiver.

"Fine," she said. "But I'm keeping it out."

"That's fair," said Antho mildly. He turned his head at last and looked at her over his shoulder, and Lex saw Wren take an involuntary half step back. In the green light from the cavern, Antho's gaunt bearded face looked like something carved out of old wood. But his eyes were grey, and tired, and only human.

"You said *quadruple*," Wren said to Lex, not taking her eyes off Antho. "I should have said *ten times*."

---

Antho's lair was the cavern at the end of the passage.

It was bigger than Lex had expected. Round and high-roofed, like the inside of a bell, with walls of black rock that glistened with frost and a floor worn smooth by water a thousand years ago. Here, too, there were lanterns: hanging from every spike and outcrop, crowding the walls, dangling from ropes strung across the ceiling. Hundreds of them, maybe. Unlit, all of them, silent, swaying slightly in some draft Lex couldn't feel.

There was a fire in a ring of stones near the entrance. A real fire, small and smoky, burning on twisted lengths of dead pine. There was a heap of furs and rags against the wall that Lex supposed was a bed. There was a crude table made of a flat slab of rock balanced on two others, and on it, and around it, and spilling off it onto the floor in great drifts, there was *paper*.

Paper, and parchment, and birch bark, and the flat inside surfaces of torn leather, and anything else that would take a mark. All of it covered in writing. Charcoal and soot and ink made from something dark that Lex didn't want to think about. Drawings and diagrams, maps and lists, columns of numbers. And glyphs. Endless glyphs, Wellscript, written and rewritten and crossed out and written again, the way they'd been written on the walls of the passage.

And at the far end of the cavern, where the floor sloped down into darkness, there was the breach.

Lex felt it before he saw it. A cold that went deeper than the cold of the mountain. A pull, like standing at the edge of a cliff. The smell, wet ash and rot and nothing, so thick here that it coated the back of his throat. And then, as his eyes adjusted, he saw it: a place in the air at the bottom of the slope where the darkness was *wrong*. Not black. Blacker than black. A crack, ragged and vertical, perhaps the height of a man and a hand's width across, hanging in empty space with nothing on either side of it. As if someone had taken a knife to the world and cut it, and the cut had never healed.

The Gloam was seeping out of it. Slowly. A thin dark trickle, like water seeping from a cracked dam.

And around it, in a circle on the floor, there was a ring of fire.

Green fire. That was the light he'd seen from across the valley. It burned in a perfect circle around the crack, low and steady, without fuel, without smoke, a cold green flame like foxfire in a marsh. Glyphs had been scratched into the rock beneath it. And where the trickle of Gloam from the crack reached the green fire, it hissed and recoiled, like a slug touching salt.

"*Lady of Lanterns*," Wren whispered. She'd come in behind them, the arrow still in her hand. "What *is* that?"

"A wound," said Antho. He'd gone to the fire by the entrance and was crouched beside it, feeding it twigs, not looking at the crack. "A hole in the Lattice. A door into the place the thing comes from." He set Ashka down by the fire, and the little dragon curled up close to the flames with a contented chirp. "It opened about four years ago. I was hunting in the valley and I felt it tear, like a seam ripping in a coat. I came up here and found it. It was smaller then. I've been keeping it from getting bigger ever since."

"For four years," said Lex.

"For four years." Antho broke a stick over his knee. "The green fire's mine. A ward. I worked it out myself, from bits and pieces. It doesn't close the hole. I don't know how to close it. But it slows it down. Keeps most of the Gloam inside the circle. What gets past the fire, the ring of stones outside catches." He fed the broken stick into the flames. "Most of it."

"The black mist in the gullies," said Wren slowly. "The stuff the trappers talk about. The grey grass. The sheep."

"Is what gets past both." Antho didn't look at her. "I'm sorry. I'm doing my best. I can't always hold it. When I'm tired. When the thing in my head is loud. When the Wardens come, and I have to—" He stopped. "It gets out. A little. I can't stop all of it."

"And the stories," said Wren. "The warlock in the Hollowmaw. That the black mist comes from him. That he's the one doing it."

Antho laughed. It wasn't a nice sound. "I expect it looks that way from Thistledown."

Lex looked at the crack in the air. At the green fire guttering around it. At the trickle of Gloam seeping out, slow and steady and endless.

He thought about Elric on the green at Ashbourne, staring north at the mountains. *They came here tonight because they felt your seal crack. They'll be back.* And he thought about the hounds coming down the north road, and where they might have come from.

"How much has it grown?" he said quietly. "Since you found it?"

Antho was silent for a moment.

"It was a finger's width," he said at last. "When I found it. Four years ago." He looked at the crack, finally, for the first time since they'd come into the cavern. "It's been growing faster, these last few months. Like something on the other side is pushing harder. Or like something on this side that was holding it shut is getting weaker."

Lex thought about the seal. About the walls of a house getting thinner.

*[Lex,]* said Ember quietly. *[I think your father isn't only feeding your seal. I think he's feeding something much bigger. And I think it's failing.]*

---

They sat around Antho's fire and ate Mother Orla's bread, the last of it, gone hard as a brick, and some dried meat from Wren's pack, and a stew Antho made in a dented iron pot from snowmelt and something he said was rabbit, and that Lex decided not to ask about.

Antho ate like a starving man. Then he stopped halfway through his bowl and looked at it, and looked at Lex, and set it down.

"You should have the rest," he said. "You've come a long way."

"I'm fine."

"You're bigger than me. You need more."

"Antho. Eat the stew."

His brother looked at him for a long moment. Then, slowly, he picked up the bowl again. Lex pretended not to notice that his hands were shaking.

Wren sat on the far side of the fire with her bow across her knees and watched them both. She'd put the arrow back in her quiver eventually, after Antho had made the stew and given her a bowl and gone and sat as far from her as the cavern allowed. But she hadn't stopped watching him. Every time he moved, her eyes followed.

"You can ask," Antho said to her at last, without looking up from his stew. "Whatever it is you want to ask."

Wren's jaw tightened. "Did you kill the Wardens? The ones who came up here."

"Some of them."

"How many?"

Antho was quiet for a while. "Eleven," he said at last. "That I know of. That I remember." He set down his spoon. "There may be more. When the thing is out, when Clarity drops low enough, I don't always remember what happens. I just wake up afterward and there's—" He stopped. "And there's whatever there is."

"And you don't feel bad about it?"

Antho looked up at her then. Lex saw her flinch at the look on his face.

"I feel bad about all of it," Antho said quietly. "Every single one. Every day. I can tell you their faces. I can tell you which ones had wives, because they talked about them when they were dying. I can tell you that one of them was nineteen and had a cleft chin and kept calling for his mother." His voice didn't rise. It got flatter, and quieter, and somehow worse. "They came up here with crossbows and chains, to drag me back to the Spire, where the Wardens would have locked me in a white room and *studied* me for the rest of my life. And they shot Ashka out of the sky when she tried to protect me. And I still feel bad about every single one of them. I'd give anything to undo it." He looked back down at his stew. "But I can't. So I sit up here, and I keep the hole from getting bigger, and I try not to let anyone else come close enough to get hurt. That's all I've got."

Wren didn't say anything. After a moment, she looked away into the fire.

Lex watched his brother eat. He thought about the green fire burning around the breach, and the ring of charcoal-marked stones in the snow, and four years of keeping a door shut on something that wanted to come through.

*Look after your brother, Antho. I'll keep the door.*

"You've been doing what she did," he said slowly. "Haven't you. Keeping the door."

Antho went very still.

Then he set down his bowl, and got up without a word, and walked away to the far side of the cavern, and stood there with his back to them, looking at the green fire.

Lex didn't follow. After a while, Ashka uncurled from beside the flames and went padding across the cavern floor, her torn wing dragging slightly, and climbed up onto Antho's shoulder. He reached up and touched her without turning round.

"That was the wrong thing to say," Wren said quietly.

"I know."

"Or the right one."

"I know that too."

---

Later, when Antho had come back and sat down by the fire again and they'd all pretended nothing had happened, Lex asked if he could look at him.

"Look at me?"

"My eyes. The gold thing. It lets me see the Lattice. Your status, all of it. Even the parts that are hidden." Lex hesitated. "Elric said it's rude to do it without asking."

"Elric." Antho's face went sharp. "Elric Thorne? Father's friend? The one with the lightning?"

"He found me. In Ashbourne. He told me about you."

Antho was quiet for a moment. "He came up here once," he said. "Six years ago, I think. Seven. He stood outside the ring of stones and called my name. Said he was a friend of Father's and he wanted to help." He stared into the fire. "I didn't go out. It was a bad night. Clarity was low and the thing was loud, and it wanted him. It *really* wanted him." He frowned. "I remember that. It wanted him more than it wanted the Wardens. I thought it was strange at the time."

"Why would it want him more?"

"I don't know. Maybe it just liked the taste of him." Antho shook his head. "He went away after a while. I never saw him again." He looked at Lex. "Go on, then. Look. I want to know what it says too. I've never been able to read my own panel properly. It's all broken."

Lex looked.
