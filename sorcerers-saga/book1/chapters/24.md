# Fort Caldris

The crystal moved at night.

That was the first thing they learned, in the long hard weeks after Avaloria. It moved only at night, and only along the old Founders' roads, and only from waystone to waystone. Each evening at sunset, Lex and Antho would kneel beside it wherever it stood, in a clearing or a ruined waystation or the lee of a hill, and lay their hands on its cold faceted surface, and reach together through the Bloodbond into the Root. And each evening the crystal would go faint, and fainter, like a reflection when the water is stirred, until they could see the grass through it. And then it would be gone.

And in the morning, it would be standing beside the next waystone along the road. Ten miles on. Fifteen. Waiting for them.

"It's walking the threads," Seraphine had explained, the first time. She'd been white with exhaustion and shaking with excitement. "The Lattice's own threads. The ley-lines the Founders built the roads along. It can travel along them the way a bead slides along a string. But it needs the blood to push it. And it needs an anchor at each end. And it needs—" She'd stopped, and pushed her spectacles up her nose, and looked at the empty grass where the crystal had stood a moment before. "It needs to stop, every night. To rest. Like a man walking a long way."

"Like Father," Antho had said quietly.

It took them twenty-six days to walk from Mirrormere to the Vhessan border.

Lex remembered very little of it afterward. Only pieces. The crystal standing beside a grey waystone in the dawn mist, frost on its facets, their father's body upright inside it with eyes closed. His father's shape of light, so faint now that it appeared only at dusk and only for a few minutes, flickering beside the crystal like a candle at the end of its wick, speaking in a voice like wind through dry grass. Ashka spiraling high overhead every morning, growing a little bigger every week, until she was the size of a large dog and could carry a hare in her claws. Wren beside him on the road, her hand finding his when the others weren't looking. Antho walking alone at the front, the Keeper's Lantern glowing at his belt, Seraphine a few steps behind him, always a few steps behind him.

And the people.

They came in ones and twos at first. Then in dozens. Then more.

Elric's friends.

---

The first was a woman named Teodra Hale, who met them at a crossroads in the Fells on the fifth day: a broad weathered woman in battered leather armor, with a greatsword on her back and two missing fingers on her left hand. She was captain of a free company called the Ashen Banners, forty swords strong, and she'd fought beside Elric in the border wars twenty years ago and owed him her life twice over.

"He wrote me," she said, looking the brothers up and down with frank, unimpressed eyes. "Said there was work. Said there was a dead man in a crystal who needed carrying, and a white-faced thing in the east who wanted to stop us carrying it." She spat. "I said, Thorne, that's the stupidest thing you've ever written me. And he said, Teodra, you've never once turned down a stupid fight." She grinned. "He was right."

The Ashen Banners fell in behind them on the road.

Then came the Wardens.

Not many. A dozen, at first. Then thirty. Men and women in grey cloaks with their silver lattice-pins torn off, who came out of the hills and the forests and the back roads in small frightened groups, and stood at the edge of the camp, and asked if this was where the Reckoning was. They'd seen the World Notice. Every person under the Lattice had seen it. *The blood of the Founder walks the world again.* And some of them, a few, the ones who'd been quietly sickened for years by the Ceiling and the rigged Stones and the burned villages, had read it and walked away from their posts that same night.

"We didn't know where to go," said one of them, a thin young Warden with a burn scar on his cheek. "We just knew it wasn't the Spire anymore."

Then the Knights.

They came on the eighteenth day. Twenty of them, in plain armor with no plumes and no blue cloaks, riding hard down the road from the south. Their leader swung down from her horse in front of Lex and pulled off her helm, and it wasn't Elowen Dunmore. It was a stocky grey-haired woman with a face like a closed fist.

"Dame Ottilie Brand," she said. She looked at Garrick, who'd come up beside Lex, and something in her fist of a face softened very slightly. "Dunmore. Your girl sends her regards. She'd have come herself, but she's been told to stay in Highcrest by someone very important." A pause. "Someone with a crown on her shoes."

Garrick went very still. "The Princess?"

"Didn't say. Not my place to say." Dame Brand handed Lex a sealed letter. The seal was blue wax, with a small silver crown. "She said give you this. And she said tell the smith with the gold eyes: *I saw the World Notice. I keep helping the Sisters.*"

Lex opened the letter later, alone. It was very short. Four lines, in a firm, neat, elegant hand.

*To the blood of the Founder.*

*I cannot help you openly. Not yet. The High Warden watches me as closely as she watches you.*

*These twenty are mine. They are the best I have.*

*Bring him home. Then come and see me. —L.*

---

By the time they reached Fort Caldris, they were three hundred strong.

It stood on a high rocky spur at the edge of the Vhessan border, where the green hills of Caldor ended and the grey began. It was old. Older than the kingdom, Garrick said. A square keep of black stone with four squat towers, surrounded by a curtain wall, surrounded by a ditch, surrounded by a great open slope of rock and heather where nothing could approach without being seen. The Order had used it once as a border garrison. Now it was half-abandoned: a skeleton guard of thirty old soldiers, commanded by a castellan who'd been sent here as a punishment twenty years ago and had never been recalled.

His name was Brisk. Old Tamsin Brisk. He was seventy, bald, lame in one leg, and had served with Garrick in the eastern march when they were both young men. When he saw Garrick coming up the road at the head of three hundred people and a crystal, he limped out through the gate with his walking stick and stood in front of his old comrade and looked at him for a long time.

"You look terrible," he said at last.

"So do you."

"You're on notices. I've had twelve of them sent to me. *Unknown accomplice, large, grey, sword that freezes.*" Brisk squinted at Oathkeeper. "That it?"

"That's it."

Brisk grunted. He looked past Garrick at the crystal, standing beside the waystone at the foot of the road where it had appeared that morning. At the body inside it.

"Is that Arion Vey?" he said.

"It is."

"Thought so." The old castellan was quiet for a moment. "He came through here once. Sixteen years ago. On his way east, to Vhessa. Him and Morvane. Stopped the night. Arion beat me at cards." Brisk shook his head. "Nobody ever beats me at cards."

He turned and limped back toward the gate.

"Well, don't stand there," he called over his shoulder. "Bring him in. Bring them all in. I've got a cellar under the keep nobody's been allowed into for a hundred years. I expect that's what you want."

---

The Twin Stone was in the cellar.

They went down to see it the first evening: Lex and Antho and Seraphine and Elric and Garrick, with Brisk limping ahead of them with a torch. Down a long spiral stair of black stone, so old the steps were worn into hollows, to a heavy door bound with iron that hadn't been opened in a century. Brisk had the only key. It took him and Garrick together to turn it.

Beyond the door was a round room. Small. Low-ceilinged. Cold. The walls were the same black stone as the keep above, but carved, every inch of them, with Wellscript. Faint and old and half-worn away. And in the center of the floor, there was a stone.

It wasn't tall. Waist-high. White, like the stone of Avaloria, and glowing faintly from within. It was roughly the shape of the cracked old Stone on the green at Ashbourne, Lex thought, and his heart turned over. And set into its top, worn smooth, there were three carved hands. A right hand. A left hand. And between them, a third, larger hand, palm up.

"*Three of the blood*," whispered Seraphine. "Three pairs of hands. Mending the net from inside."

The crystal came that night. They didn't carry it. They didn't need to. Lex and Antho knelt beside it at sunset at the foot of the road, and reached together, and it faded away into nothing. And when they went down into the cellar at dawn, it was there. Standing beside the Twin Stone, upright, faceted, glowing softly, with their father's body inside.

Linked. Anchored. Home, or as near as it could get.

Arion came out that night.

Not his body. His shape. Faint and gold and flickering, beside the crystal, beside the Twin Stone. Fainter than he'd been at Avaloria. So faint that Lex could see the carved Wellscript on the walls straight through him. But his voice was steadier here. Stronger. The Twin Stone, he said, was feeding him a little.

"Four days," he said. "And then we try."

They were all there. Lex and Antho, and Seraphine, and Elric, and Garrick, and Wren, who'd come down the spiral stair behind Lex and stood close beside him. And Teodra Hale, and Dame Brand, and old Brisk leaning on his stick. The leaders of their small, ragged army, crowded into the low round room under the keep.

"Tell us how it works," said Garrick.

Arion told them.

On the night of the full moon, when the Lattice was strongest, Lex and Antho would lay their hands on the Twin Stone. The right hand and the left. And they would reach together, through the Bloodbond, through the Stone, all the way back along the threads to Avaloria, to the Anchor. And they would *take the weight*.

"All of it," said Arion. "Everything I've been holding for fifteen years. For a little while. Long enough." He looked at his sons. "It will be very heavy. It will be the heaviest thing you've ever carried. You'll feel the whole net pulling on you. Every thread. Every knot. Every breach." His voice was quiet. "You may not be able to hold it. If you can't, let go. Don't try to be brave. Let go, and it will come back to me, and we'll find another way."

"And if we can hold it?" said Lex.

"Then I'll step out." Arion looked at the crystal. At the body inside it. "Out of the light, and back into myself. It will take a few minutes. Ten, perhaps. I'll have to remember how to be a body again. How to breathe. How to have a heartbeat." Something like a smile touched his translucent face. "I've forgotten, a little."

"And then?"

"And then I lay my hand on the third place. The center. And the three of us hold it together. And we start to mend." Arion's grey eyes were very bright. "Not all at once. It will take a long time. Months. Years, maybe. But we'll have started. The net will stop unraveling. The breaches will start to close."

There was a silence in the low round room.

"And while you're doing all this," said Teodra Hale, "Morvane sits on his hands in the Blightmarch and lets you?"

Arion's face went still.

"No," he said. "He won't."

---

He wasn't sitting on his hands.

The reports came in every day. Scouts, mostly: the Ashen Banners' outriders and the young Wardens who'd deserted to them, who knew how to move quietly in the grey country. They rode out east from Fort Caldris every morning into the edge of the Blightmarch, and came back every evening, and their faces were grey.

He was gathering.

The Gloamed were moving. Not wandering, not drifting, the way Elric had seen them on the border in the spring. *Marching*. In columns. In ranks. Hounds and wraiths and stretched grey men and black-armored Hollow-knights, thousands of them, moving west through the grey country in great silent rivers, converging on a point three days east of Caldris where the old Vhessan road crossed the border. And over them, every day, circling in the grey sky: a vast dead shape with silent wings.

"Three days," said Teodra Hale, the evening after the last report came in. She was standing at the big table in the keep's hall, where they'd spread out Seraphine's map of Aldermere. She jabbed a three-fingered hand at the grey country east of the fortress. "He's three days out. Massing. When he's done massing, he'll come." She looked around the table. "Full moon's in two."

"So we have a day to spare," said Garrick.

"We have a day to spare if he doesn't come early. If he doesn't know what we're doing down in that cellar. If he can't feel it when you start pulling the net around." Teodra looked at Lex. "Can he feel it?"

Lex looked at his brother.

"He'll feel it," said Antho quietly. "The thing in him will. It always knows when the Root moves. It knew when Lex's seal cracked. It'll know when we pick up the Anchor."

"So the moment you start, he comes."

"Yes."

Teodra looked down at the map for a long moment. Then she straightened.

"Then we hold the walls," she said. "Three hundred, behind good stone, on a high spur, with a ditch. Against however many thousand he's got." She shrugged. "I've had worse odds." She paused. "Not *much* worse."

"We only have to hold until the ritual's done," said Garrick. "Ten minutes, Arion said. Then three of the blood are holding the net, and Morvane can't take the Anchor anymore, because it's not one man in a crystal. It's three of them, awake."

"Ten minutes," said Dame Brand. "Against an army." She smiled grimly. "I like those odds better."

"Ten minutes for him to step out," said Elric quietly from the end of the table. "And however long after that for the three of them to start the mending." He was looking down at the map. His face was unreadable. "And if anything goes wrong while they're doing it—if anyone gets into that cellar—"

"Then nobody gets into the cellar," said Garrick.

Elric looked up at him. "*Nobody*?"

The hall went quiet.

"Say what you mean, Thorne," said Garrick.

---

Elric said what he meant.

He did it slowly and carefully and with great reluctance, standing at the end of the long table in the firelit hall with the whole of the war council watching him. He took the silver token out of his cloak. The passkey. The lattice on one side and the tower and star on the other. He laid it on the map, in the middle of the grey country, where everyone could see it.

"This fell out of Seraphine Ilvane's pack," he said, "in the Old Drains under Highcrest. Three weeks ago. Lex saw it. Garrick saw it. It's a passkey from the Office of the High Warden. It opens every ward in the Spire. There are perhaps a dozen in existence."

Seraphine had gone white. She was sitting at the other end of the table beside Antho, and Lex saw her hands clench in her lap.

"She says it isn't hers," Elric went on. "She says she's never seen it. And Lex looked at her with his Root-sight, and her panel says she has no passkey bond." He held up a hand before anyone could speak. "I know. I know. I'm not saying she's lying about that. I'm saying a passkey bond can be scrubbed, by someone with enough skill. And this token's bearer signature has been scrubbed. Deliberately." He looked down the table at Seraphine. "By someone with Master-rank Inscribe."

"I didn't have Master-rank Inscribe until the Barrows!" Seraphine's voice cracked. "*You* know that! You were *there*!"

"I was." Elric nodded slowly. "And you've had it for six weeks since."

The silence in the hall was terrible.

"Here's what I'm asking," said Elric quietly. "Only this. On the night of the ritual, when the three of them are in that cellar with their hands on the Stone, holding the whole weight of the Lattice, completely defenseless, I'm asking that the door to the cellar be guarded. By people we're *sure* of." He looked around the table. "And that anyone we're *not* sure of be kept somewhere else. Safe. Comfortable. Watched." He spread his knotted hands. "That's all. For one night. Until it's done. And then, if I'm wrong, I'll get down on my knees and beg her pardon in front of all of you."

Nobody spoke.

Antho stood up.

He did it very slowly. The shadows rose up around his feet as he did, and the Keeper's Lantern at his belt flickered, and the temperature in the hall seemed to drop.

"No," he said.

"Antho—" said Lex.

"*No*." His brother's voice was quiet and very cold. "She's not going anywhere. She's going to be in that cellar with me. Writing down every thread we touch, every knot we tie. Because she's the only one here who can read what we're doing." He looked at Elric. "And because I trust her."

"I know you do," said Elric gently. "That's what worries me."

The shadows surged.

"*Enough*," said Garrick.

On the third night, the full moon rose over Fort Caldris.

It came up huge and gold over the eastern hills, over the grey country, over the bruise on the horizon that had grown and grown in the last two days until it filled half the sky. Lex stood on the wall and watched it rise. Beside him, Wren stood with her bow. Below them, in the courtyard, the Ashen Banners and the deserted Wardens and Dame Brand's twenty knights were taking their places on the walls. Torches. Crossbows. Cauldrons of oil. Teodra Hale's voice, rough and steady, calling orders.

Out on the grey slope below the fortress, nothing moved. Yet.

"He'll come," said Wren quietly. "The moment you start."

"I know."

"We'll hold." She didn't look at him. "Ten minutes. We'll hold for ten minutes."

"I know." He took her hand. "Not for me."

"No," she said. "*With* you."

She turned and kissed him, quick and hard, the way she had in the chamber at Avaloria. Then she let go and went to her place on the wall.

Lex went down to the cellar.

They were all there. Antho, kneeling by the Twin Stone, with Ashka curled on his shoulder and the Keeper's Lantern glowing at his belt. Their father's shape, beside the crystal, so faint now that he was barely more than a gold shimmer in the torchlight. Old Brisk at the door with his walking stick and a sword he hadn't drawn in twenty years.

And Elric.

He was standing by the door, beside Brisk, with his staff in his hand. His grey cloak. His brass storm-charms, glinting faintly. His face, lined and tired and unreadable.

"I'll guard the stair," he said quietly. "Me and Brisk. Nobody gets down here. Nobody."

Arion's shape turned toward him.

For a long moment, the two of them looked at each other across the low round room. The old sorcerer and the shape of light.

"Elric," said Arion softly. "Before we begin. Will you walk with me? Just for a moment?"

Something flickered across Elric's face. Lex couldn't read it.

"Of course," said Elric.

They went to the far side of the cellar together. Arion's faint gold shape and Elric's tall grey one. They stood by the curved black wall, close together, and spoke in low voices. Lex couldn't hear what they said. He didn't try. He knelt by the Twin Stone beside his brother and looked at the three carved hands on its white surface and tried to breathe.

It didn't take long. A minute. Two.

When Elric came back, his face was grey. His hands on his staff were white at the knuckles. He walked past Lex without looking at him and took his place by the door beside Brisk and stood there, very straight, staring at nothing.

Arion's shape came back to the crystal. He looked at his sons. His translucent face was very sad.

"What did he say?" said Lex quietly.

His father didn't answer for a moment.

"He asked me for something," he said at last. "Something he's asked me for before. Something I couldn't give him." He looked at the crystal. "I told him no. Again." His voice was very soft. "Gently. As gently as I could."

Lex turned his head and looked at Elric, standing by the door in the torchlight.

The old sorcerer was looking at the Twin Stone. At the three carved hands. And on his face was the expression Lex had seen in the mirror on the silver road. The starving man at the window.

"*Lexander*," said his father. "*Anthoren*. The moon is up. It's time."
