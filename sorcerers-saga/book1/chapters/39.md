# What Remains

They buried Garrick Dunmore in Highcrest, in a garden, under a pear tree.

It was the garden of a small grey house on a quiet street in the middle tiers of the city, halfway between the crowded Lower Town and the mansions above. The house had been his, once. His and Mara's. Elowen had kept it after her mother died, and kept the garden, and the roses her mother had planted, and the pear tree her father had planted on the day Elowen was born.

Mara Dunmore was buried under the roses. Elowen had asked if her father could lie beside her. Nobody had said no.

It was a small funeral. Elowen had wanted it small. Lex and Antho and Wren and Seraphine. Teodra Hale, in a clean shirt for once, with her greatsword on her back. Daxian Roole, in a plain brown coat. Hedda Vorne, with her cudgel. Brannoc, who had insisted on coming, who had walked into the garden with his huge hands clasped in front of him and his bald head bowed and said, very quietly, to Elowen, *I didn't know him long. But I knew him well enough.* Pell, with Wren's old bow. Dame Brand and the twenty knights who'd ridden with them from Mirrormere, what was left of them.

And Arion Vey, standing at the back, very straight, with his forgettable face and his silver temples and his hands clasped behind his back.

And at the gate, in a plain grey cloak with the hood up, so plain that nobody would have looked at her twice if not for the two Royal Guards standing very quietly on either side of her in the street, Queen Lysandra of Caldor.

The old king had died eight days after the World Notice that said *the first wound has been closed*. In his sleep, the physicians said. Peacefully. Some people in Highcrest whispered that he'd seen the notice, and smiled, and closed his eyes.

Lysandra had been crowned four days later. On the same day, she had dismissed High Warden Isaura Kell from office, in front of the whole High Council, for crimes against the realm. Kell had walked out of the Spire before the guards could reach her. Nobody knew where she'd gone.

The new queen didn't come into the garden. She stood at the gate, with her hood up, through the whole thing. And when it was over, she turned and walked away up the street without a word, and her guards went with her.

But Lex saw her face, just for a moment, before she turned. And it was wet.

---

Seraphine read the words.

Not the old Warden words, the ones they said over knights of the Silver Lattice. Elowen hadn't wanted those. *He broke that oath*, she'd said. *Or they broke it for him. Either way, he wouldn't want it.* So Seraphine had written something new. She stood at the head of the grave under the pear tree with a sheet of paper in her scarred hands and her cracked spectacles on her nose, and read it in her clear precise schoolteacher's voice.

"*Garrick Dunmore was a knight. Then he wasn't. Then he was again.*

"*He stood on the steps of a chapel for three days with half a sword so that forty people could live.*

"*He stood in the gate of Fort Caldris with three holes in him so that a hundred could run.*

"*He stood over me on the floor of Duskhold, so that I could write one name, and took a spear meant for me.*

"*He made a warlock wash in a stream. He taught a dragon to steal bacon. He was terrible at cards. He made up proverbs and pretended they were his father's. He told a smith's apprentice that a man who sharpens his sword at the battle has already bled.*

"*He swore an oath, on a sword that had been broken in shame and made whole in purpose. To stand on the steps. Wherever the steps were. For as long as he could stand.*

"*He kept it.*"

She folded the paper. Her hands were shaking.

"*Sir Garrick Dunmore*," she said. "*Who stood on the steps.*"

Nobody said anything for a long time.

---

Lex gave Elowen the sword afterward.

He'd carried it all the way from Duskhold. Wrapped in Garrick's own cloak, on Ashka's back, across the healing Blightmarch, over the green hills of Caldor, to Highcrest. He hadn't unwrapped it once. He hadn't been able to.

He unwrapped it now, in the garden, under the pear tree, and held it out to her across the fresh-turned earth.

Oathkeeper.

The blue-white sheen of the blade. The old cross-guard. The old leather-wrapped hilt. The silver pommel engraved with a lattice, which had been her great-grandfather's, and her grandfather's, and her father's. The three runes along the fuller: *Bulwark. Bind. Oath.* Dark now. Not glowing. The frost that had always formed on it in warm air wasn't forming.

Elowen looked at it for a long time.

"It's dark," she said. "The runes. They were always lit. When he carried it. I saw them on the quay, at the Rivergate. They were gold."

"*Oath*," said Lex. "*Bound to the one who swears upon it. Grows keener while the oath is kept.*" He swallowed. "He kept it. All the way. And then it was finished. So it went dark."

Elowen didn't say anything.

"It's yours," said Lex. "It was always going to be yours. He'd want you to have it." He held it out further. "You don't have to swear anything. You don't have to do anything. It's just your father's sword. And your grandfather's. You can hang it on the wall."

Elowen reached out and took the hilt.

She held it in front of her, point-down, the way her father had held it in the burned smithy on the night Lex reforged it. She looked at the dark runes. At the blue-white blade. At the silver pommel.

And then she did what her father had done.

She went down on one knee in the earth of the garden, beside the fresh grave, under the pear tree he'd planted on the day she was born. With the sword point-down before her and both hands on the cross-guard. And bowed her head.

"*I swear*," she said. Her voice was very steady. "*To stand on the steps. Wherever the steps are. For as long as I can stand.*" She lifted her head. Her dark eyes were wet. "*Like he did.*"

And Oathkeeper *rang*.

A long, clear, singing note, rising up through the branches of the pear tree into the grey Highcrest sky. The three runes along the blade flared gold. All at once. *Bulwark. Bind. Oath.* And frost bloomed along the steel, and spread, and sparkled in the thin autumn light like a field of snow at dawn.

Elowen stayed on her knee for a long moment, looking at the sword.

Then she stood up, and sheathed it in the battered old scabbard Lex had brought along with it, and it slid home with a sound like a sigh.

She looked at Lex across her father's grave.

"Thank you," she said. "For making it remember."

---

There were other funerals. Too many.

Old Tamsin Brisk, castellan of Fort Caldris, who had beaten everyone at cards except Arion Vey. They found what was left of him in the cellar under the burned keep, beside the dark Twin Stone. Arion buried him himself, at the foot of the spur below the fortress, and stood over the grave for a long time without speaking. And then knelt and laid his palm on the earth, and Lex felt him do something with the Root. Small. Gentle. When he stood up, there was a mark on the stone they'd set over the grave. An open hand, palm up. And in the palm, a playing card.

Lucius Vane, Grand Inquisitor of the Spire, who had died in the middle of the Thornhollow Pass with a thousand Gloamed things sitting confused in the road around him. Daxian Roole buried him at the top of the pass, on a ledge looking east, where Pell had lain on his belly and watched the grey come up the valley. Roole carved the name on the stone himself, with a chisel and a mallet, badly. *LUCIUS VANE.* And under it, after a long time thinking: *HE CUT THE STRINGS.*

"He did terrible things," Roole said, standing over it. "On the green at Ashbourne. In a hundred places before that. I hunted beside him for ten years. I saw what he was." He was quiet for a moment. "And at the end, he walked out alone into twenty thousand of them and gave his life to cut a hole. For people he'd have chained a month before." He shook his head. "I don't know what that makes him. I don't know if it balances anything."

"Maybe it doesn't have to," said Lex. "Maybe it's just what he did at the end."

Roole looked at him.

"You sound like your father," he said.

And the others. The ones whose names Lex didn't know. Ashen Banners. Wardens with their pins torn off. Unbound farmers from Fenmoor and Ashbourne. Knights from the causeway at Saltreach. Seraphine wrote down every name she could find. Pages and pages of them. Lex struck them into stone with *Remembrance*, the rune he'd found on the causeway in the Barrows. A long wall of grey stone, at the top of the Thornhollow Pass, beside Vane's grave. Every name glowing faintly gold for a moment as it went in, and then fading into the grey.

*I was here.*

Farmer Tobbin's name was on it. Lex struck it himself, and then had to stop for a while.

---

The Blightmarch began to heal.

Not quickly. Not all at once. Sixteen years of Gloam don't wash out of the land in a season. But the source was sealed. The first wound was closed. And without it, the grey had nowhere to come from.

Lex saw it from Ashka's back, flying west from Duskhold toward Highcrest. Here and there, in the grey country below, a smudge of green. A patch of grass in a grey field. A single tree with a few leaves on it, standing among grey skeletons. A stream running clear between grey banks. Small things. Very small.

But green.

*[It'll take years,]* said Ember. *[Maybe decades. The land remembers being hurt. Like Seraphine's scars.]* A pause. *[But it'll heal. The way she's healing.]*

And the people. The eleven thousand who'd been Quiet in Duskhold. And the twenty thousand who'd sat down in the road at the Thornhollow Pass when Morvane fell. And the three thousand on the causeway at Saltreach. Many of them came back. Slowly. Blinking. Like people waking from a long fever. Some of them remembered their names. Some of them didn't, at first, and had to be told. Some of them remembered everything they'd done while they were Quiet, and those were the worst, the ones who sat by themselves and wouldn't speak.

Ilsa, the Healer from Fenmoor, went among them. With the young Stormcaller who'd been a Laborer, and the old Herbalist who'd been a Farmhand, and every Unbound Healer and Herbalist and Lanternkeeper who'd marched east. They went among the waking and laid their hands on them and did what they could.

And some of them didn't come back at all. Some of them had been Quiet too long. Since the first year. Since before. They sat in the grey fields with their white eyes and didn't move.

Antho went among those. Every day, for weeks. With Shade. He'd sit beside them, one at a time, with his scarred hand on their shoulder, and listen. And sometimes, very rarely, something would stir. A flicker in the white eyes. A word. A name. And he'd call it out, gently, the way he'd called the names of the Drowned Dead on the causeway, and sometimes, very rarely, they'd come back.

Mostly, they didn't.

"I keep thinking," he said to Lex once, sitting by a fire in the healing grey country, "that it could have been me. If you hadn't come up the mountain. If you hadn't sung." He stared into the flames. "I'd have ended up like them. Eventually. Sitting in a field. Quiet." He was silent for a long moment. "I want to do for them what you did for me. And I can't. Not most of them."

"You're doing it for some of them," said Lex.

"Not enough."

"It's enough," said Lex. "It has to be enough. It's what we've got."

---

Wren's mother stopped coughing.

Not all at once. Not completely. Master Fenwick the physic said the grey-lung would always be there, a little, a scar on the inside where the Gloam had been. But with the source sealed and the Gloam gone from the mines at Coldwater, it stopped getting worse. And then, slowly, it started getting better.

Wren went to see her. In Highcrest, in the narrow stone house on Fennel Street. Lex went with her.

Maren Ashdown was sitting on the step in the thin autumn sunshine, as she'd been sitting the first time, with Tam and Robbie on either side of her. She was still thin. Still grey-threaded. But her color was better. And when she saw her daughter coming up the street, she stood up.

On her own.

Wren stopped in the middle of the street.

And then she did something Lex had never seen her do. She didn't run. She *flew*. She stepped off the cobbles into the air and the wind took her, Windstep, Tempest, Gale at Speaking, and carried her up the street in a rush of air that sent every shop sign rattling and every hat flying, and set her down on the step in front of her mother as lightly as a leaf.

Maren Ashdown stared at her daughter.

"*Wrennie*," she said faintly. "*You're—you just—*"

"*Pathfinder*," said Wren. She was grinning so widely her face looked like it might split. "*Rare. Level forty-one. No cap.*" Her voice cracked. "*Mam. No cap.*"

Maren Ashdown looked at her for a long moment.

Then she started to cry, and to laugh, and to cough, all at once, and pulled her daughter into her arms. And Tam and Robbie piled on top of them both, shouting, and the whole street came out of their houses to see what the noise was.

Lex stood at the bottom of the step and watched.

After a while, Maren looked up at him over Wren's head.

"*Hammer*," she said.

"Ma'am."

"You brought her back."

"She brought herself back. I just—"

"*Don't*," said Maren Ashdown, exactly the way her daughter said it. "*Don't try. Do.*" She held his gaze. "You did."

---

Arion went to Ashbourne in the first week of winter.

Lex went with him. Antho too. And Wren, and Seraphine, and Ashka, who landed on the green beside the white Stone and sent every child in the village shrieking for joy.

Brannoc was in the forge.

Of course he was. He was at the anvil, with Hedda Vorne on a stool beside him reading from the *Book of Making*, and Pell pumping the bellows, and a half-finished sword glowing orange in the tongs. The forge was full of the music of the work. The roar of the bellows. The hiss of the slack tub. The *chang* of the hammer. And every few strikes, a faint gold flicker, as a rune went into the steel.

Straighter, now. Still a little crooked. But straighter.

He looked up when they came in.

He saw Lex first. His soot-streaked face split into a grin. And then he saw the man standing beside Lex. Lean and grey-eyed and silver at the temples, with a forgettable face and his hands clasped behind his back.

The grin went away.

Brannoc set down the hammer.

He set down the tongs. He wiped his hands on his leather apron, slowly, very carefully, the way he did when he was thinking about something. And then he came around the anvil and walked across the forge and stopped in front of Arion Vey.

He was a head taller. Twice as broad. He looked down at the man in front of him for a long, long time.

Arion looked back up at him. He didn't say anything.

"*You're him*," said Brannoc at last.

"*I'm him*."

"*You left.*"

"*I left.*"

"*Fifteen years.*" Brannoc's voice was very quiet. Very even. The voice of a man holding something very heavy very carefully. "*She waited for you. Seren. Every night. A lantern in the window. Every single night until the fire.*"

"*I know.*"

"*And the boys. The little one came to me with nothing. Eleven years old. Nothing. Not a coat. Not a shoe. Not a family.*" Brannoc's jaw worked. "*I gave him a cot in the loft and a hammer and eight years of breakfasts. And I'd do it again. Every day. But it should have been you.*"

"*Yes*," said Arion. "*It should have been me.*"

The forge was very quiet. Pell had stopped pumping the bellows. Hedda had closed the *Book of Making*. Lex stood in the doorway with his heart pounding.

Brannoc looked at Arion for a long moment more.

Then he drew back his huge right fist, and punched him.

---

It wasn't a hard punch. Not by Brannoc's standards. Lex had seen him punch through a barn door once, for a bet. This was gentler than that.

It still knocked Arion Vey flat on his back on the forge floor.

He lay there. Staring up at the rafters. Blood trickling from his nose.

Brannoc stood over him, flexing his hand.

"*That*," said Brannoc, "*was for Seren.*"

Arion looked up at him.

"*Fair*," he said, through the blood.

And then Brannoc reached down with the same huge hand and took Arion's, and hauled him up off the floor in one easy pull, as if he weighed nothing. And held on.

"*And this*," said Brannoc, and his voice cracked, "*is for bringing the boy back. Both of them. And for the anvil singing. And for every Ceiling in this village. And for whatever you did, for fifteen years, in that crystal, holding the world together.*" He was shaking Arion's hand like he was trying to pump water out of it. "*Your son's the best smith I ever trained. You hear me? The best. And if you want to argue about it—*"

"*I don't*," said Arion. He was laughing. And crying. And bleeding. "*Lady help me, I don't. I agree with every word.*"

"*Good*," said Brannoc. "*Then you can buy me a drink.*"

---

They spent the whole night in the Crooked Kettle.

Arion and Brannoc, at the corner table, by the fire. Talking. Lex watched them from across the room. Brannoc doing most of it, at first, in his great rumbling voice, with his huge hands moving. And Arion listening. Leaning forward. Asking questions. Laughing, sometimes. Once, very quietly, weeping, with his face in his hands, while Brannoc sat across from him and didn't say anything at all, just put one huge hand on his shoulder and left it there.

*[What do you think they're talking about?]* said Ember.

*Me*, thought Lex. *Antho. Mam. The forge. Fifteen years.*

*[Fifteen years is a lot to talk about.]*

*They've got time now.*

Mother Orla kept bringing them ale. Old Mag came in and sat down with them for an hour and told Arion something that made him go very still and then laugh until he choked. Pell hovered nearby, wide-eyed, listening to every word.

Toward dawn, Lex saw Brannoc pull something out of his apron pocket and put it on the table between them.

It was a letter. Old. Folded. The paper yellow and soft.

Arion looked at it. Then at Brannoc. Then he picked it up, very carefully, and unfolded it, and read it.

His face did something Lex couldn't see from across the room. But he saw his father's shoulders shake.

He found out later what it was. Brannoc told him, the next morning, at the anvil.

"*Your mother*," said Brannoc. "*She gave it to me. The year before the fire. Said if anything ever happened to her, and that man ever came back, I was to give it to him.*" He struck the iron. *Chang.* "*I've had it in my apron eight years. Never read it. Never opened it. Wasn't mine to open.*"

"What did it say?"

Brannoc struck the iron again. *Chang.*

"*Don't know, lad*," he said. "*Didn't ask. Didn't need to.*" He paused, the hammer raised. "*But your father read it three times. And then he folded it up and put it inside his coat, against his chest. And then he said: She knew. She always knew. Even then.*"

He brought the hammer down. *Chang.* A rune went into the steel. Gold. Almost straight.

"*Knew what?*" said Lex.

Brannoc looked at him across the anvil.

"*Didn't ask that either*," he said.

---

Antho went back to the Hollowmaw in the second week of winter.

Lex went with him.

They flew up on Ashka, on a clear cold morning, over the grey Vale that was beginning, here and there, to go green again. Up past Thistledown, where someone had taken down the painted sign that said *NO WARLOCKS* and burned it on the green, and replaced it with a new one that said *THE LAST HEARTH: ALL WELCOME*. Up past the Teeth and the Frostvault. Up to the great black cleft in the face of the highest mountain.

The Gloam was gone. The ring of charcoal stones was still there, scattered in the snow, but nothing pressed against it anymore. The stunted grey pine at the base of the cliff had a single green shoot growing out of its side.

And above the cleft, on its flat rock, facing out over the world, Seren's lamp was still burning.

Antho stood in front of it for a long time.

Then they went into the cave.

The lanterns were all still there. Hundreds of them. Hanging from every spike and outcrop and rope in the long passage and the great bell-shaped cavern. Tin lanterns and brass lanterns and paper lanterns and the fine glass carriage-lantern with the broken handle and the battered miner's lamp. Eight years of them. Collected one by one.

Unlit.

"I want to light them," said Antho quietly. "All of them." He looked at Lex. "Can you?"

It took them nine days.

Lex struck *Ardent* into every one. Every tin frame, every brass bowl, every clay base. Some of them he could strike with Anvilsong. Some of them, the fragile ones, the paper and the glass, he had to coax, the way he'd coaxed the rune into Seren's lamp: warming the surface with Flame until it would take the mark, pressing the rune in with the tip of Aurelian's little hand-hammer, slowly, a fraction at a time. His hands ached. His eyes watered. Antho sat beside him every hour of every day, holding each lantern while he worked on it, and saying nothing.

And when each one was done, Antho lit it. Not with Flame. He couldn't. With a single spark from Ashka, who sat in the mouth of the cavern breathing tiny careful sparks into each lantern as Antho held it up to her, her great golden eyes half-closed with concentration.

And then he'd hang it back up. Where it had been.

On the ninth night, they lit the last one.

It was the fine glass carriage-lantern with the broken handle. The one that looked like it had come from some noble's coach. Antho held it up. Ashka breathed a spark. The little *Ardent* rune Lex had coaxed into its brass base caught, and the flame came up gold behind the glass.

Antho hung it on its spike at the mouth of the passage, where the first lantern had hung, the one Lex had seen on the night he came up the mountain alone.

Then they walked out of the Hollowmaw together, down the long passage, between the lanterns, and stood on the snow outside and turned around and looked back.

The mountain was full of light.

Every lantern. Hundreds of them. Burning gold in the long passage and the great cavern, so many that the light poured out of the great black cleft in the mountain's face like light pouring out of an open door. It lit the snow. It lit the ring of charcoal stones. It lit the rock above, where Seren's lamp sat on its flat stone, small and gold among all the others now. Not alone anymore.

From across the valley, from the Frostvault's exit, from the Teeth, from Thistledown far below, from anywhere in the whole eastern Greyspine, anyone looking up would see it. The mountain that had been dark for eight years. The cleft where the mad warlock lived.

Blazing with lanterns.

*A light left on for the lost.*

Antho stood in the snow and looked at it with tears frozen on his face.

"*I'm going to stay*," he said.

Lex turned his head.

"*Here*. In the Hollowmaw." His brother's voice was very quiet. "*Not hiding. Not this time.*" He looked at the blazing cleft. "*Malachi said there are things that need keeping. Old things. Forgotten things. The Listeners' records. The Founders' codices. Everything the Order tried to destroy.*" He pulled the grey Codex out of his coat, the one from Qel'Varran, which Seraphine had given him to keep. "*Somebody needs to keep them. Learn them. Teach them. Somebody who listens.*" He looked at Lex. "*And somebody needs to watch the door. In case it ever opens again.*"

"Alone?"

"*Not alone.*" Antho looked at Ashka, sprawled in the snow beside them, enormous and blue and shimmering in the lantern-light. "*And Seraphine's going to be Royal Archivist in Highcrest. The Queen asked her.*" His mouth twisted. "*She says Highcrest is only four days' flight by dragon. She says she'll come every month. With books.*" He paused. "*She says she'll finish her sentences.*"

Lex laughed. It came out wet.

"And you?" said Antho. "What will you do?"

Lex looked out over the dark mountains. At the Vale, far below, where a white Stone glowed on a green. At the faint lights of Ashbourne, where an old smith and a one-armed armorer were teaching each other to strike runes.

"I'm going to teach," he said. "Anyone who wants to learn. To forge. To listen. To strike runes." He looked down at his hands. "There are four hundred and twelve Unbound in Ashbourne alone who don't know how to use their new classes. Thousands more, everywhere we've been. And none of them have a Malachi." He swallowed. "Someone has to show them."

"A school," said Antho.

"A forge," said Lex. "A big one. With lots of anvils."

His brother looked at him for a long moment.

Then he smiled. The crooked one. The one that was exactly like Lex's.

"Of course," he said. "Of course you'd make it a forge."

---

They stood on the snow in front of the blazing Hollowmaw for a long time, the two of them, side by side, shoulders touching, with a dragon at their feet and a mountain full of lanterns at their backs.

"Lex," said Antho at last.

"Yeah."

"Do you remember the stone? At the millpond? That skipped five times?"

"I remember."

Antho was quiet for a moment.

"When this is all done," he said. "When the school's built, and the Codex is copied, and everything's quiet. The good kind of quiet." He looked at Lex. "Will you come back here? To the mountains? There's a lake, on the other side of the Teeth. Very still. Very flat." Something moved at the corner of his mouth. "Perfect for skipping stones."

Lex looked at his brother.

"Yes," he said.

"I'll beat you," said Antho. "I've been practicing. For eight years. On that tarn behind the cave."

"You won't."

"I will. I'll count."

"You always count."

"I always count," Antho agreed. "And this time, I won't lie about the number."

They laughed. Both of them. Standing in the snow in front of a mountain full of lanterns, two brothers who had been estranged for eight years and had found each other in the dark. Laughing like idiots.

And above them, on its flat rock, Seren's little lamp burned on, gold and steady.

Not alone anymore.
