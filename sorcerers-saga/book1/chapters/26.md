# The Depths of Desolation

The survivors scattered on the third day.

There was no choice. Teodra Hale said it bluntly, standing in the heather at the head of the ragged column with her greatsword over her shoulder and a bloody bandage around her head. A hundred people moving together through open country left a trail a blind man could follow. And they were being followed. Ashka had seen them from the air: grey riders on the southern road, and black shapes moving in the hills to the east, and once, very high, a vast grey dragon circling.

"We split," said Teodra. "Small groups. Different directions. Meet again at the Hollin's Cross ruins in a month, if we're alive." She looked at Lex. "Not you."

"What?"

"Not you. Not your brother. Not the girl with the burns." Teodra's face was hard. "They're not hunting us, smith. They're hunting *you*. Every group you travel with is a group that dies." She jerked her head south, toward the road where the grey riders were. "The Spire's put out a new notice. I heard it from a shepherd yesterday. *The wildcaster brothers opened Fort Caldris to the Blight. Three hundred dead.* Five thousand crowns. Each."

"That's a lie."

"Course it's a lie. Kell doesn't care. She's got a story that makes her look right and makes you look like monsters, and Thorne's not around to contradict it." Teodra spat. "Five thousand crowns is enough to make honest men into dogs."

Lex didn't say anything.

Teodra looked at him for a long moment. Her broad scarred face softened, very slightly.

"I've been a sellsword for thirty years," she said. "I've fought for kings and merchants and lords and thieves. I've never once fought for something I believed in." She paused. "I believed in this. In that crystal. In your father walking out of it." She shook her head. "Thorne took that from me too. But I'm not done." She put her three-fingered hand on his shoulder. "Get your fire back, smith. However you do it. Then send for me. I'll come."

Dame Brand said much the same, more briefly, before she took her knights west toward Highcrest. *I'll tell her what happened. All of it. She'll know the truth, even if no one else does.*

By noon, the hills were empty.

There were four of them left. Lex. Antho. Wren. Seraphine, on a litter of cloaks and ash-poles that Lex and Antho carried between them. And Ashka, gliding overhead on her silver-framed wing, watching.

---

They found the tower on the fifth day.

It was Wren who saw it. A grey stub of stone on a ridgetop, half-hidden in a stand of twisted rowans. An old Founders' watchtower, Seraphine said faintly from the litter, when they got close enough for her to see the open hand and the flame carved over the door. Built nine hundred years ago, abandoned for six hundred. The roof had fallen in. The stair had rotted away. But the ground floor was dry, and the walls were thick, and the door, when Lex put his shoulder to it, still swung on its hinges.

They carried Seraphine inside and laid her on a heap of dead bracken against the wall.

And then they stopped.

They hadn't stopped since Caldris. Five days of walking, carrying, hiding, walking again. Five days of looking over their shoulders, of sleeping in shifts, of eating whatever Ashka caught and whatever Wren could find. Five days of not thinking.

Now they stopped. And the thinking came.

---

Lex tried to forge on the second night.

He knew it was stupid. He knew it wouldn't work. But Antho had been bad the night before, very bad, and Lex had sat up with him until dawn, and he'd been thinking, all through the long dark hours, about the Keeper's Lantern. About twelve *Hush* runes on a black iron frame. About how he'd made it once, in Hedda's forge, and how he could make it again.

He had iron. A few crossbow bolts left in his pack, the last of the Wardens' bolts from the Hollowmaw. He had Anvilsong. He had a fire, a real fire, built of dead rowan in a ring of stones in the middle of the tower's floor.

He didn't have Heat Shaping. He didn't have Ember Strike. He didn't have Runic Forging, or Forge Sense, or anything else. When he reached for them, in the old way, through the silver panel, there was nothing there. Just the dark. Just the locked door.

So he did it the old way. The way Brannoc had taught him before there was any Lattice in it at all.

He heated a bolt in the fire until it glowed. He laid it on a flat stone. He struck it with Anvilsong.

It was all wrong. The fire wasn't hot enough; rowan wood burned too fast and too cool. The stone wasn't an anvil; it was a rock, and the bolt skidded across it. Anvilsong was far too heavy for fine work; it was a war-hammer, not a tool. The iron cooled before he could shape it. He put it back in the fire. Heated it again. Struck again. It bent wrong. He straightened it. It cracked.

He tried to put a rune into it.

*Hush.* He knew the shape. He'd struck it twelve times into the Keeper's Lantern, in Hedda's forge, with Ember reading the notes aloud to him. He knew every curve of it.

He struck it into the cooling iron.

Nothing. No gold. No glow. No ring. Just a dent in a bent crossbow bolt. A shape. A scratch. Meaning nothing.

He tried again. And again. And again.

At some point he realized Wren was sitting beside him.

He didn't know how long she'd been there. He didn't look at her. He was staring at the bolt on the stone, at the twelfth dead *Hush* scratched into its cooling iron, at the crack running through the middle of it.

"It doesn't work," he said. His voice sounded strange. "It doesn't work. I can't—I can see the shape. I know the shape. But it doesn't *go in*. It just sits there." He put down the hammer. His hands were shaking. "It's just a dent."

Wren didn't say anything.

"Three months," said Lex. "Three months ago I was a smith. Level nine. Spirit of four. And I was fine. I was *fine*." He laughed. It came out cracked. "And then Elric walked into the forge and told me I was something else. Something more. And I believed him. And it was *true*. It was true for three months." He picked up the bent, cracked bolt and looked at it. "And now I'm a smith again. Level nothing. Spirit of four. And I can't even make a lantern."

"Lex—"

"What am I?" He turned and looked at her. His eyes were burning. "Without it? Without the fire, without Ember, without the class? What am I? I'm *nothing*. I'm a blacksmith's apprentice with a heavy hammer and a dead man's name. I can't heal you. I can't protect Antho. I can't save Father. I can't do *anything*."

Wren looked at him for a long moment.

Then she reached out and took the bent bolt out of his hand, and set it down on the stone, and took his hand instead.

"I met you," she said, "in the Last Hearth. In Thistledown. You walked in with a hammer on your shoulder and asked Grell for a guide to the Hollowmaw, and everyone in the room went quiet." She turned his hand over in hers, looking at his palm. At the burn scars. At the calluses. "You were level eight. You'd forgotten to spend your points. You didn't know how to fight. You'd nearly been eaten by a bear."

"I know."

"And I watched you. While you talked to Grell. And then while you walked back across the room." Her thumb moved across his palm. "You had a hole in your coat, on the left shoulder, where you'd fallen in the stream. You had mud to your knees. You looked like you hadn't slept in a week." She looked up. "And when Grell told you no, you didn't shout. You didn't threaten him. You said *thank you for your help*, and turned to go." Something moved at the corner of her mouth. "Nobody ever says thank you to Grell."

Lex didn't say anything.

"I didn't follow you because of your class, Hammer," said Wren. "I didn't even know your class. I didn't know about the fire or the gold eyes or the Root or any of it." She held his gaze. "I followed you because you paid me in full before we left. Because you fixed my bow without being asked. Because you sat by my campfire and told me about your brother, and you weren't ashamed." Her voice dropped. "Because you're kind. And stubborn. And you don't give up. And none of that was ever in your panel."

Lex's throat had closed up.

"The Lattice didn't make you," said Wren. "It just gave you some numbers. You were already *you* before the numbers." She squeezed his hand. "You're still you. Level nothing. Spirit of four. You're still the idiot who walked into the warlock's cave and sang to him."

He couldn't speak.

So he didn't. He just sat there by the fire, in the ruined tower, with the bent bolt on the stone and his hand in hers, and after a while she leaned her head on his shoulder, and they sat like that until the fire burned down.

---

Antho's worst night came on the fourth.

It had been building since Caldris. Since the lantern shattered. Lex could feel it through the Bloodbond: the voice in his brother's head, roaring, unchained, hammering at the door Antho was holding shut with nothing but his own will. Every day his Clarity sank a little lower. Every night it sank further.

It did, a little. Small things. Antho would stop in the middle of a sentence and his eyes would flicker black and his lips would move with words that weren't his. *Warm. Hungry. Little brother.* And then he'd shake his head and blink and his eyes would come back grey and he'd say, very quietly, *sorry. Sorry. What was I saying?*

He stopped sleeping. He sat up all night by the fire with his hands pressed to his temples and his bone flute in his lap, playing the Lantern Song over and over, until his lips cracked and bled. Ashka wouldn't leave him. She lay across his feet and crooned to him in her deep rumbling purr, and sent him pictures Lex could feel through the Bloodbond: *Sky. Lightning. Antho laughing. Antho, Antho, stay.*

On the fourth night, the flute stopped.

Lex woke to silence. Total silence. No flute, no crooning, no crackle of the fire. He sat up. The fire had burned down to embers. In their faint red glow, he saw his brother.

Antho was standing in the middle of the tower floor. Very still. His back to Lex. His arms at his sides. And the shadows were rising off him. Slowly. Silently. Not in a storm, the way they had in the cellar. In a column. A single smooth black column rising from his feet to the broken roof and out into the night sky, like smoke from a chimney on a windless night.

Ashka was pressed against the far wall, her wings mantled, her golden eyes wide, absolutely silent.

"Antho," said Lex.

His brother turned around.

His eyes were black. Completely black. Not a pinprick of grey. And his face was calm. That terrible, peaceful, serene calm Lex had seen in the mirror on the silver road. The face of a man who had never once heard a voice in his head, because he *was* the voice.

"*Hello, little brother*," it said.

Lex got up.

"*You're so warm*," said the thing wearing Antho's face. It took a step toward him. "*I've been wanting you since the cave. Since you walked in with your fire and your hammer and your stupid song. So bright. So warm.*" Another step. "*And now the fire's locked away. And the little voice in your head is gone. And you're just warm. Just meat and blood and a little bit of heat.*" It smiled. "*Nobody to stop me.*"

"I'm right here," said Lex.

"*I know. That's what I'm saying.*"

It came at him.

Not with shadows. With hands. Antho's hands. Long and scarred and cold as ice. They closed around Lex's throat.

Lex didn't fight. He didn't have anything to fight with. He was a nineteen-year-old smith with base stats, and his brother was level thirty-eight, and the thing wearing him had nine hundred years of hunger behind it.

He put his arms around his brother instead.

He wrapped them around Antho's thin body and held on. The way he had in the passage of the Hollowmaw, the first night. The way he had in the Thornwood. The way he had in the cellar at Caldris. He held on, with the cold hands closing around his throat, and he put his mouth next to his brother's ear, and with what breath he had left, he sang.

*Little light, little light, burning low.*

The hands tightened.

*Keep the door for the ones who go.*

He couldn't breathe. The grey was creeping in.

*If the dark comes calling—*

And from the bracken against the wall, faint and cracked and feverish, another voice joined his.

*—don't you mind—*

Seraphine. She'd woken. She was lying on her litter with her burned face turned toward them and her eyes open, unfocused without her spectacles, and she was singing. She didn't have much of a voice either. It shook and cracked and wandered off the tune. She'd only heard the song a handful of times, around the campfire, on the road north. But she knew the words.

*—a lantern's lit for the ones behind.*

The hands loosened.

Just slightly. Just enough for Lex to drag in one breath.

He sang again. Seraphine sang with him. Two broken voices in a ruined tower in the middle of the night.

*Little light, little light, burning low...*

And then a third voice.

Very faint. Very far away. Coming from somewhere deep inside the thing that had its hands around Lex's throat. Antho's voice. Cracked to pieces. Drowning.

*...keep the door...*

The hands came off Lex's throat. He felt his brother's body go rigid in his arms. Felt it shake. Felt the war going on inside it, the voice and the man, the hunger and the boy who'd collected lanterns.

"*Lex*," said Antho. His own voice. Barely. "*Lex, I can't—it's too strong—I can't hold it—*"

"*You don't have anything. He took it.*"

On the silver road. *I keep the door. That's what I am.*" He pressed his forehead against his brother's. "So keep it. Just for tonight. Just until morning. I'll be right here. I won't let go."

They stood like that all night.

Lex didn't know how. He would never know how. His legs should have given out. His arms should have given out. But they didn't. He stood in the middle of the ruined tower floor with his brother in his arms and sang the Lantern Song until his voice was gone, and then kept singing it in a whisper, and then just kept *thinking* it, through the Bloodbond, over and over and over. And Seraphine sang from her bracken until she fell back into her fever. And Wren woke, and saw them, and didn't say anything at all. She just came and stood on Antho's other side and put her arms around both of them. And Ashka crept out from the wall and wound herself around their ankles, crooning.

"It's never been that bad," Antho said later. His voice was a ruin. He was sitting by the rekindled fire with a blanket around his shoulders, shaking. "Not since the first year. Not since before the cave." He stared into the flames. "It was the lantern. Without it—" He stopped. "I didn't know how much it was doing. I didn't know how close it was."

"I'll make you another," said Lex.

Antho looked at him. "With what?"

Lex didn't answer.

"You tried," said Antho quietly. "I saw you. Two nights ago. With the bolt and the rock." He wasn't unkind. Just very tired. "It didn't work, Lex. It doesn't work without the fire. You know that."

"Then I'll get the fire back."

"How?"

Lex didn't have an answer for that either.

"Elric locked it with Root," said a voice from the bracken.

They both turned. Seraphine was awake again. Her face was still grey and her burns still raw, but her eyes were clear, and she'd found her spectacles somewhere, one lens cracked. She was looking at them both.

"Elric locked your class with Root," she said. Her voice was hoarse and thin but very precise. "Stolen Root, but Root. That's the deepest layer. The foundation. Nothing in the Lattice can touch it." She coughed, and winced. "Nothing in the Lattice can *unlock* it, either. No skill. No Prerogative. No item. Not even another Vey, I think, unless they have more Authority than the one who set the lock. And Elric took sixty-four points from your father."

"So it's permanent," said Lex flatly.

"I didn't say that." Seraphine pushed herself up on one elbow. It obviously hurt. She did it anyway. "I said nothing *in the Lattice* can unlock it."

Antho went very still.

"There was magic before the Lattice," said Seraphine. "The Barrow-King was a sorcerer-king before the Lattice. The Thorn Mother was bound before the Lattice. And the Founders themselves were Listeners. They used the old magic. The elemental magic. The magic that doesn't have classes or levels or panels." She looked at Lex. "Aurelian was a Listener. And a smith. That's how he made the Lattice. He *forged* it. With the old magic. With fire." She coughed again. "The Lattice is built on top of the old magic, Lex. Like a house built on a foundation. If Elric's locked the house, then you need to go *under* the house."

"How?" said Lex. "How do I go under it?"

"I don't know." Seraphine sank back onto the bracken. "Nobody knows. The Listeners died out nine hundred years ago. The Order hunted down the last of them. All their records were destroyed."

"*All* of them?" said Antho.

Seraphine closed her eyes. She was quiet for so long that Lex thought she'd fallen asleep again.

"Not all of them," she said at last. "There's a place. The Drowned Archive. Qel'Varran. It's in my footnotes. A Founders' library, older than the Leafbound Archive. It was half-sunk in a fen when the rivers changed course, six hundred years ago. The Order never found it. They didn't know where to look." She opened her eyes. "Some of the commentaries say the Founders kept the Listeners' records there. The old ones. From before the Weaving. If there's anything left anywhere about magic without the Lattice—"

"Where?" said Lex.

"North," said Seraphine. "Two days, maybe three. In the Saltmere Fens." She smiled weakly. "I've always wanted to see it."

---

They left the tower the next morning.

Lex carried the front of Seraphine's litter. Antho carried the back. Wren went ahead, scouting, and Ashka glided overhead, and they walked north through rolling grey hills under a grey sky.

Antho's Clarity held at twenty-four. Barely. He didn't play the flute anymore; his lips were too cracked. But he walked. And when the voice in his head grew loud, and his eyes flickered black, Lex would set down his end of the litter and go back and put his hand on his brother's shoulder, and wait, and after a while Antho's eyes would come back grey, and they'd pick up the litter and go on.

On the second day, the hills gave way to flat land. Wet land. Reeds and sedge and black pools of still water, stretching away to the horizon under a sky the color of old pewter. The air smelled of salt and rot. Herons stood motionless in the shallows and rose on great slow wings as they passed. Mist lay on the water in long grey skeins.

"The Saltmere," said Seraphine from her litter. "The fens. The rivers come down out of the hills and spread out into the sea marshes here. Hundreds of square miles of it." She was quiet for a moment. "People drown here. Every year. The ground looks solid and isn't."

"Wonderful," said Wren.

They went carefully. Wren led, testing every step with a pole she'd cut from a willow. They went slowly, single file, through the reeds and along the edges of the black pools, following the faint, barely visible line of an ancient causeway that Seraphine said ran out into the fen from the hills. Stone, under the mud. Founders' stone. If you knew where to put your feet.

On the third day, at evening, they saw it.

A dome. Rising out of the reeds a mile ahead. Grey stone, green with moss and black with age, half-sunk in the fen so that only its upper half showed above the water, like the top of a skull rising out of a bog. There were windows in it, round ones, most of them broken. And at its very crown, carved into the stone, so worn that Lex could barely make it out, an open hand. And above the palm, a flame.

"*Qel'Varran*," whispered Seraphine.

Lex stood at the edge of the black water and looked at the half-drowned dome.

He thought about the Leafbound Archive, and the *Book of Making*, and the hammer drawn on its last page with four runes on its head. He thought about the Founders' smith who'd forged the Lattice with fire. The old magic. The magic under the house.

He looked down at his hand. At the burn scars. At the calluses.

*You were already you before the numbers.*

"Come on," he said. "Let's go and find out what's under the house."
