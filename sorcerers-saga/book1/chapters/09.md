# The Old Founders' Road

"If I leave," said Antho, "it gets out."

He was standing at the bottom of the slope in the cavern, a careful three paces back from the ring of green fire, looking at the breach. In the daylight leaking in through the passage, the crack in the air looked smaller than it had at night. Thinner. Almost nothing, a dark scratch on the world. But the cold still came off it in slow waves, and the trickle of Gloam still seeped out of it, and the green fire still hissed and spat where the two met.

"The fire holds it," Antho went on. "Mostly. But the fire needs feeding. Every day. Sometimes twice a day, when it's pushing hard. If I go, the fire burns down. Three days, maybe four. Then the stones outside have to hold it alone." He shook his head. "They won't. Not for long. And then it'll come down the gullies, and into the valleys, and into Thistledown."

"So we fix the fire," said Lex.

"I've been trying to fix the fire for four years."

"You've been trying alone."

Antho turned and looked at him.

*[He's right, you know,]* said Ember, to Lex. *[About the fire. I've been studying it. The glyphs he's used are clever but crude. About a third of them are wrong, and the right ones are held together by sheer stubbornness. It's like a wall built of uncut stones. It stands, but it takes constant mending.]* A pause. *[But I think there's something else he hasn't realized. Ask him about the link.]*

"The breach-link," Lex said. "In your status. The thread that ties you to it."

Antho's face went tight. They'd talked about it, late the night before, after the Wardens left. After Lex had told him what he'd seen with True Sight: the fine dark thread running from his brother's panel down the slope and into the crack. Antho hadn't said much. He'd just sat by the fire for a long time, staring at the green flames, and then got up and walked away into the dark.

"What about it?" he said now.

"Ember thinks—" Lex stopped. It still felt strange, saying the name out loud to someone else. "The voice. The construct. It thinks the link works both ways. The thing on the other side uses you to hold the door open. Like a wedge. Like a foot in the doorway." He hesitated. "Which means if you're *here*, right next to it, it's got a foot in the door. But if you go away—"

"It loses its grip," said Antho slowly.

*[Not entirely,]* said Ember. *[He's still bound. But distance weakens the pull. The further he goes, the less purchase it has. The door won't close on its own. But it won't be pushed open as hard, either.]*

Lex repeated it. Antho listened in silence, his long scarred hands clenched at his sides. When Lex finished, his brother turned back to the breach and stared at it for a long time.

"So I've been making it worse," he said at last, very quietly. "Four years. Sitting here, guarding it. And all that time I've been the thing holding it open."

"You didn't know."

"That doesn't—" Antho's voice cracked. He stopped. Took a breath. "That doesn't make it better, Lex."

"No," Lex agreed. "But it means you can stop."

---

They made new wards.

It took them the whole of that day and most of the next. Lex didn't have a forge. He didn't have an anvil. He had Anvilsong, and a flat slab of black rock by the cavern entrance that would serve, and his own two hands. And he had Heat Shaping, which had climbed to Novice 9 in the Frostvault and was, Ember assured him, quite capable of bringing a lump of iron up to working heat without coal or bellows, if he was patient.

He was patient. He was a smith.

For iron, he had the Wardens' crossbows.

They'd left most of them behind on the slope, tangled in the snow where Wren's frost-arrows and Antho's shadows had torn them from their owners' hands. Fifteen crossbows, steel-limbed and iron-fitted, and nearly two hundred bolts. Lex gathered them up in the morning, and Wren helped him carry them into the cavern, and Antho sat on a rock and watched with a strange expression as Lex took the first crossbow apart with his bare hands and laid its steel limb on the black stone and began to heat it.

"Those are Warden weapons," Antho said.

"They were."

"They shot Ashka with ones just like those."

Lex looked up. Antho's face was unreadable.

"I know," said Lex. "That's why I'm using them."

He made nails.

Not ordinary nails. Ward-nails, Ember called them: iron spikes as long as his forearm, thick as his thumb, tapered to a point at one end and flattened to a broad head at the other. And on each head, he struck a glyph.

That was where Antho came in.

"Not that one," Antho said, the first time, leaning over the black stone to look at the shape Lex was tracing in the cooling iron. "That's *bar*. You want *bind*. The bar keeps things out. The bind keeps them in."

"How do you know?"

"I've been getting it wrong for four years. I've had a lot of practice." Antho reached out, hesitated, and then traced a shape in the air above the iron with one long finger. "Like this. The upstroke curves back on itself."

*[He's right,]* said Ember, sounding surprised. *[That's better than the one I was going to show you. It's not the textbook form, but it's... cleaner. More suited to the purpose.]* A pause. *[He has a good instinct for this. Self-taught, and frequently wrong, but with a real instinct underneath.]*

"Ember says you're right," Lex told him.

Antho blinked. "It does?"

"It says you're frequently wrong but you've got good instincts."

Antho stared at him for a moment. Then, unexpectedly, he laughed. It was a short, rusty sound, as if the mechanism hadn't been used in a long time, but it was a real laugh.

"That's the nicest thing anyone's said to me in eight years," he said.

They worked together after that. Lex heated and shaped. Antho drew the glyphs in the air, and argued with Ember through Lex about the details, and corrected and was corrected in turn. Wren kept the fire going and fetched snowmelt for quenching and, when she got bored, went outside and practiced her archery on the trees at the edge of the ward-stones until Antho asked her very politely to stop because the noise was upsetting Ashka. Ashka was not upset in the least. Ashka spent most of the day sitting on Lex's shoulder, watching the iron glow, and occasionally reaching down to sniff the hot metal with every sign of profound interest.

*[Storm-drakes like heat,]* said Ember. *[And lightning. And shiny things. And, apparently, you.]*

By the evening of the second day, they had twelve nails.

They drove them into the rock in a ring around the green fire, a pace outside it. Lex held each one and Antho set it, and then Lex struck it home with Anvilsong. *Chang.* With every strike, he put the glyph into the stone as well as the iron. *Bind.* The rock took it. The nails sank in like they were going into clay.

And when the twelfth nail went home, the green fire *changed*.

It didn't go out. It didn't flare. It simply settled. As if it had been straining against something for four years and suddenly, finally, didn't have to anymore. The flames dropped to a low, steady glow. The hissing where the Gloam touched them faded to a whisper. And the crack in the air, the breach, seemed to *shrink*. Not much. A hair's width. But Lex saw it.

Lex remembered.

"I never lit it," said Antho. "I never lit any of them. I didn't—" He stopped. "It felt like I didn't have the right. I was the reason she was gone. I didn't get to leave a light on for her." Lex looked at the lamp.

Then he held out his hand.

"Give it here," he said.

---

He did it on the black stone, by the cavern entrance, in the last of the evening light.

It wasn't iron, so he couldn't strike it. He had to *coax* it, the way he'd done with Wren's bow in Thistledown: warming the clay with Heat Shaping until it was soft at the very surface, then pressing the rune into it with the tip of a heated ward-nail, slowly, a fraction of a fraction at a time. His hands ached. His eyes watered. Antho sat beside him and didn't say a word.

The rune was *Ardent*. The same one he'd struck into Anvilsong. The rune that held heat.

When it was done, he set the lamp on the stone and held his palm over it and pushed a single thread of fire down into the little clay bowl where the wick should have been.

It caught.

A small flame. Gold. Steady. It burned in the empty bowl without oil, without wick, without anything, and it didn't flicker even when the draft from the breach came drifting up across the cavern.

Antho made a sound Lex had never heard a person make before.

He picked up the lamp very carefully in both hands, the way you'd pick up a bird's egg, and held it close to his face, and looked at the flame. It lit him from below: the hollows of his cheeks, the wild beard, the grey eyes swimming with tears.

"It won't go out?" he whispered.

"Not while I'm alive."

"*Lex*." Antho's voice broke completely. He didn't say anything else.

He carried the lamp out of the cavern. Down the passage, between the hundreds of unlit lanterns, out through the great cleft of the Hollowmaw into the blue mountain dusk. Lex and Wren followed. Antho walked out past the ring of charcoal stones, past the place where the Wardens had stood, and stopped at the very edge of the slope, where the mountain fell away into the valley and the whole of the eastern Greyspine lay spread out below, ridge after ridge going dark under the first stars.

"For you, Mam," he said quietly. "For when you come home."

The Old Founders' Road began on the north side of the Greyspine, where the mountains fell away in great ragged ridges toward a land of grey moors and dark forests and wide, silver, mist-filled valleys. Wren knew the way down. It took them two days.

The Road itself, when they found it, was barely a road at all. It had been paved once, long ago, with great slabs of grey stone fitted together so precisely you couldn't have slipped a knife blade between them. But nine hundred years of frost and root and rain had heaved most of the slabs up out of true, and moss had grown over them, and grass between them, and in places the forest had swallowed them entirely. Still, it ran north. Straight as a ruled line, over hill and through valley, as if whoever built it had decided where it was going to go and the land had simply been told to get out of the way.

And at intervals along it, every few miles, there were stones. Waystones, waist-high, carved with the open hand and the flame.

"Founders' Road," said Antho, running his scarred fingers over the first one they came to. "Nine hundred years old. Built when the Lattice was woven." He looked north along the line of heaved and mossy slabs, vanishing into the mist. "I read about it once. In a book I stole off a dead Warden." He paused. "That sounds worse than it was."

Lex turned his head.

"It skipped five times," said Antho. "I counted. I always count." He was looking up at the stars, not at Lex. "I told you four, because I'd never done more than four myself, and I didn't want my little brother beating me on the first day he got it right." He paused. "I was a petty, jealous fifteen-year-old. I'm sorry. It was five."

Lex stared at him.

Then he started to laugh. Helplessly, uncontrollably, the way he hadn't laughed since he was a child. He laughed until his ribs hurt and his eyes streamed, and beside him Antho started laughing too, that rusty, unpracticed laugh, and the two of them sat against the cold stone wall of a nine-hundred-year-old ruin in the middle of nowhere and laughed like idiots until Wren came back with the waterskins and stood in the empty doorway and stared at them both.

"What," she said. "What's funny?"

"*Five*," Lex managed. "It was *five*."

Wren looked at Antho. Antho had his face in his hands, shoulders shaking.

"You're both idiots," she said, and sat down by the fire. But Lex saw her smiling as she said it.

---

Later, when the fire had burned low and Wren was asleep and Antho was a dark shape wrapped in his furs on the far side of the coals, Lex lay awake looking at the stars and thinking.

*[You're happy,]* said Ember quietly.

He thought about it.

*I think I am,* he admitted. *Is that strange?*

*[No. I don't think so.]* A pause. *[I think you've been carrying something very heavy for a very long time. And you've just put it down. It would be strange if you didn't feel lighter.]*

*I hated him so much. For so long.*

*[I know.]*

*And it was all wrong. Everything I thought. Everything the village said.* He stared up at the sky. *How do I know the rest of it isn't wrong too? Father. Elric. The Order. Any of it.*

Ember was quiet for a long time.

*[You don't,]* it said at last. *[That's the hard part. You don't know. You just keep looking. And when you find out you were wrong about something, you change your mind, and you keep going.]* A pause. *[That's what your brother did tonight, I think. Over a stone that skipped five times instead of four. He's been carrying that for nine years. Tonight he put it down.]*

Lex lay looking at the stars for a long time.

*Ember,* he thought. *Do you think my father is a good man?*

The silence that followed was the longest yet.

*[I don't know,]* said Ember, very softly. *[I think he loved your mother. I think he loved you both. I think he did something very hard and very frightening because he thought it was right.]* A pause. *[Whether that makes a man good... I'm not sure I'm the one to judge.]*

*Why not?*

Another long silence.

*[I don't know that either,]* said Ember. *[It's just a feeling. Like there's a partition I can't open, and behind it, the answer to that question. And I'm not sure I want to know what it is.]*

Lex lay awake for a long time after that, looking up at the stars, until the fire was only embers and the sky in the east had begun, very faintly, to grey.
