# Leave a Light On

Antho opened the door the way you'd open a door on a sickroom.

Slowly. Quietly. With his hand flat on the black glass and his breath held. Not pushing. Asking. The way Kestrel had told him. The way he'd knocked, days ago, or weeks, or however long it had been down there in the dark.

*Kell*, he thought. *It's me again. We're home. May we come out?*

Far below, so far he could barely feel it, a bony hand on the other side of the glass. Faint. Steady. Sitting very straight beside something enormous and soft.

*[...go on, then...]*

*[...don't let the cold in...]*

The crack widened.

From the middle out. Like ice melting. Like breath clearing off a window. The thin gold line becoming a sliver, becoming a gap, becoming an arch. Tall. Round-topped. Full of gold light. Lamp-light. Lantern-light. The light of a thousand small flames on the walls of a long passage, and three in a niche, and the warm still air of a cavern at the bottom of a mountain, that smelled of stone and oil and home.

His mother stood in it.

She didn't move.

---

Antho had imagined this.

Every night. For thirteen years. From the first night in the cave in the Greyspine, with the Murmur grinding in his skull and Ashka a cat-sized bundle of hurt in his lap. He'd imagined it the way you imagine things you know will never happen. In detail. Carefully. Like a man building a house in his head, room by room, so he'll have somewhere to go when the real house is too cold.

He'd imagined her coming through a door. Any door. The cottage door at Harrow, with the lantern in the window. The door of the cave. The black door at the bottom of the Hollowmaw, after he'd built the Keep round it and lit the first lantern and started saying *evening, Mam* to the dark.

He'd imagined her face.

He'd imagined his father's.

He'd never once imagined her standing in the doorway, not moving, with the clay lamp in her hand and the letter against her chest and her face gone white, looking at an old man asleep in a chair.

Afraid.

---

"*Mam*," he said. Very softly.

She didn't look at him.

"*I can't*," said Seren Vey. In a whisper. "*Antho. I can't. Look at him. He's so old.*"

"*I know*."

"*He was young*." She didn't take her eyes off the chair. "*In the dark. In my head. The voice was young. I thought—I don't know what I thought. I thought he'd be*—" She stopped. "*I don't know him. I don't know his face. I'm going to walk in there and he's going to wake up and look at me and I'm going to look at him and I won't know him.*"

"*You'll know him*."

"*How do you know*?"

Antho looked at her. At his mother. Small and fierce and freckled, standing in a doorway at the bottom of the world with her lamp. The way she'd stood on the stairs at Harrow, thirteen years ago, with the dark coming in behind the hearth and her son screaming.

She'd walked into the dark then. Without stopping. Without looking back.

She'd been less afraid of the dark than she was of this.

"*Because Lex is right*," said Antho. "*You always know. You always have.*"

"*I don't remember*—"

"*You didn't remember Ember*," said Antho. "*And you knew him.*"

His mother turned her head. Slowly. And looked at him.

"*You knew his voice*," said Antho. "*In Lex's chest. Through a hearth. A piece of him from sixteen years ago. You put your hand on Lex and said, that's the voice. You didn't remember it. You knew it.*" He swallowed. "*Go and listen, Mam. That's all. You're a Listener. Go and listen to him.*"

---

She went.

One step. Out of the dark. Onto the black floor of the cavern. Into the gold light.

The clay lamp in the niche leaned so far over its flame nearly touched the stone. Toward her. Toward the lamp in her hand. Its other half. Two small gold flames, bending toward each other across the cavern, like hands reaching.

Another step.

The iron lamp leaned. The *ARDENT* one. All three. Leaning. Toward her.

The old man in the chair stirred.

---

He woke the way old people wake. Slowly. In pieces. His white head lifting off his chest. His thin hand moving on the arm of the chair, feeling for something. For the oil-flask that had fallen. His eyes, behind Seraphine's spare spectacles, opening. Blinking. Grey. Clouded with sleep.

He looked at the lamps first.

Antho saw him do it. Saw the old habit. A year and a half of it. Waking in the chair by the niche, and looking at the lamps, to see if they were still burning.

He saw them leaning.

And went very still.

And then, slowly, very slowly, the way you turn your head toward a sound you've been listening for so long you've stopped believing you'll ever hear it—

He turned.

And saw her.

---

Nobody moved.

Not Antho, in the arch of the door. Not Lex, behind him. Not Seraphine, or Kestrel. Not Wren, who was at the mouth of the cavern, where the passage came down, with her hand on the wall and Pim beside her, who must have been there all along, who must have been sitting at the bottom of the passage for days, waiting. Not Odile. Not the students crowded in the passage behind them. Not the twins, or Tilda, or Kit, or Ivy Moss.

Nobody breathed.

His father sat in the chair and looked at his mother.

His mother stood on the black floor and looked at his father.

He was eighty. Antho could see it. Eighty, or more. White. Thin. A face like a walnut, all furrows. Hands like a bundle of twigs, brown-spotted, shaking on the arms of the chair. A blanket over his knees. Spectacles on his nose.

He didn't say anything.

He didn't try to get up. Antho saw him think about it. Saw his hands tighten on the arms of the chair. And stop. And let go.

He'd known. Antho thought. He'd known he wouldn't be able to stand. He'd known for weeks. He'd sat in the chair by the lamps every night and filled them and said good evening, and known that if she came, when she came, he'd be sitting down.

He took off his spectacles.

Slowly. With both shaking hands. And folded them. And put them in his lap.

And looked at her. With his own eyes. Grey. Clear. Steady. The same grey as Antho's.

And said the only thing he'd said to her every night for a year and a half.

"*Good evening*," said Arion Vey.

---

His mother made a sound.

Antho would never be able to describe it. Not to Seraphine, who asked. Not to Lex, who'd heard it too and didn't need it described. Not to anyone. Not ever.

It wasn't a word. It wasn't a sob. It wasn't a laugh.

It was the sound of a lamp being lit.

She was across the cavern. Antho didn't see her go. One moment she was standing on the black floor three yards from the door, and the next she was on her knees in front of the chair, with the clay lamp set down on the stone beside her, and her square strong freckled hands on the old man's face.

On his face. His cheeks. His jaw. His forehead. His white hair. Touching. Not holding. Touching. The way a blind woman touches a face to learn it. Fast. Light. Everywhere. Her fingers running over the furrows and the hollows and the lines, over his eyebrows, his nose, his mouth.

He didn't move.

He sat in the chair with her hands on his face and his eyes on hers and didn't move at all. Antho saw his hands, on the arms of the chair. Gripping. White-knuckled. Shaking so hard the chair shook. Holding on.

Not touching her.

Waiting.

---

"*Say it again*," said his mother.

Her voice was a whisper. Her hands had stopped. On his face. One on each side. Cupping it.

"*Good evening*," said his father.

His voice was thin. Cracked. Old. An old man's voice, reedy, with a wobble in it at the end. Not the voice Antho remembered from the cottage at Harrow, deep and quiet and dry. Not the voice from the Spire, or the council, or the crystal at Avaloria.

But underneath—

Underneath, the same. The same shape. The same quiet. The same way of saying a thing as if it mattered very much and he didn't want anyone to know it mattered at all.

His mother shut her eyes.

And listened.

---

She stayed like that for a long time.

Kneeling. Eyes shut. Hands on his face. Listening. To his breathing. To the small sounds of him. The creak of the chair. The tremor in his hands. Whatever it was a Listener heard, when she listened to a person.

Antho didn't know. He'd never know. He listened to the dark. To doors. To the little one. He'd never once listened to a person like that.

He thought, standing there: *I should. I should learn. Seraphine. Lex. Pim. I should listen to them like that.*

His mother opened her eyes.

They were wet. Her whole face was wet. She didn't seem to notice.

"*You have a nice voice*," said Seren Vey.

---

His father laughed.

It came out of him like something breaking. Cracked. Wet. Startled. An old man's laugh, wheezing, that turned into a cough, that turned back into a laugh. He laughed with her hands on his face and his own hands gripping the arms of the chair and tears running down into the furrows of his cheeks, and he couldn't stop.

"*I'm told*," said Arion Vey. When he could. Gasping. "*I'm told it used to be nicer.*"

"*Who told you that*?"

"*You did*." He was still laughing. Crying. Both. "*The first night. On the steps of the Spire. In the rain. You said I had a voice like a librarian telling off a choir.*"

His mother stared at him.

"*I did not*."

"*You did*." He was shaking. "*I've written it down. It's in the letter. Page four.*"

"*That's a terrible thing to say to someone*."

"*It was*," Arion agreed. "*I fell in love with you on the spot.*"

---

She kissed him.

Antho looked away. Everyone did. Kestrel turned round and studied the wall of the cavern with enormous interest. Seraphine put her face in Antho's shoulder. Lex made a sound like a man who has been hit very hard in the chest and found it didn't hurt.

At the mouth of the cavern, Wren Ashdown said, very quietly, "*Oh, for the Lady's sake,*" and burst into tears.

And Pim—

Pim, standing beside her, holding her hand, small and solemn in his blue coat, looked at the two old people by the niche. At the old man in the chair and the small woman kneeling in front of him, with her hands on his face.

And smiled.

---

They didn't stay in the cavern long.

His father couldn't. He was cold. He was always cold now. And he was shaking so hard Seraphine was worried about his heart.

Lex carried him up. Two hundred and eleven steps. Up the long passage. Past the lanterns.

And his mother walked beside them. With her hand on the old man's arm. And the clay lamp in her other hand. Not the one from the niche. The one she'd carried. The flame. Its other half.

And as she passed them, the dark lanterns *lit*.

---

Antho saw it first. Because he was behind. Because he was counting. Because he always counted.

The first was a lantern from Fenmoor. Old tin, battered, with a horn pane. Just inside the cavern, where the passage began. It had been dark since the first night of the moths, with dust on its pane that wouldn't come off.

His mother walked past it.

And it lit.

Not suddenly. Not all at once. Slowly. A glow. Behind the horn pane. Faint. Then less faint. Then gold. The dust on the pane didn't go. It was still there. Grey-gold. Smeared. But the light came through it anyway. Soft. Steady. Like a lamp lit behind a dirty window.

The next one. Brass, from the Saltreach, with green glass. Dark. Dusty.

She walked past it.

It lit.

And the next. And the next. All the way up the passage. Every dark one. Every one the moths had touched. Two hundred and six of them. As she passed. Each one. Lighting. Slowly. From inside. As if the light had been there all along, folded up very small, hiding, the way the little one had folded his mother up small in the dark when the moths came. Waiting for someone to walk past who knew how to unfold it.

She didn't seem to notice.

She was looking at the old man in Lex's arms. Talking to him. Low. Too low to hear. Her hand on his arm.

Antho walked behind them. And counted.

He didn't say the numbers out loud. He didn't need to. They went through him like a song. Like the Lantern Song. Like a bell.

*Nine hundred and eight. Nine hundred and nine. Nine hundred and ten.*

All the way up. All the way to the cave mouth. All the way to the last one. The very first one. The one he'd lit himself, the summer after Duskhold, with his own hands, in the cave where he'd lived alone for eight years.

*One thousand one hundred and thirteen*.

---

"*How did she do that*?" said Seraphine.

At the cave mouth. In the dusk. With the sun going down red over the Greyspine and the slope below full of people. The students. The twins. Tilda, Kit, Ivy Moss. Odile with Pim on her hip, too big to be carried, being carried anyway. Wren. Kestrel. Ashka on the ridge, with her great blue head up, watching.

Antho looked down the long passage. At the lanterns. Gold. All of them. Every one. Burning. With dust on half their panes, and the light coming through it anyway.

"*I don't know*," he said.

"*It was the lamp*," said Ivy Moss. From her stool by the door, where someone had put her. Very small. With her crutch across her knees. Looking down the passage with enormous eyes. "*Her lamp. The one she carried.*"

Everyone looked at her.

"*It's not kept*," said Ivy Moss. She went red. But she didn't stop. "*It's given. I could hear it. When she walked past. It's a light for someone else. That's all it is. That's all it's ever been.*" She looked at the lanterns. "*And the moths took the lights out of the lanterns. But they couldn't take them* away.*"* She frowned. Looking for the words. "*They just—folded them up. Small. Hid them. Like the little one hid her.*"

She looked at Seren. At the end of the passage. Standing by the old man, who Lex had set down in a chair by the kitchen fire.

"*And when a light that's given walks past a light that's hiding*," said Ivy Moss, "*the hiding one remembers what it's for. And comes out.*"

Nobody said anything for a long time.

"*How do you know that*?" said Antho. Very gently.

Ivy Moss looked at her knees.

"*I don't know*," she said. "*It just—it sounds like that. Under. In the room. It sounds like that's what it is.*"

---

Lex was at the kitchen door.

With Wren.

Antho saw them. Over the heads of the students. Standing in the doorway, close. Not touching. Wren's hand was up. On his face. On his left cheek. Where the handprint was.

Grey-gold. Glittering. From his temple to his jaw.

She wasn't crying anymore.

She was looking at it. Her face very white. Very still. Her green eyes very hard.

"*She did this*," said Wren.

"*Yes*."

"*She touched you*."

"*She hit me*." Lex tried to smile. His broken nose made it lopsided. "*Across the world. I flew. Kestrel set my nose. It's fine.*"

Wren's thumb moved. Very lightly. Over the print. Over the long fingers of it.

"*It's not fine*," she said. Very quietly. "*It's a mark. She left a mark on you. On your face. Where I'll see it every day.*" Her hand dropped. To her own stomach. Flat. Over it. "*So she can find you again.*"

Lex didn't say anything.

"*Your mother told me*," said Wren. "*Just now. On the stairs. She came up to me and put her hand on me, here, without asking, the way old women do in the market, and she looked at me with those eyes of hers, and she said, you're carrying a light. Has the moth seen you?*" Her voice was very steady. "*And I said yes. At Greyfall. And she went white. Whiter than your father.*"

"*Wren*—"

"*She said the Harrow women never let a moth near a light that's carrying*," said Wren Ashdown. "*She said never. Not once. Not in nine hundred years.*" She looked up at him. "*She said that's the one thing they want more than anything. More than the Well. More than the blood.*"

The kitchen doorway was very quiet.

"*A new light*," said Wren. "*One that hasn't been given to anyone yet. That's never been anyone's but its own.*"

---

Seren read the letter by the window.

That night. In the kitchen of the Lantern Keep, which had one small high window cut through the rock of the mountain, looking east, over the valley, toward the Vale and Ashbourne and Harrow.

It wasn't daylight. It wasn't the window she'd wanted. There wasn't a kettle; Odile's terrible tea had to do.

She read it anyway.

Sitting on the bench under the window, with a lamp beside her, the clay one, and the old man asleep in the chair by the fire, wrapped in three blankets, with his white head on his chest. She'd said she wanted to read it with him there. She'd said she didn't care if he was asleep. She'd said she wanted to be able to look up from it and see him.

Antho sat at the long table, at the other end of the kitchen. Not watching. Not listening. Just there. The way you sit in a room with someone who's reading something important. So they won't be alone.

She read for a long time.

Sometimes she laughed. Softly. Into her hand.

Sometimes she stopped. And looked up. At the old man in the chair. For a long while. And then looked down again.

Once, she put the letter down in her lap. And put both her hands over her face. And sat like that, very still, for so long that Antho half got up.

Then she took her hands away. And picked up the letter. And kept reading.

---

She came to the end at midnight.

He knew it was midnight because the old man in the chair woke up. All on his own. The way he'd woken every night for a year and a half. At midnight. To go down to the door and fill the lamps.

He woke. And lifted his head. And reached for the oil-flask that wasn't there.

And then remembered.

And looked up.

His wife was sitting on the bench under the window. With the letter in her lap. Forty pages. In his small neat hand. Looking at him.

"*Did you read it*?" said Arion. Very quietly.

"*Yes*."

"*All of it*?"

"*All of it*." She folded it. Carefully. Along the creases. "*Page four's my favorite. The librarian.*"

He looked at her. With his grey eyes. For a long time.

"*Do you*—" He stopped. Tried again. "*Is any of it*—"

"*No*," said Seren. Very gently. "*I don't remember any of it. Not one word. Not the steps. Not the rain. Not the librarian.*" She looked at the folded letter in her lap. "*It's like reading a story about two other people. Two people I'd have liked very much.*"

He nodded. Slowly. As if he'd expected it. As if he'd known.

"*But*," said Seren.

He looked up.

"*But I read the bit about the lanterns*," said his wife. "*Page nine. Where you told me the story of the Nine. As a joke. And I stopped laughing. And you wrote that you couldn't remember what I said next. That it was the one thing from that night you'd never been able to remember. That it was like a page torn out.*"

He'd gone very still.

"*I know what I said*," said Seren Vey.

---

The kitchen was very quiet.

"*I didn't remember it*," said Seren. "*Not until I read your letter. Not until I read that you couldn't.*" She looked down at the clay lamp beside her. "*And then I did. All at once. Like a stone turning over in a field.*" She looked up at him. "*Because it wasn't about you. That's why. It wasn't yours. It was mine. It was Mam's. The Ferryman only took you.*"

"*What did you say*?"

His mother looked at his father across the kitchen of the Lantern Keep, at midnight, with the letter in her lap and the lamp beside her and a thousand lanterns burning in the passage behind her.

"*I said, they're not stories*," said Seren Vey. "*I said, my mother's mother's mother saw one. A moth. In a window. At the end of a hot summer. And she packed her cart that same night and ran.*" She swallowed. "*And then I said: don't ever leave a light on for me, Warden. Not ever. If you love me. I'd rather walk home in the dark.*"

Arion Vey stared at her.

"*And then*," said his wife, "*you said—*"

"*I said, too late*," whispered his father. "*I've already lit one.*"

She smiled. Wet. Wide.

"*Page nine*," said Seren. "*You wrote that bit down. You just didn't know it was the answer.*"

---

That was when the World Notice came.

Antho felt it before he saw it. Everyone did. Everyone in the world. A *pressure*. Behind the eyes. The way a storm presses on your ears before it breaks. The net, reaching out. Through every thread. To every person in it. All at once.

He'd felt it four times before. *A Ceiling has been broken*. *A Key has been turned*. *The lid has lifted*. *The Ceiling is lifted, the Tithe is ended, the Root is relinquished, the net is reforged.*

It came now.

Not silver. Not gold. Not violet.

Grey-gold. Glittering. Like dust in a sunbeam.

Nobody said anything.

The kitchen was silent. Antho heard the fire crackle. He heard his father breathing, quick and shallow, in the chair. He heard, very far off, down the passage, through the rock, through the door left on the latch, something enormous and soft and frightened, at the bottom of the world, go very still.

*[...brother...]*

*[...brother, she's up there...]*

*[...she's up there with my light in her...]*

His mother was on her feet. With the letter clutched to her chest. And her face white and set and hard. The Harrow face.

"*Highcrest*," said Seren Vey.

---

They went out to the cave mouth.

All of them. Everyone in the Keep. Students in their nightclothes. Lex and Wren. Kestrel with her hair burning. Seraphine with her notebook. Antho with the Lantern. Lex carrying their father again, wrapped in blankets, with his white head on his son's shoulder. Seren beside them. With her lamp.

They stood in the snow at the mouth of the Hollowmaw, under the stars, and looked east.

Toward the Vale. Toward Ashbourne. Toward the long road south and east to Highcrest, a hundred miles off, far beyond the edge of seeing.

The moon was up. Nearly full. Low over the eastern ridges. Silver. Bright.

And as they watched, it went out.

---

Not out. *Covered*.

Something passed in front of it. From the west. From behind them. From over the Greyspine, from the bare lands and the edge of the world. A cloud. Low. Vast. Moving east.

Not a cloud.

It glittered.

Grey-gold. In the moonlight. Like frost. Like dust in a sunbeam. Like the surface of a lake at night. Stretching from one edge of the sky to the other. North to south. As far as the eye could see. Drifting east. Slowly. Endlessly. Over the mountains. Over the Keep. Over their heads.

So high they couldn't hear it. So many there was no counting.

The moon went out behind them. And the stars. One by one. Swallowed. Until the whole sky over the Greyspine was a glittering grey-gold dark, turning, drifting, going east.

Toward Highcrest.

Toward the light.

---

Antho stood in the snow and looked up.

He felt Seraphine's hand find his. He felt his brother beside him, with their father in his arms. He felt Wren on Lex's other side, with her hand on her stomach. He felt his mother.

He felt the little one. Far below. At the bottom of the world. With a gaunt grey woman sitting beside it, very straight, telling it the one about the miller's cat. Trembling.

And he did what he always did. What he'd done every night for thirteen years. In a cave, in a keep, at a door, on a mountain.

He counted.

But there was nothing to count. Not the moths. Not the stars. There were too many of one and none of the other.

So he counted the only thing he could.

The people beside him. In the snow. Under the glittering dark.

*Mam. Father. Lex. Wren. Seraphine. Kestrel. Pim. Odile. Ivy. Tilda. Kit. Ilse. Ivo. Cass. The twins.*

*And one more*. *Very small. The size of a pea. Carrying a light.*

*Seventeen*, thought Antho Vey.

And behind them, down the long passage, one thousand one hundred and thirteen lanterns burned. Every one. With dust on half their panes. And the light coming through it anyway.
