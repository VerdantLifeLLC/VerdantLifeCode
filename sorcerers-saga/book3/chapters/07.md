# Sanctuary

Highcrest put its lanterns out for her.

Not because anyone told it to. Not at first. Queen Lysandra's order came later, at noon, carried through the streets by heralds on horseback. *By command of the Crown, all lanterns in the city are to be shuttered or extinguished from dusk until the Queen's further word.* By then it was too late. By then the city had already done it on its own.

Lex watched it happen from the West Gate.

He'd been standing there since dawn. On the wall. In the cold spring wind. Watching the road. The long white road that ran west from Highcrest across the green downs toward the Blightmarch and the bare lands and the edge of the world. He'd been standing there for two days, on and off. Since the raven came from Fenn, saying *returning with the woman. Ten days. Wren safe.*

*Wren safe*. That was all. Two words. He'd read them two hundred times.

And now, at last, at the far end of the road, a column. Grey coats. Horses. Carts. Coming slowly. And above them, high, small, a figure on the wind. Coming fast. Ahead of the rest. A speck of green and red against the grey sky.

He was down off the wall before he knew it. Through the gate. Running.

---

She came down out of the wind onto the road in front of him so hard she staggered. And he caught her. And held on.

He didn't say anything. Neither did she. They stood in the middle of the West Road with the city walls behind them and the wind settling round them, and held on.

She smelled of dust. Grey-gold. Faintly. Of plums.

"*You're all right*," said Lex. Into her hair.

"*I'm all right*."

"*Fenn said safe. He didn't say all right.*"

"*I'm safe and I'm all right*." She pulled back. Just enough to look at him. Her face was thinner. Her green eyes were very bright and very strange. Not frightened. Something else. Something he couldn't read. "*Lex. Listen. Before they get here. Before she gets here.*"

"*What*?"

Wren opened her mouth.

Then closed it.

He watched her do it. Watched something come up in her face and go down again. Like a fish in deep water.

"*Later*," said Wren. "*Tonight. Not here. Not with—*" She glanced back down the road. At the column. Closer now. "*Just—later. I'll tell you later. It's not bad. It's not—I don't think it's bad.*" She swallowed. "*But not here.*"

"*Wren*—"

"*Don't look at her eyes*," said Wren.

It came out fast and low and hard. She had his coat in both fists.

"*Whatever you do*," said Wren Ashdown. "*Whatever she says. However kind she sounds. Don't look at her eyes, Lex. Look at her hands. Look at her feet. Look at the ground round her feet.*" Her own eyes were fierce. "*She doesn't leave tracks.*"

---

The column came in through the West Gate at noon.

Forty Keepers in grey. Fenn on his bay mare at the front. Dame Elowen Dunmore beside him on a big grey warhorse, in her armor, with Oathkeeper across her saddle-bow and her face like a shut door.

And behind them, carts.

Six of them. Farm-carts, borrowed from the last village before the downs. With straw in the beds and blankets over the straw. And sitting in the straw, in rows, wrapped in blankets, with their faces turned west, twenty-eight Keepers.

Dim.

The crowd that had gathered at the gate went quiet when the carts came through. Lex heard it go. Like a wave going out. People who'd come to see the strange woman from beyond the Veil, laughing and shoving and holding children up on their shoulders, went silent. One by one. As the carts went by.

Twenty-eight faces. Pale. Still. Eyes open. Looking west. Past the gate, past the walls, past the crowd. Looking back down the road the way they'd come. As if they'd left something there. As if they were waiting for it to follow.

And between the fourth cart and the fifth, walking, with an iron chain on her wrists, the woman.

---

He didn't look at her eyes.

He wanted to. That was the worst part. He'd think about that afterward, for a long time. How much he wanted to.

He looked at her hands.

Long. Pale. Grey-gold, like the dust on the lanterns. The fingers very fine and very long, too long, with joints in them that shouldn't have been there. Folded in front of her. With a chain between the wrists. Ordinary iron. Keepers' issue. The kind Roole's people used for drunks and thieves.

She wore it like jewelry. Like a bracelet. Her hands held it as if it were a string of pearls.

He looked at her feet.

He couldn't see them. Her gown went down to the ground. Long and grey-gold, moving in the wind like smoke. And under the hem, on the cobbles of the West Gate, where she'd walked—

Nothing.

Not a mark. Not a scuff. Not a print in the dust of the road that had come in on the cart-wheels and the horses' hooves and forty Keepers' boots.

The cobbles behind her were clean.

As if she'd never been there at all.

---

And the lanterns leaned.

He saw it. Everyone saw it. It was noon, broad day, and the lanterns of Highcrest weren't lit, they were never lit at noon. But they hung in every window and from every eave and every bracket along the West Way, the way they had for a year and a half, since the Heart. Dark. Unlit. Waiting for dusk.

And as she walked past them, every one of them *swung*.

Toward her.

Not much. Just a little. The way a lantern swings in a breeze. Except there wasn't a breeze. Except they all swung the same way. Toward her. As she passed. Like heads turning. Like flowers turning toward the sun.

And then swinging back. Slowly. When she'd gone by.

The crowd saw it.

Lex watched them see it. Watched the faces change. Watched the people at the front of the crowd take a step back. And then, slowly, watched some of them take a step *forward*. Toward her. Toward the tall grey-gold woman with the chain on her wrists, walking up the West Way between the carts. Looking at her face.

Looking at her eyes.

A child broke away from its mother. A little girl. Five. Six. In a blue smock. And ran out into the road. Toward the woman. With her arms up.

And Maelis stopped walking.

And looked down at the child.

---

Lex was moving. He didn't know when he'd started. Pushing through the crowd at the side of the road. Fire coming up in him, without his asking, the way it did when someone he cared for was in danger. He didn't know the child. He didn't care. He was moving.

And Maelis knelt down.

In the road. In her long grey-gold gown. With the chain on her wrists. On the cobbles of the West Way. So that her face was level with the little girl's.

She didn't touch her. She didn't reach out. She just knelt there. And looked at the child. And smiled.

"*Hello*," said the Moth Queen. Softly. "*Aren't you a bright one.*"

The little girl stared at her. With her arms still up.

"*Go back to your mother*," said Maelis. Gently. "*She's frightened. Can you see? She's frightened for you.*" She tilted her head. "*Never make your mother frightened. It's the worst thing in the world. Go on.*"

And the little girl turned. And ran back. Into the crowd. Into her mother's arms.

And Maelis stood up. Unhurried. And brushed off her gown. Though there was nothing on it. And walked on up the West Way. Between the carts. Toward the castle.

And Lex, standing at the edge of the crowd with fire in his hands and nowhere to put it, looked down at the cobbles where she'd knelt.

Where the hem of her gown had brushed.

There was dust on them.

Grey-gold. A little. A smudge. The shape of a moth's wing.

---

*[Lex*,] said Ember. Very quietly. *[Lex. Put the fire out.]*

He put it out.

*[That was*—]

*I know what it was*.

*[She could have*—]

*I know*, thought Lex. *She could have. She didn't. She wanted everyone to see her not do it.*

He looked up the West Way. At the tall grey-gold figure walking between the carts. With the lanterns swinging toward her as she passed.

*She's very good at this*, he thought.

*She's been doing it for a very long time*.

---

The Queen received her in the Hall of Lanterns.

It had been the throne room. It had been called the Hall of the Crown for three hundred years. Lysandra had changed the name, the summer after the Heart, and taken the throne out, and put in a long table instead. Oak. Plain. With chairs down both sides and one at the head that was no bigger than the others. And above the table, hanging from the high stone ceiling on iron chains, a hundred lanterns. Brass and tin and glass. From every corner of Caldor.

They were dark today. Shuttered. Every one.

Lysandra had done that herself. Lex had watched her. Climbing up a ladder in her plain blue dress at dawn, with Elowen holding the bottom of it, swearing, closing the shutters on her lanterns one by one.

*If she wants light*, the Queen had said, coming down the ladder, *she can bring her own.*

---

Maelis came in on Fenn's arm.

Not because she needed it. Because she'd asked. *I'm a little tired*, she'd said, at the door of the Hall, in her low soft hoarse voice. *It's been a long walk. Would you mind?* And Fenn, who had straightened every leaning Stone in the west with his own hands and stood beside Roole at the Hollowmaw on the night of the Blade and didn't talk much, had looked at her for a long moment.

And given her his arm.

Lex saw his face as they came in. Fenn's narrow sandy face. Pale. Set. Confused. Like a man who's done something without deciding to, and doesn't know why.

The Hall was full.

Not the whole court. Lysandra hadn't wanted the whole court. Just the council. The ones who mattered. The Queen at the head of the long table, in blue, with her silver circlet. Arion at her right hand, where he always sat. Commander Roole on her left, in Keepers' grey. Seraphine further down, with a notebook open in front of her and her pen already moving. Elowen standing behind the Queen's chair, with Oathkeeper drawn and point-down on the stone. A dozen lords and ladies of the council. Duke Harlowe, old and white-bearded. Lady Cressa Moore, who'd taken Sorn's seat. Others Lex knew by face but not by name.

And Antho.

His brother was at the far end of the table. Alone. Sitting very still. In his black coat. With the Lantern on the table in front of him, its shutter closed, and his long hands folded round it. He'd come down from the Hollowmaw that morning on Ashka. He hadn't said anything when he arrived. He'd just looked at Lex, and nodded, and gone and sat at the end of the table and folded his hands round the Lantern.

He was looking at the door. At the woman coming through it on Fenn's arm.

His face was white.

---

Lex sat down beside Wren. Halfway down the table. She took his hand under the table and held it so hard his fingers went numb.

He didn't look at the woman's eyes.

He looked at the Queen.

Lysandra was looking at Maelis. Steadily. With her sharp dark face very calm, the way it went before a fight. She'd been crowned at twenty-four, in a city that had just lost its king and its High Warden and half its army. She'd faced down a coup in a ballroom with no shoes on. She'd named her people one by one in a square full of singing to open a path for Lex's brother. She was not, Lex thought, a woman who was easily charmed.

"*You asked for sanctuary*," said the Queen of Caldor.

"*I did, Majesty*."

"*Sit down*."

Maelis looked at the chair at the foot of the table. The one that had been set for her. Plain oak. Like the others.

She didn't sit in it.

She sat on the floor.

---

She did it slowly. Gracefully. Folding herself down onto the stone flags of the Hall of Lanterns at the foot of the long table, in her long grey-gold gown, with her chain in her lap. Like a child sitting down to hear a story. Like a petitioner kneeling before a throne. Her silver hair pooled round her on the floor.

She looked up at the Queen.

"*Forgive me*," said Maelis. "*I've sat on a throne. A long time ago. It wasn't comfortable. I've never liked chairs since.*" Her smile was small and wry. "*And I think it's better, don't you? If I'm lower than you. While we talk. So nobody forgets who's asking.*"

There was a ripple round the table. Lex felt it. Like a breeze through grass. Something in the room relaxing. A lord at the far end chuckled.

Lysandra didn't.

"*Who are you*?" said the Queen.

---

Maelis told them.

She told it well. Lex would give her that, afterward, when he went over it again and again in his head, trying to find the place where it went wrong. She was a wonderful storyteller. Better than Ilse. Better than Ember. Her low hoarse voice filled the Hall of Lanterns without ever rising. She never hurried. She never paused too long. She looked at each of them in turn as she spoke, the way a good teacher does, so that each of them felt she was speaking only to them.

Lex looked at her hands. The whole time.

He heard every word.

---

*Before your net*, said Maelis, *there were no Ceilings.*

*You know that. You've read it in your books. But you don't know what it meant. You can't. You've only had a year and a half without one.*

*It meant that anyone could rise. Anyone. A shepherd's daughter. A fisherman's son. As high as they could go. As far as they could reach. Nobody stood beside you at a Stone and put a hand on your shoulder and pushed down. There were no Stones. There was only the world. And the Wells. And whatever you could make of them.*

*Some of us rose very high.*

She looked down at her hands. At the chain in her lap.

*Nine of us. Nine who rose higher than anyone. We built kingdoms. Great ones. Beautiful ones. Cities of glass and gold. Gardens that bloomed in winter. Roads that ran straight across the world from sea to sea. We made peace where there had been war. We fed people who had been hungry.*

*They called us the Crowned.*

*And then we found the spring.*

---

The Hall was very quiet.

*A pool*, said Maelis. *In a hall of glass, in the heart of the eldest kingdom. Black. Still. Deep. My brother found it. Oszric. The eldest of us. He found it under the floor of his palace, when they were digging new cellars.*

*And whoever drank from it lived.*

*Not forever. I won't lie to you. Not forever. But long. Very long. Hundreds of years. Without growing old. Without growing sick.*

*We drank.*

She looked up. At the table. At each of them. Her face was very sad.

*Wouldn't you*? she said. Softly. *If you found a spring, and the water in it would let you live five hundred years, and see your grandchildren's grandchildren grow up, and never watch anyone you loved grow old and die—wouldn't you drink?*

Nobody answered.

Lex felt it go round the table. A stillness. Every person at it thinking the same thing. Thinking of someone. A mother. A father. A child. Someone they'd watched grow old.

He felt Arion go very still. At the Queen's right hand. With his white temples and his brown-spotted hands.

---

*And then*, said Maelis, *a man came.*

Her voice changed. Not much. Just a little. A coolness.

*A Listener. A clever one. The cleverest of his generation. The eldest of seven. You know his name. You've spent the last two years learning it.*

*Aurelian Vey*.

Lex's hand closed on Wren's under the table.

*He came to us*, said Maelis. *To the Nine. To our courts. He said the spring was dangerous. He said it was a Well, not a spring, and we were draining it. He said if we drank any more, it would go dry, and what was left would be a hunger that would eat the world.*

*We didn't believe him.*

She spread her long pale hands. The chain clinked.

*Why would we*? she said. *He was a Listener. A hedge-mage. A man who talked to fire. He'd never ruled anything. He'd never fed anyone. He came to us in his plain robe with his little hammer and told us we were wrong, and we laughed at him. Not cruelly. We were never cruel. We just—laughed.*

*And he went away.*

*And he wove his net.*

---

The Hall of Lanterns was silent.

*You know the rest*, said Maelis softly. *You know it better than I do. You've lived it. Nine hundred years of it.*

*He wove the world into a net. Every person in it. Every thread. And he set himself at the center, as its Root, and he put a lid on the spring and called it the Hollow, and he put a Ceiling on everyone so that no one would ever rise high enough to challenge him again.*

*And he took a Tithe. From all of you. Every one. A tenth of everything you had. To feed the thing under the lid.*

*And us*— She looked down. *Us, he threw out. Past the edge. Into the grey. And tied the knot behind us, so we could never come home.*

*Nine hundred years*, said Maelis of the Crowned. *Nine hundred years in a country with no sun. Watching our people come apart into dust. One by one. Because there was nothing to live on. Because he'd taken the spring and put a lid on it and kept it for himself.*

She looked up.

At Lex. At Antho. At Arion.

*And then you broke his net*, she said. *His own blood. His great-great-grandsons, forty times over. You went down to the bottom of the world and found him there, rotting, merged with the thing he'd made, and you broke his net and threw away his Root and lifted his Ceiling off every head in the world.*

Her lit eyes were wet.

*You set everyone free*, said Maelis softly. *Everyone. Even us.*

*Thank you*.

---

Nobody spoke for a long time.

Lex didn't look at her eyes. He looked at her hands. Folded in her lap. Long and pale and grey-gold. With dust in the creases of the knuckles.

He felt the room.

He could feel it the way he could feel a forge. The heat of it. The pressure. Every person at that table had been listening. Every one of them had been *moved*. He could feel it in the silence. In the way Duke Harlowe was looking at his own hands. In the way Lady Cressa Moore had her fingers pressed to her lips.

It was all true.

That was the trouble. That was what made the back of his neck go cold. Every word of it was true. Aurelian *had* woven the net. He *had* set himself at the center. He *had* taken a Tithe from everyone and fed it to the thing under the lid. He *had* put a Ceiling on the world. Lex had stood at the Heart and seen it. He'd seen the old man at the gold anvil, merged with the hunger, nine hundred years rotten.

It was all true.

And it was all wrong.

He couldn't have said how. Not then. He sat at the table with Wren's hand crushing his and the fire very low in him and knew, the way he knew when a weld was bad before he struck it, that something in the story was cracked. Something at the center. Something she'd left out. Or put in the wrong place.

*[She's very good*,] said Ember. Very quietly.

*Yes*.

*[She hasn't told a single lie. Not one. I've been checking. Against everything he knew. Against everything in the Book of What Was.]* A pause. *[She's just—arranged it. Like a jeweler arranges a setting. So the light hits it the way she wants.]*

---

"*Why are you here*?" said the Queen.

Lysandra hadn't moved. She was sitting at the head of the long table exactly as she had been when Maelis came in. Back straight. Hands folded. Face calm.

Lex loved her for it.

"*Because the knot came loose*," said Maelis. "*When your smith reforged the net. He tied it again with a kinder hand. He left a little give.*" She smiled at Lex. He looked at her hands. "*Just enough for a moth.*"

"*That's how. I asked why*."

Maelis looked at the Queen for a long moment.

"*Because of what's coming up*," she said.

The Hall went still.

"*You've felt it*," said Maelis. "*Haven't you. All of you. Your springs. Your wells. Your strange new roads. Your Delves that open in village greens.*" She looked at Lex. "*Your Delves that your net doesn't recognize.*"

She leaned forward. Slightly. On the floor. Her chain clinked.

"*The Well we drank*," said Maelis. "*The spring under Oszric's palace. The one Aurelian put a lid on and called the Hollow. It went dry when we drank it. And what was left was a hunger. A terrible hunger. That's what he caged. That's what he fed your Tithe to. For nine hundred years.*" Her voice dropped. "*You called it the Unmaker.*"

"*We know*," said Lex.

He hadn't meant to speak. It came out of him.

Maelis turned her face toward him. He didn't look up.

"*Do you*?" she said. Softly. "*Then you know what it did. You know what it ate. Worlds. Whole worlds. Before Aurelian caught it. After. Under his lid. You saw them yourself, at the bottom of the deep. Drifting. Eaten.*"

"*It was starving*," said Antho.

---

Everyone turned.

His brother hadn't moved. He was still sitting at the far end of the table, alone, with his long hands folded round the Lantern. His face was white. His grey eyes were fixed on the woman on the floor.

He was the only one at the table looking straight at her eyes.

Lex felt his stomach drop.

"*It was starving*," said Antho Vey again. Quietly. "*Because you drank it. You and your brother and the rest of you. You knelt at the pool and drank until there was nothing left.*" He didn't blink. "*Aurelian didn't make the hunger. You did. He just caught it.*"

Maelis looked at him.

For a long time.

And Lex, watching her hands, saw them do something. For the first time since she'd come into the Hall. A tiny movement. The long pale fingers curling. Very slightly. In her lap. Round the chain.

"*Yes*," she said.

---

The table went very quiet.

"*Yes*," said Maelis. Simply. Looking at Antho. "*We drank. I told you we drank. We didn't know what it would do. We were told, by a hedge-mage with a hammer, and we didn't believe him.*" She tilted her head. "*Have you never done something terrible, Threshold Warden, because you didn't believe someone who told you not to?*"

Antho didn't answer.

"*I think you have*," said Maelis. Gently. "*I think everyone at this table has. That's what it is to be a person.*" She looked round at them. "*We were wrong. We were young, and greedy, and frightened of dying, and we were wrong. And we've paid for it for nine hundred years.*" Her voice was very soft. "*Haven't we paid enough?*"

She looked back at Antho.

"*And now it's coming back*," she said. "*The thing we made. The hunger. It's healed, you think. Grown back. Full again. Your old Listener sat with it, and sang to it, and you think it's good now.*" Something in her face, then. Something almost like pity. "*Wells don't heal, Threshold Warden. Not that one. It remembers. It remembers being drunk dry. It remembers being caged. It remembers nine hundred years of the Tithe.*"

She leaned forward.

"*It's coming up*," said Maelis. "*Under your feet. Toward your lights. Toward the woman who's leading it.*" A pause. "*And when it comes, it will be hungry again. It can't help it. That's what it is now. That's what we made it.*"

She looked at the Queen.

"*I know how to bind it*," said Maelis of the Crowned. "*I'm the only one alive who does. That's why I've come. That's what I'm offering.*" She lifted her chained hands. "*Give me sanctuary. And when it comes, I'll help you put it back in the dark where it belongs. Before it eats your world the way it ate ours.*"

---

The council erupted.

Not loudly. They weren't that kind of council. But all at once. Duke Harlowe and Lady Cressa and three lords Lex didn't know, all speaking at the same time. *Majesty, we must*—*Majesty, consider*—*if what she says is true*—*the springs, the Delves, the dust on the lanterns*—

Lysandra held up one hand.

They stopped.

The Queen of Caldor looked down the long table at the woman sitting on the floor at the foot of it. For a long, long time.

Then she looked at her advisor.

"*Arion*," she said.

---

His father hadn't said anything. The whole time. He'd sat at the Queen's right hand with his white head bowed and his thin brown-spotted hands folded on the table. Listening. The way he always listened.

He lifted his head.

And looked at Maelis.

Not at her hands. At her face. At her eyes. Lex saw him do it. Saw his father look straight into the lit gold eyes of the Moth Queen, steady and grey and two hundred and thirty-three years old.

And not move.

"*Hello*," said Arion Vey.

Maelis looked back at him. And for the first time since she'd come into the Hall of Lanterns, something in her face was not arranged. Something was *surprised*.

"*Warden-Architect*," she said slowly. "*You've grown old.*"

"*I have*."

"*How lovely*," said Maelis. And she sounded as if she meant it. As if it was the loveliest thing she'd ever seen. And the most delicious. "*How very lovely. I've never seen a Vey grow old. Not one. Not in all my life.*"

"*Neither had I*," said Arion. Mildly. "*Until last month. I'm finding it very educational.*"

He looked at her for a moment longer.

"*There's a story they tell in the Spire*," he said. "*To frighten new cadets. About Nine at a Pool. And how they were thrown out past the edge of the world. And how they're still there.*" He tilted his head. "*It ends with a warning. Never leave a light on. Or the Nine will come for it.*"

Maelis smiled.

"*Children's stories*," she said.

"*Yes*," said Arion. "*I thought so too. For two hundred years.*" He looked at the dark lanterns hanging over the table. Shuttered. Every one. "*I've lived in this city a very long time, my lady. Through nine kings and one Queen. And I've never once seen the lanterns of Highcrest swing toward anyone the way they swung toward you this morning.*"

The Hall was very quiet.

"*Majesty*," said Arion Vey. Without looking away from Maelis. "*My advice is this. Give her sanctuary.*"

Lex stared at him.

"*Give her a house*," said his father. "*In the Upper City. Where we can see her. With Keepers on the doors and no lanterns within a hundred yards. Keep her people outside the walls. Out at Greyfall, where they came from. Let her talk to anyone who wants to talk to her. Let her tell her story. Let her offer whatever she likes.*"

He folded his hands.

"*And if one more lantern in your kingdom goes dark*," said Arion, "*put her back on the road. And walk her to the edge of the world. And watch her go.*"

---

Lysandra looked at him for a long moment.

Then she looked at Maelis.

"*You heard him*," said the Queen of Caldor.

"*I did, Majesty*."

"*Those are my terms. A house. Keepers. No lanterns. Your people stay at Greyfall.*" Lysandra's voice was very even. "*One lantern. One. And you're gone.*"

Maelis bowed her head. On the floor. Her silver hair falling forward over her face.

"*You're very generous*," she said. "*More generous than I deserve. Thank you.*"

She got up. Slowly. Gracefully. The chain clinking. And turned toward the door, where Fenn was waiting, very pale, to take her arm.

And stopped.

Beside Lex's chair.

---

He didn't look up.

He looked at her hands. Hanging at her sides now, with the chain between them. Close. Very close. He could have touched them. He could see the dust in the creases of the knuckles. He could smell her. Plums. Old silk. Something underneath, like a room that's been shut up for a very long time.

He felt Wren's hand go rigid in his.

"*You have her hands*," said Maelis.

Her voice was very soft. Just for him. And for Wren. And maybe for Antho, at the end of the table, who had stood up.

"*My sister's*," said Maelis. "*Square. Strong. Never still. Too big for the rest of her, our mother used to say. Good for making things.*" She was quiet. "*I'd know those hands anywhere. I held them once. When they were very small.*"

A pause.

"*Does your mother have them too*?" said Maelis. "*Seren. Seren Harrow.*"

Lex looked up.

He didn't mean to. It happened before he could stop it. His head came up and his eyes came up and he was looking into her face, her beautiful sad face, and her eyes.

Lit. Gold. Warm. Like two windows at the end of a long lane on a winter night.

*There*, said something in him. *There. Someone's home.*

He couldn't look away.

"*How do you know her name*?" said Lex. His voice sounded very far off.

"*Your father says it every night*," said Maelis. Gently. "*To a little clay lamp. At the bottom of a mountain. Good evening, Seren.*" She smiled. "*My moths are very good listeners.*"

He couldn't breathe.

"*Oh, child*," she said. "*Didn't anyone ever tell you?*"

She leaned down. Close. Her silver hair brushing his shoulder. Her lit eyes very near.

"*Harrow isn't a hedge-witch's name*," said the Moth Queen softly. "*It never was. It was my sister's.*"

---

And then she straightened. And turned. And took Fenn's arm.

And walked out of the Hall of Lanterns, slowly, gracefully, in her long grey-gold gown, leaving no tracks on the stone floor at all.

Above the table, a hundred shuttered lanterns swung on their chains as she passed under them.

Toward her.

And Lex sat very still in his chair with his wife's hand crushing his and his brother standing white-faced at the end of the table and his father's grey eyes on him from the Queen's right hand, and couldn't move.

On his shoulder, where her hair had brushed it, there was dust.

Grey-gold. Fine as flour.

It smelled of plums.
