# Epilogue: The Gilded King

There was a moth in the Spire that nobody had found.

It was in the round room at the top. The room with the cold grate and the long window looking north, and the shelves of grey books, and the tall bookcase that swung open if you pulled the right book down. It was on the desk. On the desk where a Warden-Architect had written two hundred years of ledgers in a small neat hand.

It was very small. Smaller than the others. Grey-gold. Its wings folded. Sitting quite still on the corner of the desk, beside the pale ring in the wood where a teacup had stood for two hundred years.

Beside a glass bottle.

Small. The size of a thumb. Stoppered. Sealed with grey-gold wax. And inside it, dark. Red-dark. A finger's depth.

It had been sitting there since the night the Moth Queen flew east.

She'd told it to.

*Stay*, she'd said. Very softly. Bending down over the desk with her lit eyes and her long glowing hands. *Stay with this, little one. Don't let anyone touch it. And if I don't come back*—

She'd smiled.

*If I don't come back*, she'd said, *take it home*.

---

It felt her go.

On the last night of autumn. Far off. A hundred miles east. On a hill of black rubble at the edge of a word written in the ground. It felt the light go out of her. All of it. Pouring. Down the point of a hewer's pick. Into the breach. Into the little one.

And then it felt the other thing.

The thing that came after. Small. Gold. Warm. In the middle of her. Where she'd always been cold.

A lamp. Given.

And then she came apart into it. Gently. Like a dandelion. And was gone.

The little moth sat on the corner of the desk in the dark round room at the top of the Spire and didn't move for a long time.

It didn't know what it felt. It was a moth. It didn't feel things. It went toward the light. That was all.

But the light it had always gone toward was gone.

---

On the third night of winter, when the Queen of Caldor came home to Highcrest and the lanterns came back on in every window, and the whole golden city on its hill was lit up like a heap of embers in a grate for the first time since summer—

The little moth unfolded its wings.

And took hold of the glass bottle with its legs. Six of them. Thin as hairs. Round the neck of the bottle.

And lifted.

It was too heavy. A moth can't lift a glass bottle. Everyone knows that.

It lifted it anyway.

Slowly. An inch off the desk. Two. Its wings beating so hard they shed. Grey-gold dust falling on the desk, on the ring where the teacup had stood, on the open grey book that a Warden-Architect had left there in the summer, on a line in his small neat hand about the price of bread.

Out through the window. The long one. Facing north. Which someone had left open a crack, to air the room.

Into the night.

West.

---

It took a long time.

It flew by night, because by day it couldn't see. It flew low, because the wind was too strong higher up. It flew round every lantern, every lamp, every lit window in every village between Highcrest and the edge of the world, because it was a moth, and it went toward the light, and it had to make itself go *past*.

It was very hard.

It went over the downs. Over the Blightmarch's western edge, where the grey was going green. Over the old Greyfall fort, empty now, its gates open, its lanterns gone. Over the bare lands. The cracked grey ground. The black stumps. The wind that never stopped.

It nearly died a dozen times.

It didn't.

It had been told to take it home.

---

On the fortieth night, it came to the end of the world.

The Veil.

Grey. Hanging from nowhere. Falling to nowhere. Stretching north and south as far as there was anything to stretch. Not quite still. Moving, very slightly, the way a curtain moves when there's a window open somewhere behind it.

The moth came down out of the dark onto the bare frozen ground at the foot of it.

And couldn't get up again.

Its wings were done. Shredded. Most of the dust gone off them. The painted eyes on them faded to nothing. It lay on the frozen ground at the foot of the Veil with the glass bottle beside it, too tired to move.

And the Veil pushed.

Not hard. Not angrily. Just firmly. The way it always had. *Not you. Not ever*.

The moth lay on the ground and felt it push.

---

And then it moved.

One leg. Then another. Crawling. Dragging the bottle. Across the frozen ground. An inch. Two. Toward the grey.

The bottle's cork came loose.

It had been loosening for forty nights. In the cold. In the wind. In the long flight west over a hundred villages full of lamps. The wax had cracked. The cork had worked. And now, on the frozen ground at the foot of the Veil, with a moth dragging it the last inch on legs as thin as hairs—

It came out.

And the bottle tipped.

And a finger's depth of dark red-dark blood ran out across the frozen ground. And touched the bottom of the Veil.

---

The Veil didn't know it.

That was the thing. That was the whole thing. The Veil had been woven nine hundred years ago, by a man with a little hammer, at the edge of the world, to keep nine people out. It had learned them. Every one. What they were. What they tasted like. Everyone who'd knelt at the pool and drunk.

It had never learned the blood that touched it now.

Hesper's line. The tenth. The sister who wouldn't drink. Who'd poured back instead. Whose daughters had never Kindled and never stayed and never let a moth in, for nine hundred years, so that the net never once got a proper look at them.

The Veil didn't know it.

And so the Veil did the only thing a door can do when a hand it's never seen before is laid on it, and there's no reason in the world to keep that hand out.

It *opened*.

---

Not much.

Not like a curtain. Not like a door swinging wide. A finger's depth of blood isn't enough for that. Seren Vey's whole heart wouldn't have been enough for that. It would take more blood than one bottle to open the edge of the world.

But a crack.

A thin one. Low down. At the foot of the grey. Where the blood had run. A line of something that wasn't grey. That was dark. That had a smell coming through it. Dust. Old dust. And underneath, faint, very faint—

Gold.

---

The moth crawled into it.

On its hair-thin legs. With its shredded wings dragging. Through the crack. Out of the world.

Into the grey country.

And lay on the other side, on a broad white road that ran straight as a ruler across a plain of grey-gold dust, under a dim grey-gold light that came from nowhere, toward a palace of glass and bone and gold on the edge of the sky.

And died.

---

In the tallest of the glass palaces, in a hall with a ceiling like a frozen waterfall, four cocoons hung from the rafters.

There had been five. The fifth was empty. Split from top to bottom. Hanging open like a seed-pod in winter. Under it, on the glass floor, in the dust, a crown of silver wire, fine as frost, shaped like folded wings.

Nobody had picked it up.

The other four hung in a row. Grey-gold silk. Thick as a man's arm. Swaying very slightly, though there was no wind.

And under the first one, the biggest, on the floor, a crown of gold leaves.

---

The first cocoon moved.

---

Not a tremor. Not a shiver. Not the slow waking of a sleeper hearing his name in another room.

It *heaved*.

All of it. At once. The great grey-gold mass of silk bucking against its ropes like a sack with something alive in it. The dust pouring off it. The rafters creaking.

And then it split.

Not along a seam. Not like a seed-pod. *Torn*. From the inside. By hands. Big ones. Strong ones. Golden.

---

He came out of it all at once.

Not crouching. Not slow. He dropped out of the torn silk onto the glass floor of the hall on his feet. Like a man jumping down off a cart. And stood there. Tall. Broad. Enormous. Taller than any man. Broader than Brannoc. With a great gold beard that came down to his chest, and gold hair to his shoulders, and skin like old ivory.

And his eyes.

Lit. Like hers had been.

But not like windows. Not like lamps at the end of a long lane on a winter night.

Like *furnaces*. Like the open mouth of a forge, white-hot, roaring. Like the sun, if the sun were angry.

---

He stood in the dust under his torn cocoon and breathed.

And looked down the hall.

At the empty cocoon at the end. Split. Hanging open. At the crown of silver wire on the floor beneath it, in the dust, untouched.

He looked at it for a long time.

Then he walked down the hall. Past the other three cocoons. Past the crown of glass on the floor, and the crown of black iron, and the crown of thorns that had once been roses.

To the end.

And bent down. And picked up the crown of silver wire.

And held it in his enormous golden hand. Very gently. The way you'd hold a moth.

---

"*Orrin*," said the Gilded King.

His voice was deep. Like a bell. Like a mountain. Rough with nine hundred years of not being used.

Something moved at the far end of the hall. Near the tall glass doors. A shape. Bent. Grey. Thin as a rake. With a steward's chain gone black round its neck. And two eyes barely lit at all. Dim sparks. Like the ends of cigars in a dark room.

It came forward. Slowly. On its knees. Shedding dust.

"*Majesty*," whispered Orrin. "*Majesty. You wake.*"

"*Where is my sister*?"

The old steward didn't answer.

He didn't need to. The Gilded King was holding her crown.

---

"*She went first*," said Orrin. Very quietly. Kneeling in the dust. With his face bowed. "*As she always did. To the light. Through the Veil. When it loosened.*" His dry voice shook. "*She said she'd bring you something back.*"

"*And*?"

"*And she felt the Well grown back*," said Orrin. "*Full again. Rising. And she went to it. And drank, once, in the deep. And grew bright. Sovereign. They called her Sovereign. The net did.*"

The Gilded King said nothing.

"*And then they made a room for it*," whispered Orrin. "*The smith. And his people. A hundred miles. A word in the ground.*" He shook his head. "*And it came up into the room. And she went to drink it there. And*—"

He stopped.

"*Say it*."

"*And a hewer broke her on his pick*," said Orrin. "*And the smith struck the flaw. And everything she'd ever drunk poured out of her. Nine hundred years of it. Back into the Well.*" His dim eyes flickered. "*And then a Harrow woman knelt down in front of her. And gave her a lamp.*"

The hall was very silent.

"*And she took it*," said Orrin. "*Majesty. She took it. And it stayed. In the middle of her. Where she was always cold.*" His voice broke. "*And she smiled. And came apart. Into light.*"

---

The Gilded King looked down at the crown of silver wire in his hand.

For a long, long time.

"*A Harrow woman*," he said.

"*Hesper's line*," whispered Orrin. "*The last of it. And her sons. The smith and the warden. Vey on the father's side. Harrow on the mother's.*" He swallowed. "*It was the Harrow blood that opened the crack, Majesty. In the Veil. Just now. Her own little moth brought it. The last one. She told it to.*"

The Gilded King's furnace eyes moved.

Down the hall. To the tall glass doors. To the broad white road beyond them, running straight across the grey-gold plain toward the edge of the world.

Toward a crack. Low down. At the foot of the grey. With something coming through it. A smell. Faint. Very faint.

*Green*.

---

He didn't say anything for a long time.

Then he turned. And walked back up the hall. To the other three cocoons. And stood under them.

And put his enormous golden hand flat on the first. The one above the crown of glass.

It stirred.

On the second. Above the black iron.

It stirred.

On the third. Above the thorns.

It stirred.

All three. Trembling. The dust sliding off them. The silk heaving, very slightly, the way a sleeper twitches when someone says their name in another room.

"*Wake*," said Oszric of the Crowned. Very softly. "*Ysenne. Caddoc. Rhosyn. Wake up.*"

He looked down at the crown of silver wire in his other hand.

"*Our sister is dead*," said the Gilded King.

---

He turned back to the old steward. Kneeling in the dust.

"*Who killed my sister*?"

Orrin bowed his head lower. Until his brow touched the glass floor.

"*Everyone*," he whispered. "*Majesty. Everyone killed her. A hewer. A smith. A boy who whistled. A Harrow woman with a lamp.*" His voice was barely a sound. "*Three hundred and fourteen miles of people with spades.*"

The Gilded King looked at him.

And then he smiled.

It was not a lovely smile. It was not sad. It was not fond. It was not patient.

It was the smile of a man who has been asleep for nine hundred years and has woken to find his house robbed and his sister dead and a door left open a crack.

"*Then we'll go and see everyone*," said Oszric.

---

He put the crown of silver wire inside his robe. Against his chest. Over his heart. Very carefully.

And then he bent down. And picked up his own. The crown of gold leaves. From the dust under his torn cocoon.

And put it on.

"*Orrin*."

"*Majesty*."

"*The Harrows*," said the Gilded King. "*Hesper's line. The woman with the lamp. Her sons. All of them.*" His furnace eyes were very bright. "*I want them brought to me. When the crack is wider. When my sisters and brother are awake.*"

"*Majesty*."

"*And Orrin*." He looked toward the doors. Toward the white road. Toward the crack at the edge of the world, with green coming through it. "*The smith. The one with the little hammer. Who struck my sister's flaw.*"

"*Majesty*?"

"*He has a child*," said Oszric of the Crowned. "*Doesn't he. I can smell it. Through the crack. Something new. Something born on the night my sister died. Something that's never been anyone's.*"

The old steward didn't move.

"*Bring me the smith's child*," said the Gilded King.

---

Far away. On the other side of the world. At the bottom of it. In a place that wasn't a place, at the very center of the net, where the first knot was tied and the last thread ran through, there was a man.

He'd been there for nearly three years.

He didn't have a body anymore. Not really. He had a shape. A thread. Grey. Thin. Wound tight round something that sat in the middle of the First Knot like a stone in a fist. A key. In three pieces. Bow, bit and blade. Barred. Locked. Held shut by a Lockward forged out of the bones of the world, and his thread wrapped round it, holding it in place.

He'd been holding it for nearly three years.

He was very tired.

He'd been tired his whole life. *Always someone's second*, he'd said once. In a cold fortress. To a man with white skin and black veins who called himself the Pale Warden. *Always second. Always behind. Always earning what others were born holding.*

He wasn't second now. He was the only one. Holding a key in the dark at the center of the world, so nobody could ever turn it again.

He'd made his peace with that.

Mostly.

---

He felt the Veil crack.

Far off. At the edge of the net. A thread pulling. A knot loosening. The way you feel a draft from a door that's been opened in a house where you thought all the doors were locked.

And then he felt something on the other side of it. Waking. Big. Hot. Golden. *Angry*.

And then he felt it *look*.

Not at the smith. Not at the warden. Not at the Harrow woman or the child.

At the net. The whole of it. The great weaving. Reforged two years ago by a smith with gold in his eyes and given away.

Looking for a way to unwrite the edge of it.

Looking for the Key.

---

Elric Thorne, who had been Arion Vey's friend for forty years and his betrayer for one night and had given back sixty stolen threads at the lid of the world and gone into the Knot to hold it shut, opened what he had left instead of eyes.

In the dark. At the center of the world. With a key in his fist and a Lockward round it and his thread wound tight.

And said, to no one. To the dark. To the net. To a smith asleep in a cottage at Harrow with a baby on his chest and a lantern in the window that said *HERE*.

"*Lex*," said Elric Thorne. Very quietly. "*Lad. Wake up.*"

"*They're coming for the Key*."

---

And in a cottage at Harrow, in the birch wood, by the stream, in the deep east window where a small iron lantern burned for whoever was in the room—

On a cord round the neck of a man asleep in a chair, with his daughter asleep on his chest and his wife asleep against his shoulder—

The little silver bell with the crooked clapper—

Rang.

Once.

Flat.

*Ting*.

---

Lex opened his eyes.

---

*Here ends* The Sorcerer's Hollow, *Book Three of the Lattice Chronicles.*
