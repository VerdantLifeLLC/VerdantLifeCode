# Growth: Unbounded

"*Good morning*," said Lex to the fire.

It was a small fire. The kitchen hearth of a small house. Four rooms and a loft, of grey Vale stone and new oak beams, with a slate roof that still smelled of the quarry and a door that stuck in wet weather because he'd hung it himself, in a hurry, in the autumn, and hadn't had time to plane it.

The fire was banked down to a few red coals under a heap of ash. He knelt and blew on them, gently, the way you'd wake a sleeping child.

They woke. Flickered. Caught the kindling he fed them. Climbed.

*Good morning*, said the fire. Not in words. It never used words. Just a warmth, a lift, a small bright sense of being glad to see him. The way a dog is glad. The way it had been every morning for five years, since Malachi's cave in the Wastes, since he'd learned that fire was a thing you could *ask*.

He sat back on his heels and watched it.

*[You do that every morning*,] said Ember.

*I know*.

*[Every single morning. Without fail. Even the morning after the wedding, when you had a headache like a kicked anvil and Wren threw a boot at you.]*

*It would be rude not to*.

*[It's a fire*.]

*It's a Well*, thought Lex. *It's the Flame. It's the oldest thing there is. It's been burning since before there were people to burn for.* He fed it another stick. *The least I can do is say good morning.*

Ember was quiet for a moment.

*[Malachi said that*,] it said. *[In almost exactly those words. To Aurelian. Nine hundred and forty years ago. I have it in the second volume.]* A pause. *[Aurelian said he was being sentimental.]*

*And*?

*[And look how that turned out*,] said Ember.

---

The house stood at the far end of the Vale, at a place called Harrow.

It wasn't a village. It had never been a village. It was a fold in the hills where the Ashbourne road gave out into a track, and the track gave out into a footpath, and the footpath went up through a birch wood to a clearing with a stream in it. There had been a cottage in the clearing once. Grey stone. One storey. A lantern in the window.

It had burned down thirteen years ago, on the night of Antho's Kindling, with their mother inside it. Or so everyone had thought.

Lex had come back in the autumn, before the wedding, with Brannoc and Tilda and six of the older students from the Forge, and they'd cleared away the black stones. All of them. Down to the footings. And then they'd built it again.

Not the same. He couldn't remember it well enough for the same. He'd been eleven, and most of what he remembered was smoke. But in the same place. With the same stream. And the same birch wood. And a window on the east side, facing the road, deep and wide, with a sill broad enough to sit on.

There was a lantern in it.

He'd made it himself. Iron and glass. With *ARDENT* on the pane, so it would never go out, and *HOME* cut into the base, because Wren said every lantern ought to say what it was for.

It was burning now. In the grey of the morning. Small and gold. Facing the road.

---

He made the porridge.

Wren came down the ladder from the loft while it was bubbling, in one of his shirts and a pair of thick wool socks and nothing else, with her red hair in a wild tangle over one shoulder and her eyes half shut. She stood at the bottom of the ladder and swayed.

"*Good morning*," said Lex.

"*Is it*?" said Wren. She sniffed. "*Is that porridge*?"

"*Yes*."

"*With honey*?"

"*Yes*."

She came across the kitchen and leaned her forehead against the middle of his back, between his shoulder blades, and stayed there. Breathing. He stirred the porridge. She didn't move.

"*You're very warm*," she said into his shirt.

"*I'm a Worldforger*."

"*You're a hot-water bottle*," said Wren. "*A large one. With opinions about porridge.*"

He smiled. He kept stirring.

They had been married for three weeks.

He still wasn't used to it. Every morning he woke up in the loft, under the slope of the new roof, with the light coming grey through the little window, and she was there. Next to him. Asleep on her stomach with one arm flung over his chest and her hair everywhere. And every morning he lay there for a while and looked at the black iron ring on her finger, and couldn't quite believe it.

*[You're doing it again*,] said Ember.

*Doing what*?

*[Smiling at the porridge*.]

---

They ate at the table by the east window, with the lantern between them.

Outside, the birch wood was grey and bare and wet. The snow was going. It had been going for a week. The track down to the Ashbourne road was a river of brown slush, and the stream in the clearing was loud and high and full of meltwater, and the air coming in round the edges of the window smelled of mud and new green and the first thin edge of spring.

"*You're going to the Forge*," said Wren. It wasn't a question.

"*Kindling-day*."

"*Today*?" She frowned. "*It's early.*"

"*Reeve Dunstan moved it up*." Lex shrugged. "*Twelve of them this spring. From Ashbourne and the farms round. He says they won't wait. Their parents won't wait. Everyone wants it done before planting.*"

"*Because they're unbounded*," said Wren.

"*Because they're unbounded*."

She looked at him over her porridge.

"*You don't like it*," she said.

"*I like it*." He frowned at the lantern. "*I made it. Of course I like it. No more Ceilings. No more children being told what they'll be before anyone asks who they are.*"

"*But*," said Wren.

He didn't answer.

She waited. She was good at waiting. She'd waited for him on a lot of rooftops.

"*It's fast*," said Lex at last. "*That's all. It's going so fast.*"

---

He walked to Ashbourne along the river path.

It was three miles. He could have flown. Ashka would have come if he'd called her, from the Hollowmaw, in an hour. He could have Windstepped, the way Wren did, on the wind, though he was terrible at it and usually landed in a hedge.

He walked. He liked walking. It gave him time to think, and the Vale was beautiful in the thaw, and he wanted to look at it.

He'd lived here all his life. Eleven years at Harrow, thirteen at Ashbourne, with a few years in between of walking up mountains and into Wellworlds and down to the bottom of the world. He knew every field. Every hedge. Every stile. He knew which bridge was rotten and which ford was deep and which farmer would set his dog on you for crossing his barley.

It looked the same.

It wasn't.

He couldn't have said how. He'd been trying to say it for a year and a half. Since the Heart. Since the World Notice. Since every Ceiling in the world had lifted at once, like a lid coming off a pot.

It was in the people. The way they walked. The way they *stood*. Old Tobbin's widow, out in her yard with her geese, straight-backed, with her chin up, who'd stooped as long as Lex had known her. The Dunstan twins, twenty now, Kindled [Laborer] and [Laborer] at sixteen and capped at twenty-four, running past him on the river path in the thaw with packs on their backs and swords at their hips and their faces lit up like lamps.

"*Morning, Master Lex*!" shouted one of them.

"*Where are you going*?"

"*Greyspine*!" shouted the other. Not stopping. "*There's a nest of rock-wyrms up past Thistledown. Wardens never cleared it. Pell says it's worth a level each.*"

"*You're laborers*!"

"*Not anymore*!" They were already past. Already far down the path. One of them turned, running backward, grinning, and shouted back over his shoulder. "*Level thirty-one! Both of us! Since the autumn! We're Wardens now, Master Lex! Proper ones! Well. We're* Brawlers. *But we're going to be Wardens!*"

And they were gone round the bend of the river, running, laughing, two farm lads with swords, toward the mountains and the rock-wyrms and a level each.

Lex stood on the path and watched them go.

*[They're happy*,] said Ember.

*I know*.

*[Happier than they've ever been. In their lives. You did that.]*

*I know*, thought Lex. *That's what frightens me.*

---

The Ashbourne Forge filled most of the east end of the village now.

It had started as Brannoc's forge. One anvil. One hearth. One old smith and one boy. Now there was a long hall with twenty hearths and twenty anvils and a great brick chimney at each end. There were two reading rooms and a library and a copying-house. There was a dormitory for the students who came from too far away to go home at night, and a kitchen where Mother Orla's niece cooked for forty, and a yard with a pump and a woodpile and a lean-to where Quill the raven had built a nest of stolen spoons on the chimney-top and lived like a king.

There were more than forty students now. Fifty-three, at the last count. From all over Caldor. From the Sunreach. Three from the Saltreach, who argued with everyone about everything. Two from the twelve villages, who'd come because Ilse Marrow told them to.

All of them unbounded.

He heard them before he saw them. Hammers. Twenty of them. Ringing, overlapping, in the long hall. The rhythm of it came down the street to meet him, and he felt his shoulders go loose the way they always did, the way they had since he was eleven and first heard Brannoc's hammer ringing through the forge wall at night.

Tilda Crane met him at the door.

She was nineteen. Small and fierce and freckled, with a smith's forearms already and a burn-scar on her chin and her mouse-brown hair tied back in a knot so tight it pulled her eyebrows up. She had Brannoc's hammer on her back. She carried it everywhere. She'd carried it to the wedding. Lex suspected she slept with it.

"*Master Lex*." She was breathless. "*There's a problem.*"

"*There's always a problem*."

"*No*. *A real one.*" She grabbed his sleeve. "*It's Dunny Tobbin. They've brought him in.*"

---

Dunny Tobbin was fourteen.

He'd been Kindled in the autumn. The first Kindling-day after the Heart. The first one in Ashbourne with no Warden at the Stone, no hand on anyone's shoulder, no lid. He'd put his hand on the cracked old Lattice Stone on the green and it had lit up red and gold and the whole village had gasped.

*[Berserker]*. Rare. Growth: unbounded.

He'd been Level 1 in the autumn.

He was Level 34 now.

He lay on a cot in the infirmary at the back of the Forge, where Old Mag's apprentice kept her herbs, and he was grey. His left arm was splinted from wrist to shoulder. His right hand was bandaged. His face was bruised down one side, purple and yellow, and there was a cut over his eye with nine stitches in it. His feet, in their wrappings, were black at the toes.

"*Frostbite*," said Hedda Vorne. Grimly. Standing at the foot of the cot with her arms crossed and her cudgel tucked under one elbow. "*He'll lose two. Maybe three. The left foot.*"

Lex looked at the boy.

He knew him. He'd known him since he was born. Farmer Tobbin's grandson. A big slow sweet kid who used to come and watch at the forge door with his thumb in his mouth. Who'd sold Lex a copper penny's worth of conkers once, when he was six, and come back an hour later to give the penny back because he'd decided the conkers weren't very good.

"*Where*?" said Lex.

"*Up past the Teeth*." Hedda's mouth was a thin line. "*Alone. In the snow. For eleven days.*"

"*Doing what*?"

"*Killing things*," said Hedda Vorne.

---

He'd gone up into the Greyspine in midwinter. On his own. With a woodcutter's axe and his da's old coat and a sack of bread.

He'd been killing things ever since.

Gloam-beasts, mostly. The last of them. The ones that had been left behind when Morvane fell and the Quiet were released and the breaches closed. Gloamed bears and gloamed wolves and gloamhounds that had gone feral in the high passes, half-mad, half-starved, with the grey still in them. Nobody had bothered to clear them. They were too high and too scattered and too few. They'd been dying out on their own.

Dunny had found them.

He'd found that they were worth a great deal. A gloamed wolf, at Level 12, was worth a third of a level. A gloamed bear was worth a whole one. And a [Berserker], it turned out, got stronger when he was hurt. *Fury*, the skill was called. The more he bled, the harder he hit.

So he'd let himself bleed.

"*He said he couldn't stop*," said Tilda. Very quietly. Standing beside Lex at the cot. "*When they found him. Pell found him. Coming down the Thistledown track with a dead bear over his shoulders and his arm broken in two places. He said every time he killed one, he went up. And every time he went up, he could feel it. More. Higher. He said it was like being hungry and eating and getting hungrier.*"

She swallowed.

"*He said there wasn't any top*," said Tilda. "*He said he kept waiting for it to stop, and it didn't. He said nobody ever told him when to stop.*"

Lex didn't say anything.

He stood at the foot of the cot and looked down at a fourteen-year-old boy with black toes and a broken arm and nine stitches in his face. Level 34. Rare. Unbounded.

*Nobody ever told him when to stop*.

---

He opened his mouth to ask the boy's status.

And stopped. Because he couldn't.

He'd reached for it. Without thinking. The way you reach for a tool on a bench that isn't there anymore. *True Sight*. The gold flicker behind his eyes that had let him see anyone's thread. Their level. Their stats. Their seals and Sanctions and Ceilings.

It wasn't there.

It hadn't been there for a year and a half. He'd given it away at the Heart. With the rest of the Root. *ROOT: RELINQUISHED*. He'd laid it down on a gold anvil at the bottom of the world and walked away and never once regretted it.

But he still reached for it.

A dozen times a day. More. Every time he met someone new. Every time a student came to him with a question. Every time he looked at Wren across the breakfast table and wanted to know, without asking, how she was.

His hand closed on nothing.

*[Lex*.]

*I know*.

*[You can ask him*,] said Ember. Gently. *[People do. That's how everybody else does it.]*

*I know*.

He crouched down by the cot. Dunny Tobbin's eyes opened. Brown. Bloodshot. Frightened.

"*Hello, Dunny*," said Lex.

"*Master Lex*." The boy's voice was a croak. "*I'm sorry. I'm sorry. I know I shouldn't have—Gran said—*"

"*How do you feel*?"

Dunny stared at him.

"*Not the arm*," said Lex. "*Not the toes. Inside. How do you feel, now?*"

The boy was quiet for a long time.

"*Hungry*," he whispered.

---

Lex went and stood in the forge yard for a while.

By the pump. In the thin spring sunshine. With his hands in his pockets.

The yard was full of puddles. And in the middle of it, where there'd never been anything but packed earth and cinders and the stump Brannoc used for splitting kindling, there was a spring.

A real one. Small. Bubbling up out of a crack in the cinders, round and clear, the size of a dinner plate. And warm. Lex could see the steam coming off it in the cold air. It ran across the yard in a little channel it had cut for itself, and under the gate, and away down the street toward the river.

It had appeared six days ago. Overnight. Hedda had come out to the pump in the morning and found it there, steaming, and stood and looked at it for a long time, and then gone and fetched Brannoc, who had stood and looked at it for even longer, and then said, *well, that'll save on the pump*.

It wasn't the only one.

There'd been others. All winter. All over the Vale. Little things. Strange things. Things people called *the oddities* now, in the Crooked Kettle, with a laugh, the way you laugh at things you don't understand.

A well at Coldbrook, dry for forty years, that filled up in a night. A chimney at the mill that grew a second chimney, smaller, beside it, like a sapling, brick by brick, over a week. A road between Ashbourne and Millbridge that was a mile longer than it used to be, though nobody could say where the extra mile was or when it had been added.

And in Old Mag's garden, at the back of her cottage by the chapel, an apple tree that had been dead for twenty years had bloomed.

In the snow. In midwinter. Every branch. White and pink. Like a bride.

---

"*Something's making room*," said Old Mag.

She was ninety-five. Or ninety-six. She'd lost count, she said, and didn't intend to start again. She sat in the chair by her fire with a shawl round her shoulders and a cat on her lap and her one good eye bright and black as a bird's, and the other one milky and blind, and she looked at Lex across her little kitchen as if she'd been expecting him.

She'd been Kindled a [Herbwife], a lifetime ago. Common. Capped at twenty-four. Lex had Unbound her at the Ashbourne Stone, five years ago. She'd become a [Wisewoman]. She'd said it was about time somebody noticed.

"*Room*?" said Lex.

"*That's what it feels like*." Old Mag stroked the cat. "*You know when you're baking bread, and you leave it in the bowl by the fire with a cloth over it, and you come back, and it's risen? Pushed the cloth up. Filled the bowl.*" She nodded at the window. At the apple tree outside, still blooming, in the thaw. "*Like that. Like the ground's rising. Pushing things up. Making room for something underneath.*"

"*For what*?"

"*How should I know*?" said Old Mag. "*I'm a herbwife. I know nettles.*" She looked at him sideways with her bright black eye. "*You're the one with the fire in him. You're the one who went down to the bottom of the world. What's down there?*"

Lex didn't answer.

He was thinking about a door of black glass. And three lamps. And a message on the glass. *Not alone*.

And his brother, on a rock on the night of the wedding, rubbing his temple with two fingers.

*Something big*, he thought. *Antho said. A big shape.*

*Coming up*.

---

The Kindling was at noon.

The whole village came out for it. The way they always did. The way they had for nine hundred years. But it was different now. There was no Warden at the Stone in a grey cloak with a silver badge. There was a Keeper. A young woman from Highcrest named Ada Fell, in the plain grey coat of the Keepers of the Stones, who'd been a Warden-cadet three years ago and had cried, she told Lex once, when Roole made her take off her badge, and then cried again, harder, at her first Kindling as a Keeper, when a farmer's daughter put her hand on the Stone and came up [Healer] and nobody pushed down.

She stood beside the Stone now. With her hands behind her back. Not on anyone's shoulder.

The Stone stood where it had always stood. On the green. Old and grey and leaning and cracked down the middle from top to bottom, the crack Lex had stared at every day of his childhood, the crack he'd always thought looked like a river.

Twelve children stood in a line in front of it.

Sixteen years old. Fifteen. One of them fourteen, because her mother said she was ready and the Keepers didn't argue anymore. They stood in a nervous row in their best clothes, with their parents behind them and the whole of Ashbourne behind their parents, and Reeve Dunstan in front of everyone in his chain of office, which he'd polished for the occasion.

Lex stood at the back. Next to Brannoc. With his arms crossed.

"*You look like a thundercloud*," said Brannoc.

"*I'm fine*."

"*You're thinking about Dunny*." The old smith looked at him sideways. "*Lad, he'd have done it anyway. Ceiling or no Ceiling. He'd have gone up the mountain to kill something because his gran's farm's failing and he thought if he got strong enough he could save it.*" He scratched his beard. "*The Ceiling never stopped anyone being stupid. It just stopped them getting anywhere.*"

"*It stopped them getting hurt*."

"*Did it*?" said Brannoc.

Lex didn't answer.

Brannoc was quiet for a moment.

"*I was capped at twenty-four*," he said. "*For nineteen years. I used to wake up in the night. Every night. Thinking, if I could just get to twenty-five. Just one more. I'd be able to do the big work. The proper work. I'd be able to make something that mattered.*" He shrugged his enormous shoulders. "*Couldn't. Never could. And I'll tell you what that does to a man, lad. It doesn't keep him safe. It just keeps him small.*"

Lex looked at him.

"*Let 'em get hurt*," said Brannoc Holt. Gruffly. "*Then teach 'em how not to. That's what you're for. That's what all of us are for, now. There's no lid. So we'd better be the ones standing round the pot.*"

---

The first child put her hand on the Stone.

It was Nan Hob. The cooper's daughter. Small and dark, with her hair in two plaits. She put her palm flat on the cracked grey stone and shut her eyes.

The Stone lit.

Silver. Faint. Then brighter. Silver and gold, running up through the crack like a river in spate, the way it had since the Heart. The whole green held its breath.

A panel rose over Nan Hob's head. Everyone could see it. They always could, at a Kindling. It was the one time the net showed itself to the world.

There was a small silence.

Then the cooper, at the back of the crowd, let out a whoop that sent the rooks up out of the chapel elms. And Nan Hob opened her eyes and read it and burst into tears, and her mother swept her up, and everyone was laughing and clapping, and Lex was clapping too.

*Common*. A cooper. Like her father. Like her grandfather. Three generations of barrels.

*Growth: unbounded*.

A cooper who could be anything. A cooper who could go as high as she wanted. A Common class that would never, ever stop.

---

The second child was Wat Lister's boy. Then the miller's twins. Then a girl from one of the hill farms who came up [Shepherd] and a boy from the same farm who came up [Shepherd] too, and they looked at each other and laughed, because they were cousins and had been fighting over the same flock since they could walk.

Then a girl named Ivy Moss.

She was the fourteen-year-old. The one whose mother said she was ready. She was thin and quiet and had been born with a twisted foot, and walked with a crutch, and had spent her whole life on the edge of things, watching.

She put her hand on the Stone.

And the Stone *hummed*.

Not lit. Not at first. It hummed. Low. Deep. A sound Lex felt in his chest before he heard it with his ears. Like a bell struck very far off. Like a note sung under the ground.

The crowd went quiet.

Ada Fell, the Keeper, took a step back. Her hands came out from behind her back.

The hum went on. Rising. The crack down the middle of the Stone began to glow. Not silver. Not gold. Something else. A dark glow. A deep, soft, purple-black, like the sky an hour after sunset. Like the inside of a closed eye.

*[Lex*,] said Ember. Sharply.

*I feel it*.

He did. Under his feet. Through his boots. Through the Stone in him, the Well he'd Commanded at the bottom of the world. The ground under the green was moving. Not shaking. *Rising*. Slowly. Pushing up. Like bread under a cloth.

Making room.

Ivy Moss stood with her hand on the humming Stone, very still, with her eyes wide and her crutch trembling under her other arm. She didn't take her hand away. She didn't scream.

She was listening.

"*Ivy*," said Lex. Quietly. Coming through the crowd. Slowly. Not running. Not frightening her. "*Ivy, love. Take your hand off the Stone.*"

She looked at him.

"*It's singing*," said Ivy Moss. In a small, wondering voice. "*Master Lex. Can you hear it? It's singing.*"

And then the ground beside the Stone opened.

---

It didn't crack. It didn't split. It didn't fall in.

It *opened*.

The way an eye opens. Smoothly. Silently. All at once. A circle, perfectly round, ten feet across, in the grass of the Ashbourne green beside the Lattice Stone, where an instant ago there had been nothing but turf and a few early daisies.

And inside the circle, dark.

Not a hole. Lex could see that at once. A hole had sides. A hole had dirt and roots and stones, and a bottom, somewhere, even if it was a long way down. This had none of those. It was simply *dark*. Perfectly flat. Perfectly black. Like a pool of ink set into the grass. Like a door left open into a room with no lights.

Air came out of it.

Warm. Soft. Breathing out against Lex's face in a long slow sigh. It smelled of—

He knew that smell. He knew it. Stone and cold and lamp-oil, and under that something older, something he'd only smelled once before, at the bottom of a long passage, in front of a sealed black door.

*The Hollowmaw*.

---

Somebody screamed.

Then everyone did. The crowd broke backward from the green like a wave going out. Parents grabbing children. The miller's twins tripping over each other. Reeve Dunstan's chain of office swinging as he ran. Ada Fell, the Keeper, standing her ground beside the Stone with her face white and her hands up in front of her, empty, as if she could hold the dark back with them.

Ivy Moss didn't run.

She stood at the edge of the round dark, with one hand still on the humming Stone and her crutch under the other arm. And looked down into it.

Lex reached her. He put his hand on her shoulder. Gently. Not pushing down. Just there.

"*Ivy*."

"*It's all right*, *Master Lex*," said Ivy Moss. She didn't look up. "*It's not angry. It's not anything. It's just—*" She tilted her head. Listening. "*It's just very big. And it doesn't know where to go.*"

She took her hand off the Stone.

The humming stopped. The dark glow in the crack faded. Back to silver. Then to grey. The Stone was a Stone again. Old and leaning and cracked.

A panel rose over Ivy Moss's head.

---

Lex read it.

*Not spawned by the net*.

Delves were the net's. They always had been. The Lattice spawned them at mana knots, in old places, deep places. The Frostvault, in the Greyspine, where he'd fought his first ice-wight with Wren at his back. The Drowned Archive at Qel'Varran. A thousand others, all over Aldermere. Floors and guardians and rewards. Tidy. Predictable. Ordered. The net made them, and the net labelled them, and the net knew what was in them.

The net didn't know what this was.

*[Lex*.] Ember's voice was very quiet. *[Lex, I've never seen that before. In nine hundred years of his memory. In all the records. I've never seen the net say it doesn't know.]*

*I know*.

*[What is it*?]

*I don't know*.

He crouched at the edge of the round dark. Close. Closer than he should have. He held his hand out over it, palm down, the way you'd hold your hand over a forge to feel the heat.

Warm. Soft. Breathing.

And very, very faintly, so faint he thought for a moment he was imagining it, from somewhere far down in the dark—

Humming.

A tune. He knew it. He'd known it all his life. He'd heard his mother sing it at the window of a cottage at Harrow. He'd heard his brother play it on a bone flute in a cave. He'd heard his father play it badly at his wedding, three weeks ago, in front of a door.

*Little light, little light, burning low*.

Flat on the high note.

---

He stood up.

Wren was there.

He didn't know how. She'd been three miles away at Harrow, in his shirt and her socks, eating porridge. Now she was on the green beside him, in her leathers, with her bow strung and an arrow on the string and her hair in a hasty braid, breathing hard, with the wind still settling round her ankles.

"*Mistwalker*," she said, before he could ask. "*I heard it. Through the wind. The whole Vale heard it.*" She looked down at the round dark. "*What in the Lady's name is that?*"

"*A Delve*."

"*That's not a Delve*." She stared at it. "*Delves have doors. Stairs. Things.*"

"*The net says it's a Delve*." Lex looked at the panel. "*The net says it didn't make it.*"

Wren was quiet for a moment.

Behind them, the crowd was coming back. Slowly. The way people do when the first fright wears off and the curiosity sets in. Edging forward across the green. Craning. Whispering. Parents holding their children back by the collars.

And the children. The unbounded children. Nan Hob and the miller's twins and the two shepherd cousins, fresh-Kindled, Level 1, with their panels still glowing faintly over their heads. Staring at the round dark beside the Stone with wide bright hungry eyes.

*A Delve*. Lex could see them thinking it. *A new one. Right here. On the green. Nobody's been in it. Nobody knows what's in it.*

*Floors. Guardians. Rewards*.

*Levels*.

Behind them, already, at the edge of the green, Lex saw the Dunstan twins come running back down the river road. Swords out. Faces alight. Two farm lads who were going to be Wardens. Who'd heard the hum from a mile off, and turned round, and come back.

And behind them, more. Pell, in his ranger's green, with his bow. The blacksmith's boys from Millbridge, who'd been at the Forge for the Kindling. Half a dozen of the older students. All of them coming. All of them with that look.

He thought of Dunny Tobbin on his cot, grey, with his black toes.

*It was like being hungry and eating and getting hungrier*.

---

Lex turned round.

He didn't raise his voice. He'd learned that much, in five years. From Arion, who never raised his. From Antho, who did it with silence. From Queen Lysandra, who could stop a room with an eyebrow.

He just stood between the round dark and the green, with his arms at his sides, and his fire very slightly up, so that the air around him shimmered and the snow-melt on the grass at his feet began, gently, to steam.

And everyone stopped.

"*Nobody goes in*," said Lex Vey.

The green was silent.

"*Nobody*," said Lex. "*Not the twins. Not the students. Not anyone who Kindled today, or yesterday, or last year. I don't care what level you are. I don't care what the net says it's worth.*" He looked at the Dunstan twins. They lowered their swords. Slowly. "*The net doesn't know what this is. Do you understand? For the first time in nine hundred years, the net doesn't know.*"

He let that sink in.

"*Until I know*," said Lex, "*nobody goes in.*"

---

Wren was standing beside him. With her bow.

"*Except us*," she said. Not a question.

He looked at her.

She looked back. Her green eyes very steady. The black iron ring on her finger, on the hand that held the bow.

"*Don't*," said Wren. Very quietly. Just for him. "*Don't you dare say it.*"

He didn't say it.

He'd learned that too. The hard way. On the south road, in the Glass Desert, at the bottom of the world. In a wrecked ballroom with a girl with no shoes on, saying *never again*.

"*Except us*," said Lex.

Wren smiled. Fierce. Bright. And nocked her arrow.

And behind them, Tilda Crane came running across the green with Brannoc's hammer in both hands and her face white and determined, and Pell came with his bow, and stopped, and looked at Lex. Waiting.

Lex looked down into the round dark.

It breathed out against his face. Warm. Soft. Smelling of the Hollowmaw.

And somewhere far, far down, a voice that was not quite a voice, that he had heard before, once, years ago, coming out of his brother's mouth in a cave in the Greyspine, whispered up out of the dark.

Not hungry. Not cruel. Not broken and grinding like it had been then.

Small.

Frightened.

*...room...?*

*...please...make room...*
