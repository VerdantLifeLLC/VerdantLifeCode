# Listening to Nothing

Seraphine Ilvane had spent her whole life in rooms with too many books in them, and she had never once been inside a room with nothing in it at all.

She thought about that on the ninth night in the Blightmarch. Sitting in the tent. With the lamp turned low and the grey book open on the folding table and an old man asleep on the cot beside it, breathing slow and shallow and uneven. With the parish registers of Coldwater on one side of her and the Keepers' Unbinding list on the other and the Order's Kindling rolls on her knees.

Crossing them.

One name at a time. A finger on each list. *Born. Kindled. Not living.* *Born. Kindled. Not living.* *Born. Kindled.*

*Not living*.

And then, every hour or so, when he woke, reading them out to Arion. Slowly. One at a time. So he could write them in the grey book. In his own hand. In the place where the line was. Between the line and the price of bread.

*Abel Mason. Hewer. Thirty-one.*

*Bette Mason. His wife. Twenty-nine.*

*Nell Hask. Eight.*

He'd stopped at that one. For a long time. With the pencil shaking in his hand.

And then written it. Very carefully. Very small. Very neat.

*Nell Hask. Eight. She whistled at the window when her brother came home.*

That wasn't in any of the registers. Seraphine hadn't said it. Arion had added it himself.

---

Two thousand one hundred and six, so far.

Coldwater alone. Not counting Ashpit. Not counting Greyford, or Kell's Hollow, or the farms in between, or the drovers on the roads, or the travelling tinkers who never got written down anywhere.

She'd known it would be a lot. She hadn't known it would be like this.

Every one of them had a line. A name, a class, an age. Some of them had more. A note from the priest in the margin of the parish book. *Drowned in the millrace, 4th Harvestmonth.* *Sang in the choir.* *Was kind to my mother when she was dying.* The priest had been old and tired and he'd written things down that didn't need writing, the way old tired men do.

Seraphine had copied every one.

And Arion had written every one. In the grey book. In the space between.

It had taken him nine days to write two thousand one hundred and six names. His hand shook so badly that some of them were nearly illegible. He'd had to stop every twenty names to rest. He'd fallen asleep mid-word, twice, and woken, and found his place, and gone on.

He hadn't missed one.

---

She looked at him now. Asleep on the cot. White. Thin. With the bandage off his head now, and a scab there instead, and the grey book on the blanket on his chest where it had fallen when he slept.

Eighty-five, the book said. She'd checked. She checked every morning. She'd taken over the counting from him; his hand was needed for the names.

*Eighty-five*.

A season, she'd told them. In the library at the Keep. Perhaps two. Not past Lantern Night.

She'd done the sum again last night. With the new numbers.

She hadn't told anyone.

---

There was something wrong with him.

Not the age. Not the shaking. Not the falls. Something else. Something she couldn't name. She'd noticed it on the hill, the first day, when they set the litter down at the edge of the grey and he looked at the Blightmarch for the first time. Something in his face. Something *decided*.

She'd seen that face before. On Antho, on the night before the Highcrest singing. On Lex, at the Heart, before he went down into the anvil. On Seren, on the stairs at Harrow, in a memory Antho had told her once at three in the morning.

The face of someone who has made up their mind about something and isn't going to tell anyone.

She'd asked him. Twice. *Arion. What is it?* And he'd smiled. The wide startled smile. And said *I'm just tired, my dear. I'm eighty-five. I'm allowed to be tired.*

She didn't believe him.

But she was a scholar. And a scholar knows when a page has been torn out. You don't always know what was on it. You just know there's a gap.

---

She put the lamp out.

Not all the way. Down to a glow. And left the tent. Quietly. Without waking him. And went out into the dark.

The camp was asleep.

Thousands of them. Under the stars. On the bare grey ground. In tents and under wagons and wrapped in blankets by the dying fires. The forges were banked. The trench, the great long trench that went away east into the dark further than she could see, was quiet. Nobody digging. Nobody hammering.

Except one.

There was always one. Somewhere. In the dark. One anvil. Slow. Steady. Far off. The sound came across the grey like a heartbeat. Seraphine had stopped hearing it after the third night. She heard it now.

She knew whose it was.

---

She didn't go toward it.

She went the other way. Toward the ruins. Toward the fallen hall of Duskhold, black and broken under the stars. Through the gap in the wall where the gate had been. Across the rubble.

To the scar.

The dark line on the black floor. Warm. Running from one side of the hall to the other. The first wound. The place where Arion had knelt twenty-one years ago, and pulled, and torn the world.

Antho was there.

Of course he was. He was always there. Lying on his side on the black floor, with his hand on the line and his head on his arm and the clay lamp beside him. Asleep. Mostly. Not asleep. In the place between, where he went when he was listening to the dark.

She sat down beside him.

He didn't wake. But his free hand moved. Across the floor. And found hers. And held it.

---

She'd come here every night.

To listen. That was what she told herself. To listen to the little one, through the scar. To learn the shape of it. So she could tell Lex. So he'd know what shape to make the room.

That was true. She did listen. She did learn. She'd filled forty pages of her notebook with it. Shapes. Sizes. Folds. The way it curled and uncurled in the deep. The places where it was thick and the places where it was thin. The bite, where the Moth had drunk, still aching. The wounds where the shafts were, twenty-nine of them now, still open, still leaking.

She'd drawn it. Over and over. Trying to get it right.

She hadn't got it right yet.

It wasn't a shape. That was the problem. It kept *not* being a shape. Every time she thought she had it, every time she drew a line and thought *there, that's the edge*, it moved. Not much. A little. The way a room seems a different size depending on who's in it.

*It's the room*, she kept telling herself. *It's not a thing. It's the room the thing goes in. You can't draw a room. You can only draw the walls.*

*And it hasn't got walls*.

*That's what we're making it*.

---

But that wasn't the only reason she came.

She came because it was the only place in the world where she could hear it properly. And because, for the first time in her life, she could hear *something*.

She'd sat by Malachi's fire in the Pale Wastes for nineteen days. Five years ago. And heard nothing. While Lex learned to talk to flame and Wren learned to walk on the wind and Antho learned to rest in the dark.

Nothing.

*Some people don't*, Malachi had said. Gently. *It's not a failing. Someone has to write it down.*

She'd written it down. Five years. Every word. Every lesson. She'd copied the Codex nine times.

And then, in the kitchen of the Lantern Keep, with a black pebble on the table between the porridge and the teapot, Antho had said *pebble*.

And she'd heard the space round the word.

---

She closed her eyes now.

On the black floor of the fallen hall. With her hand in her husband's. With the scar warm under her other palm.

And listened.

---

At first, nothing. The way it always was at first. The wind over the broken walls. Antho breathing. The anvil, far off. Slow. Steady.

And then, under it—

*Not* under it. Between. In the spaces. In the gaps between one hammer-stroke and the next. Between one breath and the next. Between the wind and the wall.

The quiet.

Not silence. Silence was nothing. Silence was what you got when you took everything away. This was different. This was *full*. Full the way a held breath is full. Full the way a cup is full before you pour anything into it. Full of the possibility of being filled.

Room.

She could hear it. She could hear the *room*. Not the little one's voice; she'd never heard that, not the way Antho did. Not words. Not *brother* and *Mal* and *the cat wears boots*.

Just the space it made. Everywhere. In everything. In the gaps between things. The way, if you looked at a page long enough, you stopped seeing the letters and started seeing the white.

---

*Where are you*? she thought.

Not to it. She'd never spoken to it. She didn't know how. She thought it the way you think a question to yourself, sitting at a desk, staring at a page of Wellscript you can't read.

*Where's your edge*?

*How big are you, really*?

*What shape do you want to be*?

And the quiet—

*Moved*.

---

She felt it.

Not heard. *Felt*. Under her palm. Through the scar. Through the black floor. A shift. A ripple. The way a pond moves when something swims underneath it.

The room changed shape.

Not much. Not far. Just—round her. Close. The quiet that was everywhere drew in. Toward her. Toward her hand on the scar. The way people in a crowded hall draw back to make a space round someone who's fallen.

And there was a space.

Right there. Round her hand. Round her. On the black floor of the fallen hall. A small round room of quiet, the size of a bed, with her in the middle of it. With nothing in it. Not the wind. Not the anvil. Not Antho's breathing. Not even her own heart.

Nothing.

*Room*.

And in the room, very faintly, very far off, like someone speaking in a house three streets away—

*[...you asked...]*

---

Seraphine's eyes flew open.

The room was still there. Round her. Quiet. Empty. The wind had stopped. The anvil had stopped. She couldn't hear Antho, though he was right beside her, though his hand was in hers. She couldn't hear anything.

Except that.

*[...you asked me...]*

*[...what shape I want to be...]*

*[...nobody ever asked me that...]*

She couldn't breathe.

She'd never heard it. Never. Five years. She'd heard the space. The quiet. The room. She'd never once heard the *voice*.

She heard it now. Not the way Antho did. Not in the back of her skull, in an old place, where something used to live. There was no old place in her. Nothing had ever lived in her.

She heard it in the *room*.

In the space it had made round her. Like a voice in an empty church. Like a word written on a blank page.

*[...the one with the glass eyes...]* it said. Wondering. *[...the one who hears the gaps...]*

*[...you made me make a room...just by asking...]*

---

"*I didn't*—" She didn't know if she said it out loud. In the room, it didn't seem to matter. "*I didn't make you. I just asked.*"

*[...that's how...]*

*[...that's how it works...that's what I AM...]*

*[...somebody asks...and I make room...]*

She looked at her hand. On the scar. In the middle of the small round quiet.

"*I asked what shape you want to be*," she said.

*[...yes...]*

"*Do you know*?"

The room was quiet for a long time.

*[...no...]* it said. Very small. *[...I've been all the shapes...I was a pool...I was a hunger...I was the dark under the lid...I'm the room now...]*

*[...I don't know what shape I am when nobody's using me...]*

*[...nobody's ever let me just BE...]*

---

Seraphine Ilvane sat on the black floor of the fallen hall of Duskhold with her hand on a warm scar and listened to a Well tell her it didn't know what it was.

And for the first time in her life, she understood something before she'd read about it.

---

It wasn't a shape.

That was the trouble. She'd been trying to draw a *shape*. A thing. An outline. Edges. Walls.

The little one wasn't a thing. It was the space *for* a thing. It didn't have edges because edges were what the *thing* had. A room didn't have edges. The walls did.

*You can't draw a room*, she'd been telling herself. *You can only draw the walls*.

She'd been right. And she'd been wrong.

You couldn't draw a room.

But you could *write* one.

---

She was on her feet.

The small round quiet broke round her. Like a soap bubble. The wind came back. The anvil came back, far off. Antho's breathing. Her own heart, going so hard she could feel it in her ears.

Antho was sitting up. Awake. Staring at her.

"*Seraphine*—"

"*It talked to me*."

He went very still.

"*It talked to me*," said Seraphine. "*Not you. Not through you. To me. In a room. It made a room round me and talked to me in it.*" She was shaking. "*Antho. I heard it. I heard its* voice.*"*

His face—

She'd never forget his face. Sitting on the black floor of the fallen hall in the starlight with the clay lamp beside him and his long black hair everywhere and his eyes going wide and wet.

"*Oh*," said Antho Vey. "*Oh, love.*"

"*And I know what shape it is*," said Seraphine.

---

She ran.

She'd never been good at running. She'd learned late, from a book. She ran anyway. Across the rubble. Out through the gap where the gate had been. Across the grey ground. Between the sleeping tents and the banked forges and the dying fires.

Toward the anvil.

---

He was at the head of the trench.

Where it started. At the edge of the ruins. Where Duskhold's great gate had been, and the black rubble of it lay in a long slope down to the grey ground. He'd built an anvil out of a block of the fallen Gloam-stone, black and smooth, and set a hearth beside it out of more of the same, and he was standing at it in the dark, alone, in his shirtsleeves, with a hammer.

Not working iron. There was no iron on the anvil.

Working *stone*.

A lump of grey Blightmarch dust, packed hard. The same dust as the trench. As the whole hundred miles of it. He was hammering it. Slowly. Steadily. Asking it. Shaping it. Making it hold.

Testing. The way you test sand for a mould. Pack it. Strike it. See if it keeps the shape.

It didn't.

She watched it crumble under his hammer. Watched him look at it. Watched him sweep it off the anvil with the side of his hand. And scoop up more. And pack it. And lift the hammer.

His face was grey. His eyes were red. The handprint on his cheek glittered in the light of the hearth.

"*Lex*," she said.

---

He looked up.

"*Seraphine*." His voice was hoarse. "*What's wrong? Is it Father? Is he*—"

"*He's asleep*. *He's fine.*" She was breathless. "*Lex. Lex, stop. Stop hammering.*"

He stopped.

"*It doesn't hold*," he said. He looked at the dust on the anvil. "*Nine days. It doesn't hold. Not the trench, not the walls, not the floor. I've tried everything. Brannoc's tried everything. The Ashari bake it and it cracks. Teo's tide wets it and it slumps.*" He rubbed his face. "*It's dead, Seraphine. The whole Blightmarch. Twenty years of Gloam. There's nothing left in it to hold a shape. It's like trying to make a mould out of ash.*"

"*That's because you're making the wrong thing*," said Seraphine.

He looked at her.

---

"*You're making a hole*," said Seraphine. "*A pit. A bowl. A shape. Walls and a floor and edges.*" She was gasping. She'd run the whole way. "*That's what a mould is. I know. You're right. It needs a shape.*"

"*Then*—"

"*It doesn't have a shape*." She grabbed his arm. His big scarred forearm, grey with dust. "*Lex. It doesn't have one. I asked it. Just now. At the scar. It made a room round me and I asked it what shape it wanted to be.*"

He went very still.

"*It said it doesn't know*," said Seraphine. "*It said it's been every shape. A pool. A hunger. The dark under the lid. A room.*" Her voice cracked. "*It said nobody's ever let it just* be.*"*

"*Then what do we*—"

"*We don't make it a shape*," said Seraphine Ilvane. "*We make it a* word.*"*

---

She pulled out her notebook.

Right there. At the anvil. In the light of his hearth. She flipped past forty pages of shapes, of outlines, of drawings that hadn't been right. To the back. To a page she'd drawn on the very first night, in the library at the Keep, before any of this.

A circle. With a break in it.

The glyph. From Malachi's vellum. The one scratched into the black. The one Lex had cut into every Hush-cup, on the inside, on the curve, where the palms would be.

*Hollow*.

"*Wellscript*," said Seraphine. "*Lex. Listen. The Founders' glyphs. You used to read them. With the Root. You know what they are.*"

"*Runes*."

"*They're not runes*." She shook her head. Hard. "*Runes are what you cut into iron. To tell it what to do. Ardent. Stay. Hush.*" She tapped the page. "*Wellscript's older. Wellscript's what the Listeners wrote before the net. Before runes. Before anything.*" She looked at him. "*Do you know what a Wellscript glyph* is?*"*

He shook his head.

"*It's a name*," said Seraphine. "*Not a word for a thing. The thing's own name. The name a Well would answer to. If you wrote it right. If you wrote it big enough.*" She looked at the broken circle on the page. "*That's why the Codex is written in them. That's why Malachi drew the seventh in the middle and filled it black and scratched this into it.*"

She looked up at him.

"*He wasn't labelling it*," said Seraphine. "*He was calling it.*"

---

Lex stared at the page.

At the broken circle. At the gap. At the place where the ring didn't quite close.

"*A hundred miles*," he said slowly.

"*A hundred miles*." Seraphine was nodding. Fast. "*Not a bowl. Not a pit. A* glyph. *Cut into the ground. Into the Blightmarch. Round. A hundred miles across.*" She drew her finger round the circle on the page. "*The trench is the line. You've already started it. You started it nine days ago. You just didn't know what you were writing.*"

She stopped her finger. At the break. At the gap in the circle.

"*And here*," she said. "*Where it doesn't close.*"

He looked at it.

"*Where's that*?" said Lex Vey.

Seraphine looked past him. Past the anvil. Past the hearth. At the black ruins rising behind him in the starlight. At the gap in the fallen wall where the gate had been. At the hall beyond it. At the warm dark scar on the floor.

"*There*," she said. "*The breach.*"

---

He didn't say anything for a long time.

He stood at his anvil of black Gloam-stone in the middle of the grey plain at two in the morning with the hammer hanging from his hand and looked at the page in her notebook. At the broken circle. At the gap.

And she watched it happen.

She'd watched it happen before. In the gold room at the bottom of the world, when his mother said *somewhere with room*. In the long hall of the Ashbourne Forge, when Ivy Moss said *change them*. It came up out of his hands. Always. Out of the part of him that had stood at an anvil since he was eleven.

"*A mould*," he said. Very slowly. "*Is the shape the metal takes.*"

"*Yes*."

"*So if the mould's a word*—"

"*Then whatever pours into it*," said Seraphine, "*takes the shape of the word.*"

"*It takes its own name*."

"*It takes its own name*." Her eyes were stinging. "*It comes up through the breach. Into the circle. Into a hundred miles of its own name, written in the ground, by everyone who came here to make room for it.*" She swallowed. "*And it's not a pool. And it's not a hunger. And it's not the dark under a lid.*"

"*It's itself*," said Lex.

"*It's itself*," said Seraphine Ilvane. "*For the first time. In nine hundred years. Since before the Nine. Since it was a little pool in a valley that nobody ever stopped at.*"

She closed the notebook.

"*We're not digging it a hole*," she said. "*We're writing it a name. So it knows what it is when it gets here.*"

---

Lex put down the hammer.

On the anvil. Very carefully. The way you'd put down something you weren't going to need for a while.

Then he picked her up.

Off the ground. Notebook and all. And swung her round. Once. In the starlight. In the middle of the grey plain. The way Brannoc had picked up his mother on the slope of the Hollowmaw.

"*Put me down*!" Seraphine was laughing. Shrieking. "*Lex! Put me down! You'll wake the whole camp!*"

He put her down.

And kissed her on the forehead. Hard. The way his mother had, in the gold room.

"*You heard it*," said Lex Vey.

"*I heard it*."

"*It talked to you*."

"*It talked to me*." She was crying now. She couldn't stop. "*It made a room. And I was in it. And it talked.*"

He looked at her. With his red eyes and his grey face and the handprint glittering.

"*Malachi would be so*—" He stopped. His voice cracked. "*He said someone has to write it down. He said it wasn't a failing.*"

"*I know*."

"*He was right*," said Lex. "*Someone had to. Someone had to sit there for five years writing it all down. Every word. Every glyph.*" He tapped the notebook in her hands. "*So that when the time came, someone would know how to write its name.*"

---

The panel came while she was standing there.

In the dark. At the anvil. With Lex's hands on her shoulders and her notebook against her chest and her face wet.

Silver. Gold. And something else. The clear color. The window color. The color of the air in an empty room.

---

She read it twice.

*Scribe of the Seventh*.

Then she laughed. Wetly. Into her hands.

"*It doesn't know what to call it*," she said. "*Look. Unrecognized. The net still doesn't have a name for it.*"

"*Not yet*," said Lex.

She looked up at him.

"*It will*," said Lex Vey. "*On Lantern Night. When it comes up into a hundred miles of its own name.*" He looked out at the trench. At the long dark line of it going away east under the stars. "*Then the net'll have to call it something.*"

---

She went back to the tent at dawn.

Exhausted. Dizzy. With the notebook full. With Lex already gone to wake Brannoc and Grandmother Asha and Teo and every smith in the camp, shouting, *it's a word, it's a word, we're writing a word*, so that the whole Blightmarch woke at once to the sound of a Worldforger running between the tents like a boy on Kindling-day.

She lifted the tent-flap.

And stopped.

The cot was empty.

The blanket was folded. Neatly. At the foot. The way a Warden folds a blanket, in the Spire, in the dormitory, every morning for two hundred years.

The grey book was on the table.

Open.

---

She went to it. Slowly. She didn't want to. She went.

It was open to the page where the names were. Two thousand one hundred and six of them. In his shaking hand. Between the line and the price of bread.

And under the last one, a space.

And under the space, three lines she'd never seen before. In a hand that shook more than all the others. As if he'd written them in the dark. By feel.

*Price: known. Her household drinks from her hands.*

*So will I. Once. Long enough to climb the stairs.*

*Then I'll give it back*.

---

Seraphine read it three times.

Then she put the book down. Very carefully. On the table. Open. The way he'd left it.

And went outside. Into the dawn. Into the grey. Into the noise of the camp waking, and Lex shouting, and Brannoc shouting back, and the Ashari cackling, and the first hammers starting up, one by one, down the long line of the trench.

She looked south.

Toward Highcrest. A hundred miles off. Beyond the edge of seeing.

Toward the Spire. And a room at the top of it. And a chair by a window.

"*Antho*," she said. Not loudly. Not to anyone.

And then louder. Running. Toward the ruins. Toward the fallen hall. Toward the scar on the floor, where her husband was asleep with his hand on it and the clay lamp beside him.

"*Antho*!"

---

Ashka was gone from the camp.

So was the litter.

And in the soft grey dust at the edge of the trench, where the dragon had been sleeping, Seraphine found the marks. Ashka's great claws. The drag of the litter-poles. And between them, very faint, so faint she'd never have seen it if she hadn't spent her whole life noticing what wasn't there—

Footprints.

Small ones. Bare. A boy's.

Going to the dragon. Climbing up.

Not coming back.
