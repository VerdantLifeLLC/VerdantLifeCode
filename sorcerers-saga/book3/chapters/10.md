# The Ferryman's Keeping

It had felt like the first night.

That was what Antho told his brother, afterward, lying on the cot in the library with a cold cloth on his forehead and Seraphine's hand in his and Lex sitting on the floor beside him, still in his Thistledown dust, with dragon-wind in his hair. It had felt like the first night. The night of his Kindling. Sixteen years old. With his hand on the Ashbourne Stone and the Stone *screaming* and flashing black, and something coming in through the crack in him like water through a broken dam.

Not the same. Not that bad. Nothing would ever be that bad again.

But the same *shape*.

He'd been at the long table. Reading. A Founders' record about the old Wells, in a hand so crabbed he'd had to hold it up to the lamp. Seraphine had been at the other end, with her map and her pins. Pim had been under the table, with a cat. It had been very quiet. A spring evening. The best kind of quiet.

And then something fifty miles south had *broken*.

He'd felt it go. Not heard. Felt. Like a bone snapping somewhere in his own body. And then the thing under the world, the vast soft young thing that had been pressing against his door for two months, had *flinched*. All of it. At once. And the flinch had come up through the door and through the floor and through the Threshold and through the old place in the back of his skull where the Murmur used to live—

And it had screamed.

And he'd screamed with it. He hadn't been able to help it. It had come out of his mouth the way it used to. In the old days. In the cave. In a voice that wasn't his.

*It HURTS*.

---

"*Your Clarity dropped to seventy-one*," said Seraphine.

She said it the way she said everything. Precisely. Holding his hand very tight.

"*I counted*," she said. "*I watched your panel. It went down like a stone. Ninety-eight. Ninety. Eighty-two. Seventy-one.*" Her voice shook. Just a little. "*And then it stopped. And then it started to come back.*"

"*It's back to ninety-four*," said Antho. "*I can feel it.*"

"*I know*. *I'm still watching.*"

He looked at her. At her spectacles, crooked. At the ink on her nose. At the line between her eyebrows, which was very deep.

"*It wasn't the Murmur*," he said. "*Seraphine. It wasn't—it wasn't trying to get in. It wasn't trying to use me. It was just—*" He didn't know how to say it. "*It was just screaming. And I was the nearest thing with a mouth.*"

"*I know*," said Seraphine. "*I know, love. I know.*"

She didn't let go of his hand.

---

Lex told him about Silas Hask.

All of it. Sitting on the library floor with his back against the cot and his big scarred hands hanging between his knees. The inn yard. The grey-lung. Coldwater. Four thousand people going up like mine-damp. The pick. The fault. *You took off my Ceiling, smith. Don't you dare put a new one on.*

The moth on his shoulder.

Antho listened with his eyes shut.

"*He's not wrong*," he said, when Lex had finished.

"*I know he's not wrong*."

"*That's the trouble*," said Antho. "*Isn't it. He's not wrong. He's dying and it makes him live. He's poor and it makes him strong. He's been capped his whole life and now he isn't.*" He opened his eyes. "*And every time he goes up, it screams.*"

They were quiet for a while.

"*I need to know what it is*," said Antho. "*Properly. Not what Seraphine's books say. Not what Malachi's notes say. Not what she says.*" He didn't need to say who *she* was. "*I need to know from someone who was there.*"

"*Who was there*?" said Lex. "*Nine hundred years ago? Everyone's dead. Malachi's dead. Aurelian's dead. The only person who was there is*—"

He stopped.

Antho looked at him.

"*No*," said Lex. "*Not her.*"

"*Not her*," Antho agreed. He sat up. Slowly. The cold cloth fell off his forehead into his lap. "*Someone else. Someone who was there before her. Before the pool. Before the Wells. Before the world.*"

He looked at the window. The narrow slit window high in the library wall. West.

"*Someone who's been here since before the world was made*," said Antho Vey. "*And will be here when it's gone.*"

---

He didn't go alone.

He'd thought about it. Lying on the cot that night, after Lex had gone to sleep on the library floor and Seraphine had gone to sleep in the chair with her head on his chest. He'd thought, *I could go now. On Ashka. Before anyone wakes up. Be there and back in four days.*

He'd thought it the way you think about an old road you used to walk every day. Without meaning to. Your feet just start going that way.

He made them stop.

*I'm trying*, he'd told his brother. *I'm trying very hard to be the kind of man who doesn't carry things alone.*

So in the morning, at breakfast, in the kitchen of the Lantern Keep, with porridge and Odile's terrible tea and Lex half asleep over a bowl and Seraphine reading a letter from Highcrest, he put down his spoon and said, out loud, to everyone: "*I'm going to see the Ferryman.*"

The kitchen went quiet.

"*Today*," said Antho. "*On Ashka. West past the Teeth. Four days. Maybe five.*" He looked at his brother. "*I'm telling you before I go. Not after. Not from the road. Before.*"

Lex looked at him for a long moment.

Then he nodded. Once.

"*Who's going with you*?" he said.

Antho opened his mouth.

And Pim, at the end of the table, with porridge on his chin, put down his spoon. And got up. And went and stood beside Antho's chair. In his blue coat with the brass buttons.

And didn't say anything at all.

---

They flew west in the morning.

Over the Teeth. The great jagged white peaks at the western end of the Greyspine, where the mountains rose highest and sharpest, like the jaw of something buried under the world. Ashka climbed and climbed, beating hard in the thin cold air, until the snowfields were blinding below them and the wind cut like glass and Pim, wrapped in two coats and a blanket in front of Antho in the saddle, had his face buried in the dragon's neck.

And then down. The other side. West.

Into the thinned.

---

It was different.

Antho had come this way twice before. Two years ago, alone, walking, with Ashka limping beside him, looking for a grey river and a man who took payment in memories. And a year and a half ago, at the very end, coming back from the Heart with everyone, on dragons, too tired to look.

Two years ago it had been grey.

All of it. The land west of the Teeth. Grey grass. Grey trees. Grey sky. A grey that wasn't a color, that was the *absence* of color, the way the world looks just before you faint. The further west you went, the thinner it got. Things lost their edges. Their weight. You could put your hand through a tree, almost, if you pushed. The ground under your feet felt like wet paper. The world, Malachi had said, was worn through there. Like the elbow of an old coat. Like a place where the Tithe had been drawing off the world for so long that there was hardly any world left.

It wasn't grey now.

Not all of it. Not as much. As Ashka came down out of the mountains into the long low hills west of the Teeth, Antho saw green. Faint. Patchy. Like the first green on a field in early spring, when you can't tell if it's grass or wishful thinking. But green. On the hills. In the hollows. Along the streams.

And the streams had *sound*.

He hadn't noticed, two years ago. That the water here didn't make a noise. That it slid over its stones without a murmur, like a thing too tired to talk. He noticed now. Because it was talking. Small. Faint. A chuckle over a shallow. A gurgle round a root.

"*It's coming back*," said Antho. Out loud. To no one.

Pim lifted his head out of Ashka's neck. And looked down at the faint green hills. With his grey-violet eyes very wide.

"*Since the Tithe stopped*," said Antho. "*The world's been worn through here for nine hundred years. And now it's*—" He didn't have the word.

"*Healing*," said Pim.

Antho looked down at him.

Pim didn't look round. He was staring at the hills.

"*Like a scab*," said Pim. "*Under. Where the new skin comes.*" He touched his own elbow. Where there was an old white scar. From Kessick. From the man who'd tried to beat the voices out of him. "*It itches.*"

---

The river was silver.

Antho saw it from a long way off. From the top of the last hill. A line across the bottom of the long valley, running north to south, wide and flat and slow. Two years ago it had been grey. The grey of slate. The grey of a dead man's face. *The Tithe runs through it*, the Ferryman had said. *Every thread cut, every mana taken. It runs through my water. It makes it heavy.*

It was silver now.

Clear. Bright. Catching the light. With the sky in it.

And on the near bank, on the grey shingle at the water's edge, a long flat boat. Grey. Low. Like an eel-fisher's punt.

And beside it, leaning on a long pole, a tall thin figure in grey robes.

On top of the pole, a lantern.

Lit.

---

The Ferryman turned his head as they came down the shingle.

He had no eyes. Antho had known that. He'd stood in front of this face twice before. It still made his stomach turn over, every time. Not because it was frightening. It wasn't, exactly. It was a long thin face, grey, smooth, old as river-stones. With a mouth, and a nose, and where the eyes should be, nothing. Not holes. Not scars. Just smooth grey skin. As if no one had ever thought to put eyes there. As if he'd never needed them.

"*Anthoren Vey*," said the Ferryman.

"*Ferryman*."

"*You skipped it again*." The eyeless face turned toward him. Slowly. "*The stone. On the lake past the Teeth. Last summer. Five times.*" A pause. "*I felt it. In the water. All lakes are my water, a little.*"

Antho didn't say anything.

He'd been fifteen. With a small boy beside him, on a riverbank, on a summer evening. *Low, low, like you're trying to make it fly under a door*. The stone had skipped five times. He'd told his brother it was four. And years later, under the stars, he'd told him the truth.

He'd paid that memory to this river. Two years ago. To cross. It was gone. A warm empty hollow where it used to be. He'd only seen it once more. From the river's side. Through the water. On the way to the Heart.

And then last summer, at the lake past the Teeth, he'd picked up a flat grey stone and thrown it. Low. Like he was trying to make it fly under a door.

And it had skipped five times. And he'd counted.

"*I'm glad*," said the Ferryman. "*It was a good memory. I've kept it very carefully.*" His long grey hand moved on his pole. "*It is still here. In the river. If you ever want to look again.*"

"*I know*," said Antho. "*Thank you.*"

The eyeless face turned. Away from him. Down. To the small figure beside him in a blue coat with brass buttons.

"*And who*," said the Ferryman, "*is this?*"

---

Pim didn't hide.

Antho had thought he might. Pim hid from everything new. Strangers. Dragons. Loud noises. The first time Lex came to the Keep, Pim had spent three days in the cold-room with the bats.

He didn't hide from the Ferryman.

He stood on the grey shingle at the edge of the silver river in his blue coat and looked up into the tall grey eyeless face. Steadily. With his grey-violet eyes.

And was silent.

Not the way he was usually silent. The *other* way. The deep way. The way he'd been silent in the middle of the moths at the Keep. A place in him where nothing was. Where nothing had been allowed to be.

The Ferryman went very still.

For a long time. Longer than Antho had ever seen him be still, and he was the stillest thing Antho had ever seen.

"*Oh*," said the Ferryman at last. Very softly. "*Oh, little one. They hurt you very badly, didn't they.*"

Pim didn't answer.

"*And you made a room*," said the Ferryman. "*Inside. Where it couldn't reach. And you went into it. And you've been sitting in it ever since.*" The eyeless face bent lower. "*And it's grown. Your room. Hasn't it. Bigger than you. Bigger than the hurt.*"

Pim's mouth trembled.

"*That's the seventh*," said the Ferryman. "*That's what you've got. Did you know? A little piece of it. Of the room in the middle. You made it yourself. Out of nothing. Out of being hurt.*" His long grey hand came up. And stopped. In the air. An inch from Pim's face. Not touching. "*No wonder the moths won't go near you. There's nothing in you for them to drink. You've made yourself into a room with the lamp out.*"

Pim stood very still.

And then, very slowly, he reached up. And took hold of the Ferryman's long grey fingers. In his small thin hand.

And held them.

---

"*You came to ask me something*," said the Ferryman.

He was sitting in his boat. On the bench at the stern. With his pole across his knees and his lantern at his feet, burning. Antho sat on the shingle in front of him. Pim sat in the boat. In the bow. With his knees pulled up and his chin on them, looking at the river.

The Ferryman hadn't said anything about Pim getting into the boat. He'd just moved his pole so the boy had room.

"*Yes*," said Antho.

"*Ask, then*."

Antho looked at the river. Silver. Slow. Wide. The far bank was there now. He could see it. Two years ago there'd been nothing on the far side. Just grey. Just mist. The far shore of the dead.

There were hills on the far side now. Low. Faint. Green, a little.

"*What's coming up*?" said Antho.

---

The Ferryman was quiet for a long time.

"*The seventh*," he said at last. "*The one in the middle.*"

"*The Hollow*."

"*That's what your Founders called it*." The eyeless face turned toward the river. "*It had other names. Before. Older ones. The Room. The Cup. The First Quiet. The place before a thing.*" A pause. "*It was very small, when I first knew it. Smaller than the others. Flame was a great wild roaring thing even then. Stone was older than anything. Tide and Gale were everywhere. Radiance and Shade went on forever.*" His long hand moved on his pole. "*The seventh was only a little pool. In a valley. In the middle of everything. Very still. Very dark. Very deep.*"

"*What did it do*?"

"*It made room*."

The Ferryman was quiet again.

"*When a new thing came into the world*," he said, "*a new spring, a new kind of bird, a new child, it came through the seventh. Through the room in the middle. That's what it was for. It was the door new things came in by.*" He tilted his head. "*Without it, nothing new. Only the old things, going round and round. Fire burning. Water running. Stone sitting. The same, forever.*"

He looked toward Antho with his eyeless face.

"*They built a palace over it*," said the Ferryman. "*The sorcerer-kings. They found the pool, and they built a hall of glass round it, and they knelt at the edge of it, and they drank.*"

---

"*How many*?" said Antho.

"*Nine*."

"*She said nine*."

"*She told the truth*." The Ferryman's voice didn't change. "*She always does. That is her art. She has never needed to lie. She only needs to choose where to stand.*"

Antho went very still.

"*Nine*," said the Ferryman. "*Nine crowns. The eldest found it. The Gilded King. Oszric. He drank first. And then he brought his brothers and his sisters, and they drank. And then they brought their households. A little each. From their own cupped hands.*"

"*And it went dry*."

"*It went dry*." The Ferryman looked at the river. "*It was only a little pool. It was never meant to be drunk. It was meant to be a door. They drank the door.*" He was quiet. "*And what was left, when the water was gone, was the shape of the water. The hollow where it had been. Empty. And an empty room wants to be filled. That is what a room is for.*"

"*The hunger*," said Antho.

"*The hunger*." The Ferryman nodded slowly. "*It began to pull. At everything. To fill itself. Fire and stone and water and wind. People. Worlds.*" He looked toward the far bank. "*There were other worlds then. Near this one. Like boats moored side by side. It pulled them in. One by one. It couldn't help it. It was the shape of a room with nothing in it, and it was trying to stop being empty.*"

Antho thought of the Forbidden Lands. At the bottom of the deep. Eaten worlds, drifting. Grey. Endless.

"*And the Founders caught it*."

"*Aurelian caught it*. *And his brothers and sisters.*" The Ferryman's voice was very quiet. "*They wove the net over the world, and they put a lid on the hunger, and they fed it the Tithe to keep it quiet. And Aurelian sat on the lid. For nine hundred years. Holding it.*" A pause. "*You know the rest. You were there at the end of it.*"

"*And the Nine*?"

---

The Ferryman didn't answer for a long time.

The river ran past. Silver. Slow. The lantern on the end of his pole swung gently in the wind off the water.

"*I carried them*," he said.

Antho stared at him.

"*Out*," said the Ferryman. "*Past the edge. Past the place where the net was tied off. Into the grey country beyond.*" His long hands lay still on his pole. "*Aurelian came to my shore. With the Nine. In chains. Not iron chains. Net. Woven. Threads of the net he'd made, round their wrists and ankles.*" He was quiet. "*He asked me to take them across. Out of the world. Where they couldn't drink anything ever again.*"

"*And you did*."

"*He paid*." The eyeless face turned toward Antho. "*Everyone pays. That is the river's law.*"

"*What did he pay*?"

The Ferryman was silent for a very long time.

"*His sleep*," he said at last.

Antho didn't understand.

"*He never slept again*," said the Ferryman. "*From that night. Nine hundred years. He sat on the lid and held the hunger and never once closed his eyes.*" A pause. "*I think that is why it ate him, in the end. A man who never sleeps has no door to go back through. No room to rest in. He was all holding and no letting go.*"

Antho thought of an old man at a gold anvil at the bottom of the world. Curled up. Holding a little hammer. *I'm the flaw*.

He couldn't say anything.

---

"*Four of them came apart*," said the Ferryman. "*Against the edge. When I pushed them through. The net was very tight, then. New. It didn't like them.*" He looked at the river. "*Four came apart into dust. Five went through.*"

"*Five*."

"*The Gilded King. The Glass Queen. The Iron Crown. The one with thorns, whose name they don't say.*" The Ferryman paused. "*And the Moth.*"

"*Maelis*."

"*Maelis*." The eyeless face was turned toward the water. "*She sat in the bow of my boat. Where your boy is sitting now. The whole way out. With her lit eyes. Looking back.*" He was quiet. "*Not at the world. At the lights in the world. Every lamp. Every window. Every fire. She looked back at them the whole way across, the way a starving child looks back at a bakery window.*"

He turned his face toward Antho.

"*She came to my shore three nights ago*."

---

Antho was on his feet.

He didn't remember standing. He was standing on the grey shingle with the Lantern in his hand and his heart going like a drum, staring at the tall grey figure in the boat.

"*Here*?"

"*Here*." The Ferryman didn't move. "*At midnight. Walking. Leaving no tracks.*" He tilted his head. "*She asked me very politely to take her down. To the deep. To the bottom of the river, where it runs into the dark under the world.*" A pause. "*She said she wanted to meet the little one. Before it reached the light. She said she had something to say to it.*"

"*And*?"

"*I said no*."

The Ferryman's voice was very quiet. And very cold. Colder than Antho had ever heard it. Cold as the river had been, two years ago, when it ran grey with the Tithe.

"*I don't carry the Crowned*," said the Ferryman. "*Not down. Not ever again. I carried them out once. I was paid for it. I have kept that payment nine hundred years.*" His long hands tightened on his pole. "*I will not carry her to the thing she drank. Not for any price.*"

Antho breathed out.

"*She laughed*," said the Ferryman. "*She has a lovely laugh. She thanked me. She said she quite understood. She said there were other ways down.*"

He turned his eyeless face toward Antho.

"*Every time a Delve opens*," said the Ferryman, "*there is a way down. From the world to the deep. A hole. A thread of the little one, reaching up toward the light.*" A pause. "*When the core is claimed, the hole closes. Softly. The thread draws back. Like a hand drawn back from a flame.*"

Antho felt his stomach go cold.

"*And when the core is broken*," said the Ferryman, "*the thread snaps. And the hole stays open. A wound. A shaft. Straight down. All the way to the little one.*" He was quiet. "*Twenty-three, so far. I can feel them. Like splinters in the river.*"

---

*Twenty-three*.

Antho thought of Seraphine's map. Forty-one black pins. And the Uncapped. Thousands of them. With picks and belaying pins and bright hungry faces. Breaking cores in tavern cellars and inn yards. Going up three levels in a breath.

And a moth. Grey-gold. Settling on Silas Hask's broad patched shoulder. And Silas Hask not brushing it off.

"*She's using them*," said Antho. "*The Uncapped. She's—she doesn't need to break the cores herself. She just needs someone else to. And then she walks down the hole.*"

"*Yes*," said the Ferryman.

"*Does Hask know*?"

"*I don't know who that is*." The eyeless face turned toward him. "*But I know her. She doesn't need anyone to know. She only needs them to want.*" A pause. "*That is what a moth is, Anthoren Vey. It doesn't break the window. It waits for someone to open it.*"

---

He asked the last thing at dusk.

He hadn't meant to. He'd meant to go. He'd got what he came for. More. He should be on Ashka already, beating east over the Teeth, to tell Lex. To tell Seraphine. To tell the Queen.

But the sun was going down over the far bank, red and gold on the silver river, and the lantern on the Ferryman's pole was glowing brighter as the light went, and Pim was asleep in the bow of the boat with his head on his arms. And Antho sat on the grey shingle and looked at the tall eyeless figure in the stern, and the question came up out of him before he could stop it.

"*My mother paid you*," he said.

The Ferryman didn't move.

"*Thirteen years ago*," said Antho. "*The first winter. To cross back. To come home. She paid you the memory she'd least wish to lose.*" He swallowed. "*She paid you my father.*"

"*Yes*."

"*All of him*."

"*All of him*." The Ferryman's voice was very quiet. "*Every day. Every word. The steps of the Spire in the rain. The first time he laughed. The night your brother was born. Everything.*" He was silent for a moment. "*I told you once she paid more than anyone has ever paid me. I meant it. Most people pay one memory. She paid a man.*"

"*And then she didn't cross*."

"*She crossed*." The eyeless face turned toward him. "*She stood on this shore. On the world's side. With the way home in front of her.*" A pause. "*And she heard you. Far off. Over the mountains. At the Hollowmaw. Sixteen years old, in the cold, with the dark coming through the crack you'd torn. Screaming.*" The Ferryman was quiet. "*And she turned round. And walked back down my shingle. Into the dark. To hold it shut from the other side.*"

Antho couldn't breathe.

"*She paid*," said the Ferryman. "*She didn't get what she paid for. I don't give refunds. The river takes what it's owed.*"

---

"*Can she have it back*?"

It came out of Antho in a rush. Hoarse. Too loud. Pim stirred in the bow and didn't wake.

"*My father*," said Antho. "*Her memories of him. Can she have them back? If someone pays? If—*"

"*An equal price*," said the Ferryman.

"*What's equal*?"

The Ferryman was quiet for a long time.

"*A love for a love*," he said at last. "*That is the only thing that weighs the same.*" He turned his face toward the east. Toward the Teeth. Toward the world. "*Someone would have to pay me all their memories of someone they loved as much as she loved him. Every day. Every word. The whole person.*" A pause. "*And then I would give her back hers.*"

Antho stared at him.

"*Your father has thought of it*," said the Ferryman. Quietly. "*I know he has. He'll come to my shore. Before long.*" The eyeless face didn't change. "*Old men always do.*"

---

He flew east through the night.

He didn't stop. He didn't sleep. He sat in the saddle with Pim asleep against his chest and the Lantern hanging from the pommel and the wind in his face, and Ashka beat up and over the Teeth in the dark and down the other side, and the stars went round.

*He'll come to my shore. Before long. Old men always do*.

*A love for a love*.

*Someone would have to pay me all their memories of someone they loved as much as she loved him*.

*Your father has thought of it*.

He thought about his father. In the library. At the long table. With his white temples and his brown-spotted hands. Saying *I've been counting*.

He thought about his father saying *good evening* to a little clay lamp. Every night. For a year and a half. For a woman who didn't know who he was.

He thought: *he would*.

*He'd do it. He'd go down to that shore and give the Ferryman every memory he has of her. Every one. The steps of the Spire and the rain and the night Lex was born. So she could have hers back. So she'd come home and know him.*

*And he'd stand in front of her and not know who she was*.

Antho put his face down against Pim's hair and held on.

---

They came down onto the slope of the Hollowmaw at dawn.

The Keep was quiet. Too quiet. The cave mouth was a dark arch in the grey morning. No one on Pim's rock. No smoke from the kitchen chimney.

And then Odile came running out of it.

She was white. In her nightgown. Barefoot. In the snow. She'd never run in her life, not that Antho had seen. Odile walked. Carefully. Like a scholar. Like a Shade-Listener.

She came down the slope at a run and grabbed Ashka's bridle before the dragon had folded her wings and looked up at Antho with her dark eyes enormous.

"*Master Antho*," she said. "*It's your father.*"

---

He was at the door.

At the bottom of the long passage. In the bell-shaped cavern. On the black floor in front of the black glass door. Where he'd gone, at midnight, the way he always went, with his copper oil-flask, to fill the lamps.

He was lying on his side. With the flask spilled beside him. And his hand stretched out toward the niche. Toward the three lamps.

Seraphine was kneeling beside him. With his head in her lap. And Lex was there, on his other side, with Wren, and Lex's face was grey.

Antho stopped in the mouth of the cavern.

He couldn't go any further.

His father opened his eyes.

Grey. Steady. The same grey as Antho's. In a face that was—

*Old*.

Not fifty-eight. Not sixty. Old. The face of a man of seventy. More. The lines had gone deep, deep as plough-furrows, across his forehead and down his cheeks and round his mouth. His hair was white. All of it. Not white at the temples. White. Thin. Fine as thistledown. His hands, on the black floor, were all knuckle and bone and brown spots, and they shook.

He'd been sixty, four days ago. When Antho left for the Ferryman.

"*Antho*," said Arion Vey. His voice was thin. Cracked. An old man's voice. But it was his. "*You're back.*"

Antho came across the floor. And knelt. And took his father's shaking hand.

"*What happened*?"

"*I came down to fill the lamps*." His father smiled. A little. "*And I bent down. To the niche. And I couldn't get up again.*" He looked at his own hand, in Antho's. "*I think I've got the numbers now. I think I can tell you.*"

"*Tell me*."

"*Ten years*," said Arion Vey. "*Last night. In one night.*" He looked up at his son. "*It's not slowing down, Antho. It's speeding up.*"

He was quiet for a moment.

"*Two hundred years*," he said. "*The Root held them back for two hundred years. Like a dam. And I took the dam away.*" His thin old fingers tightened on Antho's. "*And now the water's coming. All of it. All at once.*"

Beside him, in the niche, the three lamps burned. Small and gold and steady.

And the clay one leaned. Very slightly. Toward the door.

As if someone a long way off, walking up out of the deep, had stopped. And turned. And was listening.
