# Interlude: The Sister Who Would Not Drink

Nine hundred years before a smith's son was born in a cottage at Harrow, a girl of seventeen sat on the edge of a fountain in a garden of glass and listened to a pool cry.

Nobody else could hear it.

She'd known that since she was small. Since she was four, and had sat on the cold grey stone at the edge of the pool in the cellars of her father's palace, before there was a hall of glass built over it, before anyone but the masons knew it was there. She'd sat there for hours. Listening. To the quiet. To the *full*. To the soft deep dark in the stone ring that sounded, if you listened long enough, like the moment before somebody says your name.

Her nurse had found her and smacked her and carried her upstairs. *Strange child*, the nurse had said. *Sitting in the dark talking to a puddle.*

She'd never told anyone else.

She'd never needed to. She was the tenth child. The youngest. The one nobody noticed. Her brothers and sisters were kings and queens. She was a girl in a grey dress who liked gardens.

Nobody asked what she could hear.

---

It had been glad, at first.

That was the worst of it. She'd think about that for the rest of her life, in twenty-two cottages in twenty-two valleys, by twenty-two fires. That it had been *glad*.

When Oszric found it. When he came down into the cellars with his masons and his torches and his great gold beard, and knelt at the edge of the stone ring, and looked down into the dark, and laughed. When he cupped his hands and drank, the first time, and stood up with his eyes wide and his face flushed and said *brothers, sisters, come and see what I've found.*

The pool had been glad.

She'd heard it. Standing at the back, behind the others, in her grey dress. A ripple. A brightness. Like a cat that has been sitting by a door for a hundred years and hears, at last, a hand on the latch.

*Someone's looking at me*, it had said. Not in words. It didn't have words. In the way it went warm. *Someone's kneeling down. Someone's come to see.*

*Nobody ever stops*.

---

They built the hall over it in a year.

Glass. All of it. Walls and roof and floor. Ysenne's idea. Ysenne, who loved mirrors, who couldn't sleep without seeing her own face, wanted to see the pool from every side. From above. From below. So the masons dug out the cellars and the glassmakers came from every kingdom of the Nine, and they built a cathedral of ice over a puddle in the dark.

And every evening, in the gold light, the Nine came down and knelt.

All of them. Every brother and sister but her. Oszric first, always. Then Maelis. Then Ysenne and Caddoc and Rhosyn, whose crown was roses then. Then the four who weren't hers by blood but by marriage, kings of the eastern kingdoms, cousins, with crowns of bronze and bone and copper and coral.

Nine.

They knelt round the stone ring. And cupped their hands. And drank.

And she stood in the doorway in her grey dress and listened to the pool get smaller.

---

"*You should drink*," said Maelis.

She'd come and found her. In the garden. On the edge of the dry fountain. In the dusk.

Maelis was twenty-nine. The second-eldest. The most beautiful of all of them, everyone said, and the cleverest, and the kindest. She was the one who'd brushed Hesper's hair when she was small, when the nurses were too busy. The one who'd sat with her when she had the fever at six and told her stories all night about a girl who married a bear.

She sat down on the edge of the fountain beside her. Close. With her silver hair loose and her long hands in her lap. Her eyes were warm brown. Like honey. Like anyone's.

Not lit. Not yet.

"*You should drink*," she said again. Gently. "*Hes. Little sister. Everyone else has.*"

"*I don't want to*."

"*Why not*?"

Hesper didn't answer.

"*It's not wrong*," said Maelis. "*Oszric's asked the scholars. The old texts. There's nothing about it anywhere. No law. No curse. It's just a spring. A very old one. A very good one.*" She put her arm round Hesper's shoulders. "*Have you seen Father? Since he drank? He's walking again. Without the stick. His hands don't shake.*"

"*I've seen him*."

"*Then why*—"

"*Because it's crying*," said Hesper.

---

Her sister went very still.

Hesper had never said it out loud. Not to anyone. Not in seventeen years. She'd said it now without meaning to, sitting on a dry fountain in the dusk, with her sister's arm round her and the pool under the palace getting smaller every evening.

"*Crying*," said Maelis.

"*The pool*." Hesper didn't look at her. "*I can hear it. I always could. Since I was little. It's—it's not a spring, Mae. It's not water. It's a—*" She didn't have the word. Nobody had the word. Not yet. Not for nine hundred years. "*It's a room. A space. It's the place new things come through. And it was glad when you came. And now it's frightened. And it's getting smaller. And you're all getting*—"

She stopped.

"*Getting what*?" said Maelis. Very softly.

Hesper looked at her.

At her face. Beautiful. Kind. Twenty-nine and never going to be thirty. At her hands. Long. Pale. Steady.

At her eyes.

There was something in them. Far back. A flicker. A glint. Like the first spark when you strike flint. Not lit. Not yet. But *beginning*.

"*Hungry*," said Hesper.

---

Maelis laughed.

It was a lovely laugh. Low and warm and fond. She kissed Hesper on the forehead. The way she'd kissed her when she was six, with the fever.

"*Oh, Hes*," she said. "*You always were the strange one.*"

And she got up. And brushed off her gown. And went back inside. To the hall of glass. To the gold light. To kneel.

And Hesper sat on the edge of the dry fountain in the dusk and heard, far down, under her feet, under the palace, the pool cry.

---

The boy came in the autumn.

She found him in the kitchen garden. Among the cabbages. Sitting on an upturned bucket with his feet bare and his knees up and his hands round them, waiting. Small. Brown. Thin as a rake. Seventeen, maybe, the same as her. With a face that looked as if it laughed a great deal, when it wasn't waiting outside a palace being ignored.

"*They won't let me in*," he said. Before she could ask. "*I've been here three days. I've asked every guard. They say the King doesn't receive Listeners.*" He looked up at her. "*I've been eating your cabbages. I'm sorry. They're very good.*"

"*Who are you*?"

"*Malachi*." He stood up. Bowed. Badly. He'd clearly never bowed to anyone in his life. "*Of the House of Vey. The youngest. My brother sent me.*"

"*Your brother*."

"*Aurelian*." He said it with an odd mixture of pride and weariness. The way a boy says the name of an older brother who is very clever and very serious and has never once let him win at anything. "*He'd have come himself. But they threw him out last time. And the time before. So he sent me. He said they'd be less likely to throw out a child.*"

"*You're not a child*."

"*I'm the seventh child*," said Malachi. "*That's close enough, in my family.*"

---

She brought him bread. And cheese. And a cup of milk from the kitchen. And sat with him on the edge of the cabbage bed while he ate, very fast, the way boys eat who've been sleeping in a garden for three days.

"*What's the message*?" she said.

He looked at her over the cup.

"*I'm not supposed to tell anyone but the King*."

"*The King's never going to see you*." She pulled her knees up. "*He's in the hall. Every evening. Drinking. He won't see anyone who isn't bringing him something.*"

Malachi was quiet for a moment.

"*The message is*," he said, "*stop.*"

Hesper looked at him.

"*That's all*," said Malachi. "*Just stop. My brother says it's a Well. Not a spring. One of the seven. The one in the middle. The one that makes room.*" He put the cup down. "*He says if they keep drinking it'll go dry. And a Well that goes dry doesn't just stop. It turns. It becomes the opposite of what it was. A room with nothing in it.*" He looked at his hands. "*And an empty room wants to be filled.*"

"*How does he know*?"

"*He listens*," said Malachi simply. "*We all do. All seven of us. That's what we are. Listeners. We sit by the fires and the rivers and the mountains and we listen until they tell us their names.*" He looked at her. "*He's been listening to it from three hundred miles away for a year. He says he can hear it crying.*"

---

Hesper didn't say anything for a long time.

She sat on the edge of the cabbage bed with her knees up and looked at the boy. At his bare brown feet. At his laughing face that wasn't laughing.

"*So can I*," she said.

Malachi went very still.

"*I always could*," said Hesper. "*Since I was four. I used to sit by it in the cellar. Before the hall. Before any of it.*" She swallowed. "*I thought I was mad. Everyone said I was strange. My nurse said I talked to puddles.*"

The boy looked at her. For a long time. With his eyes, which were brown, and very steady, and didn't laugh at all.

"*You're not mad*," said Malachi of the House of Vey. "*You're a Listener. That's all. Same as me.*"

He held out his hand. Small. Brown. Callused from goats.

"*And you're the only one in this whole palace who can hear it*," he said. "*Which means you're the only one who can help.*"

---

She tried.

She tried for a month. She went to Oszric, in his great gold rooms, and stood in front of him in her grey dress and told him the pool was crying. He laughed at her. Kindly. And gave her a bracelet. And sent her away.

She went to Ysenne, who was looking in a mirror, and didn't look round.

She went to Caddoc, who didn't laugh, who never laughed, who listened to her with his heavy dark face very still and then said, *if it's dying, all the more reason to drink it while we can*.

She went to Rhosyn, who was in the garden with her roses, and who listened. Who actually listened. Who looked at Hesper with her green eyes going wide and frightened, and said *oh*. And said *are you sure*. And said *oh, Hes*.

And went down to the hall that evening and drank anyway.

She went to Maelis last.

---

Maelis was in the hall.

Alone. After the others had gone. Kneeling at the edge of the stone ring in the last of the gold light, with her silver hair pooled round her on the glass floor. Not drinking. Just looking. Down. Into the pool.

It was so low now. Hesper could see the stone sides of it. Grey. Dry. Going down. And at the bottom, a dark that wasn't full anymore. That was thin. Shallow. Like a puddle after rain.

And it was afraid.

She could hear it. So clearly, now. Not words. Never words. A shrinking. A pulling-in. Like an animal backing into a corner. *Please*, it was saying. *Please, please. Not again. Not again. I don't understand. I don't understand what you're doing.*

"*Mae*," said Hesper.

Her sister didn't look round.

"*It's so beautiful*," said Maelis. Softly. Looking down at the shallow dark. "*Isn't it. Even now. I come down here every evening after the others have gone. Just to look at it.*"

"*It's dying*."

"*I know*." Maelis didn't move. "*I know. The Vey boy told me. In the garden. I found him there with you yesterday.*" She was quiet. "*He's very sweet. He said stop. As if it were that easy.*"

"*It is that easy*."

Maelis turned her head.

And her eyes were lit.

---

Not much. Not yet. Not the windows they'd be, nine hundred years later, at the edge of the world. Just a glow. Faint. Gold. Behind the honey-brown. Like a candle behind a curtain.

But lit.

She looked at Hesper with them. With her lovely kind face and her lit eyes. And smiled.

"*Nothing's that easy, Hes*," she said. "*Nothing. You'll learn that when you're older.*" She looked back at the pool. "*Do you know what it feels like? When you drink? It feels like—like someone's left a light on for you. In a window. At the end of a long road. Like you've been walking in the cold your whole life and you didn't know it, and suddenly there's a house, and it's warm, and someone's waiting.*"

Her voice was very soft.

"*I've never felt that before*," said Maelis. "*Not once. Not in twenty-nine years. Not with Father. Not with any of the men they wanted me to marry. Not with any of you.*" She looked down at her own long pale hands. "*I've always been cold, Hes. Always. Inside. Right in the middle. And I never knew why.*"

She cupped her hands.

"*And then I drank*," said Maelis. "*And I wasn't.*"

She bent down. And dipped her cupped hands into the shallow dark. And lifted them. And drank.

And Hesper heard the pool scream.

---

The Vey boy took her out of the palace that night.

Over the garden wall. Through the cabbages. Down the long dark road toward the hills, with a lantern, with nothing but the clothes she stood in and a pair of boots she'd stolen from the kitchen.

"*Where are we going*?" she said.

"*To my brother*," said Malachi. "*He's going to do something. Something big. He won't tell me what. He just said, bring her. If she can hear it, bring her.*" He looked at her sideways. In the lantern-light. "*He said you'll need to be somewhere safe. When it happens.*"

"*When what happens*?"

Malachi didn't answer.

She found out three months later. In the winter. On the night the world was woven.

---

She'd never forget it.

She stood on a hilltop in the cold, with Malachi beside her and four of his brothers and sisters round them in a ring, and his eldest brother in the middle. Aurelian. Tall and serious and grey-eyed, in a plain robe, with a little hammer in his hand. And she watched him strike it.

Once. On nothing. On the air. On the world.

*Tink*.

And the net came down.

Silver. Gold. Fine as cobweb. Out of the sky. Out of the ground. Out of everything. Falling over the whole of the world like a fisherman's cast. Over every hill and every river and every person. Over her. She felt it settle. Light. Cool. Like a veil. Like a hand on her hair.

And she felt it go down. Into the ground. Down and down and down. To a palace of glass three hundred miles away. To a cellar. To a stone ring. To a dark that wasn't dark anymore, that was *empty*, that was a hollow with nothing in it, screaming.

And settle over it.

Like a lid.

---

She heard the scream stop.

Not end. *Stop*. Muffled. Covered. The way a scream stops when you put your hand over someone's mouth.

And beside her, Malachi made a sound.

She looked at him. At the boy with the laughing face. Standing on the hilltop in the cold with tears running down his cheeks, staring at his eldest brother in the middle of the ring with the hammer in his hand.

"*He's gone into it*," whispered Malachi. "*Hes. He's gone* into *it. He's put himself in the middle. He's going to hold it.*"

She didn't understand. Not then.

She understood later.

---

The Ferryman's shore was grey.

All of it. The shingle. The water. The sky. A long grey river running north and south across a long grey valley at the edge of the world. And a long grey boat drawn up on the shingle. And a tall grey figure leaning on a pole, with no eyes.

And nine people, in chains.

Not iron. *Net*. Silver and gold. Fine as cobweb and strong as steel. Round their wrists and their ankles. Woven out of the same threads that had come down over the world.

They stood on the shingle in their silks and their crowns. Oszric with his gold leaves, white-faced, furious. Ysenne with her glass, weeping because there was no reflection in the grey water. Caddoc with his iron, silent, arms crossed. Rhosyn with her roses, which were already dying, the petals falling one by one onto the grey stones.

The four cousins. Bronze and bone and copper and coral. Grey. Shaking.

And Maelis.

Silver wire. Fine as frost. Shaped like folded wings.

With her eyes lit.

---

Hesper came down the shingle.

Alone. Malachi had wanted to come. She'd said no. She'd said *this is mine. They're mine*. He'd stood at the top of the shingle in the grey light with his hands clenched and let her go.

She stopped in front of them.

Her brothers. Her sisters. Her cousins. In chains. At the edge of the world.

Oszric looked at her and didn't speak. He'd never speak to her again. Not in nine hundred years. She'd hear, much later, through the grey, through the Veil, through the dreams the Harrow women had at the end of summer, that he still said her name sometimes. In his sleep. In his cocoon. As a curse.

Caddoc looked at her and nodded. Once. As if she'd won a game he hadn't known they were playing.

Rhosyn was crying. She held out her chained hands. And Hesper took them. And held them. And Rhosyn said, *I'm sorry. I'm sorry. I heard you. I should have listened. I'm sorry*, over and over, until the Ferryman said *it is time*, and she had to let go.

And Maelis—

---

Maelis didn't say anything.

She stood on the grey shingle in her chains and her silver-wire crown and looked at her little sister. With her lit eyes. For a long time.

And Hesper looked back.

She'd brushed her hair. When she was small. When the nurses were too busy. She'd sat with her all night with the fever, telling stories about a girl who married a bear.

She'd always been cold. Right in the middle. And never known why.

"*You could come with us*," said Maelis.

Hesper didn't answer.

"*Wherever we're going*," said her sister. "*You could come. He'd let you. The old man.*" She nodded at the Ferryman. "*You're family. You belong with us.*"

"*I didn't drink*."

"*You could*." Maelis's lit eyes were very soft. "*It's not too late. There's a little left. In me. In all of us. I could give you some. From my own hands. Like I gave my household.*" She held out her chained hands. Cupped. "*Then you'd never be cold. Then you'd never be alone.*"

Hesper looked at the cupped hands.

At the long pale fingers. At the glow in them. Faint. Gold. Leaking, a little, between the fingers, like water through a sieve.

"*No*," she said.

Her sister's face didn't change.

"*Not me*," said Hesper. "*Not ever.*"

---

The Ferryman put them in the boat.

One by one. Oszric first. Then the others. Then Maelis, last, in the bow. Facing back. Toward the shore. Toward the world.

And Hesper stood on the grey shingle and watched.

"*Hes*," said Maelis.

From the boat. As the Ferryman pushed off. As the grey water opened between them. Not loud. Soft. The way she'd said it when Hesper was six, with the fever, in the dark.

"*Leave a light on for me*," said Maelis.

Hesper stood on the shore.

"*Please*," said her sister. "*Somewhere. In a window. So I'll know where you are. So I'll have something to look at, out there, in the grey. So I'll know someone's waiting.*"

The boat was drifting. Out. Into the grey. Into the river. Toward the far shore, where there was nothing. No hills. No sky. Just grey.

"*No*," said Hesper Harrow.

It was the first time she'd ever used the name. She didn't know where it came from. It came up out of her the way the pool's voice used to. *Harrow*. *To break the ground. Before sowing.*

"*No light*," she said. "*Not for you. Not ever. Not in any window I ever live behind.*" Her voice was shaking. She didn't let it stop her. "*If I leave a light on, you'll come for it. I know you will. You'll come for it the way you came for the pool. And you'll kneel down and cup your hands and you'll drink it dry and you'll still be cold.*"

Maelis's lit eyes looked at her across the widening grey.

"*Then I'll find yours*," said the Moth Queen. Very softly. "*Someday. Somehow. Whatever window you hide behind. Whatever light you light.*"

She smiled.

"*I'm very patient, little sister*," said Maelis. "*You'll see.*"

And the grey took her.

---

The Ferryman wouldn't take Hesper.

She asked him. When the boat came back. Empty. When he stepped out onto the shingle and leaned on his long grey pole and turned his eyeless face toward her.

"*Take me across*," she said. "*After them. Please. I'll pay.*"

"*No*," said the Ferryman.

"*I'll pay whatever you want*."

"*It is not the price*." The eyeless face didn't move. "*You did not drink. You poured back. I felt it, in my water. A cupful. Of the room you were born with.*" He was quiet. "*The grey country is for those who drank. It will not hold you. It will push you back. You would come apart against the edge, the way four of them did, coming through.*"

"*Four*—"

"*Four*." The Ferryman's voice was very quiet. "*Bronze and bone and copper and coral. They came apart into dust against the edge of the world. Your cousins. Your sisters' husbands.*" He turned his face toward the grey. "*Five went through.*"

Hesper sat down on the shingle.

She didn't remember sitting down. She just was. On the cold grey stones. With her knees up and her arms round them. Like a girl on the edge of a cabbage bed.

"*What do I do*?" she said.

The Ferryman was quiet for a long time.

"*Live*," he said at last. "*In the world. Under the net. Without it reading you. Without it knowing your name.*" His long grey hand moved on his pole. "*And never, ever leave a light on.*"

---

Malachi found her on the shingle at dusk.

He sat down beside her. On the cold grey stones. Not touching her. Just there. The way you sit beside someone at a grave.

He had something in his hand. He held it out.

A crown.

Small. Plain. Silver. With seven points. Rising from the rim at even spaces, like the tines of a fork.

Hers. The one she'd set down on the stone at the edge of the pool, on the last evening, when she cupped her empty hands and poured.

"*I went back for it*," said Malachi. "*To the hall. Before it fell.*"

"*I don't want it*."

"*I know*." He didn't take it back. "*But someone has to keep it. My brother says. The other nine are gone. Out there. In the grey. But this one never drank. This one's the only crown of the Ten that never touched the pool.*" He looked at it. In his brown hands. "*He says that matters. He says it's the only one that can say no.*"

"*Say no to what*?"

"*He didn't tell me*." Malachi's mouth twisted. "*He never tells me anything. He just goes and does things.*" He put the crown down on the shingle between them. "*Keep it. Hide it. Don't wear it. Pass it on.*"

Hesper looked at the crown for a long time.

Then she picked it up. And held it in her lap. Like a bowl.

---

They sat on the shingle until it was dark.

Neither of them said anything. There was nothing to say. Her brothers and sisters were gone into the grey, and his brother was gone into the net, into the middle of it, holding a starving thing in the dark under a lid. And the world had been woven. And they were seventeen.

When it was fully dark, Malachi reached into his robe and took out something else.

Small. Silver. A bell. The kind you'd hang round a goat's neck. With a clapper inside it that was bent, a little, crooked, so that when it rang it rang very slightly off.

"*It's yours*," he said.

She stared at it.

"*It is*," said Malachi. "*You dropped it. In the garden. The day I came. Off your belt. You didn't notice. I picked it up and I was going to give it back and then*—" He went red. Even in the dark. "*I didn't.*"

She looked at the bell. She remembered it. A little silver bell from her room in the palace. She'd hung it from her belt since she was nine so the nurses would know where she was. She'd dropped it on the stone floor of the kitchen once and bent the clapper.

"*Keep it*," said Hesper.

"*It's yours*."

"*Keep it*." She closed his fingers round it. "*Then I'll know. Wherever I am. If you're sad.*"

He looked at her.

"*How*?" he said.

"*I don't know*," said Hesper Harrow. "*I'm a Listener. You said. Same as you.*" She held his hand closed round the bell. "*I'll listen for it.*"

---

She left the next morning.

East. Into the world. With a crown in a bundle on her back and the boots she'd stolen from the kitchen and no light.

He watched her go. From the top of the shingle. With a little silver bell in his hand.

She didn't look back. Harrow women never did.

But at the bend in the grey valley, where the river turned and the shingle ended and the world began, she stopped. And stood for a moment. With her back to him.

And somewhere behind her, very faint, very far, carried on the wind off the grey water—

*Ting*.

Slightly off. A little crooked. Like a bell with a bent clapper.

It would ring for nine hundred years.

She'd hear it, sometimes. All her life. In the evenings. In the cottages. Faint. Far. At the edge of hearing. And she'd stop whatever she was doing. And listen. And know that somewhere, by some fire, in some valley, a man who'd been a boy with bare feet in her cabbage bed was sad.

She never went back.

She never once left a light on.

And she taught her daughter the rule. And her daughter taught hers. All the way down. Twenty-two cottages. Thirty houses. Twenty generations. A crown in a box. A moth carved in a lid and crossed out.

*We break the ground. We never take the crown. We never let a moth in.*

---

And on the far side of the world, beyond the grey, in a country with no sun, in a hall of glass and bone and gold, five cocoons hung from the rafters.

Asleep.

And under the fifth, on the floor, a crown of silver wire, shaped like folded wings.

And in the cocoon above it, a woman with lit eyes, dreaming.

Of a window. A light. A long road.

And at the end of it, someone waiting.
