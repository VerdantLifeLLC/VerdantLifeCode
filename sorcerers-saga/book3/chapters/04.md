# The Voice Comes Back

The first time, Antho had thought he was going mad again.

He'd been expecting it, in a way. For five years. Ever since the night in Malachi's valley when his Clarity went up past ninety for the first time and stayed there, and he lay on his back by the eternal fire and looked at the stars and thought, very clearly, in his own voice, *this won't last*.

It had lasted.

Through the war, and the Key, and the long night under the lid. Through the Highcrest singing, when the Murmur finally went out of him for good, like a tooth pulled, and his Clarity touched a hundred and didn't move. Through the Heart. Through the doorway at the bottom of the world, where he'd stood with his back to the hunger and shut it out with his own body and the net had named him *Threshold Warden*.

A year and a half of a hundred. Of quiet. Of his own head, with nobody else in it.

He'd started to believe it.

And then, a week before the wedding, at midnight, standing in front of the black door at the bottom of the Hollowmaw with his father filling the lamps, he'd heard a whisper.

In the old place. In the back of his skull, on the left side, where the Murmur used to live. Where it had grated and ground and muttered for eight years like a millstone turning on nothing.

*...lamp...?*

That was all. One word. Soft as a moth's wing.

He'd stood very still in front of the door. With his hands at his sides. With his father beside him, saying *good evening* to the clay lamp the way he did every night. And he'd felt the bottom fall out of the world.

---

"*I thought it was over*," said Antho.

He was sitting on the bench by the great hearth in the long hall of the Ashbourne Forge. In his black coat, with frost melting out of his beard. His brother sat on the anvil block across from him. Wren was awake now, wrapped in her blanket, cross-legged on the other end of the bench, with her green eyes very sharp.

The black pebble sat on the anvil. Between them. Humming.

"*I checked my status*," said Antho. "*Right there. In front of the door. I thought, that's it, then. It's back. The Clarity's gone. A hundred to nothing in a breath. I thought I'd look down and see a number like six.*" He looked at his hands. "*It said a hundred.*"

"*So it wasn't the Murmur*," said Wren.

"*It was the Murmur's voice*." Antho was quiet. "*I'd know it anywhere. I lived with it for eight years. I know every crack in it. Every place it catches.*" He shook his head. "*But it wasn't* in *me. The Murmur came through a crack. A door in me. Malachi said so. Father's broken seal, and the hunger leaking through.*" He touched his chest. "*There's no door in me now. I shut it. At the Highcrest singing. And I gave the last of it away at the Heart.*"

"*Then where's it coming from*?" said Lex.

Antho looked at him.

"*From the other side*," he said. "*From the door. The real one. The black one.*" He turned the Lantern in his hands. "*I'm the Threshold Warden, Lex. The net made me the thing that stands between. I'm tied to that door the way you're tied to a fire. I feel it. Everything that comes near it. Everything that leans on it from the other side.*"

He looked at the pebble on the anvil.

"*Something's been leaning on it*," said Antho Vey. "*For a month. And talking. In the only voice it knows.*"

---

He told them the rest.

He hadn't meant to. He'd meant to tell them a little. Enough. And keep the rest, the way he'd always kept the rest, folded up small inside him where it couldn't frighten anyone.

But Lex was looking at him across the anvil with that face. The one he'd had since he was ten. The one that said *I'm not going anywhere*. And Wren was looking at him with the other face. The one that said *and neither are you*.

So he told them.

How it had come back the next night. And the next. Always at the door. Always at midnight. Always one word, or two. Soft. Frightened. *Lamp*. *Lamp-lady*. *Where*.

How on the fourth night it had said *brother*.

"*Brother*," said Lex.

"*It means me*." Antho's mouth twisted. "*I think. I think it thinks—I don't know what it thinks. It lived in me for eight years. It talked through me. Maybe that's what it thinks a brother is. The one whose mouth you used.*"

How on the sixth night, the night before the wedding, before the message came, it had said a name.

"*Mal*," said Antho. Very quietly. "*Over and over. Like a child calling for someone in the dark.* Mal? Mal? *It doesn't understand where he's gone.*"

The long hall was very quiet. Only the fire. And the pebble on the anvil, humming.

---

"*Why didn't you tell me*?" said Lex.

Antho had known it was coming. He'd known it all the way from the Hollowmaw, on Ashka's back, in the dark, with the wind in his face. He'd been rehearsing an answer.

He didn't use it.

"*Because I was frightened*," he said.

His brother didn't say anything.

"*It was the week of the wedding*," said Antho. "*I was frightened. That it was the old thing back. That I was going mad again, Lex. Properly. That I'd stand up in front of that door with Seraphine and open my mouth and the wrong voice would come out.*" He swallowed. "*I was the mad one for eight years. The warlock in the cave. The one who killed his mother. I didn't want to be that at my own wedding. Not for one day.*"

"*Antho*—"

"*I'm not saying it was right*," said his brother. "*I'm saying that's why.*" He looked up. "*I'm telling you now. I came here tonight because I felt the Delve open. I felt you go into it. I felt it touch you, the voice. And I thought, he's heard it. He knows. And I got on the dragon.*"

Lex was quiet for a long time.

"*Who else knows*?" he said at last.

"*Seraphine*."

Lex looked at him.

"*She asked*," said Antho. "*On the wedding night. After the feast. I was standing at the cave mouth looking at the dark lantern, the Fenmoor one, and she came up behind me and said, how long has it been going on.*" Something moved at the corner of his mouth. "*Not what's wrong. How long. She'd known for days. She said I'd been rubbing my temple like I used to. She'd counted.*"

"*And you told her*."

"*I promised her*," said Antho Vey. "*Three weeks ago. In front of a door. With a ninety-one-year-old Sister of the Lady telling me to hurry up.*" He looked at his brother. "*I'm trying, Lex. I'm trying very hard to be the kind of man who doesn't carry things alone. I'm not very good at it yet. I told my wife first. I'm telling you second.*"

He shrugged. Thin shoulders under the black coat.

"*That's better than eight years*," he said. "*Isn't it?*"

Lex looked at him.

Then he got up off the anvil block. And came round. And sat down on the bench next to Antho. Shoulder to shoulder. The way they'd sat on the woodpile behind the cottage at Harrow, a long time ago, when Antho was fifteen and hearing things and Lex was ten and didn't know what else to do.

He didn't say anything.

He didn't need to.

---

Lex picked up the pebble.

He held it out. In his big scarred palm. Black and smooth and the size of a hen's egg, humming very faintly in the firelight.

"*Hold it*," said Lex.

Antho looked at it.

He didn't want to. That was the truth. He'd been listening to the voice for a month, at the door, through the glass, from the far side of the world. He didn't want to hold a piece of it in his hand.

He held out his hand anyway.

Lex put the pebble in it.

It was warm. Lighter than it looked. Smooth as skin. And the moment it touched his palm, the humming stopped.

And the voice came.

Not through the door. Not from the far side of the world. Right there. In the old place in the back of his skull. Clear and close and small, like a child whispering in his ear.

*...brother?*

Antho's hand closed on it.

*...brother...it's you...it's you...*

*...dark...so dark...where's the lamp...where's Mal...*

*...moths...*

*...moths coming...*

*...frightened...*

He sat on the bench in the Ashbourne Forge with the pebble in his fist and his eyes shut and his brother's shoulder against his, and listened to the voice that had broken him for eight years. The voice that had told him to burn things. That had told him his mother was dead and he'd killed her. That had whispered *little brother smells of warm iron* in a cave in the dark, while he held a knife to Lex's throat.

The voice said: *...please...*

*...please don't be angry...*

*...I didn't know...I didn't know it hurt...*

*...I didn't know what I was...*

---

He opened his eyes.

His face was wet. He hadn't noticed.

Wren was looking at him. Very still. With her blanket round her shoulders and her green eyes enormous.

"*What did it say*?" said Lex. Quietly.

Antho looked down at the pebble in his hand.

"*It said it was sorry*," he said.

He laughed. It came out wrong. Cracked down the middle like the Ashbourne Stone.

"*Nine years*," said Antho Vey. "*Eight in the cave and one before. It told me to kill people. It told me I'd killed our mother. It made me hold a knife to your throat.*" He looked at the pebble. "*And now it's here, in my hand, the size of an egg, saying it's sorry. Saying it didn't know it hurt. Saying* please don't be angry.*"

He closed his hand.

"*I don't know what to do with that*," he said. "*Lex. I don't know what to do.*"

His brother put his arm round his shoulders.

He didn't say *it's all right*. He didn't say *you don't have to forgive it*. He didn't say anything at all.

He just held on.

---

He flew home at dawn.

Lex wanted to come. Antho said no. Then he said yes. Then he said *not yet—let me talk to Father first, and Seraphine. Come in three days. Bring Wren. Bring the twins if you can find them. Bring that Ivy Moss girl. The one who heard it singing.*

He took the pebble with him.

In his coat. In the inside pocket, over his heart. It hummed the whole way. Over the Vale and the foothills and the long white teeth of the Greyspine, with the sun coming up gold on the snow and Ashka's great blue wings beating slow and steady under him. Humming. Flat on the high note.

*[...high up...]* it said once. Wondering. *[...so high...is this what the sky is?...]*

Antho didn't answer.

But he didn't take it out of his pocket either.

---

His father was at the cave mouth when he came in.

Sitting on Pim's rock. In the cold. In a grey coat with a blanket over his knees, with a cup of something steaming in both hands. Watching the sky. Waiting.

He'd been waiting all night. Antho could see it. In the set of his shoulders. In the frost on the blanket.

He stood up as Ashka came down onto the slope. Slowly. One hand on the rock.

And Antho, sliding down off the dragon's neck into the snow, looked up at his father and felt something go cold in his chest.

He'd only been gone a day.

A day and a night. That was all.

His father's hair was white at the temples.

Not grey. Not threaded. *White*. Two broad wings of it, swept back over his ears, like snow on a dark roof. And the grey had spread through the rest. All of it. Pepper and salt. The bold question-mark curl at the front was silver now.

And the lines. At the corners of his eyes. Around his mouth. Deeper. And new ones. Across his forehead. Down his cheeks. The skin under his jaw had gone soft.

He looked fifty-five.

He'd looked forty-eight at the wedding. Three weeks ago.

---

"*Father*," said Antho.

"*I know*." Arion Vey looked at his son's face. And then, with something that was almost a smile, he touched his own temple. Two fingers. Lightly. "*I saw it this morning. In Odile's mirror.*" He was quiet. "*It's going faster.*"

"*How much faster*?"

His father didn't answer.

Antho came up the slope. Through the snow. He stopped in front of his father.

"*No more secrets*," said Antho. "*You said. At the door. The night of the message. You woke us up and said* no more.*"

"*I did say that*."

"*How much faster*?"

Arion Vey looked at his elder son for a long moment. With his lined face and his white temples and his grey eyes, the same grey as Antho's, very steady.

"*I'm not sure yet*," he said. "*That's the truth. I'm keeping a record. Hairs. Lines. The stiffness in my hands.*" He held one up. The knuckles were swollen. Faintly. "*I'll know more in a week or two. When I've got enough numbers.*"

He smiled. Properly, this time.

"*You'd be proud of me*," said Arion. "*I've been counting.*"

---

Seraphine was in the library.

She was always in the library. It had been Antho's library first, the summer after Duskhold. A cave off the main passage, dry and high, where he'd carried the Listeners' Codex and the bronze foils and the Founders' records and everything else the Order had tried to burn, and stacked them on rough shelves and started to read. Now it was three caves. Knocked through. With proper shelves, oak, that Lex had made, and lamps on every one, and a long table down the middle covered in paper.

She was at the table when he came in. With her spectacles pushed up on her forehead and ink on her nose and her dark hair coming down out of the knot she'd put it in at dawn. Surrounded by books. Open. Stacked. Bristling with paper markers.

She looked up.

She didn't say *how was it*. She didn't say *what happened*. She looked at his face, the way she always did, as if it were a page in an old hand that she was learning to read.

"*It talked to him*," she said. "*Didn't it. To Lex.*"

"*Yes*."

"*And he's not angry with you*."

"*No*." Antho came and sat down on the bench beside her. Heavily. "*He's not angry. He sat next to me. And put his arm round me. And didn't say anything.*"

Seraphine looked at him.

"*That's worse*," she said.

"*That's much worse*," Antho agreed.

---

He took the pebble out of his coat and put it on the table.

Between a stack of Founders' records and a cup of cold tea. In the lamplight.

Seraphine looked at it for a long time.

Then she pulled her spectacles down off her forehead. And leaned close. Very close. Her nose almost touching it. The way she looked at a page of Wellscript she couldn't read yet.

"*It's humming*," she said.

"*Yes*."

"*The Lantern Song*." She tilted her head. "*Flat. On the high note.*"

"*Yes*."

She didn't touch it. She looked at it. And Antho watched her look at it, and watched the line come between her eyebrows, the one that came when something in a book didn't add up.

"*Antho*," she said slowly. "*What's it humming with?*"

"*What*?"

"*Sound needs something to move*." She didn't look up. "*Air. Water. A string. A bell. Something has to shake.*" She leaned closer. "*It's a stone. It's not shaking. I'd see it. I can see the grain in it, it's perfectly still.*" Her voice went very quiet. "*So what's making the sound?*"

He looked at the pebble.

He didn't know.

Seraphine sat back. And took off her spectacles. And rubbed her eyes with ink-stained fingers.

"*I can't hear it*," she said. "*Not properly. Not the way you can. I hear the tune. Faintly. The way you'd hear someone singing in another room.*" She put her spectacles back on. "*But I can hear something else.*"

"*What*?"

Seraphine Ilvane, who had never heard a Well in her life, who had sat by Malachi's eternal fire in the Pale Wastes for nineteen days and copied down every word he said and never once heard the fire answer, looked at the black pebble on the table.

"*The gap*," she said. "*Around it. The—space. Where the sound isn't.*" She shook her head. "*I don't know how to say it. It's like reading. When you read, you don't see the letters. You see the words. And the words are made of the spaces between the letters as much as the letters.*" She frowned. "*That's what I can hear. Not the song. The space the song is sitting in.*"

Antho stared at her.

"*It's probably nothing*," said Seraphine. Hastily. Going pink. "*I haven't slept. I've been reading about Wells all night. It's probably—*"

"*It's not nothing*," said Antho.

He didn't know how he knew. He just did. The way you know, sometimes, in the dark, that there's someone else in the room.

---

The moths came that night.

He'd been half-expecting them. Since the wedding. Since the first one drifted down the passage brushing the lanterns with its wings. They'd come back twice since. Single ones. Late. Drifting in out of the dark at the cave mouth, grey-gold, the size of a hand, with painted eyes on their wings. Brushing a lantern. Two. Then gone.

Each time, a lantern went dark.

Not out. *Dark*. The way the Fenmoor one had been. The oil still in it. The wick still trimmed. The light simply lifted out of it, neatly, like a pearl from an oyster. And a fine soft dust on the glass, grey-gold, that wouldn't wipe off. It smeared. It stuck. Odile had scrubbed one pane for an hour with soap and sand and it had still been there, faint, like a fingerprint.

The Fenmoor lantern had taken three days to relight. Antho had sat with it. Every evening. Trying. Striking flint over the wick. Holding a taper to it. The wick wouldn't catch. It would smoke, and sputter, and go out, as if the lamp had forgotten how to burn.

On the third evening, Pim had sat down beside him. And watched. And then, very quietly, without asking, he'd reached out and cupped his small thin hands round the lantern. Not touching it. Just round it. Making a space.

And the wick had caught.

Antho hadn't said anything. Neither had Pim. But he'd thought about it every night since.

---

This time there were more than two.

Odile saw them first. She was on watch at the cave mouth, as she was every third night, wrapped in a blanket on the rock with a lamp beside her and a book of Founders' poems she pretended not to like. She was nineteen now. Tall and dark and solemn, with a scholar's stoop and a Shade-Listener's stillness. The first of Antho's students. The first one who'd climbed the mountain to the mad warlock's cave and knocked.

She came down the passage at a run. Not shouting. Odile never shouted. She came to the door of the library and stood in it, white, with her book still in her hand.

"*Master Antho*," she said. "*The sky.*"

---

He went up.

He stood at the mouth of the Hollowmaw, in the snow, under the stars, with Seraphine beside him and Odile behind, and looked out over the slope and the valley and the long white ridges of the Greyspine going away to the west.

There was a cloud.

Low. Coming over the western ridge. Not a storm-cloud. Not snow. It moved wrong. Too slow and too soft and too low, drifting down the far slope toward the valley like smoke from a chimney when the wind drops.

And it glittered.

Faintly. Grey-gold. In the starlight. Like frost on a window. Like dust in a sunbeam.

"*Moths*," said Seraphine. Very quietly.

Hundreds of them. Thousands. Coming down out of the west. Drifting. Not hurrying. Spreading as they came, like ink in water. Down the far slope. Across the valley. Up the near slope. Toward the cave mouth.

Toward the light.

---

*[...moths...]* said the pebble in his pocket. In the back of his skull. Small and high and terrified. *[...moths moths moths...they're coming...they're HUNGRY...brother brother brother...]*

"*Odile*," said Antho. "*Wake the students. All of them. Every one who can hold a Shade.*" He didn't look round. "*Seraphine. Find Father. Then go down to the door and stay there.*"

"*I'm not going to*—"

"*The lamps*," said Antho. "*Mam's lamp. The three in the niche. If they get to the door*—"

Seraphine went.

---

He opened the Lantern.

The shutter slid back. And the Keeper's Lantern, Lex's lantern, Well-forged, Legendary, with twelve Hush-runes round its glass and the word *SHELTER* across its base, blazed up in his hand.

Not bright. Not like fire. A deep, soft, steady gold that spread out from him across the snow like the light from an open door on a winter night. And with it, the hush. The quiet that lived in the Lantern. The silence it made, the silence that kept voices out.

The first of the moths reached the edge of the light.

And *stopped*.

Hung there. In the air. At the very edge of the gold. Wings beating. A grey-gold moth the size of his hand, with a painted eye on each wing, hanging in the night two yards from the Lantern as if it had hit glass.

Then another. Then ten. Then a hundred. A wall of them. Building up along the edge of the Lantern's light like snow against a door. Beating. Fluttering. Glittering. Wanting in.

And where their wings touched the edge of the gold, the gold *thinned*.

Antho felt it. Through the Lantern. Through his hand. A pull. Soft and steady and terrible, like a child sucking the juice out of an orange. They weren't attacking the light. They were *drinking* it. Every wing that brushed the edge of the Lantern's glow came away brighter. And the glow came away dimmer.

"*Master Antho*!"

The students. Coming up the passage. Nine of them. Odile at the front. The Millbridge twins. A boy from the Saltreach named Cass. The others. All of them with their eyes going dark, the way a Shade-Listener's eyes go when they reach into the dark and ask it for help.

And Pim. At the back. Small. In his blue coat with the brass buttons. With his thin face set.

---

It was a strange fight.

Antho had been in a lot of fights. He'd fought ice-trolls and hollow-knights and Umbral Wardens. He'd fought his own brother in a cave. He'd fought the hunger at the bottom of the world.

He'd never fought anything that didn't fight back.

The moths didn't attack. They didn't bite. They didn't swarm. They just *came*. Endlessly. Drifting in out of the dark. Toward the light. Toward the Lantern, and the lanterns in the passage behind him, and the lamps on the students' belts. Brushing. Drinking. Moving on.

Shade didn't hurt them. The students wrapped them in darkness and they went still in it, folded, the way a moth goes still in a closed hand. And when the darkness opened, they flew out again, unhurt, glittering, and went straight back to the light.

Gale didn't help. Antho called a wind off the snow, a hard cold west wind that would have knocked a man flat, and the moths rode it. Effortlessly. Up and over and down again, like leaves.

And they kept coming.

Behind him, in the passage, one by one, the lanterns began to go dark.

He felt it. Every one. A thousand small lights. Lit for the lost. Going out. Not out. *Dark*. Emptied. Smeared with dust.

*One thousand one hundred and ten*, he counted. Without meaning to. *One thousand one hundred and nine. One thousand one hundred and*—

---

Odile screamed.

She never screamed. Antho turned.

She was on her knees in the snow at the cave mouth. Holding her right hand in her left. Staring at it.

There was dust on it. Grey-gold. All over the back of her hand and her fingers, where a moth had settled. Just for a moment. And flown off again. And left its mark.

"*Odile*—"

"*It's cold*," said Odile Marsh. Her voice was strange. Flat. Faraway. "*Master Antho. It's—it went in. It went* in.*" She looked up at him. And her dark eyes were—

Dim.

Not dark. Odile's eyes went dark when she Listened. Deep and black and full of stars. This was different. This was *dim*. Like a window when someone inside has turned down the lamp. Like the light had gone out of them, and left only the glass.

"*It took something*," said Odile. Quietly. Wonderingly. "*I don't know what. I don't—it's gone. I can't remember what it felt like. It was warm. It was mine.*" She looked down at her dusty hand. "*It doesn't matter. Does it. It doesn't seem to matter. Nothing seems to—*"

She stopped talking.

She sat back on her heels in the snow, very slowly, with her hands in her lap. And looked out at the moths. Drifting. Glittering. And her dim eyes followed them. Followed the light on their wings. The way a cat's eyes follow a string.

And she started to get up.

To go *toward* them.

---

Antho caught her arm.

She didn't fight him. She didn't pull. She just kept leaning. Toward the moths. Toward the glitter. Gently. Like a plant leaning toward a window.

"*Odile*!"

She didn't hear him.

He didn't know what to do. He'd never seen anything like it. He held her arm and felt the cold in her, the empty quiet cold, like the Unfinished in Lex's Delve, and the moths were thick now all round the cave mouth, a blizzard of grey-gold, and the Lantern was flickering in his other hand, thinning, and behind him in the passage the lanterns were going dark and dark and dark—

And Pim walked past him.

Out of the light. Into the moths. Small. Thin. In his blue coat.

And stopped. And stood there.

And was *silent*.

---

Antho had never been able to explain Pim's silence.

He'd tried. To Seraphine. To Lex. To himself, late at night, in the cold-room, with the bats. It wasn't just not talking. Lots of people didn't talk. It wasn't Shade, though Pim Listened to Shade, and was good at it, better than anyone Antho had ever taught. It was something else. Underneath. Something Pim had carried up the mountain with him at nine years old, from Kessick and the man who'd tried to beat the voices out of him. A place in him where nothing was. Where nothing had been allowed to be. Where he'd gone, for years, to hide.

It was the silence that had hidden Antho's thread from the Key, on the night of the Blade. It was the silence that had made the Cantor go out at the Highcrest singing, like a candle in a draft.

Pim stood in the middle of the moths. And let it out.

---

Nothing happened. That was what it looked like. A small boy standing in the snow.

But the moths *moved*.

All of them. All at once. A great soft lurch through the whole glittering cloud, the way a flock of starlings turns in the sky. Away. Away from Pim. Out. A space opened round him. A ring. Ten feet across. Empty. Moths on every side of it, beating, glittering, and none of them coming in.

As if he were a hole in the light. A place with nothing to drink.

Pim walked forward.

Slowly. Step by step. Through the snow. And the empty ring went with him. And the moths drew back in front of him like water in front of a ship's bow.

He walked to Odile. Who was still leaning toward the glitter, in Antho's grip, with her dim eyes. And he stood in front of her. And the ring of nothing closed round her too.

And Pim reached out. And cupped his small thin hands round her dusty one. Not touching it. Just round it.

Making a space.

Odile blinked.

---

The moths went at dawn.

Not driven off. Not beaten. They just went. When the sky in the east went grey over the Greyspine, and the first thin light came up over the snow, the whole cloud of them lifted off the slope at once, like smoke when a door opens, and drifted away west. Over the ridge. Glittering. Gone.

Antho stood in the cave mouth with the Lantern in his hand, flickering, and watched them go.

Behind him, in the passage, he counted the dark lanterns.

Two hundred and six.

---

Odile was by the fire in the kitchen, wrapped in three blankets, with a cup of tea she wasn't drinking. Pim sat beside her. Not touching her. Just there.

Her eyes were better. Not right. But better. There was light in them again. Faint. Like a lamp turned very low.

"*It's coming back*," she told Antho. When he crouched down in front of her. "*Slowly. It's like—it's like when your hand goes to sleep. And the feeling comes back. And it hurts.*" She looked at the tea. "*I remember what it was now. What it took.*"

"*What*?"

Odile was quiet for a long time.

"*The first time I came up the mountain*," she said. "*When I was fifteen. And I knocked on the door of the cave. And you opened it.*" She looked at him with her half-lit eyes. "*And you looked so frightened. More frightened than me. And you said, oh. Are you here to learn? And I said yes. And you said*—" She stopped. "*You said, thank goodness. I've no idea what I'm doing.*"

Antho didn't say anything.

"*That*," said Odile Marsh. "*That's what it took. How that felt. When you said that.*" She swallowed. "*It's the warmest thing I have. And it took it. And for a while I didn't care.*"

She looked at Pim. Sitting beside her. Small and solemn.

"*He gave it back*," she said. "*I don't know how. He just—made room for it. To come back.*"

Pim looked at his knees.

Antho put his hand on the boy's thin shoulder. And held it there.

---

He went down to the door at midnight.

Alone. He didn't wake his father. Arion had sat up all night by the passage with a tinderbox, relighting what he could, which was nothing. He was asleep now. In the chair in the library. With his white head on his chest.

Antho went down the long passage past two hundred and six dark lanterns. Past nine hundred and seven lit ones. Into the bell-shaped cavern. To the black door.

The three lamps were burning in the niche.

All three. The clay one and the iron one and the *ARDENT* one. Straight up. Small and gold and steady. Untouched.

Seraphine had stood in front of them all night. With her back to the niche and her arms spread and a lamp of her own in each hand, held out, so that if anything came down the passage it would go to hers first. She hadn't needed to. Nothing had come this far. But she'd stood there. All night. In her nightgown and her spectacles.

He loved her so much it frightened him.

---

He put his hand on the black glass.

Cold. Smooth. Sealed. On the other side, the deep. The soft endless dark under the world. Shade's dark, again. Not the hunger. Never the hunger again.

He listened.

He was the Threshold Warden. He was the thing that stood between. He could feel everything that leaned on this door from the far side, the way you feel someone sit down on the other end of a bench.

Something was leaning on it now.

Enormous. Soft. Far down. Pressed close against the glass from the other side, the way a frightened child presses against a door, with its whole body. Trembling.

*[...brother...]*

The pebble in his coat. And the thing behind the door. The same voice. The same small frightened whisper. One close and one far. Like an echo.

*[...brother...moths...the moths came...]*

*I know*, thought Antho. *I know they did.*

*[...were they...did they hurt...]*

*They took some light*, thought Antho. *Two hundred and six lanterns. And something of Odile's. It's all right. We're all right.*

*[...they're HUNGRY...]* said the voice. And something came with it, through the door, through his palm, through the old place in the back of his skull. Not words. A feeling. A memory. Old. Very old. A great black pool in a hall of glass. And kneeling round it, cupping their hands, drinking, drinking, drinking, faces. Beautiful faces. Lit eyes.

*[...they drank me...]* said the voice. *[...before...a long time before...they drank and drank and drank until I was...]*

It stopped.

*[...until I was the other thing...]*

---

Antho stood very still.

He didn't understand it. Not yet. But something in him went cold and sharp and clear, the way it did when he was reading an old text and a word he'd been staring at for hours suddenly turned over and showed him its other side.

*They drank me*.

*Until I was the other thing*.

He opened his mouth.

And a different voice came through the door.

---

Not the small one. Not the frightened one.

A woman's voice. Low. Warm. A little rough, the way a voice is when it hasn't been used for a while, or has been used too much, singing in the dark to someone who couldn't sleep.

Close.

Closer than she'd ever been. Closer than the night of the Blade, when she spoke to Elric through the door. Closer than the Heart. As if she were standing on the other side of the glass, right there, with her hand flat against it, where his was.

"*Antho*," said his mother. "*Love.*"

He couldn't breathe.

"*Don't be afraid of it*," said Seren Vey. Gently. Through the door. Through the dark. Through the long long road home. "*It's only little. It's only very young.*"

The glass under his hand was warm.

"*It's frightened of the moths*," said his mother. "*It's been frightened of them for nine hundred years.*"

A pause. And then, very quietly, in a voice that had gone hard and cold and certain in a way he had never once heard it, not in all the memories of his childhood, not in the Threshold or the Heart—

"*And so should you be, my darling*," said Seren Harrow. "*Don't let them in. Whatever they say. Whoever comes. Don't ever let a moth in.*"
