# What the Harrow Women Knew

Wren told him at midnight, on the roof of the Spire.

He hadn't wanted to go up. It was cold, and it was dark, and it was eight hundred feet, and he hadn't stopped shaking since the Hall of Lanterns. Not on the outside. On the inside. Somewhere under his ribs. Like a bell that had been struck and wouldn't stop humming.

*Harrow was my sister's*.

He'd washed his shoulder three times. With soap. In the basin in his father's rooms in the Spire. The dust wouldn't come off. It smeared. It stuck. It smelled of plums.

Wren had watched him do it from the doorway. Then she'd taken the cloth out of his hand. And taken his hand. And said, *come up on the roof*.

So he went up on the roof.

---

She sat on the parapet. The way she always did. With her back to the drop and her boots dangling over the roof and the whole of Highcrest spread out behind her, dark.

That was the strangest thing. The dark. He'd never seen Highcrest dark. Not since the Heart. Every night for a year and a half the golden city had been lit from end to end. Every window. Every eave. A lantern for the lost in every house. Even the bakery in the Lower Town with its great loaf-shaped lantern in the window.

Tonight there was nothing. A city of black roofs under black sky. A few candles, here and there, behind thick curtains. The watch-fires on the walls. The stars.

Lysandra's order. *All lanterns in the city to be shuttered or extinguished from dusk*.

And somewhere down there, in a tall grey house on the Crown Terrace with Keepers on the doors and no lights within a hundred yards, a woman with lit eyes, sitting in the dark. Waiting.

"*Sit down*," said Wren. "*You're looming.*"

He sat down. On the cold stone of the roof. At her feet. With his back against the parapet.

She didn't say anything for a while.

Then she reached down and took his hand. And pulled it up. And put it flat on her stomach. Under her coat. Through her shirt. Warm.

And held it there.

---

He didn't understand.

For a moment. A long moment. He sat on the roof of the Spire with his hand on his wife's stomach and didn't understand. He thought, *she's cold*. He thought, *she's sick, she's been sick, the bad fish*. He thought—

He looked up at her.

She was looking down at him. In the starlight. With her fox-red braid over one shoulder and her green eyes very bright. And her mouth doing a thing he'd never seen it do before. Wobbling.

"*Wren*," he said.

"*I counted*," said Wren. "*At Greyfall. On the barracks steps. After the fight.*" Her voice was very steady. Her mouth wasn't. "*Elowen said it wasn't bad fish. She grew up with four aunts. She said she knows what bad fish looks like.*" She swallowed. "*So I counted.*"

He couldn't speak.

"*It's early*," said Wren. "*Very early. Six weeks. Maybe seven. I think it was—*" She went red. Very red. Even in the starlight. "*I think it was the night after the wedding. In the tent. When the cow got in.*"

Lex made a sound.

He didn't know what kind of sound it was. It came up out of him from somewhere under his ribs, where the bell had been humming since the Hall of Lanterns, and it wasn't a laugh and it wasn't a sob and it wasn't a word. It was all three. At once.

Wren slid down off the parapet. Onto the roof. Into his lap. And put her arms round his neck. And held on.

"*Hammer*," she said into his shoulder. The clean one. "*Say something. Please. You're frightening me.*"

He held her.

He held her so tight she squeaked. And then he loosened his arms, terrified, as if she'd break. And then held her again. And put his face in her hair. And breathed.

"*A baby*," said Lex Vey.

"*Yes*."

"*Ours*."

"*Well, I certainly hope so*," said Wren. "*I've been very careful about that.*"

He laughed.

It cracked in the middle. He didn't care.

---

They stayed up there a long time.

He kept his hand on her stomach. He couldn't stop. He didn't feel anything; there was nothing to feel, she said, not yet, not for months. It was the size of a bean. A pea. A grain of barley. He didn't care. He kept his hand there anyway. As if he could hold it. As if he could keep it warm.

"*I heard it*," said Wren. Very quietly. After a long time. "*At Greyfall. On the tower.*"

"*Heard what*?"

"*Its heart*." She was quiet. "*I think. I don't know. It was very faint. Like a bird's heart. Like a drum a long way off.*" She looked at her hand on his. "*Seraphine says that's impossible. Too early. Nobody can hear a heart at six weeks.*"

"*You're a Mistwalker*."

"*I'm a Mistwalker*," Wren agreed. "*I hear things in the wind. I heard the whole Vale when your Delve opened. Three miles off.*" She smiled. A small strange smile. "*Maybe I can hear things in me, too. Maybe that's how it works now. Maybe the wind knows.*"

He didn't say anything.

He was thinking about the tower at Greyfall. And a tall grey-gold woman at the bottom of the rise in the dusk, looking up. With her lit eyes on Wren's hand.

On what was under it.

*You're carrying a light*.

---

Wren felt him go cold. She always did.

"*I know*," she said. Very low. "*I know. I thought it too.*"

"*She knew*."

"*Before I did*." Wren's arms tightened round his neck. "*She looked at my hand and she knew. Before I'd finished counting. Before I'd heard the heart.*" Her voice went very hard. "*And she looked at it like*—"

She stopped.

"*Like what*?"

"*Like you look at the fire*," said Wren. "*In the morning. When you say good morning to it.*" She shivered. "*Like it was the most beautiful thing she'd ever seen. And she wanted to put her hands round it.*"

Lex held her.

He looked out over the dark city. At the black roofs. At the Crown Terrace, high on the north side, where a tall grey house stood with Keepers on its doors and no light anywhere near it.

*If one more lantern in your kingdom goes dark*, his father had said. *Put her back on the road.*

He wanted to go down there. Now. Tonight. With fire in his hands. And kick in her door. And walk her to the edge of the world himself. And watch her go.

He didn't.

He sat on the roof of the Spire with his wife in his lap and his hand on her stomach and the fire very low in him, banked, the way you bank a forge at night so it'll still be there in the morning.

And made a promise. Silently. To a grain of barley.

*Nobody puts their hands round you*, thought Lex Vey. *Nobody. Not ever.*

---

They flew to Harrow in the morning.

Antho came. Lex hadn't asked him. He'd just been there, at dawn, in the courtyard of the Spire, with Ashka saddled and his face white and set and the Lantern on his hip. He'd looked at Lex. Then at Wren. Then at Wren again, harder. And his clear grey eyes had widened. Very slightly.

"*Oh*," said Antho Vey.

"*How*?" said Wren. "*How do you* do *that? Both of you? It's the size of a bean!*"

"*You're standing differently*," said Antho. And then he did something Lex had never seen him do. He came across the courtyard. And put his arms round Wren. Carefully. Very carefully. The way you'd hold a bird. And held her for a long moment. And let go.

"*I'm going to be an uncle*," he said. To no one. Wonderingly. "*I'm going to be an* uncle.*"*

"*You're going to be a terrible uncle*," said Wren. Her eyes were wet. "*You're going to teach it to talk to bats.*"

"*Yes*," said Antho. "*Yes, I am.*"

---

They went to Harrow because there was nowhere else to go.

*Harrow was my sister's*. That was all Maelis had said. She'd walked out of the Hall of Lanterns and up to her tall grey house on the Crown Terrace and shut the door, and when Seraphine went to see her in the evening, with Fenn and two Keepers and a notebook, she'd said she was very tired. That it had been a very long walk. That she'd be glad to talk tomorrow. Or the day after. There was plenty of time. She'd been waiting nine hundred years; she could wait a few days more.

*She's making us come to her*, Seraphine said. Furious. Over a cold supper in Arion's rooms. *She dropped it on you in front of the whole council and walked out. She wants you to wonder. She wants you to come and ask.*

*Then we won't ask*, said Antho. *Not her.*

*Who, then*?

His brother had looked at Lex across the table. And Lex had known what he was going to say before he said it.

*Mam*, said Antho. *We ask Mam.*

*She's in the Hollow*.

*Not all of her*. Antho's face was very still. *Some of her's at Harrow. Some of her's in the Vale. Some of her's in every person who ever knew her.* He looked at his hands. *Someone knew her before Father did. Someone knew her mother. Someone in that Vale knows what a Harrow is.*

---

Lex had built the new cottage on the old footings.

He'd told himself it was because the footings were good. Deep. Well-laid. Dressed stone, set without mortar, the way the old builders did it in the Vale. Better than anything he could have dug himself in an autumn.

It was true. It wasn't why.

The fire had taken everything above the ground. The walls and the roof and the loft and the furniture and the iron pots, which had melted. Thirteen years ago. On the night of Antho's Kindling. When the Stone screamed and flashed black and the seal on his brother broke and the dark came in.

But it hadn't taken what was under the ground.

There was a cellar. Lex remembered it. Barely. A small square stone room under the kitchen floor, cool and damp, where his mother kept apples and potatoes and the crock of butter in summer. A trapdoor with an iron ring. A ladder. He'd been afraid of it when he was little. Antho used to shut him in it. For about ten seconds. And then open the trapdoor again, laughing, before he could cry.

When they cleared the black stones in the autumn, the trapdoor was gone. Burned. The ladder was gone. But the cellar was there. Under the ash. Under the fallen roof. Full of rubble and charcoal and the melted lumps of the iron pots, which had fallen through when the floor burned.

They'd cleared it out. Carted the rubble away. Swept it. Lex had put in a new trapdoor and a new ladder. Wren had put apples in it.

He'd never looked at the walls.

---

"*It's here*," said Antho.

He was standing at the bottom of the new ladder. In the cellar. With the Lantern in his hand, shutter open, its soft gold light filling the little stone room. It was smaller than Lex remembered. Everything was. Eight feet square. Low. Lex had to stoop. The walls were dressed grey stone, the same as the footings, fitted tight. The floor was packed earth. There was a smell of apples and damp and, very faintly, under everything, thirteen years later, smoke.

"*What's here*?" said Lex.

His brother didn't answer. He was standing in the middle of the cellar with his eyes half shut. Listening. The way he listened to the black door.

"*Something*," said Antho. "*Something quiet. Something that's been quiet a very long time.*" He turned. Slowly. In a circle. With the Lantern held up. "*It's hiding. Like Pim hides. Like a thread in Shade.*"

He stopped.

Facing the north wall. The one under the old hearth. Where the great chimney of the cottage had stood, before it fell.

"*There*," said Antho Vey.

---

It was a stone.

An ordinary one. Grey. Dressed. Fitted into the wall like all the others. A foot and a half wide and a foot high. Lex had looked at it a dozen times in the autumn, sweeping. He'd never seen anything.

He looked at it now. And still saw nothing.

"*Put your hand on it*," said Antho.

Lex put his hand on it.

Stone. Cold. Damp. Ordinary.

And then, under his palm, through the Well he Commanded, the deep slow certain sense of stone that had been in him since he was nineteen—

*Hollow*.

The stone was hollow.

Not hollow like a jug. Hollow like a held breath. Like a pocket. Like a space inside it that wasn't empty, exactly, but *waiting*. Folded. Hidden. Wrapped in something so quiet he'd never have felt it if his brother hadn't stood in the middle of the cellar with his eyes shut and pointed.

"*It's warded*," said Lex slowly. "*Isn't it.*"

"*Hushed*." Antho came and stood beside him. "*Like the Lantern. Like Pim. Something made it quiet. So no one would look.*" He put his own hand on the stone. Beside Lex's. "*Someone who knew how to hide things from Wardens. From Root. From True Sight.*" His voice was very quiet. "*Father lived in this house for eight years. With all his Authority. And he never knew it was here.*"

---

Lex asked the stone to move.

That was all it took. He didn't break it. He didn't burn it. He asked. The way the Hearth had taught him, in the Wellworld of Flame. The way the Heartiron had taught him, in the Wellworld of Stone. *You're not making it do anything, lad. You're asking it nicely to be something else for a while.*

The stone slid out of the wall. Smoothly. Into his hands. As if it had been waiting thirteen years for someone to ask.

Behind it, in a hollow in the thickness of the wall, there was a box.

---

It was small.

Smaller than Malachi's. Oak, gone almost black with age. Bound with iron at the corners. No lock. A plain iron hasp with a twist of old blue cloth through it instead of a pin.

Blue cloth.

Lex knew that cloth. He'd seen it a thousand times, when he was small. In his mother's hair. *She was small and fierce and freckled, her hair tied back with a strip of blue cloth*.

He couldn't move.

Antho reached past him. And lifted the box out of the wall. Carefully. With both hands. And held it for a moment. Against his chest.

Then he gave it to Lex.

"*You open it*," said his brother. "*I can't.*"

---

Wren was sitting at the top of the ladder. With her legs dangling down into the cellar. She'd been told to stay up there. She'd ignored it as far as the top rung, which was, she said, a compromise.

She watched him untie the blue cloth.

It was rotten. It came apart in his fingers. He held the pieces for a moment. Then put them very carefully in his coat pocket.

He opened the lid.

---

Inside, there were three things.

A book. Small. Thick. Bound in soft brown leather gone dark and supple with handling. The kind of book a hedge-witch keeps. Recipes. Remedies. Charms.

A letter. Folded. Sealed with a blob of plain brown wax with a thumbprint in it.

And the box itself.

That was the third thing. Lex didn't see it at first. He saw the book and the letter and thought that was all. And then Antho, crouching beside him with the Lantern, made a small sound. And reached out. And touched the inside of the lid.

There was a carving there.

Small. Neat. Cut into the dark oak with the point of a knife, by someone who'd been patient, and careful, and very sure of what they were doing.

A moth.

Wings spread. Four of them. The two upper ones with a ring on each, like an eye. The body long and narrow. Every line very fine. Very exact. As if whoever carved it had seen one very close, for a long time, and never forgotten.

And through the moth, from corner to corner, deep and hard and angry, one long straight cut.

Crossing it out.

---

And in the bottom of the box, under the book and the letter, a shape.

Not a thing. A *shape*. A hollow. Pressed into the wood of the box's floor, the way a ring presses a mark into the velvet of its case if you leave it there long enough. A circle. A little bigger than a man's hand could span. With points on it. Seven of them. Small and sharp, rising from the rim at even spaces, like the teeth of a comb. Or the tines of a fork.

Or the points of a crown.

Lex stared at it.

Whatever had made that shape had lain in this box a very, very long time. Long enough to press itself into the oak. Long enough to leave its mark.

And it wasn't there anymore.

---

The letter was from Seren's mother.

Lex knew it before he opened it. He didn't know how. The thumbprint in the wax was small, and old, and not his mother's. He knew his mother's. He'd seen it a thousand times, in flour, in butter, in the soft clay of a little brown lamp.

He broke the seal.

The paper was thin and brown and spotted. The ink was faded. The hand was small and slanting and quick. A hand that wrote the way a person walks when they're used to leaving places in a hurry.

*Seren*, it said.

*My bright girl. My last one.*

*If you're reading this, I'm gone, and you've stayed. You've stayed for the Warden-man with the grey eyes. Don't look like that. I know. I've known since the first night you came home from Highcrest with rain in your hair and your face lit up like a Lantern-Night window.*

*I'm not angry. I want you to know that. I was, at first. I'm not now.*

*Every Harrow woman for nine hundred years has kept moving. Every one. Thirty houses in thirty years, my mother had. Twenty-two, I had. We don't stay. We don't Kindle. We don't put our hands on their Stones and let their net read us. We sing to the dark and we keep the door and we never, ever stay long enough for anyone to look at us too closely.*

*And you've stayed*.

*Maybe that's right. Maybe it's time. Maybe nine hundred years is long enough to run. The knot is old and tight and nobody has felt a moth this side of the grey since your great-great-grandmother's time. Maybe they've given up. Maybe they've gone to sleep.*

*But if they haven't*—

The ink was blotted there. As if the pen had stopped. And rested on the page. And waited.

*If they haven't, my girl, you remember. You remember everything I taught you. You remember the song, all of it, all the verses, not just the one for babies. You remember the rule.*

*We break the ground*. *We never take the crown*. *We never let a moth in*.

*I've taken the crown where it belongs. Where it was always meant to go. It's not in the box anymore. Don't look for it. Don't tell anyone it was ever there. Not even your Warden-man. Not even if you love him.*

*Especially not if you love him*.

*If they come, they'll come for the blood. They'll come for you, and for anything of yours. They'll be kind. They'll be so kind. They'll have the loveliest eyes you ever saw, and they'll tell you the truest story you ever heard, and every word of it will be true, and it will all be a lie.*

*Don't let them in*.

*I love you. I've loved you every day of your life. I'm sorry I made you move so much.*

*Light the lamp for me*.

*— Your mam*

*Wenna Harrow*

---

Nobody said anything for a long time.

The Lantern hissed softly in Antho's hand. The apples sat in their barrel in the corner. Upstairs, through the open trapdoor, Lex could hear the fire in the kitchen hearth, ticking, and the stream outside, loud with meltwater, and a blackbird in the birch wood.

He read the letter again.

*We break the ground. We never take the crown. We never let a moth in.*

*They'll come for the blood*.

He put his hand on the hollow in the bottom of the box. The circle. The seven points.

*I've taken the crown where it belongs*.

"*Lex*," said Wren. From the top of the ladder. Very quietly.

He looked up.

She was sitting very still. With her hand flat on her stomach. Under her coat.

*They'll come for you, and for anything of yours*.

---

They went to see Old Mag in the afternoon.

She was in her chair by the fire, with her shawl and her cat and her one bright black eye. The apple tree outside her window was still in bloom. More than before. Every branch. White and pink and heavy with it, so that the boughs bent down to brush the grass.

She looked at the three of them coming in at her door. Lex and Antho and Wren. With the box.

She didn't say anything for a long moment.

Then she looked at Wren.

At Wren's face. At the way she was standing. At her hand.

And her old wrinkled face cracked open into a smile so wide and so sudden that the cat jumped off her lap.

"*Well*," said Old Mag. "*Well, well, well. Look at you, girl.*"

Wren went red.

"*You're carrying a light*," said Old Mag.

---

Wren went white.

All of it. All at once. The red drained out of her face as if someone had pulled a plug. She stood in Old Mag's doorway with her hand on her stomach and stared at the old woman. And Lex saw her knees go.

He caught her.

"*Lass*?" Old Mag was struggling up out of her chair. "*Lass, what is it? What did I say?*"

"*That*," said Wren. Hoarsely. In Lex's arms. "*That. What you said. Carrying a light.*" She swallowed. "*Where did you—who says that?*"

Old Mag stared at her.

"*Everyone*," she said. "*In the Vale. When a girl's expecting.*" She looked at Lex. Then back at Wren. "*It's just a saying, lass. An old one. Carrying a light. Like a lamp in a window. Because there's someone coming home.*" She frowned. "*My gran said it. And hers. Everyone says it.*"

"*Since when*?" said Antho. Very quietly.

Old Mag looked at him.

"*Since always*," she said. "*Since*—" She stopped. And her one bright eye narrowed. And went far away. The way old people's eyes go when they reach back past a great many years for something they've half forgotten.

"*Since the Harrow women*," said Old Mag slowly. "*That's right. That's where it's from. My gran told me. The Harrow women brought it. Back when they first came into the Vale. Before my gran's gran was born.*"

---

She told them.

Sitting in her chair by the fire, with the cat back in her lap and the box on the table beside her and Wren on the settle with a cup of something hot and herbal and foul that Old Mag swore by. Lex and Antho on stools at her feet. Like children.

It came out slowly. In pieces. The way old stories do. Some of it was her gran's. Some of it was her gran's gran's. Some of it was hers.

*There were always Harrow women*, said Old Mag. *That's what my gran said. Always. Every few generations. A mother and a daughter. Never a son. Never a husband, or not one anybody ever saw. They'd come into the Vale from somewhere else. North, or east, or over the Greyspine. With a cart, or on foot, with a bundle on their backs. And they'd stay a while. A year. Two. Five. In a cottage somewhere out of the way.*

*They were hedge-witches. Herb-women. Good ones. Better than me. Better than anyone. They knew things nobody else knew. They could stop a fever. Turn a breech baby. Talk a frightened horse down.*

*And they never Kindled.*

*Never. Not one. Not the mother and not the daughter. When the girl turned sixteen, they'd be gone. Off. Moved on. Before the Warden came round. Before anyone could put her hand on a Stone.*

*People talked. Course they did. Said they were wildcasters. Said they were wanted. Said all sorts.*

*But nobody ever told on them. Not once. Because every one of them, every Harrow woman, had saved someone's baby. Or someone's mam. Or someone's cow. And you don't tell on the woman who saved your cow.*

---

"*And the moths*?" said Antho.

Old Mag looked at him.

"*How'd you know about the moths*?" she said.

"*Tell me*."

The old woman was quiet for a long moment. Stroking the cat.

*My gran said*, said Old Mag slowly, *there were three things about the Harrow women. Three things everyone knew. Three things you didn't ask about.*

*One. They never Kindled.*

*Two. When their daughters were expecting, they didn't say expecting. They said carrying a light. And everyone thought it was pretty and started saying it too.*

*Three*.

She stopped.

*Three*, said Old Mag. *They hated moths.*

*Hated them. More than anything. More than Wardens. They'd sit up all night, my gran said, when it was moth season, at the end of summer, with the shutters closed and a lamp inside and a broom. And if a moth got in, if one single moth got into a Harrow house, they'd kill it. And burn it. And sweep the ashes out the door and throw salt after them.*

*And then they'd pack.*

*That same night. The cart. The bundle. And be gone by morning.*

*My gran's gran saw it once. A Harrow woman, with a little girl, the year the summer was so hot. A moth got into her cottage. Just one. And my gran's gran was there, helping with a calving, and she saw the Harrow woman's face. When she saw it on the wall.*

*She said it was the face of a woman who'd seen a wolf at her child's cradle.*

Old Mag looked down at the cat.

*And they were gone by morning*, she said. *And nobody saw a Harrow woman in the Vale for sixty years. Not till your mam's mam came. Wenna. Wenna Harrow. With her little girl.*

She looked up. At Lex. At Antho.

*Your mam*, said Old Mag. *Seren. She was nine.*

---

They walked back to the cottage at dusk.

Through the birch wood. Along the stream. Antho in front, with the Lantern. Wren in the middle. Lex behind, with the box under his arm.

Nobody talked.

The light was going. Grey and gold and then grey. The birches were white in it, pale as bone, with their new leaves just showing, a mist of green. The stream ran loud and cold beside the path.

And in the window of the cottage, ahead of them through the trees, a lantern.

Lex's. Iron and glass. *ARDENT* on the pane. *HOME* on the base. Burning. The way it burned every night. Small and gold and steady. Facing the road.

He stopped on the path and looked at it.

*Never leave a light on*, the cadets whispered in the Spire. *Or the Nine will come for it*.

*We never let a moth in*.

He'd made that lantern so it would never go out. So there'd always be a light in the window at Harrow. For his mother. For whoever was coming home.

He stood on the path and looked at it and felt, for the first time in his life, afraid of a lamp.

---

Wren slept in the loft.

Antho slept in the kitchen, by the fire, rolled in his coat, with the Lantern beside him. He'd said he wasn't going back to the Keep tonight. He'd said Seraphine could manage the bats for one night, and Arion was in Highcrest, and Odile had the door, and Pim had the moths. He'd said he wanted to sleep in his mother's house.

Lex didn't sleep.

He sat in the deep window on the east side of the cottage. On the broad sill. With his back against one side of the window frame and his boots against the other, and the lantern between his feet. And the box on his knees. And the letter in his hand.

He'd read it eleven times.

*They'll be kind. They'll be so kind. They'll have the loveliest eyes you ever saw, and they'll tell you the truest story you ever heard, and every word of it will be true, and it will all be a lie.*

Outside the window, the birch wood was dark. The stream was loud. The stars were out.

And something was on the glass.

---

He didn't see it land.

He looked up from the letter, and it was there. On the outside of the window. On the pane. Right in front of the lantern.

A moth.

Large. Larger than the ones at the Keep. The size of his spread hand. Grey-gold. Its wings open, flat against the glass. Four of them. Patterned. And on each of the upper two, a ring. Like an eye. Dark. Gold-rimmed. Looking in at him.

It didn't move.

It sat on the outside of the glass with its wings spread and its eyes on him and the lantern-light shining through it, so that he could see the veins in its wings, fine as the lines on a leaf. And dust. Grey-gold. Glittering on the glass where its wings touched.

Lex didn't move either.

He sat in the window with the letter in his hand and looked at it.

---

"*Good evening*," said the moth.

Not the moth. The voice came *through* the moth. The way a voice comes through a door. Low. Soft. A little hoarse. Kind.

Hers.

"*What a pretty lamp*," said Maelis. "*Did you make it? I think you did. It has your hands all over it.*"

Lex said nothing.

"*Ardent*," said the voice. Reading the rune on the pane. From the outside. Backward. "*Home.*" A soft sound. Almost a laugh. "*Oh, that's lovely. That's the loveliest thing. A lamp that will never go out. In a window. For someone who isn't home yet.*"

The moth's wings stirred. Very slightly. On the glass.

"*It's cold out here*," said Maelis. "*It's been cold for nine hundred years.*"

A pause.

"*May I come in*?"

---

Lex looked at the moth for a long time.

At its wings. At its eyes, that weren't eyes. At the dust it was leaving on his window. On the glass he'd cut himself, in the autumn, in the Forge, for this window, so his mother would have a light when she came home.

He thought about a crown-shaped hollow in the bottom of a box.

He thought about a grain of barley.

He thought about his mother's mother, sitting up all night with the shutters closed and a lamp inside and a broom.

"*No*," said Lex.

---

The moth was very still.

Then, through it, soft and low and delighted, the Moth Queen laughed.

"*Good*," said Maelis. "*Oh, good. That's the right answer. That's exactly the right answer.*"

Her voice was warm. Fond. Almost proud.

"*Your mother would be so proud of you*," she said. "*All your mothers would. Every one of them, all the way back. They always said no to me too.*"

The moth lifted off the glass.

Slowly. Its great grey-gold wings opening. It hung there for a moment, in the dark outside the window, in the light of the lantern, glittering.

And then it was gone. Up. Into the dark. Over the birch wood. West.

On the glass where it had been, there was dust. The shape of its wings. Four of them. With two rings, like eyes. Printed on the outside of the pane in grey-gold, perfectly, the way a hand leaves a print on a frosted window.

And the lantern in the window, *ARDENT*, *HOME*, the lamp Lex had made so it would never go out—

Flickered.

Just once.

And burned on.
