# The Lady of Moths

Wren Ashdown had been sick in a ditch three times since Highcrest, and she was beginning to take it personally.

The first time was the morning they rode out. Forty Keepers in grey and Dame Elowen Dunmore in her armor and Keeper-Lieutenant Fenn on a bay mare, going out through the West Gate in the thin spring dawn with a crowd on the walls to see them off. Wren had made it as far as the first milestone. Then the smell of forty horses and the creak of leather and somebody's breakfast sausage had all come together in her stomach at once, and she'd leaned out of the saddle and been sick into the ditch beside the road.

Elowen had looked at her.

"*Bad fish*," Wren had said. "*At the inn.*"

"*We didn't eat at an inn*."

"*Bad fish somewhere*," said Wren.

The second time was on the fourth day, crossing the downs west of the Blightmarch, in a wind. The third was this morning. At the edge of the Sundered Lands. For no reason at all. She'd woken up in her blanket in the frost and sat up and the world had tipped sideways, and she'd crawled out of the tent on her hands and knees and been sick into a patch of dead heather while a crow watched her from a rock with deep interest.

She'd wiped her mouth. And sat back on her heels. And glared at the crow.

"*Don't*," said Wren. "*Don't you start.*"

The crow said nothing. It didn't need to.

---

The Sundered Lands began where the grass stopped.

There was no sign. No marker. No wall. The downs just *ended*. A long ridge of grey-green turf, running north and south as far as you could see, and on the near side of it, grass. Sheep-cropped. Dotted with gorse. Ordinary.

And on the far side, nothing grew.

Not dead. Not burned. Not Gloam-grey, the way the Blightmarch had been before the brothers closed the breach. Just—*bare*. Hard grey ground, cracked like the bottom of a dried pond, going away west in low broken ridges and long shallow valleys, all the way to the edge of the sky. A few rocks. A few black stumps of things that might once have been trees. A wind that never stopped, cold and thin and smelling of nothing.

And at the edge of the sky, very far off, the Veil.

Wren had seen it once before. Two years ago. On the way to the Umbral Rift, with Lex and Antho and Dorran Vask, on the long cold march to the end of the world. She'd never forgotten it.

A wall of grey. Hanging from nowhere. Falling to nowhere. Stretching north and south along the whole western edge of the world, as far as eyes could follow. Not quite still. Moving, very slightly, the way a curtain moves when there's a window open somewhere behind it.

You couldn't see through it. You couldn't see past it. When you looked at it too long your eyes started to water and slide away, the way they slid off something very bright, though it wasn't bright at all.

It was the end of the world. That was all. The place where the net stopped.

And something had come through it.

---

"*Greyfall's over that ridge*," said Fenn.

He was a narrow, quiet man with sandy hair going thin on top, in the plain grey coat of the Keepers of the Stones. He'd been a Warden for twelve years and a Keeper for one and a half. He'd stood beside Roole at the Hollowmaw on the night of the Blade, and at the Highcrest singing, and he'd been the one, Roole said, who'd straightened every leaning Stone in the west with his own hands.

He didn't talk much. When he did, he said exactly what he meant.

"*Six survivors*," he said. "*Of forty. They came in on foot. Walking east. In a line. Not talking.*" He was looking at the ridge. "*They didn't stop walking until somebody stood in front of them. Roole said it was like stopping sleepwalkers.*"

"*What did they say*?" said Elowen.

"*Nothing, at first*." Fenn's mouth was a thin line. "*Then one of them, a girl, a cadet, eighteen, started crying. And couldn't stop. And said the light went out. Over and over. The light went out.*" He was quiet. "*She didn't mean the lanterns.*"

Wren looked at the ridge.

Grey. Bare. Cracked. With the wind coming over it, thin and cold, smelling of nothing at all.

"*I'll go first*," she said.

---

She went on the wind.

Not high. Low. Skimming. A Mistwalker's step, the way she'd learned it at the Gale Wellworld, in the eye of a storm above the Teeth, with lightning going off around her. Not flying. Walking. On air. Each step a little gust that caught her foot and held it and let it go. Silent. Quick. Close to the ground.

She'd been the best tracker in Thistledown at fourteen. A [Tracker], Common, Level 19, capped at 24, who could follow a hare across bare rock by the way the lichen was bent. She'd been a [Pathfinder] at twenty. Unbound by a smith with gold in his eyes, in a green valley in the Pale Wastes, while he was still learning how.

She was a Mistwalker now. Epic. Level 66.

She'd seen the title the morning after the wedding. She'd laughed so hard she'd fallen out of the loft.

She hadn't told Lex. He'd think Ember did it.

Ember probably did it.

---

Greyfall was a fort.

A small one. A square of grey stone walls with a tower at one corner, built on a low rise at the edge of the bare ground, looking west. The Order had built it two hundred years ago to watch the Veil, back when the Order still thought there was something past the Veil worth watching. The Keepers had taken it over. Forty of them. A garrison. To keep an eye on the old Umbral Rift, ten miles south, where the Lock still sat with the Key of Shadows barred in it.

The gates were open.

Wren stopped on the rise outside and looked at them for a long time.

Then she looked at the ground.

There were tracks. Of course there were tracks. Forty Keepers had lived here for a year and a half. The ground in front of the gate was beaten flat by boots and hooves and cart wheels. She could see the six who'd walked out, three nights ago. Walking east. In a line. Their steps very even and very slow, like people walking in their sleep.

And there were other tracks.

She crouched. Looked closer.

Not tracks. Not footprints. Not anything she'd ever seen.

Dust.

Fine. Soft. Grey-gold. Lying on the hard ground in front of the gate in long drifts and streaks and patches, like the pattern frost makes on a window. Like something had come across the bare ground in the night, not walking, not running, but *drifting*, low, just above the earth, shedding as it came.

Hundreds of somethings.

And in the middle of the drifts, coming straight across the bare ground from the west, from the Veil, to the open gate of Greyfall, one line of it. Narrower than the rest. Neater. Straighter.

The trail of something that didn't drift.

That *walked*.

---

She went in.

Elowen came behind her, with Oathkeeper drawn and her shield up. Then Fenn. Then the Keepers, in pairs, the way Roole drilled them. Spreading out across the little courtyard. Checking doors.

It was quiet.

Not empty. That was the worst part. Wren had walked into a lot of places after something terrible had happened in them. Villages after the Gloam. Fenmoor, with the Quiet standing in rows. The Highcrest square after the Cantor's singing, with a thousand people standing with their mouths open and nothing coming out.

Greyfall wasn't empty.

There were Keepers in it.

---

They were sitting.

In the courtyard. On the walls. In the doorway of the tower. On the steps of the little stone barracks. Thirty-four of them. In their grey coats. Some with their swords still in their hands. Some with lanterns. Sitting very still, where they'd stopped, with their faces turned west.

Toward the Veil.

Watching it.

Their eyes were open. Wren could see them from across the courtyard. Open, and *dim*. Not dark. Not empty. Dim. Like windows in a house where someone has turned the lamps down very low. There was something behind them. You could see it. Faint. Far back. Like a person standing a long way back from the glass in a dark room. But it wasn't coming forward.

And on every one of them, somewhere, dust.

On a hand. On a cheek. On the back of a neck. Grey-gold. A smudge of it. The size of a moth's wing.

---

"*Lady's light*," said Elowen Dunmore. Very quietly.

She went to the nearest one. A young man. Sitting on the steps of the barracks with a lantern in his lap. Dark. Dust on its glass. Dust on the back of his hand, where he held it.

Elowen knelt in front of him. In her armor. With Oathkeeper laid across her knees.

"*Keeper*," she said. "*Keeper. Can you hear me?*"

He didn't move. He was looking past her. West. At the grey.

She put her hand on his shoulder. Shook him. Gently. Then not gently.

He swayed. Like a reed. And came back to where he'd been. Still looking west.

"*They're alive*," said Fenn. From the tower doorway. Where he was kneeling by an older woman, a sergeant, with grey hair cropped short. He had two fingers on her throat. "*Pulse is slow. Very slow. But they're alive.*" He looked up. "*Three nights. Sitting here. No food. No water.*"

"*How*?" said Wren.

Fenn didn't answer.

He didn't know.

---

She found the dead ones in the stables.

Six. Lying in the straw. Not hurt. Not marked. Just lying there, very peaceful, with their eyes shut and dust on their faces. As if they'd lain down to sleep and simply not bothered to wake up.

*Six of forty walked out*, thought Wren. *Thirty-four sitting in the yard. Six here.*

*That's forty-six*.

She counted again. Then she went back out to the courtyard and counted the sitters.

Twenty-eight.

Not thirty-four. Twenty-eight. Sitting on the walls and steps and in the doorways. Twenty-eight. Plus six walked out. Plus six dead in the stables.

Forty.

She stood in the middle of the courtyard and counted them again. Twenty-eight.

She'd counted thirty-four when she came in. She was sure of it. She was a tracker. She counted things for a living.

She turned round slowly.

And saw them.

---

They were on the walls.

Six of them. Standing up. Where six Keepers had been sitting, a moment ago, with dim eyes and dust on their hands.

Only they weren't Keepers.

Not anymore.

They were standing very straight. Very still. In grey coats that hung on them as if they'd been made for bigger people. Their skin was pale. Not pale like a sick man is pale. Pale like ash. Like the underside of a leaf. Their eyes were lit. Faintly. Two dull gold sparks in each face, like the last coals in a grate.

And on their backs, under the grey coats, pushing the cloth up in two ragged humps, something was moving.

Unfolding.

The coats tore. Quietly. Down the back seams. And out of the torn cloth came wings.

Small. Ragged. Grey-gold. Dusty. Like the wings of the moths that had come over the ridge at the Lantern Keep, the night Odile went dim. But bigger. Man-sized. Furled and creased and dry, like leaves in autumn.

The six on the wall turned their faces toward the courtyard.

Toward the Keepers' lanterns. Toward Elowen's torch. Toward the little fire Fenn's men had just lit by the gate.

Toward the light.

---

"*Elowen*!"

Wren had an arrow on the string before she knew she'd reached for one. She drew. Aimed. At the nearest. A woman, or what had been a woman, with cropped grey hair and a sergeant's stripes on the torn sleeve of her coat.

The sergeant Fenn had been kneeling beside. A moment ago. With his fingers on her throat.

Wren loosed.

The arrow went through the woman's chest. Clean. Through the heart. The way Wren had put arrows through the hearts of gloamhounds and hollow-knights and ice-trolls a hundred times.

The woman looked down at it.

Then she came apart.

Not fell. Not bled. *Came apart*. Into dust. All at once. Grey-gold. Like a sandcastle when the wave comes in. Her coat fell empty onto the wall. The arrow fell through the dust and clattered on the stone. And the dust hung in the air for a moment, glittering. A cloud of it. The shape of a woman.

And then drifted down. Slowly. And settled on the wall.

And began, very slowly, to pile up again.

---

The other five came off the wall.

Not jumping. *Gliding*. Their ragged wings caught the thin cold wind and held them, the way a sycamore seed is held, and they came down into the courtyard slowly, spinning a little, light as leaves.

Toward the light.

Toward Fenn's little fire by the gate. Toward the lanterns in the Keepers' hands. Toward Elowen's torch.

They didn't attack the Keepers. They went *past* them. Straight past. Brushing them with their wings. To the lanterns. To the fire. And bent over them. Close. Closer. With their lit eyes and their pale faces. The way you'd bend over a cup of hot soup on a cold night.

And the lanterns went dim.

And the eyes of the Mothwing went bright.

---

The Keepers broke.

Not all of them. Not at once. Roole had drilled them well. They held for a moment. Stood in their pairs. Swords up. Shields up.

But they'd come to the end of the world. Into a fort full of their friends sitting with dim eyes, looking west. And the friends had stood up and grown wings and come down off the walls to drink the light out of their hands.

A boy dropped his lantern and ran. Then another. Then four. Scattering across the courtyard. Toward the gate. Away.

And the Mothwing turned their heads, and watched them go, the way a cat watches a moth. Not interested. The lanterns were what they wanted. The running boys had no light.

Except the one at the back. Who'd kept his torch.

---

The Mothwing came for him.

Wren saw it. A pale grey shape with ragged wings, gliding across the courtyard toward a running boy with a torch in his fist. Low. Fast. Faster than they'd moved before. Its lit eyes fixed on the flame.

It reached out. A long pale hand. Too many joints in the fingers.

And caught the boy's wrist.

Not his torch. His *wrist*. Where his skin was. And the boy screamed, high and short, like a hare. And the hand left dust on him. Grey-gold. A smudge of it, the size of a moth's wing, on the back of his hand.

And the boy stopped screaming.

And stopped running.

And stood very still in the middle of the courtyard, with the torch going out in his fist, and turned his face slowly toward the west. Toward the Veil.

And his eyes went dim.

---

Wren called the wind.

She didn't think about it. She didn't decide. It came up out of her the way it always did now, since the Wellworld of Gale, since she'd carried the First Breath in her lungs for three days. A *Tempest*. Hard. Cold. Straight down out of a clear sky into the courtyard of Greyfall like a fist.

It hit the Mothwing.

And they *tumbled*.

All of them. All five. Their ragged dust-wings caught the gust like sails in a gale, and they went over. Head over heels. Spinning. Tumbling across the courtyard like leaves in an autumn storm. Smashing into walls. Into each other. Into the ground. Their wings creasing and tearing, shedding dust in great glittering clouds.

They couldn't fly in it.

They were too light. Too dry. Too *dusty*. They'd come through the Veil on a whisper of air and drifted across the bare lands on the thinnest of breezes and glided down off the walls like seeds. They had no weight. No strength. No grip on the air.

And Wren Ashdown had the whole sky in her.

"*Down*!" she shouted. "*Keep them down! Elowen!*"

Elowen Dunmore was already moving.

---

It wasn't really a fight after that.

It was a storm. And a knight.

Wren held the wind. Down, into the courtyard. Hard and cold and endless, pinning the Mothwing to the stones like leaves pinned to a fence. Every time one of them tried to rise, tried to spread its wings, she slammed it down again. She could feel them through the wind. Light. Brittle. Dry as paper. Struggling. Not angry. Not afraid. Just—*hungry*. Straining toward the lanterns that the Keepers had dropped. Straining toward the light.

And Elowen went through them with Oathkeeper.

She didn't use the edge. She'd seen the arrow. She'd seen the sergeant come apart into dust and start, slowly, to pile up again. She used the *flat*. Hard. Like a woman beating a rug. One blow each. On the back. Between the ragged wings.

And each one she hit came apart. Into dust.

And the dust tried to settle. To pile up. To gather.

And Wren's wind took it.

Picked it up. Off the stones. Out of the courtyard. Up. Over the walls. Away. Scattered it. East and west and north and south. Across the bare grey ground. A little here. A little there. Thin as smoke. Spread over a mile of the Sundered Lands.

It didn't gather again.

---

When it was over, Wren sat down on the steps of the barracks with her head between her knees.

She was going to be sick. She knew it. She'd been fighting it the whole time, the whole storm, her stomach rolling like a boat in a gale. She sat very still and breathed through her nose and willed it to go down.

It went down.

Mostly.

Elowen sat down beside her. Heavily. In her armor. With Oathkeeper across her knees, dusted grey-gold from tip to hilt. She was breathing hard.

"*That's not bad fish*," said Elowen Dunmore.

"*It's bad fish*."

"*It's not bad fish*," said Elowen. And looked at her sideways. With her hard grey eyes, her father's eyes, Garrick's eyes. "*I grew up in a house with four aunts, Wren. And eleven cousins. Every one of them born in that house.*" She looked back at the courtyard. "*I know what bad fish looks like. That's not it.*"

Wren lifted her head.

And stared at her.

Elowen didn't say anything else. She just raised one eyebrow. Very slightly. And went back to cleaning dust off Oathkeeper with the corner of her cloak.

---

Wren didn't say anything either.

She sat on the barracks steps in the thin cold wind at the end of the world and looked at the courtyard. At the Keepers going round from sitter to sitter. At Fenn, crouched by the boy with the dust on his wrist, who was standing in the middle of the yard looking west. At the arrow lying on the wall where the sergeant had been.

She was counting.

Not Keepers. Not Mothwing. Days. Weeks.

The wedding. Three weeks and four days. And before that—

*Oh*, she thought.

*Oh, no. Oh*—

And then: *oh*.

---

The sun went down.

They'd decided to stay. Fenn decided. They couldn't move twenty-eight dim Keepers across the bare lands in the dark. They couldn't leave them. And the Mothwing, if there were more of them, would come back. To the light. Better to be ready. Better to be inside walls.

So they shut the gates. And lit the lanterns, every one they'd brought, and put them on the walls. And sat the dim Keepers in the barracks, in rows, wrapped in blankets, and tried to make them drink water, and some of them did, a little. And the Keepers who weren't dim stood on the walls in pairs with their swords and their lanterns and looked west.

Wren stood on the tower.

With her bow. And the wind. And a hand over her stomach, under her coat, where nobody could see.

The sky went red, and then purple, and then grey. And the Veil, at the edge of the world, went from grey to darker grey to nothing. A place where the stars stopped.

And on the bare ground below the fort, at the bottom of the rise, in the last of the light, someone was standing.

---

Wren didn't see her come.

That was the first thing. That was the thing she'd remember, afterward. She'd been watching. She'd been watching the whole time. Every ridge. Every hollow. Every shadow on the bare grey ground between the fort and the Veil. She was a tracker. She was the best tracker in Caldor. Nothing moved on open ground without her seeing it.

She didn't see her come.

She was just there. At the bottom of the rise. Fifty yards from the gate. Standing on the cracked grey earth with her hands folded in front of her and her face turned up toward the walls.

A woman.

Tall. Taller than any man in the fort. Thin. Straight as a candle. In a long grey-gold gown that went down to the ground and moved in the wind like smoke. With silver hair, very long, very fine, loose down her back to her knees.

And something folded on her back. Close against her spine. Like a cloak. Like a train. Grey-gold. Patterned, faintly, with rings and eyes.

Her face was turned up toward the walls. Toward the lanterns.

And her eyes were lit.

---

They were the most beautiful thing Wren had ever seen.

She'd think that afterward, and be ashamed of it, and think it anyway. Two lights. Soft and gold and warm. Not like the dull coal-sparks of the Mothwing. Not like anything. Like windows. Like two windows in a house at the end of a long lane, on a winter night, when you've been walking a long time in the cold and the dark and you're tired and lost and your feet hurt and you see them, far off, and think—

*There. Someone's home*.

*Someone's waiting*.

Wren's foot moved.

Forward. Toward the edge of the tower. Toward the lights.

She stopped it.

She stood very still on the tower of Greyfall with her bow in her hands and her heart going like a hammer and made her foot stop. And her other foot. And her eyes. She made herself look away. At the ground. At the dust on the cracked earth round the woman's feet.

*No tracks*, she thought. *She's not making tracks.*

*She's standing on the ground and she's not leaving a mark on it.*

---

"*Good evening*," said the woman.

Her voice came up the rise to the walls. Low. Soft. A little hoarse. Kind. It carried on the wind the way a voice carries across water at night. Every Keeper on the walls heard it. Wren saw them turn. Saw their faces.

"*I'm so sorry*," said the woman. "*About your friends. About your lanterns.*" She looked at the walls. At the dark ones, with dust on their glass. "*They were starving. My people. You must understand. It's been so very long since anyone left a light on for us.*"

Nobody answered.

Elowen was on the wall above the gate. With Oathkeeper drawn. Wren could see her. Very straight. Very still. Not looking at the woman's eyes. Looking at her hands.

"*Who are you*?" said Fenn. From the gate. His voice was steady. Mostly.

The woman smiled.

It was a lovely smile. Sad. Fond. Patient. The kind of smile a grandmother gives a child who has asked a question with a very long answer.

"*My name is Maelis*," she said. "*It's a very old name. You won't have heard it. Nobody's said it in a long time.*" She spread her long pale hands. Empty. "*I've come a very long way. From the other side of your Veil. From a country that was taken away from us, a long time ago, by a man who thought he knew best.*" She looked up at the walls. "*I've come to ask for sanctuary.*"

---

The wall was very quiet.

"*Sanctuary*," said Fenn.

"*Sanctuary*," said Maelis. "*For myself. And my household. The ones who are left.*" Something moved in her face. Grief. Or something that looked very like it. "*There were thousands of us once. There are hundreds now. The rest came apart against the Veil. Coming through. It's very old, your Veil. Very cruel. It doesn't like us.*" She looked at the wall where the sergeant had been. At the arrow lying in the dust. "*And now six more.*"

"*They attacked us*," said Fenn.

"*They were hungry*," said Maelis. Gently. "*They've been hungry for nine hundred years. They saw your lights and they couldn't help themselves.*" She shook her head. "*I'm sorry. I'm truly sorry. I called them back as soon as I could. But I'm only one woman, and they're very old, and very tired, and very hungry.*"

She looked up at the tower.

At Wren.

"*I'd like to speak to your Queen*," said Maelis. "*If she'll see me. I'll come as a prisoner, if you like. In chains. Unarmed.*" Her lit eyes didn't leave Wren's face. "*I have things to tell her. About your net. About the man who wove it. About what was here before.*"

A pause.

"*About what's coming up under your feet*," said Maelis softly. "*Even now. As we speak. Rising toward your lights. The thing your Founders couldn't kill.*"

---

Wren's hand had gone to her stomach again.

She didn't notice. Not until the woman's eyes moved. Down. From her face to her hand. To the place under her coat where her hand was resting. Flat. Without meaning to.

And stayed there.

The lit eyes widened. Very slightly.

And Maelis of the Crowned, standing on the bare grey earth at the bottom of the rise with the Veil behind her and the stars coming out, and leaving no tracks at all, looked up at Wren Ashdown on the tower of Greyfall.

And smiled.

Differently, this time. Not sad. Not fond. Not patient.

*Hungry*.

"*Oh*," said Maelis. Very softly. So softly that only the wind carried it, and only to Wren. "*Oh, look at you.*"

Wren went cold all over.

"*You're carrying a light*," said the Moth Queen.

---

"*I don't have a lantern*," said Wren.

It came out of her before she could stop it. Stupid. Hoarse. Her voice didn't sound like her own.

Maelis looked at her for a long moment. With her lit eyes. Her beautiful face. Her long pale hands folded in front of her.

"*No*," she said. Gently. "*No, you don't.*"

And then she turned away. As if it didn't matter. As if she'd said nothing at all. And looked up at the gate, at Fenn, with her sad and patient smile.

"*Well*?" said Maelis. "*Will you take me to your Queen?*"

---

Wren didn't hear what Fenn said.

She heard the blood in her ears. She heard her own heart. She heard the wind, coming in off the bare lands, thin and cold and smelling of nothing.

And under it, very faint, so faint she thought she was imagining it—

Something else. A sound she'd never heard before. Low. Small. Quick. Like a bird's heart, beating. Like a little drum, very far off.

Not outside her.

*Inside*.

She stood on the tower of Greyfall with her hand flat on her stomach and her bow forgotten in her other hand, and listened to it. And didn't breathe.

And at the bottom of the rise, in the dusk, the Moth Queen looked up at her one last time.

And her lit eyes, warm and gold and beautiful, like windows at the end of a long lane on a winter night, were fixed on Wren's hand.

On what was under it.

The way a moth looks at a lamp.
