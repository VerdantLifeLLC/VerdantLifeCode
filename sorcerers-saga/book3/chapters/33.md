# Lantern Night Eve

They came with lanterns.

Lex watched them come from the top of the rubble. All day. The last day of autumn. From the north and the west and the south. Along the old roads into the Blightmarch that nobody had walked in twenty years. Across the grey. In carts and wagons and on foot and on horseback.

Thousands.

More than had come to dig. More than had come to bake and wash and hammer. People who'd never held a spade. Old women. Children. Shopkeepers. Fishwives from the Saltreach. Shepherds from the Greyspine. The whole of Ashbourne, Mother Orla at the front in a cart with a barrel of bacon. Bess Rook from Fen Hollow, eighty-one, on a donkey this time, because she said she'd walked three hundred miles once and that was quite enough for one life.

And every one of them carrying a lantern.

---

They were the Lantern Night lanterns. The ones everyone in Caldor lit, on the last night of autumn, every year, for nine hundred years. For the lost. For the dead. For someone who hadn't come home.

Paper ones from the Saltreach, painted with fish and boats. Tin ones from the Vale, dented, mended, older than the people carrying them. Brass ones from Highcrest. Clay ones. Glass ones. A great carved stone one from the Ironbacks that took four men and a mule.

A loaf-shaped one, empty, carried by a girl of twelve with flour on her hands and her face set like stone.

They carried them across the grey and set them down. Round the rim of the circle. On the lip of the trench, all the way round, every few feet, three hundred and fourteen miles. The way Lex had asked. The way his mother had said.

Not waiting.

*Given*.

---

He'd watched the first one.

At dawn. At the edge of the ruins. Where the trench came round from the east and stopped at the fallen wall. An old man from Coldwater, a hewer, with a pick on his shoulder and grey on his lips, who'd dug with the Banners for fifty-one days without saying a word to anyone.

He'd come to the lip of the trench with a tin lantern. Dented. Black with coal-dust. And knelt. Slowly. And set it down on the baked grey earth of the rim.

And lit it.

And then he'd put his hand on it. Flat. On the top. The way you'd put your hand on a child's head. And said, very quietly, so that Lex, standing ten yards off on the rubble, almost didn't hear:

"*For the little one. From Absalom Ware. That was my wife's.*"

And got up. And walked away.

And the next person came.

---

They did it all day.

All round the circle. Three hundred and fourteen miles. Kneeling. Lighting. Putting a hand on the lantern. Saying who it was for and who it was from.

*For the little one. From Bess Rook. I never had one before. I bought this one in Millbridge.*

*For the little one. From the Tidewater fishing families. The sea says hello.*

*For the little one. From Mother Orla of the Crooked Kettle. There's bacon in the morning.*

Lex heard some of them. Most he didn't. The circle was too big. But he heard them anyway. Through the ground. Through the trench. Through the walls of baked grey earth that he'd walked three hundred and fourteen miles with his hand on.

Every one. A small warmth. A small weight. Set down on the rim of the room. Given.

And under the ground, under the breach, under Duskhold, something enormous and soft and young, listening.

*[...that one's for me...]*

*[...and that one...]*

*[...and THAT one...]*

*[...smith, they keep SAYING it...for the little one...for the little one...]*

*[...nobody ever gave me anything...]*

---

And outside the circle, all round, on the horizon—

The glitter.

It had come in the night. Thirty days ago. Lifting off Highcrest like a cloud off a lake and drifting east. Slowly. Patiently. Across the downs and the Blightmarch's edge. And spreading. And settling.

Not in the circle. Outside it. A ring. A hundred and fifty miles across. A wall of grey-gold on every horizon. From the ground to the sky. Like a sandstorm that had stopped mid-blow. Like frost on the inside of a window. Like the edge of the world.

It hadn't moved for thirty days.

It just sat there. Glittering. Turning, very slowly, round and round. Watching.

Waiting for Lantern Night.

---

And in it, the Dimmed.

Lex could see them. If he looked. If he stood on the highest point of the rubble and looked south, toward where the West Road came into the Blightmarch, toward where the glitter was thickest.

People.

Standing. In rows. Outside the circle. At the foot of the wall of moths. Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. Swaying. Like grass. With their faces turned toward the middle of the circle. Toward Duskhold. Toward the breach.

Holding up dark lanterns.

The Dimmed of Highcrest. Walked out of the city behind her. Thirty days ago. Across the downs and the grey. Two hundred miles. Barefoot, some of them. In their aprons and their nightclothes. Following her light.

And the Keepers from Greyfall. And the shepherds from the Greyspine. And everyone else she'd touched. All summer. All autumn.

She'd brought them all.

To stand at the edge of the circle on Lantern Night. With their dark lanterns. Facing in.

*Waiting*.

---

"*She brought them*," said Wren.

She was beside him. On the rubble. Sitting on a folded blanket on a block of black Gloam-stone, because she couldn't stand for long anymore, with her back against the remains of Duskhold's gate-tower and her hands on the great round of her stomach. Eight months. Near enough. Old Mag, who had come from Ashbourne in Mother Orla's cart and was now sitting in the tent below them telling Seren everything she was doing wrong, said it could be any day.

*Not tomorrow*, Lex had said.

*It'll come when it comes*, Old Mag had said. *Babies don't read calendars.*

"*She brought them so we'd see*," said Wren. Looking south. At the rows. "*So we'd know they're there. All of them. Every one she's touched. Standing at the edge of the room with their lights out.*"

"*Why*?"

"*Because she knows we'll come for them*." Wren's hand moved on her stomach. Slowly. "*She knows how to relight them now. I showed her. On Fennel Street. Hask was there. He saw.*" Her voice was very steady. "*She knows that tomorrow night, when the little one comes up, half the people in this camp will be out there at the edge. With lamps. Looking for their mothers.*"

Lex didn't say anything.

"*Not in here*," said Wren. "*Not guarding the circle. Not standing round the lanterns with black heat. Out there. Kneeling in the dust in front of the Dimmed. Telling them what they love about them.*" She looked at him. "*She's going to make us choose.*"

---

The families had come.

That was the other thing that had happened, over the thirty days. Word had gone out. From Fennel Street. From Wren. From Maren Ashdown, who had walked out of Highcrest through the sally port with her daughter on one arm and her sons on the other and told everyone she met on the road north, for two hundred miles.

*It can be done. You need a lamp. And their name. And you need to know what she took*.

*You need to love them*.

They'd come. Wives. Husbands. Daughters. Brothers. Old friends. Apprentices. Into the Blightmarch, with the lantern-bearers. Hundreds. Thousands. Carrying two lanterns each. One for the rim. One for the edge.

They were camped now on the south side of the circle. Nearest the Dimmed. Nearest the glitter. In a long uneasy line. Looking out at the rows of swaying people with their dark lanterns. Looking for faces.

Lex had seen a woman standing at the lip of the trench at noon with her hand over her mouth, staring south. At a man in the front row of the Dimmed. In an apron. With flour on his hands.

Hollis Penn's wife.

---

He went down to the tent at dusk.

His father was dying.

Everyone knew. Nobody said it. He was in the cot, in the tent at the foot of the rubble, where he'd been for fifty days. Under three blankets. With the grey book on his chest. With Seren on the stool beside him, where she'd been for fifty days.

He was so small now. Lex could hardly see him under the blankets. A shape. A breath. A white wisp of hair on the pillow, like smoke.

His eyes were open.

Grey. Clear. Looking at the tent roof. At nothing.

"*Lex*," said Arion Vey.

His voice was almost nothing. A breath with a word in it.

Lex knelt by the cot.

"*I'm here*."

"*The book*," said his father.

---

Seren lifted it off his chest. And put it in Lex's hands.

It was heavier than it looked. Grey cloth. Two hundred pages. More. The first forty were the columns. Hairs. Lines. Stairs. Grip. Going down like water down a slope. And then the names.

Lex turned the pages.

Names. In his father's hand. In the space between the line and the price of bread. Shaky at first. Then shakier. Then, after the day Ashka brought him home, in a hand that wasn't quite his father's. Bigger. Rounder. Square. Where someone else's hand had been round his, holding the pencil.

*Abel Mason. Hewer. Thirty-one.*

*Nell Hask. Eight. She whistled at the window when her brother came home.*

*Moll Corran. Nine. She kept a hen called Duchess.*

Page after page. After page. After page.

He came to the end. The last one. Halfway down a page. In a hand so shaky it was barely letters at all.

*Tam Brindle. Drover. Fifty-two. He sang on the road.*

And under it, the count. In Seraphine's hand.

*Eleven thousand, two hundred and six*.

---

"*Not all*," said his father. "*There's more. Thousands more. The farms. The roads.*" His eyes moved. To Lex. "*I won't finish.*"

"*Father*—"

"*Finish them*," said Arion Vey. "*After. You and your brother. And Seraphine.*" His voice was a thread. "*Every one. In the book. Between the line and the price of bread.*"

Lex held the grey book in both hands.

"*And at the end*," said his father, "*after the last one*—"

He stopped. His breath going in and out. Shallow. Slow.

"*Kell*," said Seren. Very quietly. Beside him. "*He wants Kell at the end. Antho told us. Through the scar. She asked.*"

"*Kell*," said Arion. "*Isaura Kell. Of Highcrest.*" His mouth moved. "*She hated me. For forty years. Most infuriating man in the Spire. Always right. Never told anyone anything.*" Something that might have been a laugh. "*She was right.*"

He looked at Lex.

"*Put her at the end*," he said. "*After the Vhessans. And then*—"

He stopped again.

"*And then me*," said Arion Vey.

---

Lex couldn't speak.

He knelt by the cot with the grey book in his hands and looked at his father. At the tiny white shape under the blankets. At the grey eyes. Clear. Steady. Looking back at him.

"*Not with the dead*," said his father. "*Not with the names. Just*—" He swallowed. It took a long time. "*After. On a new page. One line.*"

"*What line*?"

His father didn't answer.

He looked past Lex. At Seren. On the stool. With her hand on his chest. Over his heart.

"*She'll tell you*," said Arion. "*When it's time. She knows.*"

---

*[Lex*.]

Ember. In his hearth. Very quiet.

*I know*.

*[Lex. Let me*—] *The coal was glowing.* [*Let me talk to him. Please. Just once. Before.*]

Lex looked at his father.

"*Father*," he said. "*Ember wants to talk to you.*"

His father's eyes moved. To Lex's chest. Where the hearth was.

"*Can it*?" he said. "*Can I hear it?*"

"*I don't know*." Lex looked at his mother. "*Mam could. In the deep. She heard it. She's a Listener.*"

"*I'm not*," said Arion. Faintly. "*I never was. I was a Listener's husband. I learned a little.*" He closed his eyes. "*Not enough.*"

Seren reached out.

And took Arion's thin bone hand. Off the blanket. And laid it flat. On Lex's chest. Over his heart. Over the hearth. And put her own square freckled hand over it.

And held it there.

"*Listen*," said Seren Vey. "*Love. Just listen. The way you listened to me on the stairs. You don't have to hear it. Just listen.*"

---

*[Hello, old man*.]

Lex felt it. Felt Ember say it. In his hearth. In the old dry voice. The voice of a man of two hundred and thirteen, sealing his sons in a thunderstorm in a cottage at Harrow, pressing a piece of himself into the seal like a maker's mark.

He felt his father's hand go rigid on his chest.

His father's eyes opened. Wide.

He heard it.

---

Lex didn't know how. He'd never know. Maybe it was his mother's hand on top. Maybe it was the dying. Maybe it was that Ember was a piece of him, and a man can always hear himself, at the end, if he listens hard enough.

He heard it.

*[You look terrible*,] said Ember.

His father laughed. A breath. A crack. A sound like dry leaves.

"*You look*—" He stopped. "*I don't know what you look like. I've never seen you.*"

*[I look like you*,] said Ember. *[Twenty years ago. Grey at the temples. Two fingers tapping. A face that never changed.]* A pause. *[I've been your son's friend. For five years. I've been very rude to him. I told him terrible jokes about metallurgy.]*

"*He told me*."

*[He laughed at most of them*.] *The coal glowed.* [*Arion. Listen. I've got them. All of them. Everything you were, up to the seal. The steps. The rain. The night Antho was born. Walking Lex up and down the kitchen with the fever.*] *A long pause.* [*She paid you to the Ferryman. But I've got them. I've had them all along. Copies. In a hearth.*]

His father's hand trembled on Lex's chest.

*[When you're gone*,] said Ember, very quietly, *[I'll keep them. Every one. And if she wants them, someday, I'll give them to her. Your side. What you saw. What you felt. On the steps. In the rain.]* A pause. *[She'll never remember being there. But she'll know what it looked like to you.]*

---

His father didn't say anything for a long time.

His hand lay on Lex's chest. Under his wife's hand. Trembling. His eyes were open. Wet. Looking at nothing. At the tent roof. At Lex's chest. At a coal in a hearth that sounded like himself.

"*Thank you*," said Arion Vey. At last. In a whisper. "*Thank you for looking after him.*"

*[He looked after me*,] said Ember. *[Mostly by forgetting to spend his points.]*

And his father laughed.

The real laugh. The wide startled one. Cracking out of him like ice off a roof. Shaking the cot. Shaking his bone hand on Lex's chest. Shaking the tears out of his grey eyes.

And Seren laughed too. Beside him. With her hand on his. Crying.

And Lex knelt by the cot with his father's hand on his heart and his mother's hand on his father's and Ember glowing in his hearth like a forge-fire at dawn.

And didn't laugh.

But nearly.

---

He went out to find Brannoc after.

He didn't know why. He just did. The way you go to the forge when you can't sleep. The way you put your hands on iron when you can't put them on anything else.

The old smith was at the anvil. The black Gloam-stone one. At the head of the trench. In the dusk. Alone. With a hammer.

Not hammering.

Just standing there. With his enormous scarred hand on the anvil. Looking east. Along the trench. At the lanterns. Three hundred and fourteen miles of them. Lit. Going away round the circle in a ring of small gold flames, out of sight, round the edge of the world.

"*Look at that*," said Brannoc Holt. Quietly. Without turning round. "*Lad. Just look at that.*"

Lex stood beside him. And looked.

"*When I was capped*," said Brannoc, "*I used to lie awake. You know. I told you. Thinking if I could just get to twenty-five, I'd be able to make something that mattered.*" He was quiet for a long time. "*I never did. Never once. Sixty-three years. Bells and hinges and horseshoes and nails.*"

"*Brannoc*—"

"*I dug that*," said Brannoc. Pointing. At the trench. At a stretch of it, twenty miles east, where the wall was smoother than the rest. "*That bit. With Tilda and Hedda and the lads. Four days.*" His voice was rough. "*And I knocked on it. Every foot. Like you said. Like a door.*"

He looked at Lex. With his old kind fierce wet eyes.

"*Something that mattered*," said Brannoc Holt. "*At sixty-three. With my bad knees. With a spade.*"

---

Lex didn't say anything.

He put his hand on the anvil. Next to Brannoc's. The black Gloam-stone. Warm. From fifty-one days of hammering.

Brannoc looked down at their two hands. Side by side. On the anvil. His enormous scarred one. Lex's big scarred one. The same burns. The same calluses. In the same places. Thirteen years. From the bellows to the hammer.

"*You did good, lad*," said Brannoc. Very quietly.

"*You said that at the wedding*."

"*I'll keep saying it*," said Brannoc Holt. "*Till you believe it.*"

---

Kit found him at midnight.

The Coldwater boy. Seventeen. Who'd walked out of the Drowned Archive behind them six months ago and never gone back. Who'd dug with the Banners for fifty-one days and said almost nothing and slept by the fire at the end of the line every night with his back to the others.

He came up the rubble in the dark. Slowly. With his hands in his pockets.

And stood there.

"*Master Lex*," he said.

"*Kit*."

The boy didn't say anything for a long time.

"*I knew her*," he said at last. "*Nell. Nell Hask.*"

Lex went very still.

"*She was my age*," said Kit. "*In Coldwater. Before the grey. We were in the same dame-school. She sat next to me.*" He looked at his hands. "*She taught me to whistle. The two-note one. Stop, danger, come. She said every pit-kid should know it. In case of firedamp. In case you get lost in the dark.*"

He put his fingers to his mouth.

And whistled. Very softly. Two notes. High. Sharp. The same two notes Wren had whistled from Fennel Street. That Lex had heard over twenty hammers and a hundred miles.

*Stop. Danger. Come*.

"*She used to do it at the window*," said Kit. "*When Silas came home from the pits. Every night. You could hear it all up the street. Two notes. And he'd whistle back. And everyone would laugh.*" His voice cracked. "*He was so big. And he'd stand in the street outside their house with coal all over him and whistle back at an eight-year-old.*"

He looked up at Lex. In the dark.

"*I know his warm thing*," said Kit. "*Silas Hask. I know what she took. I was there.*" He swallowed. "*I'm the only one left who was.*"

---

Lex looked at him for a long time.

At a boy of seventeen from a grey town in a grey valley. Who'd stood on a ledge at the bottom of the Drowned Archive and said *I'm staying* to the biggest man he'd ever known. And watched him walk away up the stair with the moths going with him like a cloak.

"*Tomorrow*," said Lex. "*When it comes up. When she comes. He'll be with her.*"

"*I know*."

"*He'll be in front. Between her and everyone. With his pick.*" Lex's voice was very quiet. "*He'll be the most dangerous thing on the field. After her.*"

"*I know*."

"*And you want to*—"

"*I want to give it back*," said Kit. Fiercely. "*Like Wren did. With her mam. I want a lamp. And his name. And the whistle.*" His thin face was set like stone. "*I want to stand in front of him and do it. Like Nell did. At the window.*"

Lex didn't say anything.

"*He'll kill me*," said Kit. "*Probably. If I get that close. If she tells him to.*" He looked out at the glitter on the horizon. "*Doesn't matter. He held the pick down. At Fennel Street. Wren said. When she whistled. Just for a second.*" His voice cracked. "*He heard Nell. Just for a second. Through all of it.*"

He looked back at Lex.

"*I want him to hear her all the way*," said Kit.

---

Lex gave him a lantern.

His own. The one from the window at Harrow. Iron and glass. *ARDENT* on the pane. *HOME* on the base. He'd brought it. He didn't know why. He'd taken it out of the window of the cottage at Harrow on the day he left for the Blightmarch and carried it three hundred and fourteen miles round the circle in Brannoc's cart and never once lit it.

He lit it now. On the rubble. In the dark. With his own fire.

And put it in Kit's hands.

Not left on. Given.

"*For Silas Hask*," said Lex. "*From the Ashbourne Forge. From the smith who Unbound him in the rain.*" He closed the boy's fingers round the handle. "*And from Nell.*"

Kit looked down at the lantern.

At the little gold flame behind the glass. At the word on the base.

*HOME*.

He didn't say anything.

He didn't need to.

---

Lex sat on the rubble with Wren until the stars went round.

Neither of them slept. Neither of them tried. She sat on her blanket on the Gloam-stone block with her back against the gate-tower and her hands on her stomach, and he sat on the rubble beside her with his head against her knee, and they looked at the circle.

At the lanterns. Three hundred and fourteen miles of them. Gold. All the way round. A ring of small lights at the edge of the world.

At the glitter beyond them. On every horizon. Grey-gold. Turning. Waiting.

At the Dimmed. In their rows. At the foot of the glitter. Swaying. With their dark lanterns held up.

And at the black rubble under them. At Duskhold. At the fallen hall, a hundred yards off, where the scar was. Where his father would be carried tomorrow, on his litter, to lie beside the breach with his hand on the edges. Where his brother was. Under. At the bottom. With the lamp. With Seraphine and Pim and Kell. With the little one pressing up against the underside of the world like a child against a window.

Waiting.

---

"*It's kicking*," said Wren. Very quietly. Near midnight.

He put his hand on her stomach.

It was. Hard. Fast. Over and over. Like a little drum. Like a fist on a door. Like a heart.

"*It's been doing that all night*," said Wren. "*Since the lanterns. Since they started lighting them.*" She looked out at the ring of gold. "*Like it knows. Like it can feel them.*"

"*Maybe it can*."

"*Maybe*." She was quiet for a moment. "*Seren says the Harrow women always knew. When a child was coming. They said the light in the window got brighter. All on its own. The night before.*"

Lex looked at the lantern-ring.

Was it brighter? He couldn't tell. It was three hundred and fourteen miles of small gold flames. It was the brightest thing he'd ever seen.

"*She wants it*," said Wren.

He didn't need to ask who.

"*She's out there*," said Wren. "*Somewhere in the glitter. Watching. She's been watching for thirty days.*" Her hand tightened on his. On her stomach. "*She wants the little one. And she wants this.*"

"*She won't get either*."

"*You don't know that*."

"*No*," said Lex. "*I don't.*" He looked at her. "*I know that there's three hundred and fourteen miles of people round this circle who'd stand in front of her for it. For both of them. For you.*" His voice was rough. "*I know that.*"

Wren looked at him for a long time.

Then she leaned down. Awkwardly. Round the great curve of herself. And kissed him on the forehead. On the handprint. On the grey-gold mark of a long-fingered hand.

"*Idiot*," she said softly.

---

At midnight, the bell rang.

On the cord round his neck. Against his chest. Under his shirt. The little silver bell with the crooked clapper.

Once.

Flat.

Lex put his hand over it.

And under the ground, under the rubble, under the scar, under the breach, in the deep, very faint, very far, he felt something answer. A ripple. A breath. Like something enormous turning over in its sleep.

*[...is it time...]*

*Not yet*, thought Lex. *Tomorrow. When the sun goes down. When the lanterns are all lit.*

*[...I'm frightened...]*

*I know*.

*[...are YOU frightened...]*

Lex looked at the glitter on the horizon. Turning. Waiting. At the Dimmed in their rows. At his wife beside him, with her hand on their child. At the tent at the foot of the rubble, where his father was dying.

*Yes*, he thought. *Yes. I'm frightened.*

*[...]*

*[...good...]* said the little one. *[...then it's not just me...]*

---

And on the horizon, to the south, where the West Road came into the Blightmarch and the glitter was thickest—

A light.

Not a lantern. Not a fire.

A pillar.

Grey-gold. Glowing. Rising out of the wall of moths into the night sky like a column of smoke lit from inside. Tall. Taller than the Spire. Taller than anything. Reaching up. And up. Until it touched the stars and the stars went out round it.

And at the bottom of it, so far off it was only a speck, a shape. Standing in the glitter. In front of the Dimmed. Tall. Thin. With something folded on its back.

Looking north.

Toward the circle.

Toward the rubble.

Toward a woman on a blanket with her hands on her stomach.

And on Lex's face, cool and smooth as the inside of a shell, the handprint *tightened*.

---

He didn't sleep.

He sat on the rubble until dawn with his hand on his wife's stomach and his other hand on the bell. Watching the pillar. Watching the glitter. Watching the stars go round.

And as the sky went grey in the east, over the far side of the circle, over three hundred and fourteen miles of lanterns burning gold—

A sound.

From the tent. At the foot of the rubble. Small. Quiet. A sound Lex had heard once before, a long time ago, in a cottage at Harrow, when he was four and woken in the night by a thunderstorm.

His mother. Singing.

*Little light, little light, burning low*.

Flat. On the high note.

To an old man in a cot. Who was still breathing. Who was holding on.

For one more night.
