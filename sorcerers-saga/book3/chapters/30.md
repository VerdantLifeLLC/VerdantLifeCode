# The Old Man and the Moth

Arion Vey had not stolen anything in two hundred and thirty-three years, and he was finding it very educational.

He'd stolen a dragon. That was the first thing. A full-grown storm-drake the size of a house, from the edge of a trench in the middle of the Blightmarch at four in the morning, while his son slept on a black floor with his hand on a scar and his son's wife walked across a grey plain toward an anvil to tell his other son how to write a name.

He hadn't meant to steal her. He'd meant to ask.

He'd lain on the litter at the edge of the trench and waited until the camp was quiet. And then he'd said, very softly, into the dark, "*Ashka.*"

And the dragon had lifted her great blue head off her forepaws and looked at him with one golden eye.

*Please*, he'd thought. Not knowing if she could hear him. He'd never had the Bloodbond. He'd never been able to talk to dragons. *Please. I need to go to Highcrest. I need to go now. Before I can't.*

Ashka had looked at him for a long time.

And then she'd got up. And come over. And lowered her neck. And slid her great head under the litter, very gently, the way a dog slides its nose under your hand. And lifted it. Onto her back. Into the place between the two ridges of scale where Brannoc's straps were.

And Arion had lain there on the litter on the dragon's back in the dark and laughed silently until he cried.

---

The second thing he'd stolen was a boy.

He hadn't meant to steal him either. He hadn't known he was there. Ashka had gone up off the grey in a long slow climb, quiet, careful, the way she flew with Pim on her back. And somewhere over the edge of the Blightmarch, with the stars going round and the wind cold on his face, Arion had felt something move at the foot of the litter.

A small cold hand. On his ankle. Through the blanket.

He'd lifted his head. Slowly.

Pim was sitting at the foot of the litter. Cross-legged. In his blue coat with the brass buttons buttoned wrong. With his hair blowing everywhere and his grey-violet eyes very steady.

"*Pim*," said Arion.

The boy didn't say anything.

"*You can't come*."

Pim didn't say anything.

"*Pim*. *It's dangerous. It's the most dangerous thing I've ever done. I'm going into the Spire. To her. To the Moth. I might not*—" He stopped. "*I might not come out.*"

Pim looked at him.

And then he moved up the litter. On his knees. Carefully. Holding the straps. Until he was beside Arion's head. And lay down. On his side. Next to him. On the litter. With his thin arm across Arion's chest.

The way he'd lain at the foot of the litter all the way from the Keep. The way he'd stood by the litter at the cave mouth with his hand on the edge. The way he'd sat on the rock at the Hollowmaw a year ago, telling an old man about the bats in the cold-room, waving his hands.

"*Moths can't see me*," said Pim. Into the blanket. Very quietly.

Arion didn't say anything.

"*I've got a room*," said Pim. "*Inside. Where nothing gets in. The Ferryman said.*" A pause. "*She won't see me. Nobody will.*"

Arion looked up at the stars.

"*Your master will kill me*," he said.

"*Master Antho doesn't kill people*," said Pim. "*He reads to bats.*"

---

They came down outside Highcrest an hour before dawn.

Not near. Not close. Ashka knew. She came down in a hollow in the downs ten miles north, in a stand of beech, where nobody would see. She set the litter down very gently on the leaf-mould. And lay down beside it. And curled round it. Like a cat round a hearth. With her great golden eye on the old man.

Waiting.

Arion lay on the litter and looked south. Through the beech trunks. At the glitter. The lid over the city. Faint and grey-gold in the starlight. And under it, at the top of the hill, the Spire.

Lit.

Every window.

And the top one brightest.

---

"*I can't climb*," he said. To no one. To the boy beside him. To the dragon. "*I couldn't climb the steps of the Spire if my life depended on it. I couldn't climb out of this litter.*"

Pim didn't answer.

"*But she'll let me in*," said Arion. "*She'll send someone down. She'll carry me up. I know she will.*" He looked at the light at the top of the tower. "*She's been waiting for me. She's been reading my books and waiting for me to come.*"

He was quiet for a long time.

"*And when I get there*," said Arion Vey, "*she'll offer me a drink.*"

---

He didn't have to go to her.

She came to him.

At dawn. In the beech hollow. With the light coming grey through the trunks. He heard nothing. Ashka heard nothing; he watched her head come up and her golden eye go wide, too late.

There was a moth on the litter.

On the blanket. On his chest. Large. Grey-gold. Wings spread. The size of his spread hand. With two rings on its upper wings, like eyes.

And through it, a voice.

Low. Soft. Hoarse. Delighted.

"*Warden-Architect*," said Maelis. "*You've thought about it.*"

---

They came for him at noon.

Four Mothwing. And a litter of their own. Black. Silk-hung. Like a bier. They came down out of the glitter over the beech hollow on their ragged wings, slowly, gently, the way autumn leaves come down, and landed in a ring round his litter. And bowed.

Ashka roared at them.

They didn't flinch. They didn't move. They knelt on the leaf-mould with their pale faces bowed and their dull gold eyes on the ground and waited, while the storm-drake roared and lashed her tail and lightning crackled along her horns.

"*Ashka*," said Arion. "*It's all right.*"

The dragon looked at him. With her great golden eye. And something in it, through the eye, through whatever it was that dragons had instead of words, that made his old heart turn over.

"*It's all right*," said Arion again. Very gently. "*I asked them to come.*"

He looked at the boy. Beside him. Very still.

"*Stay with her*," he said. "*Pim. Stay with Ashka. Don't come.*"

Pim looked at him.

And nodded.

Arion knew he was lying. He let him. Some things you let a boy lie about.

---

The Mothwing carried him up the West Way.

On the black litter. Through the dim streets. Past the people. Standing in doorways. Sitting on steps. Swaying. With their dark lanterns held up toward the Spire. He looked at their faces as he passed. He couldn't help it. He'd lived in this city two hundred years. He knew some of them.

The woman who sold cheese in the Tuesday market. The old porter from the Spire gate who'd carried his trunks for forty years and never once asked why he didn't age. A boy he'd taught letters to, a long time ago, in a charity school in the Lower Town, grown now, grey at the temples, swaying in a doorway with his children beside him.

Hollis Penn.

On the Spire steps. In the front row. In his apron. With flour on his hands. Holding up his loaf-shaped lantern, dark, with dust on it. Toward the gold window at the top.

Arion looked at him for a long time as the litter went past.

He didn't know Hollis Penn's warm thing. He didn't know what had been taken. He'd met the man twice. Once at the Highcrest singing, waking up holding a loaf. Once at the wedding, carrying bread up a mountain.

He thought: *someone does. Someone loves him. A wife. A daughter. An apprentice.*

He thought: *someone will come*.

---

They didn't take him up the stairs.

There was a lift. Lysandra had put it in for her grandfather. A cage of brass and wood on chains, in a shaft at the heart of the Spire, that went up six floors with a great deal of clanking. Arion had ridden in it every day for twenty years. He knew every creak.

They took him up in it now. In the black litter. With the Mothwing standing round him in the brass cage, silent, swaying.

Six floors.

And then the last stair. The narrow one. The one that went up from the sixth floor to the top, to the rooms under the roof, where he'd lived for two hundred years. Ninety-four steps. He'd climbed it ten thousand times. Twenty thousand.

The Mothwing carried him up it.

And at the top, the door. His door. Plain oak. With a brass handle that stuck in wet weather.

It was open.

---

The round room was lit.

Gold. Soft. Warm. From nowhere. From everywhere. From the walls and the floor and the ceiling and the shelves of grey books and the cold grate that had never once, in two hundred years, warmed the room properly. The light came out of all of it. Like the light in a lamp. Like the light in two eyes at the end of a long lane.

And she was in his chair.

By the window. The tall window looking north over the city. In his chair. With one of his grey books open on her knees. Reading.

She looked up.

"*Warden-Architect*," said Maelis. "*Welcome home.*"

---

He didn't look at her.

Not first. He'd decided that on the litter. On the West Way. On the lift. He'd decided it a hundred times. *Don't look at her. Look at the room. Look for*—

She was on the window seat.

Small. Barefoot. In a grey dress that wasn't hers, too long in the hem. With her grey-brown hair loose. With her hands in her lap. Bound. With something fine and grey-gold and glittering. Silk. Moth-silk. Round her wrists. Like a bracelet. Like a string of pearls.

Her right palm was bandaged.

She was looking at him.

Her face—

He didn't know what her face was doing. He'd never know. It went white. And then it went still. And then something went across it, so fast he almost missed it. Something that wasn't fear. Something that wasn't relief.

Something *furious*.

"*You stupid*," said Seren Vey, "*stupid old man.*"

---

Maelis laughed.

Delighted. Low and warm. She put down the grey book. Carefully. On the little table beside the chair. Where he'd always put his books. Where the ring from his teacup had worn a pale mark in the wood over two hundred years.

"*Oh, she's been like this the whole time*," said Maelis. Fondly. "*Six days. She won't eat. She won't sleep. She won't tell me where my sister's crown is.*" She looked at Seren. "*She sits on your window seat and tells me what she'd like to do to me. In some detail. She's very inventive.*"

"*I'll show you inventive*," said Seren.

"*I'm sure you will, my dear*." Maelis didn't take her eyes off Arion. "*But not just now. Your husband's come to visit. All this way. On a litter. To see you.*" Her lit eyes were very soft. "*Isn't that lovely.*"

---

"*Her hand*," said Arion.

Maelis looked at the bandage. On Seren's palm.

"*The first night*," she said. Simply. "*I'm sorry. I didn't want to. But I needed it. A little. Just a little. In a glass bottle.*" She smiled. "*It's on your desk. Would you like to see?*"

He didn't look.

He didn't need to. He could see it. Out of the corner of his eye. On his desk. Where he'd written two hundred years of grey books. A small glass bottle, the size of a thumb. Stoppered. Sealed with grey-gold wax. And inside it, dark. Red-dark. A finger's depth.

Harrow blood.

"*It's not enough*," said Maelis. Conversationally. "*Not for the Veil. Not for all four of them. I'll need more. On Lantern Night. When the little one comes up.*" She tilted her head. "*But it's enough to start. Enough to loosen the knot. Enough to wake them.*" Her lit eyes were very warm. "*They'll be stirring by now. In their cocoons. Feeling it. The give in the knot.*"

---

"*You came to say yes*," said Maelis.

It wasn't a question.

Arion looked at her. Finally. At her face. At her eyes. Lit. Gold. Warm. Like windows. Like lamps.

And felt it. The pull. The lane. The house at the top of the hill with every window lit and the door open and someone standing in it.

He let it pull.

He'd decided that too. On the litter. *Let it. Don't fight it. You're too old to fight it. Let her think it's working.*

"*Yes*," said Arion Vey.

---

Seren made a sound.

Small. Sharp. Like something breaking.

He didn't look at her. He couldn't. If he looked at her he'd stop. He'd look at her face and see what was on it and he'd stop.

"*I'm eighty-five*," he said to Maelis. "*I think. I lose track. A year a week. More.*" He held up his hand. Thin. Shaking. "*I came up your stairs on a litter carried by your people because I couldn't climb them. I can't hold a cup. I can't stand.*" He looked at the window seat. Not at Seren. At the window behind her. "*She's here. In my room. And I can't get up out of this litter and cross the floor to her.*"

"*No*," said Maelis gently. "*You can't.*"

"*You said you'd give me time*."

"*I did*."

"*Twenty years*."

"*As much as you like*." Maelis leaned forward in his chair. Her long glowing hands on her knees. "*Enough to be young. Enough to stand. Enough to cross a floor.*"

She held out her hands.

Cupped.

---

There was light in them.

He'd seen it before. Through Antho's eyes, through the little one's memory, in a hall of glass nine hundred years ago. Through Lex's account, in the deep, when she knelt and drank. He'd never seen it with his own.

It was beautiful.

Dark and gold at once. Soft and deep and glowing. Like the night sky far from any town. Like the moment before a word. It moved in her cupped hands like water, like light, like something alive. Pouring a little between her long fingers. Leaking.

*The little one*, he thought. *A piece of it. What she drank in the deep. What she's been keeping.*

*Its screaming, in a cup*.

"*Drink*," said the Moth Queen.

---

He drank.

She leaned down. Over the litter. Close. Her silver hair falling round them both like a curtain. And held her cupped hands to his mouth. The way she'd held them to her household, nine hundred years ago. The way she'd held them to Orrin. To Silas Hask.

And he bent his head. Slowly. His old neck creaking.

And drank.

---

It didn't taste of anything.

That was the first thing. He'd expected it to taste of something. Light. Honey. Wine. It didn't. It went into his mouth like air. Like breath. Like the first breath you take after you've been holding it a long time underwater.

And then it went *down*.

Into his throat. His chest. His heart. His old slow uneven heart, beating like a drum with a hole in it. And the hole—

*Closed*.

He felt it close. Like a door. Like a wound. Like a dam being mended, stone by stone, all at once, from the inside. The water that had been pouring through him for months, the two hundred years coming due, the flood—

Stopped.

Held.

Like the Root had held it. For two hundred years. Like a hand holding a door shut.

And then it went *back*.

---

He felt it go.

His hands first. On the litter. He was looking at them. He couldn't look away. The brown spots. Fading. The knuckles. Shrinking. The skin. Tightening. Smoothing. The tremor—

Gone.

His hands were still. Steady. Long. Narrow. Brown. Strong. A young man's hands. A Warden-Architect's hands, at thirty-one, the year he took the Root. The hands he'd had for two hundred years. The hands he'd stood in front of the mirror and prayed would change.

He lifted them. Turned them over. They didn't shake.

He felt his face. His jaw. The furrows. Going. Like snow off a roof in spring. He felt his hair. Thick. Heavy. Dark. Falling across his forehead. He felt his back straighten on the litter. His shoulders. His chest. Deep. Full. Breathing all the way down.

He felt his heart. Slow. Strong. Even.

Thirty-one.

He sat up.

He didn't decide to. His body did it. On its own. The way a young body does. And then he swung his legs off the litter. And put his feet on the floor of his own round room. And stood.

---

He was standing.

In the middle of the room. On his own feet. Without a stick. Without a hand. In his old grey coat, which was too big for him again now, which hung on his shoulders the way it had hung a year and a half ago, at the masquerade, when he was forty-five forever.

He took a step.

And another.

Across the floor. Toward the window seat. Toward the small barefoot woman with moth-silk on her wrists and a bandage on her palm, sitting very straight, staring at him.

He stopped in front of her.

And looked down at her. With his young face. His dark hair. His grey eyes.

And she looked up at him.

And he saw it. He saw her see him. A young man. Thirty-one. Dark-haired. Grey-eyed. Standing in front of her in the gold light.

The man she'd met on the steps of the Spire in the rain. The man in the forty pages. The man she'd paid away.

She didn't know him.

He saw that too. He saw her look at his young face and search it, the way she'd searched his old one, with her hands, in the cavern at the bottom of the Hollowmaw. Search it for something. Anything.

And find nothing.

She didn't know this face. She'd never known it. The Ferryman had it. All of it. The steps and the rain and the lanterns and the young man in the grey coat who had looked at her as if she were the first light he'd seen in two hundred years.

She didn't remember any of it.

She'd only ever known the old one.

---

"*Say something*," said Seren.

Her voice was a whisper.

He looked at her.

"*Say something*," she said again. Her eyes were very bright. "*Please. I don't know your face. I never did. I only knew the old one.*" Her voice cracked. "*Say something, so I'll know it's you.*"

Arion Vey knelt down.

In front of the window seat. On the floor of his own room. With his young knees, that didn't creak. And took her bound hands in his young hands, that didn't shake.

"*Good evening*," he said.

---

She shut her eyes.

And listened.

He watched her do it. Watched her face go still. Watched her listen to his voice. Young now. Deep. Quiet. Dry. The voice from the Root. The voice from Ember. The voice she'd fallen in love with in the dark, twice, without knowing whose it was.

And he watched her open her eyes.

"*You sound wrong*," said Seren Vey.

He stared at her.

"*You sound wrong*," she said. Fiercely. Wet. "*You sound like the dark. Like the Root. Like Ember. You don't sound like* you.*"* She was holding his hands so hard it hurt. "*You sound like a voice I fell in love with three years ago through a wall. Not like the man who ate my burnt porridge. Not like the man who said page nine.*"

He couldn't speak.

"*I liked the old one*," said his wife. "*You stupid man. I liked him. I liked his hands. I liked that they shook. I liked that he fell asleep in the middle of telling me about Lex.*" Her voice broke. "*Why did you do this? Why did you let her*—"

"*To climb the stairs*," said Arion.

She stopped.

"*To get to you*," he said. Very quietly. "*That's all. I couldn't climb the stairs.*"

---

Behind him, Maelis laughed.

Softly. Warmly. Like someone watching children play.

"*Isn't that lovely*," she said. "*He came all this way. To be young for you. And you don't want him.*" She rose from his chair. Tall. Glowing. Unfolding. "*I did warn him, Seren. I did. I said she doesn't remember a young man. I said, let her fall in love with you a third time.*" She tilted her head. "*I didn't think she'd fall in love with the old one.*"

She came across the room. Slowly. Her gown whispering on the floor. Her wings shifting under the silk of her back.

"*No matter*," said Maelis. "*He's mine now. Aren't you, Warden-Architect?*" She stopped behind him. Close. He felt her there. The warmth. The glow. "*You drank from my hands. Like Orrin. Like Silas. You'll need to come back. Every few days. For more.*" Her voice was very soft. "*Because it leaks. Doesn't it. Your clever son told you. It always leaks.*"

He felt it.

He'd felt it from the first breath. Under the youth. Under the strength. Under the steady even heart. A thread. Thin. Fine. Like a hair. Running out of the middle of him. Leaking. Slowly. Like water through a crack in a cup.

Pulling him toward her.

*A Vey on a string*.

"*Stay with me*," said Maelis of the Crowned. "*Be my friend. Sit in your chair by your window and read me your books. And I'll keep you young.*" She bent down. Her silver hair brushing his shoulder. "*And on Lantern Night, when your sons bring the little one up, you'll stand beside me. And help me hold your wife's hand over it.*"

---

He stood up.

Slowly. On his young legs. And turned round. And looked at her.

"*No*," said Arion Vey.

Maelis smiled.

"*You don't get to say no*," she said gently. "*Not anymore. Not with my light in you.*"

"*Your light*." He looked at her. "*Is that what it is?*"

Her smile flickered.

"*It's not yours*," said Arion. "*It's the little one's. You knelt in the deep and put your hands in it and drank while it screamed. My son heard it. My wife heard it. Kell heard it.*" He touched his chest. Where the thread was. Where the light was. "*This isn't yours. You stole it. And you poured it into me. And now I'm carrying it.*"

He looked at her.

"*And what you carry*," said Arion Vey, "*you can give back.*"

---

He didn't run.

He'd thought about running. On the litter. In the lift. A hundred times. *Drink. Stand. Grab her. Run. Down the stairs. Out of the Spire. Into the city.*

He didn't run.

He walked. Calmly. Past the Moth Queen. Across his own round room. To the tall bookcase on the far wall, crammed with old leather spines. And put his hand on a book. Third shelf. Fourth from the left. *A History of the Treaty of Scald, Volume II.*

And pulled it down.

---

The bookcase swung open.

Behind it, the little round room. Windowless. Dusty. Lavender and old paper. Portraits on every wall, floor to ceiling, frame to frame. Two hundred years of one man's face. Never older. Never younger. Staring.

Maelis was behind him. He felt her come. Fast. Glowing.

"*What's this*?" she said. And for the first time since he'd known her, her voice wasn't soft. It was *interested*. "*What's this, Warden? It's not in your books.*"

"*No*," said Arion. "*It wouldn't be.*"

He went into the little room. To the far wall. To the great dark canvas there, taller than a man, cracked and blackened with age. A portrait of himself in the robes of a High Warden two hundred years out of fashion. The oldest one. The first. Painted the year he took the Root.

Thirty-one.

He looked at it. At the young man in the painting. Dark-haired. Grey-eyed. Tired already. Trapped already. Staring out of the canvas at a room he'd never leave.

"*I always hated this one*," said Arion Vey.

And put his foot through it.

---

There was a stair behind it.

Narrow. Steep. Dark. Cut into the stone of the Spire's wall. Going down. A Warden-Architect's stair. Built by the first one, nine hundred years ago, so that the holder of the Root could leave the top of the tower without passing through any door that anyone else could see.

Arion had used it twice in two hundred years.

Once to go and meet a girl on the steps in the rain.

Once to go to Vhessa.

---

"*Seren*!"

He turned. And she was already there. In the doorway of the little room. Barefoot. With the moth-silk still on her wrists. And behind her, holding the end of the silk in both small hands, untying it, very fast, very quietly, with his tongue between his teeth—

Pim.

In his blue coat. Grey with dust. His hair everywhere. His grey-violet eyes very steady. He'd come in through the door. Through the round room. Past the Moth Queen. Past the Mothwing at the stair. Nobody had seen him. Nobody ever did.

A room with the lamp out.

The silk came off Seren's wrists. Pim dropped it. On the floor. And looked up at Arion.

"*I lied*," said Pim. "*About staying.*"

"*I know*," said Arion. "*Thank you.*"

---

Maelis was in the doorway.

Behind Seren. Behind Pim. Tall. Glowing. Her lit eyes very wide. Her long hands reaching. Not fast. Not hurried. The way you'd reach for a moth on a window.

For Seren.

And Pim turned round. In the doorway of the little room. Between the Moth Queen and the woman she was reaching for.

And was *silent*.

---

Arion felt it go out of him. The deep silence. The room in the boy. The place where nothing was. It went out of Pim and filled the doorway of the little round room like water filling a cup.

And Maelis's hands *stopped*.

In the air. An inch from Seren's back. Pressed against nothing. The way her hand had pressed against nothing in front of Antho's chest, in the deep, at the tear.

She looked down.

At the boy. Small. In a blue coat. Standing in the doorway. With his arms at his sides and his thin face set and his eyes on her.

And her face changed.

Not anger. Not surprise.

*Fear*.

"*What*," said Maelis of the Crowned, very softly, "*are you?*"

Pim didn't answer.

He never did.

---

"*GO*," said Arion.

Seren went. Past him. Into the dark of the stair. Barefoot. Fast. He heard her feet on the stone. Going down.

He looked at Pim.

"*Come on*," he said. "*Pim. Come on. Now.*"

Pim didn't move.

He stood in the doorway. With the silence pouring out of him. Holding the Moth Queen back with nothing. With a room. With a place in himself where nothing was, that he'd made out of being hurt.

He was shaking.

"*I can't*," said Pim. Without turning round. Very quietly. "*If I move, it goes. If it goes, she comes through.*"

"*Pim*—"

"*Go*," said the boy. "*She needs you to go. The lamp-lady.*" His voice was very small. "*Somebody has to stand in the door.*"

---

Arion Vey stood in the little round room behind the bookcase at the top of the Spire, surrounded by two hundred years of his own face, with the Moth Queen's stolen light in his young chest and the stair to freedom at his back and his wife's footsteps going down it.

And a thirteen-year-old boy in the doorway, shaking, holding back a Sovereign with nothing at all.

*Somebody has to stand in the door*.

He thought about Antho. In a cave. For eight years. Holding a breach shut that he didn't know was there. With his mother holding it from the other side.

He thought about Kell. In the deep. With a broken stick.

He thought about Seren. Thirteen years. With her back against a door. Singing.

He thought about himself. Two hundred years. In a crystal. In a tower. Holding the net. Alone. Because it was easier than facing what he'd done.

*I tore it alone*, he'd said to his son, on the black floor at Duskhold. *You open it with everyone.*

---

He came back across the little room.

And stood behind Pim. In the doorway. Close. With his young hands on the boy's thin shoulders.

And didn't push. And didn't pull.

He was a Warden-Architect. Two hundred and thirty-four years old. He'd stood in more doorways than anyone alive.

He knew how to stand behind someone in one.

"*I've got you*," said Arion. Very quietly. Into the boy's hair. "*I've got you. You're not holding it alone.*"

And Pim's shaking stopped.

---

Maelis looked at them.

Over the boy's head. At the young man behind him. With her lit eyes. With her long hands pressed flat against nothing.

"*Warden-Architect*," she said. "*You can't stand there forever. Neither of you can. He's a child. You're*—" She smiled. Thin. "*You're mine. You'll need me. In a day. Two. You'll feel it leaking and you'll need me.*"

"*I know*," said Arion.

"*Then step aside*."

"*No*."

"*Then what*," said Maelis, "*do you think you're going to do?*"

---

He took his hands off Pim's shoulders.

Slowly. Still standing behind him. Still in the doorway.

And cupped them.

In front of his own chest. Over his heart. Where the light was. Where the thread was. Where the little one's stolen screaming sat in him, holding back the flood. The way she'd cupped hers, to give it to him.

He'd never done this. He'd never Listened. Not properly. He'd been a Listener's husband for eight years and learned a little, nothing like his sons. He didn't know how to give a thing back.

But he'd watched.

He'd watched his wife put a lamp in his son's hands. *Not waiting. Given. To you. For you.* He'd watched Lex claim a core and hold it warm in his palm. He'd watched the little one, through his son's eyes, in a hall of glass, nine hundred years ago, feel a cupful of something pour back into it from a girl's empty hands.

*She gave me back*, it had said. *Just a little. Just what she had.*

*Nobody else ever did*.

"*It's not mine*," said Arion Vey. To the Moth Queen. To the light in his chest. To the little one, wherever it was, at the bottom of the world, pressing up against the bottom of a scar in a fallen hall.

"*It's yours*," he said. "*I'm giving it back.*"

And he *poured*.

---

It went out of him the way breath goes out after you've held it a long time.

All at once. Through his cupped hands. Out of his chest. Out of the middle of him. Dark and gold and glowing, like the night sky far from any town. Pouring between his fingers. Not leaking. *Pouring*. On purpose. Given.

Down.

Onto the floor of the little round room. Onto the stone. Into the stone. Into the Spire. Down through six floors and the lift-shaft and the foundations and the hill and the bedrock of Highcrest. Down. Into the dark under the world. Into the room under everything.

Home.

And the flood came back.

---

He felt it.

Two hundred years. Held back by the Root. Held back by the Moth's stolen light. And now nothing holding it. The dam gone. The water coming. All of it. At once.

His hands first. In front of his chest. Still cupped. Empty now. He watched them. The skin loosening. The knuckles swelling. The brown spots coming up through the young skin like stains through cloth.

He felt his back curve. His knees go. His hair—

He felt it go. White. Thin. Fine as thistledown. Falling across his forehead.

He felt his heart.

Slow. Uneven. A drum with a hole in it. Slower than before. Older than before.

Older than he'd ever been.

---

He went down.

Slowly. Onto his knees. In the doorway. Behind Pim. With his empty hands still cupped. And his white head bowed.

And the Moth Queen stood in front of them both with her hands pressed against nothing and looked at the old man on his knees.

Her face—

He'd remember it. For as long as he had left. Which wasn't long.

It was the face of someone watching a lamp go out that they'd wanted very much. That they'd lit themselves. That they'd thought was theirs.

"*Why*?" said Maelis. In a whisper. "*Why would you—you were young. You were* young. *You could have had twenty years. A hundred. Forever.*"

Arion looked up at her.

He was very tired. He was older than he'd ever been. He could feel the end of it. Close. Like a wall in the dark that you can't see but you know is there, a step ahead.

He smiled.

"*Because she liked the old one*," said Arion Vey.

---

And then hands.

Under his arms. Small ones. Strong ones. Square. Lifting.

He was being lifted. Off his knees. Out of the doorway. Back. Into the little room. Toward the stair. Toward the broken canvas and the dark beyond it.

He turned his head.

His wife.

She'd come back up. Barefoot. Up the Warden-Architect's stair. She was lifting him. With her arms under his. Dragging him. Swearing. Under her breath. Very inventively.

"*You stupid*," said Seren Vey, through her teeth. "*Stupid, stupid, stupid old man.*"

"*Seren*—"

"*Be quiet*." She had him on his feet. Barely. His arm over her shoulders. Her arm round his waist. "*Be quiet and walk. Pim. Pim, love. Come on. Back up. Slowly. Don't turn round.*"

Pim backed up. Step by step. Into the little room. With his arms at his sides and the silence pouring out of him. Toward the stair.

And the Moth Queen stood in the doorway of the little round room, between the bookcase and two hundred years of portraits, with her hands pressed against nothing, and didn't follow.

---

They went down the Warden-Architect's stair.

In the dark. Slowly. One step at a time. Seren holding him up. Pim behind them, backing down, silent. Down through the walls of the Spire. Down past six floors. Past the lift-shaft. Past the foundations.

It took a very long time.

He didn't remember most of it. He remembered her arm round his waist. Her shoulder under his arm. Her breath, hard, in the dark. Her voice, swearing, and then not swearing. Saying other things.

"*Page four*," she was saying. Somewhere near the bottom. In the dark. "*The librarian. That's my favorite. I told you.*"

"*I know*."

"*And page nine. The lanterns. Don't ever leave a light on for me, Warden.*"

"*Too late*."

"*Too late*." She was crying. He could feel it. On his shoulder. "*And page twelve. Neck like a bull. Sense of a turnip. Did I really say that?*"

"*You really said that*."

"*I don't remember*," said Seren Vey. Holding him up. In the dark. On the stair. "*I don't remember any of it. I don't remember you. I never knew your young face. I only ever knew the old one.*"

She stopped. On the stair. And held him.

"*But I think I'd like to*," she said. "*Remember. If I can. If there's time.*"

---

There was a door at the bottom.

Small. Iron. Rusted. Opening onto a lane behind the Spire. A lane he knew. A lane where, two hundred years ago, a young man in a grey coat had come out in the rain and walked round to the front steps, very casually, as if he'd just happened to be passing, and found a girl with flour on her cheek sheltering under the arch, laughing at the lanterns.

Seren pushed it open.

And outside, in the lane, in the dim grey-gold under-light of the glitter, waiting—

A dragon.

Blue. Enormous. Crackling. Crammed into the narrow lane so tightly her wings were folded flat against the walls on either side and her tail went round the corner and out of sight. With her great golden eye on the door.

Ashka.

And on her neck, with her hair burning gold and her Sunreach blade drawn and her amber eyes blazing—

"*Took your time*," said Kestrel Varn.

---

They got him onto the dragon's back.

He didn't remember that either. He remembered hands. Kestrel's. Seren's. Pim's small ones. Lifting. Pushing. Strapping. He remembered the litter, Brannoc's litter, the leather and ash-wood and oiled canvas, and lying on it, and Seren lying down beside him, and Pim at their feet.

He remembered Ashka going up.

Straight up. Out of the lane. Through the glitter. The moths burning off her in sparks. Into the blue.

And he remembered looking back.

Down. At the city. At the Spire. At the top window. The brightest one.

And in it, a figure. Tall. Grey-gold. Glowing. Standing at his window. In his room. Watching them go.

Not following.

Watching.

---

He slept on the dragon.

When he woke, it was dark. Stars. Wind. The litter rocking under him like a boat.

His wife was asleep beside him. On her side. With her head on his shoulder and her bandaged hand on his chest.

He lay very still.

He couldn't feel his feet. He couldn't feel much of anything below the waist. His heart was slow and uneven and very, very tired. He knew. He'd known on the stair. He'd known the moment he poured the light back.

*Older than before*.

*Older than I've ever been*.

He looked at the stars.

*Not long now*, he thought. *Not past Lantern Night. Seraphine said. Not past Lantern Night.*

He turned his head. Slowly. On the litter. And looked at his wife's face. Asleep. Small. Freckled. Grey-brown hair across her cheek.

*Long enough*, he thought. *Please. Just long enough.*

---

His grey book was in his coat.

He hadn't known he'd brought it. He must have. He always did. Two hundred years. He'd never once gone anywhere without one.

He got it out. Slowly. One-handed. Without waking her. And opened it. In the starlight. To the page.

*Price: known. Her household drinks from her hands.*

*So will I. Once. Long enough to climb the stairs.*

*Then I'll give it back*.

He found the pencil. In the binding. And under the last line, very small, so shaky it was almost illegible, he wrote:

*Gave it back*.

And then, under that:

*She said she'd like to remember*.

And then, under that, the old line. The one from the inside of the cover. The one he'd written two hundred times, on his birthday, at midwinter, alone.

*No change*.

He looked at it for a long time.

Then he crossed it out. One long straight stroke. From corner to corner.

And under it, smaller than anything he'd ever written in his life, he wrote:

*Everything changed*.

And put the book back in his coat.

And closed his eyes.

And slept.
