# The Seventh Circle

Ivy Moss had never seen a dragon.

She'd never seen a lot of things. She'd told Lex so, in a small fierce voice, on the morning they left Ashbourne, standing on the green beside her mother with her crutch under one arm and a bundle in the other. She'd never been further than Millbridge. She'd never seen the sea. She'd never seen a mountain up close, only the Greyspine, far off, white on the edge of the sky. She'd never slept anywhere but her own bed.

"*I've never done anything*," said Ivy Moss. "*I've just watched.*"

Her mother had looked at Lex over the girl's head. A thin tired woman named Bet, a weaver, widowed young, who'd carried her daughter on her back for the first six years of her life because the twisted foot wouldn't bear weight.

*She heard it singing*, Bet Moss had said to him the night before. Quietly. In the doorway of their cottage. *Under the Stone. She's always heard things. In the ground. In the walls. She used to sit by the well for hours and say it was talking.* She'd twisted her hands in her apron. *The Wardens said it was a fever. The old Sister said it was the Lady. I didn't know what to say.*

*It wasn't a fever*, Lex had said.

*I know*. Bet Moss had looked at him with her tired eyes. *Take her. Take her up the mountain to your brother. He's the one who listens, isn't he? Everyone says.* Her voice had cracked. *She's never been anywhere. Take her somewhere.*

---

So Ivy Moss rode up the Greyspine on the back of a storm-drake the size of a house.

Ashka had come down for them at dawn, onto the Ashbourne green, scattering geese. She'd landed beside the Stone in a great rush of wind and blue wings, and folded them, and lowered her long neck, and looked at Ivy Moss with one enormous golden eye.

Ivy Moss had looked back.

She hadn't screamed. She hadn't run. She'd stood on the green with her crutch and her bundle and looked up into the dragon's eye, with her small pale face very still. Listening.

*[...little...]* Ashka had said. In images, the way she did. Lex felt it through the Bloodbond, faint, the way he always did when the dragon spoke near him. A picture. A small thin bird with a crooked wing, sitting on a branch in the rain. *[...little broken-wing...]*

"*She's like me*," said Ivy Moss.

She'd said it out loud. To no one. Looking at the faded silver scar on Ashka's left wing, where Warden hunters had torn it, ten years ago, when she was cat-sized and Antho found her bleeding in the snow.

Ashka had lowered her great head further. All the way down. Until her chin was on the grass and her golden eye was level with the girl's face.

And Ivy Moss had put out her hand. And laid it on the dragon's nose.

Lex had never seen Ashka do that. Not for anyone. Not for him.

---

"*She's going to want to stay*," said Wren. Behind him, on the dragon's back, with her arms round his waist and her chin on his shoulder. Shouting over the wind. "*You know that. She's going to get to the top of this mountain and see the lanterns and the bats and the library and she's never going to want to go home.*"

"*Her mother knows*."

"*Does Antho know*?"

Lex thought about it.

"*He will*," he said.

Wren laughed into his ear. Then stopped. And went quiet. And he felt her arms tighten round him, and her forehead press against his back.

"*Wren*?"

"*Fine*." Her voice was muffled. "*Just the height. Just the wind. I'm fine.*"

He frowned.

Wren Ashdown had stepped off the roof of the Spire into the wind at eight hundred feet. She'd ridden the eye of a storm above the Teeth. She'd flown on her own breath through a Wellworld of Gale with lightning going off around her like fireworks.

She'd never once, in five years, said *just the height*.

---

The Lantern Keep was dark.

Not all of it. Most of it. But Lex felt it the moment he walked in through the cave mouth. Like walking into a room where someone has blown out half the candles.

He'd counted on the way down, without meaning to. A habit he'd caught from his brother. The lanterns in their niches and on their brackets, one after another, down the long passage. Gold. Gold. Gold.

Dark.

Dark. Dark. Gold. Dark.

Two hundred and six of them. Antho had told him the number in the letter, the one Quill brought to Ashbourne the morning after the moths. He hadn't understood it. Not properly. Not until he saw it.

Every dark one had dust on its glass. Grey-gold. Fine as flour. Smeared, where someone had tried to wipe it. Faint fingerprints of it, on the brass and the tin and the iron. As if something had come down the passage in the night and touched every one. Lightly. Lovingly. The way you'd touch the face of someone sleeping.

And taken the light away.

---

"*It won't come off*," said Odile.

She was standing beside him at one of the dark ones. A tall brass lantern from the Saltreach, with green glass. She had a cloth in her hand. She'd been scrubbing.

She looked better than Antho's letter had said. Thinner. Tired. But her eyes were dark and clear when she turned them on him. Mostly clear. There was something behind them still. A dimness. Like a window that's been cleaned but still has a smear in one corner.

"*We've tried soap*," said Odile. "*Sand. Vinegar. Teo's sea-water; he left a barrel. Hedda's purple thing, from the wedding. Master Arion said it might be acidic.*" She looked at the cloth. "*It wasn't.*"

"*What was it*?"

"*Nobody knows*." Odile scrubbed. "*Pim ate some. On a dare. Cass dared him. He said it tasted of plums.*"

---

His father was in the library.

He was sitting at the long table with Seraphine. Surrounded by books. Lex stopped in the doorway and looked at him, and felt something in his chest go tight and cold and quiet.

Three days.

He'd seen his father three days ago, at the wedding. In his grey coat. Playing the bone flute badly. With twenty grey hairs.

Arion Vey looked up from the table. And smiled. And his face was the face of a man of fifty-eight. White at the temples. Grey everywhere else. Lines across his forehead like the grain in old oak. And his hands, on the table, holding a page, had gone thin. Brown spots on the backs of them. Knuckles swollen.

"*Lex*," said his father. "*Wren.*" He started to get up. Slowly. One hand on the table.

"*Don't*," said Lex. "*Don't get up.*"

"*I can get up*," said Arion. Mildly. And got up. And came round the table. And took his son by both shoulders, the way Brannoc did, and looked at him.

"*It's all right*," said Arion Vey. Quietly. "*I promise you. It's all right.*"

"*You keep saying that*."

"*Because it keeps being true*." His father's grey eyes were very steady. "*I've lived two hundred and thirty-three years, Lex. I've had a long time to think about what I'd do if I ever got the chance to get old. I've had a long time to want it.*" He let go of Lex's shoulders. And touched his own white temple. Two fingers. "*I'll tell you all about it. I'll tell you everything. When I've got the numbers. I promise.*"

He looked past Lex. At the doorway. At Ivy Moss, standing in it with her crutch, staring at the shelves of books that went up into the dark of the cave roof like the walls of a canyon.

And his lined face changed. Softened.

"*Hello*," said Arion Vey. "*You must be the one who heard it singing.*"

---

Seraphine had a book open on the table.

Not a book. A box. Wooden. Old. Plain. The kind of box you'd keep tea in, or letters. With a goat-hair rope round it and a smell of woodsmoke and salt that went straight into Lex like a knife, because it was the smell of a hut by a still pool in the Pale Wastes. The smell of an eternal fire.

"*Malachi's*," said Seraphine.

She didn't need to say it.

"*Correspondence brought it*," she said. "*Last spring. After the burial. You remember. We left everything in the hut. His bowl and his blanket and his straw hat. We didn't think it was ours to take.*" She touched the goat-hair rope. "*And then three weeks later Correspondence turned up at the cave mouth with this box tied to his back. On his own. He'd walked from the Wastes. Nine hundred miles.*"

"*He'd eaten half the rope*," said Antho. From the other end of the table. Where he sat with his long legs stretched out and his hands folded on his stomach and his eyes half shut. "*And a corner of the lid. And, we think, a letter.*"

"*I put it on a shelf*," said Seraphine. "*I didn't open it. I couldn't. It was his. And he was—*" She stopped. "*I wasn't ready.*"

She looked down at the box.

"*I opened it last night*," she said.

---

There wasn't much inside.

A wooden spoon. Carved. Worn smooth. A child's spoon. A twist of dried herbs, gone to dust. A clay whistle in the shape of a bird, with a crack down its back. A smooth white stone with a hole through it, on a string.

And papers.

A thin bundle. Tied with grass. Old. Not paper. Vellum. Bark. Something that might have been a dried leaf, pressed flat. Covered in writing. Wellscript, mostly. The Founders' glyphs. Faded. Crabbed. In an old man's hand, and then, further down the bundle, in a younger one.

"*His*," said Seraphine. "*All of them. Notes. Letters he never sent. Things he wrote down to remember.*" She touched them. Very lightly. "*Some of them are nine hundred years old. Some are from last year.*"

She drew one out of the bundle. Carefully. With the tips of her ink-stained fingers.

It was a circle.

---

Lex had seen it before.

Everyone had. It was on the first page of the Listeners' Codex. The grey book from Qel'Varran, that Seraphine had copied by firelight in the Wastes. That three scribes at the Ashbourne Forge copied every day now, over and over, to send to every new forge and every new school in Caldor.

Six circles. Arranged in a ring. Each with a glyph inside it. *Flame. Stone. Tide. Gale. Radiance. Shade.*

And in the center of the ring, a seventh circle.

Empty.

Lex had asked about it once. Five years ago. In the Wastes. Sitting by the eternal fire with Malachi, with his hands blistered from talking to flame. *What's the one in the middle*? he'd said. *The empty one.*

And Malachi had looked at him with his shifting eyes. For a long moment. And then laughed. And said *that's where you sit, smith. In the middle. Listening.*

Lex had thought it was a joke.

He'd thought everything Malachi said was a joke. Most of it was.

---

The circle on the vellum was the same. Six in a ring. One in the center.

But the one in the center wasn't empty.

It was black.

Filled in. Solid. With ink so old it had gone brown at the edges, laid on thick and heavy, as if whoever drew it had pressed the pen down hard and gone round and round and round. And inside the black, scratched into it with the point of something sharp, so that the pale vellum showed through, a glyph.

Lex couldn't read Wellscript. Not anymore. He'd read it with the Root, once, when the gold text bled through the silver and every Founders' glyph made sense to him like his own name. He'd given that away at the Heart.

He didn't need to read it.

He knew the shape.

He'd seen it on the black door. He'd seen it on a gold anvil at the bottom of the world. He'd seen it carved into the floor of a forge on top of a white mountain, under a little hammer, in the place where a starving thing had been held for nine hundred years.

"*Hollow*," said Seraphine.

---

Under the circle, in the corner of the vellum, there was a line of writing.

Not Wellscript. The common tongue. The old form, with the long *s* and the looped *th*. In a small cramped hand. A young hand. Malachi's, Seraphine said. From before. From before the Weaving. When he was the youngest of seven, and Aurelian was the eldest, and the world hadn't been sundered yet.

She read it out loud.

"*Hollow*," read Seraphine Ilvane. "*The seventh. The one in the middle. The one they drank. The one we caged.*"

She stopped.

"*There's more*," she said. "*Further down. Different ink. Later. Shakier.*"

She read it.

"*Forgive us*."

---

Nobody said anything for a long time.

Lex sat at the long table in the library of the Lantern Keep with his hands flat on the wood and looked at the black circle on the vellum. Six around one. Flame and Stone and Tide and Gale and Radiance and Shade. And in the middle, where Malachi had told him he'd sit, listening, a dark thing. Filled in. Scratched.

*The one they drank*.

*The one we caged*.

*Forgive us*.

"*It's a Well*," said Lex. Slowly. "*Isn't it. The hunger. The Unmaker. The thing at the bottom of the world.*" He looked up. "*It was a Well.*"

"*I think so*." Seraphine's voice was very quiet. "*I've been up all night. Reading everything. Every Founders' record. Every bronze foil from Qel'Varran. Every page of the Codex. Every scrap Malachi ever wrote.*" She took off her spectacles. Her eyes were red. "*It's never said. Not anywhere. Not in plain words. But it's all through it. Like a watermark. Once you know to look for it.*"

She put her spectacles back on. And pulled a stack of her own notes toward her. Covered in her neat cramped hand.

"*There used to be seven*," said Seraphine. "*Seven Wells. Seven Listeners, at the first. Seven children of the House of Vey, in Aurelian's generation, and Malachi the seventh child.*" She turned a page. "*Six of them are the ones we know. Things. Forces. Fire and stone and water and wind and light and dark.*"

"*And the seventh*?" said Wren.

"*The seventh isn't a thing*," said Seraphine. "*It's the space for a thing.*"

---

She tried to explain it.

She was very good at explaining things. She'd explained the Tithe to a room full of kings and dukes in a way that made them understand it in their stomachs. She'd explained Wellscript to Lex in a cave by firelight. She'd explained to the Queen of Caldor the difference between justice and cruelty, and the Queen had remembered it.

She was having trouble with this.

"*Every Well is a—a kind of moving*," said Seraphine. "*Fire burns. Stone holds. Water flows. Wind moves. Light shows. Dark rests.*" She made a shape in the air with her hands. A ring. "*And all of them need somewhere to do it. Fire needs something to burn. Water needs a bed to run in. Wind needs room to move.*"

She put her hands together. Cupped. Empty.

"*The Hollow is the room*," said Seraphine. "*The—the empty place. The space before. The quiet at the start of a song. The mould before the metal.*" She looked at Lex. "*You'd know that one better than me.*"

Lex did.

He was thinking of a sand-mould. In the casting-pit at the back of the Ashbourne Forge. Packed tight round a wooden pattern, and the pattern lifted out, carefully, carefully, and the shape it left behind in the sand. The empty shape. Perfect. Waiting.

*You can't pour metal into nothing*, Brannoc had said. When he was twelve. *You have to make the nothing first. That's the hard part. Anybody can melt iron. Making the right kind of empty—that's the craft.*

"*Every new thing*," said Lex slowly, "*needs a hollow to be made in.*"

Seraphine looked at him.

"*Yes*," she said. Very softly. "*That's it. That's exactly it.*"

---

"*A spring*," said Wren. Suddenly.

They all looked at her.

"*In the forge yard*." Wren was frowning. "*The warm one. That came up out of the cinders overnight.*" She looked at Lex. "*And the well at Coldbrook. And the chimney at the mill that grew another chimney. And Old Mag's apple tree. And the extra mile on the Millbridge road.*" She was sitting up very straight. "*The oddities. Everyone's been laughing about them all winter.*"

"*New things*," said Seraphine. Slowly.

"*New things*," said Wren. "*Coming up. Out of nowhere. Like—like there's room for them now. Like something's made room.*"

"*Old Mag said that*," said Lex. "*She said it feels like the ground's rising. Making room for something underneath.*"

There was a silence.

And then Ivy Moss, who was sitting at the very end of the long table with her crutch across her knees, very small, saying nothing, the way she'd said nothing since she arrived, looked up.

"*It's coming up*," said Ivy Moss.

---

Everyone turned to look at her.

She went red. All the way up to her pale hair. She looked down at the table. But she didn't stop.

"*I can hear it*," she said. In a small, stubborn voice. "*Under the ground. I've always been able to. Ever since I was little.*" She swallowed. "*It used to be far away. Very far. Like someone singing at the other end of the Vale, and you can only just hear it on the wind. Mam said I was making it up. The Sister said it was the Lady.*"

She looked at Antho. At the end of the table. With his eyes open now, very grey and very still, fixed on her.

"*It's not far now*," said Ivy Moss. "*It's getting closer. All the time. Every day. It's coming* up.*"* She hesitated. "*And it's big. Bigger than anything. Bigger than the mountain.*"

"*Is it hungry*?" said Antho. Very quietly.

Ivy Moss thought about it. Seriously. The way children think about questions grown-ups ask them as if the answer matters.

"*No*," she said. "*It's—*" She frowned. "*It's like when you're little. And you've been sick. A long time. And you get better. And you get up for the first time and you walk to the window and you look out, and everything's so bright and big.*" She looked at her hands. "*It's like that. It's frightened. And it's never seen anything. And it wants to see everything.*"

She was quiet.

"*And it doesn't know where to go*," said Ivy Moss.

---

Antho got up.

He came down the long table. Slowly. And sat down on the bench beside the girl. Not close. A little way off. The way he'd sat beside Pim, at the cave mouth, in the evenings, when Pim didn't talk.

He took the black pebble out of his coat. And put it on the table in front of her.

"*Do you hear this*?" said Antho.

Ivy Moss looked at the pebble.

Then she leaned forward. And put her ear right down on the table. Next to it. With her pale hair spilling over the wood.

She lay there for a long time. With her eyes shut.

"*It's singing*," she said. "*The lantern one. The one Mam sings when she's carding.*" A pause. "*It's flat.*"

"*Yes*," said Antho. "*It learned it from my mother. She sings it flat.*"

Ivy Moss opened her eyes. Still with her ear on the table.

"*It's saying something*," she said. "*Under the singing. Over and over.*" She frowned. "*It's saying* room.*"*

---

They argued.

Of course they did. They'd been arguing at that table for five years. About Wellscript and Wardens and wars and the best way to boil an egg. It was what they did. It was how they thought.

"*It ate worlds*," said Wren. "*I'm sorry. I know. I know it's sorry. I know it's little and frightened and Ivy can hear it singing.*" She was standing up. Pacing. The way she did when the wind was restless in her. "*But it ate* worlds. *The Forbidden Lands. The drowned city. Everything at the bottom of the deep. Whole worlds full of people, gone. And it ate a quarter of everyone at the Heart, a year and a half ago, and pulled more than half of them through the Tithe, and nearly ate Lex's whole* life.*"* She stopped. "*I was there. I held his hand while it ate his memories. One by one. I watched them go out of his face.*"

The library was quiet.

"*I'm not saying kill it*," said Wren. "*I don't know how you'd kill a Well. I'm saying*—" She looked at Lex. "*I'm saying be careful. That's all. Be careful what you open the door to. Just because something's sorry doesn't mean it can't hurt you.*"

"*It was starving*," said Antho.

"*I know*."

"*For nine hundred years*." His brother's voice was very quiet. "*Somebody drank it dry. Somebody knelt down at a pool and drank and drank until there was nothing left, and what was left was a hole. A hole that wanted to be filled. That's all the hunger ever was.*" He looked at the pebble. "*It didn't choose to be the Unmaker. Someone made it into that.*"

"*That doesn't make it not dangerous*."

"*No*," Antho agreed. "*It doesn't. Neither does Lex. Neither do I. Neither do you, Wren, with the wind in you. Neither does any Well.*" He looked up at her. "*Fire burned the Sunreach once. The Sunwell burned its own people to kill a hungry thing. We still say good morning to it.*"

Wren didn't answer.

---

"*Malachi sat with it*," said Lex.

Everyone looked at him.

He hadn't meant to say it. It had come up out of him. Out of the place where he kept the things that hurt.

"*At the Heart*," said Lex. "*When it unfolded. When Aurelian let go and I struck the seam and it came apart into a—a small lonely dark. That's what it was. That's what it looked like. A small dark thing. Cold. Shaking.*" He swallowed. "*And Malachi cupped it in his hands. And said, I'm not going to cage you. I'm just going to sit with you.*"

He looked at the vellum. At the black circle. At the line in the corner. *Forgive us*.

"*And he went down with it*," said Lex. "*Into the deep. Singing. And he never came up.*"

The library was very quiet.

"*He didn't die to cage it*," said Lex. "*He died to keep it company. So it wouldn't be alone. So it could—*" He stopped. He didn't know the word.

"*Heal*," said Seraphine. Very softly.

"*Heal*." Lex looked round the table. At his brother. At his wife. At his father. At Seraphine and Odile and Ivy Moss. "*He was the seventh child. He sat in the middle and listened. That's what he told me. Five years ago. That's where you sit, smith. In the middle. Listening.*"

He put his hand on the black pebble. It was warm.

"*I'm not going to undo that*," said Lex Vey. "*Whatever it is. Whatever it did. I'm not going to kill the thing he died to keep company. And I'm not going to put it back in a cage.*"

He looked at Wren.

"*But I'll be careful*," he said. "*I promise. I'll be careful.*"

Wren looked at him for a long time.

Then she sat down. Heavily. On the bench. And put her face in her hands. And said, through her fingers, "*Idiot.*"

But she reached out, without looking, and found his hand on the table. And held it.

---

His father hadn't said anything.

He'd sat through the whole argument with his hands folded on the table and his white head bowed. Listening. The way he listened to the Queen's council, Lysandra said. Saying nothing until everyone else had said everything, and then saying the one thing nobody had thought of.

He lifted his head.

"*They*," said Arion Vey.

Everyone looked at him.

"*The one they drank*," said Arion. He reached out and touched the corner of the vellum. With one thin brown-spotted finger. Very gently. "*Not we. They. And then: the one* we *caged.*" He looked up. "*Two different groups of people. The ones who drank. And the ones who caged.*"

Seraphine had gone very still.

"*Malachi was a Listener*," said Arion. "*He was one of the ones who caged. The House of Vey. The Founders. Aurelian and his brothers and sisters, who wove the net.*" He looked at the black circle. "*So who drank?*"

Nobody answered.

"*There's a story*," said Arion. Slowly. "*In the Spire. An old one. Not a record. A story. The kind of thing junior Wardens tell each other at night in the dormitory, to frighten the new ones.*" He was quiet for a moment. Remembering. "*I heard it when I was nineteen. My first year. Nearly two hundred years before you were born.*"

"*What story*?" said Antho.

"*The Nine at the Pool*," said his father.

---

He told it.

It wasn't much. He said so. He didn't remember it well. He'd been nineteen and frightened and trying very hard not to look frightened in front of the older cadets. It was the kind of story that changed every time someone told it.

*Before the net*, said Arion. *Before the Sundering. When the sorcerer-kings ruled. Nine of them. Nine crowns. Nine kingdoms. Each one stronger than the last. And they found a pool.*

*A black pool. In a hall of glass. And whoever drank from it would never die.*

*So they drank.*

*And they drank. And drank. Until the pool was empty. And they lived forever. And the world broke.*

*And the Founders came, and wove the net, and threw the Nine out of the world. Past the edge. Into the grey. And locked the door behind them.*

*And they're still there.*

"*And then the older cadets would blow out the candle*," said Arion. "*And whisper*—" He stopped.

"*What*?" said Wren.

His father was looking at the window. The narrow slit window high in the library wall, cut through the rock of the mountain, that looked out over the valley. It was dusk. The sky was going grey-blue. The first star was out.

"*They'd whisper*," said Arion Vey, "*if you leave a light on, they'll see it. Past the edge. Through the grey. And they'll come for it. Because that's all they want. That's all they've ever wanted.*"

He was quiet.

"*Never leave a light on*," said his father. "*That was the end of the story. That was how the cadets frightened each other. In a city where every window has a lantern in it for the Lady.*" He shook his head. "*Never leave a light on. Or the Nine will come.*"

---

The library was very quiet.

Lex looked at the window. At the first star. At the dark coming down over the Greyspine.

He thought about a door of black glass. And three lamps in a niche beside it. Burning. Every night. For a year and a half. For someone who hadn't come home.

He thought about two hundred and six lanterns in the passage outside, with dust on their glass.

He thought about a moth. Drifting down the passage on the night of his wedding. From lantern to lantern. Brushing each one with its wing.

*Never leave a light on*.

*Don't ever let a moth in*.

"*Mam said that*," said Antho. Very quietly. He was looking at the window too. "*Through the door. Three nights ago. Don't let them in. Whatever they say. Whoever comes.*" He swallowed. "*She sounded—I've never heard her sound like that. Not ever. Not even when she was angry. She sounded like she knew.*"

"*Knew what*?" said Lex.

His brother didn't answer.

---

The raven came an hour later.

Not Quill. A Keeper's raven. Grey-black, with a brass ring on its leg and the Keepers' sigil, a Stone with a hand on it, stamped into the ring. It came in out of the dark through the cave mouth and down the long passage, past the lit lanterns and the dark ones, and landed on the long table in the library in a scatter of feathers and frost, and held out its leg.

It was from Roole.

Antho opened it. Read it. Read it again. And handed it to Lex without a word.

It was short. Roole's letters were always short. He wrote like he talked. Like a man reading out a list of charges.

*Sundered Lands outposts. Four of five gone dark. Last report from Greyfall, three nights ago. Lanterns failing. All of them. Dust on the glass.*

*Survivors from Greyfall came in this morning. Six. Of forty.*

*They say the moths came out of the west at dusk. Out of the Veil. Thousands.*

*They say there's a woman walking with them.*

*Tall. Grey. Beautiful. They say her eyes are lit. Like windows.*

*They say she smiled at them. And asked, very politely, the way to Highcrest.*

*Sending Fenn and forty to the Veil's edge. Asking Dame Elowen to go with them.*

*Asking Wren Ashdown to track.*

*Come if you can.*

*— Roole*

---

Lex read it twice.

Then he put it down on the table. On top of Malachi's vellum. On top of the black circle with the glyph scratched into it.

And looked at his wife.

Wren was already standing up.

She had her bow in her hand. He hadn't seen her pick it up. Her face was white. And set. And her green eyes were very bright and very hard, the way they'd been on the roof of the Spire, waiting for him, a month of dawns, not knowing if he was alive.

"*Don't*," said Wren Ashdown. Very quietly. "*Don't you dare.*"

He didn't.

He looked at her for a long moment. At the bow in her hand. At the black iron ring on her finger. At the faint grey under her eyes that hadn't been there a week ago, that he'd noticed on the dragon, and hadn't said anything about.

*Just the height*, she'd said. *Just the wind.*

"*Be careful*," said Lex.

"*I'm always careful*."

"*You stepped off the Spire*."

"*Carefully*," said Wren. And came round the table. And kissed him. Quick. Hard. The way she had at the edge of the round dark on the Ashbourne green. "*I'll be back. A week. Two. I'll track her and I'll watch her and I'll come back and tell you what she is.*"

She stopped in the doorway.

"*And Hammer*," she said. Without turning round. "*Fix the door.*"

And she was gone.

---

Lex sat at the long table for a long time after.

With the raven's letter under his hand. And Malachi's vellum under that. And the black pebble beside it, humming.

*[Lex*,] said Ember. Very quietly. In his hearth.

*Mm*.

*[I remember the story*.] A pause. *[The Nine at the Pool. He told it to me. Arion. When I was him. When he was nineteen and frightened in the dormitory.]* Another pause. Longer. *[I didn't remember it until he said it. It was in a partition. Locked. Since the seal.]*

*What else is in it*?

*[I don't know yet*.] The coal in his hearth was very quiet. *[But there's something. Under the story. Something he heard later. Much later. Two hundred years later. From someone who wasn't frightened at all.]*

*Who*?

Ember didn't answer for a long time.

*[Your mother*,] it said at last. *[On the steps of the Spire. In the rain. The first night they met.]*

Lex went very still.

*[He told her the story*,] said Ember. *[As a joke. Because she'd laughed at the lanterns. All the lanterns in all the windows of Highcrest. And he said, never leave a light on, or the Nine will come.]*

*And what did she say*?

The coal glowed. Very faintly. In the dark of his hearth.

*[She didn't laugh*,] said Ember. *[That's all I've got. She stopped laughing. And looked at him. And said*—]

It stopped.

*[It's locked*,] said Ember. *[The rest of it. I can feel it. Like a word on the tip of a tongue.]* A long pause. *[But I think, Lex. I think she said something about moths.]*
