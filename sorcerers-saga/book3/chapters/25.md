# Relighting

The sally port smelled of rats and old water, and Wren was sick in it twice.

"*Sorry*," she said. The second time. Wiping her mouth on her sleeve. Crouched in the dark of the tunnel under the north wall of Highcrest, with Pim's small hand on her back and Elowen's lantern shuttered and the rock dripping somewhere ahead.

"*Don't be sorry*," said Elowen Dunmore. "*Be quiet.*"

"*I'm being quiet*."

"*You're being sick very loudly*."

"*It's a very loud baby*," said Wren.

Ilse laughed. Behind her. In the dark. Then stopped. Then laughed again, smothered, into her brother's shoulder.

Wren got up. Her knees were shaking. Her stomach was a boat in a gale. Four months, Old Mag said. Near enough. *The sickness should be passing*, Old Mag said. *Any day now. Ginger.*

Wren had eaten so much ginger she could taste it in her sleep.

"*Go on*," she said. "*I'm fine.*"

They went on.

---

The tunnel came out under a grating in a cellar under the castle kitchens. Elowen knew the way. Elowen knew every way. She'd grown up in this castle, she said, in a house with four aunts, running wild in the corridors while her father was away being a knight.

They went up through the kitchens.

Empty. Pots on the stove, cold. Bread on the table, half-kneaded, gone grey and hard. A cat asleep on a sack of flour that lifted its head as they passed and looked at them with green eyes, and then put its head down again.

Up through the servants' stair. Out through a side door into a lane.

Into the city.

---

Wren had been in Highcrest a hundred times. She'd lived here for three years, after her mother got sick, in the narrow house on Fennel Street. She'd guarded Arion here for a month of dawns. She'd danced in a ballroom here with no shoes on.

She'd never seen it like this.

It was noon. Summer. Hot. And dark.

Not night-dark. *Under*-dark. The way it's dark under a tree, or under a bridge. Grey. Dim. Shadowed. The glitter overhead was so thick the sun was only a pale smudge, like a coin seen through dirty water. The light that came down through it was grey-gold and flat and wrong. It made everything look like a drawing. Like the Unfinished in Lex's Delve. Lines, and no color.

And it was *quiet*.

Highcrest was never quiet. Highcrest was bells and carts and hawkers and children and dogs and the clang of the smithies on Anvil Row and the shouting from the market and the gulls that came up the river from the sea. Highcrest at noon in summer was the loudest place Wren had ever been.

It was silent.

---

They were in the streets.

Everywhere. On every corner. In every doorway. Standing. Sitting on steps. Leaning on walls. People. Ordinary people. Bakers and porters and washerwomen and clerks and old men and children.

All facing the same way.

North. Uphill. Toward the top of the city. Toward the Spire. Toward the gold light that came out of every window of it, soft and warm, through the glitter, like a lamp seen through fog.

Their eyes were dim.

They weren't moving. Not really. Swaying. Very slightly. All together. Like grass in a wind that wasn't there. And on every one of them, somewhere, dust. A smudge. Grey-gold. The size of a moth's wing.

And in their hands, lanterns.

Not all of them. Many. Dark. With dust on the glass. Held up. Toward the Spire. The way you'd hold up a cup to be filled. The way you'd offer a gift.

---

"*Gods*," whispered Ivo.

He was standing at the end of the lane. Very white. Looking out into the street. At the rows and rows of them. At a woman Wren half-recognized, the woman who sold cheese in the Tuesday market, standing in the gutter with a dark lantern held up and her face slack and peaceful.

"*Don't look at them*," said Elowen. Very low. "*Look at the ground. Look at your feet. Walk.*"

"*They're not doing anything*," said Ilse. Her voice was shaking. "*They're just—standing there.*"

"*Walk*."

"*Ivo*," said Wren.

He looked at her.

"*Make us look like them*," she said. "*Dim. Grey. Swaying. With lanterns. Facing the Spire.*" She swallowed. "*Make us look like we belong.*"

Ivo stared at her for a moment.

Then he nodded. And put his hand out. And Ilse took it without looking.

And Wren felt it go over her. The illusion. Cool. Light. Like a veil. Like a cobweb on her face. She looked down at herself. Grey. Flat. Her green coat gone the color of ash. Her hands holding a lantern that wasn't there. And when she looked at Elowen, the knight was gone, and in her place was a stooped grey woman with a dark lamp held up toward the north, swaying.

Pim didn't need it.

Pim walked out into the street in his blue coat with the brass buttons buttoned wrong, and the moths drifting overhead parted round him like water round a stone. And didn't come back. And the dimmed people in the street didn't turn. Didn't look. Didn't see him at all.

A room with the lamp out.

---

Fennel Street was in the Lower Town. Near the river. A narrow street of narrow houses, tall and thin and leaning toward each other overhead, so that the sky was a strip. Wren had walked down it a thousand times. Carrying water. Carrying bread. Carrying her mother's medicine from Master Fenwick's on Physic Lane.

She knew every stone.

She walked down it now under Ivo's illusion, grey and swaying, with a lantern that wasn't there, and her heart going like a drum.

Past the cobbler's. Dim. The cobbler standing in his doorway with an awl in one hand and a dark lantern in the other. Past the widow Abel's. Dim. The widow sitting on her step with a cat in her lap, both of them facing north. Past the house with the blue shutters where the Fenwicks' apprentice lived. Dim. Dim. Dim.

Third from the end.

Green door.

---

Her mother was on the step.

Of course she was. She was always on the step. She'd sat on that step every afternoon for three years, in the sun, when she was too sick to do anything else. With Tam on one side and Robbie on the other. Waiting for Wren to come home.

She was sitting on it now.

In her grey shawl. With her stick across her knees. Thin. Small. Grey-threaded. Facing north. Up the hill. Toward the gold light.

With a lantern in her lap.

Wren knew that lantern. Tin. Old. Dented. With a crack in the horn pane that her father had mended with a strip of birch-bark the winter before he died. It had hung in the window of the grey-plank house in Thistledown, with the horseshoe over the door upside down so the luck wouldn't run out. It had hung in the window of this narrow house on Fennel Street since they came.

For her father, the first winter. For Wren, ever since.

Every time she went away. Every time she went up a mountain with a smith who walked like a bear. Every time she went to the Glass Desert, or the Wellworlds, or the bottom of the world.

Her mother lit it. And put it in the window. And sat on the step beside it. And waited.

It was dark.

There was dust on the birch-bark.

And her mother's eyes, open, turned up toward the Spire, were dim.

---

Wren stopped.

In the middle of Fennel Street. Under Ivo's illusion. Grey. Swaying. With a lantern that wasn't there.

And couldn't move.

"*Wren*," said Elowen. Very low. Beside her. "*Wren. Go.*"

She couldn't.

She stood in the street and looked at her mother on the step. And all she could think of was the first time Lex had seen her mother. Five years ago. Coming up this street with a sack of tools on his shoulder and his hood up. And her mother on the step, coughing, laughing, crying, all at once. *So. You must be the Hammer.*

And the second time. The autumn after Duskhold. When her mother stood up. On her own. For the first time in three years. Off this step. And Wren had flown up the street on the wind.

*Pathfinder*, she'd said. *Rare. Level forty-one. No cap. Mam. No cap.*

Her mother had cried. And laughed. And coughed. All at once.

She wasn't doing anything now.

She was just sitting there. With her stick. With her dark lantern. With her face slack and peaceful and turned toward the light at the top of the hill.

As if Wren had never come home at all.

---

Someone grabbed her leg.

She nearly screamed. She nearly loosed the arrow she wasn't holding. She looked down.

A boy. Nine. Ten. Thin. Filthy. With a shock of red hair and freckles and a gap in his front teeth where the new ones were coming in crooked. Holding on to her leg with both arms. Through the illusion. As if the illusion wasn't there.

Crying.

"*Wren*," said Robbie Ashdown. Into her knee. "*Wren. Wren. I knew it was you. I knew. You walk wrong. You always walk wrong. Even grey.*"

She dropped to her knees.

The illusion broke. She felt it go. Like a cobweb tearing. She didn't care. She was on her knees in Fennel Street with her little brother in her arms, holding him, and he was holding her, and he was so *thin*.

"*Robbie*." Into his hair. His filthy red hair. "*Robbie. Robbie. Are you all right? Are you hurt? Did they*—"

"*I hid*." He was shaking. "*Under the bed. When the moths came. Mam said hide. Mam said hide and put the candle out and don't come out.*" He gulped. "*And then she didn't come and get me. And I came out. And she was on the step. And Tam was on the step. And they wouldn't*—"

He couldn't finish.

Wren looked over his head.

Tam. Twelve. Sitting on the step beside their mother. On her other side. Where he always sat. With his knees up. Facing north.

Dim.

---

"*I've been feeding them*," said Robbie. Into her shoulder. "*I've been putting bread in their mouths. And water. Like you do for a baby bird. They eat it. But they don't look at me.*" His voice cracked. "*Wren, they don't look at me. Nobody looks at me. Nobody in the whole street.*"

"*I know*."

"*Four days*." He was crying so hard he could barely talk. "*I've been feeding them four days. I didn't know what to do. I didn't know where you were. I thought you were dead. I thought everyone was dead except me.*"

"*I'm here*," said Wren Ashdown. "*I'm here. I came. I'm here.*"

She held him. And looked at her mother over his head.

And didn't know what to do either.

---

Pim did.

Or tried. He came and knelt on the step. In front of her mother. Small and solemn in his blue coat. And cupped his hands. Round her mother's hands. Round the dark lantern in her lap. Not touching. An inch away.

And was silent.

Wren felt it. The room. The space. Pim's quiet, going round her mother's hands like the walls of a cup.

Her mother blinked.

Once. Slowly.

And her eyes moved. A little. Off the Spire. Down. Toward Pim.

And then back. Up. Toward the light.

Pim kept his hands there. For a long time. Wren watched him. Watched his thin face go grey with effort. Watched his hands start to shake.

Her mother didn't come back.

She didn't get worse. The dimness didn't spread. Something in her face eased a little, the way Odile's had eased, the way Kell's had eased in the drowned city. Room. A space. Somewhere for the warm thing to come back *into*.

But it didn't come.

"*It's not enough*," said Pim. Very quietly. Not looking up. "*I can make the room. I can't put anything in it.*"

He took his hands away.

He looked at Wren.

"*It's not mine*," said Pim. "*The warm thing. I don't know what it is. I don't know her.*" His grey-violet eyes were very steady. "*You do.*"

---

Wren sat on the step.

Beside her mother. On the other side from Tam. Where Robbie usually sat. With Robbie in her lap, too big to be in her lap, being in her lap anyway.

She looked at her mother's face. Thin. Grey-threaded. Turned north. Peaceful.

*What did she take*?

She'd thought about it all the way from Ashbourne. On the wind. With Pim on her back. She'd thought, *I'll know. When I see her. I'll know.*

She didn't know.

Her mother had a thousand warm things. She'd loved her husband. She'd loved Thistledown. She'd loved the grey-plank house with the horseshoe upside down. She'd loved Tam and Robbie, every day, even when they were awful. She'd loved Wren.

Which one?

*The warmest*, Odile had said. *It's the warmest thing I have. And it took it.*

*The only warm thing*, Kell had said. *I only had the one.*

Her mother had had a thousand.

*Which one was the warmest*?

---

She looked at the lantern.

In her mother's lap. Tin. Dented. With a crack in the horn pane mended with birch-bark. Dark. With dust on it.

She'd always thought it was for her father. The first winter. That her mother lit it for him, because he'd died in the autumn, of a fever he'd caught in the mines at Coldwater. Because you lit a lantern for the dead on Lantern Night. Everyone did.

But it hadn't been Lantern Night.

She thought back. Hard. The way you track an animal across bare rock, by the bent lichen.

The first winter. In the grey-plank house in Thistledown. Da dead in the autumn. Mam sick. Coughing. The cough that would last ten years. Tam a baby. Robbie not born yet. No money. No food. Snow.

And Wren, nine years old, taking Da's old bow off the wall. The one she couldn't draw. And going out into the snow.

Because if she didn't, her mother would die.

---

She'd been gone three days.

She'd never told anyone that. Not Lex. Not anyone. She'd been gone three days and two nights, in the snow, in the woods above Thistledown, with a bow she couldn't draw and a knife and a lump of cheese. She'd slept in a hollow under a fallen pine. She'd nearly frozen. She'd cried a lot.

And on the third day, she'd found a hare. In a snare she'd set herself, the way Da had shown her once, when she was six. A big brown winter hare. Dead. Frozen stiff.

She'd carried it home.

Through the snow. In the dark. With her feet so cold she couldn't feel them and her hands so cold she couldn't feel the hare. Down the mountain. Into Thistledown. Up the street.

And in the window of the grey-plank house, with the horseshoe over the door upside down so the luck wouldn't run out—

A light.

---

*That was the first time*, thought Wren.

She was sitting on the step on Fennel Street with her brother in her lap and her mother beside her, dim, facing north. And she was nine years old, standing in the snow outside a grey-plank house in Thistledown with a frozen hare in her arms, looking at a light in the window.

*That was the first time she lit it*.

*Not for Da. For me*.

*She'd lit it the first night I was gone. And sat up with it. Three nights. Coughing. With Tam in her arms. Sick. Starving. Sitting by the window with a lantern lit, waiting for her nine-year-old to come home out of the snow.*

*And I came*.

*I came up the street with a hare*.

---

She remembered her mother's face.

In the door. When she pushed it open. When she came in out of the snow with the hare in her arms and her feet like blocks of ice.

Her mother had been sitting by the window. With the lantern. With Tam asleep in her lap. Grey. Thin. Coughing.

And she'd looked up.

And seen Wren.

And her face—

Wren had never seen a face do that. Not before. Not since. Not even Lex's, on the roof of the Spire, when she said yes. It had *broken*. All at once. Every part of it. Like ice breaking on a river in spring. And under it, something so bright and so warm and so enormous that nine-year-old Wren Ashdown had stood in the doorway with snow on her boots and a frozen hare in her arms and been, for the first time in three days, not cold at all.

*You came back*, her mother had said. *Oh, Wrennie. You came back.*

---

*That's it*, thought Wren.

*That's the warmest thing*.

*Not Da. Not Thistledown. Not the house. Not Tam or Robbie.*

*Me. Coming back. Through the snow. To the light she left on.*

She looked at the dark lantern in her mother's lap.

*She's lit it every time since*, thought Wren. *Every time I went away. For that. For the feeling. Waiting at the window for me to come back up the street.*

*And Maelis took it*.

*She took the coming-back*.

---

She didn't know what to do.

She knew what the warm thing was. She knew what had been taken. She could feel it, the shape of the hole where it had been, the way you can feel the shape of a missing tooth with your tongue.

But she didn't know how to give it back.

Lex had read Kell's name out of the Ledger. And then said what Dorran said. *I've got you, High Warden. I've carried heavier.* And Kell had come back a little.

*A little*.

Not all the way.

She looked at her mother. At the dark lantern. At the dust on the birch-bark.

*I came back*, thought Wren. *I came back with a hare.*

*You lit the lamp. And I came back.*

*It's the wrong way round*.

---

She got up.

She put Robbie off her lap. Gently. Onto the step. And stood up. And turned round. And looked at her mother. Sitting on the step. Facing north. Toward the gold light at the top of the hill. With the dark lantern in her lap. Waiting.

*Still waiting*, thought Wren. *She's still waiting. That's what the moth left her. The waiting. Without the coming-back.*

*She's been waiting for me for four days. On the step. With the lantern. Facing the wrong way.*

*Toward the wrong light*.

She bent down.

And took the lantern out of her mother's lap.

---

Her mother's hands didn't fight her. They let go. Slack. Empty. They lay in her lap, palm up, the way Kell's had lain on the steps of the grey house in the drowned city. The way a pair of hands lies when it's let go of something and there's nothing left to hold.

Wren held the lantern.

Tin. Dented. Cold. Dark.

She didn't have a light. Nobody did. Not in Highcrest. Not under the glitter. The moths would come for any light that was lit.

She looked at Elowen.

"*Fire*," said Wren.

Elowen looked at her. Then at the glitter overhead. Then back. "*Wren. The moths*—"

"*I know*. *Give me fire.*"

Elowen didn't have fire. She had a tinderbox. In her belt-pouch. Flint and steel and a twist of char-cloth. She took it out. Slowly. With her hard grey eyes on Wren's face.

And handed it over.

---

Wren opened the lantern.

She knelt on the step. In front of her mother. With the dented tin lantern on the stone between them. And struck the flint.

Once. Twice. Sparks. On the char-cloth. A glow. She blew on it. Gently. The way Lex said good morning to the fire. She held the glow to the wick.

The wick caught.

A little flame. Small. Gold. In the tin lantern with the cracked horn pane mended with birch-bark. On the step of a narrow house on Fennel Street, in the dark city, under the glitter.

And overhead, the glitter *turned*.

She felt it. Everyone felt it. A shift. A ripple. Through the whole great grey-gold ceiling of moths over Highcrest. Turning. Toward the little flame. The only light in the Lower Town. The only light in the city except the gold one at the top of the hill.

They started to come down.

---

Wren didn't look up.

She closed the lantern. Very carefully. The little latch, the one that always stuck, that Da had bent the winter before he died. And picked it up.

And didn't set it in the window.

She put it in her mother's hands.

Into her lap. Into her slack empty upturned palms. And closed her mother's fingers round the handle, one by one, the way you'd close a child's fingers round a coin.

Not a light left on. Not waiting. Not for someone who wasn't home.

*Given*.

"*Mam*," said Wren.

---

Her mother didn't move.

Overhead, the moths were coming. A slow grey-gold slide, down out of the ceiling, like snow sliding off a roof. Toward the lantern. Toward the little flame in her mother's hands.

"*Mam*," said Wren again. Kneeling. With her hands over her mother's hands. Over the lantern. "*Maren Ashdown. Of Thistledown. And Fennel Street. It's me. It's Wren.*"

Nothing.

"*I came back*," said Wren. "*Through the snow. With a hare. I was nine. Do you remember? I was gone three days and you lit the lamp and sat by the window, and I came up the street in the dark with a frozen hare, and I opened the door.*" Her voice was shaking. "*And you looked up.*"

The moths were close. She could hear them. Like rain on a roof. Like sand on stone.

"*And your face*," said Wren. "*Mam. Your face. I've never seen anything like it. I never will again. It broke. All of it. Like ice on the river.*" She was crying. She didn't stop. "*And you said, you came back. Oh, Wrennie. You came back.*"

The first moth reached the lantern.

It settled on the horn pane. On the birch-bark. Grey-gold. Wings spread. And pressed against the little flame inside. And *drank*.

And got nothing.

Wren felt it. Felt the moth press and pull and find nothing. Felt it flutter. Confused. And try again. And find nothing.

*Because it's given*, she thought. *It's not waiting. It's hers. I gave it to her. It's already someone's.*

"*I came back*," said Wren Ashdown. To her mother. Kneeling on the step. With her hands round her mother's hands round the lantern. "*Mam. I came back. I'm here. I'm home. Look at me.*"

---

Her mother blinked.

Once. Slowly. Like someone waking from a very long sleep.

Her eyes moved. Off the Spire. Off the gold light at the top of the hill. Down.

To the lantern. In her hands. Lit. Small. Gold.

And then up.

To Wren's face.

And they weren't dim.

---

It didn't happen all at once. Not like the lanterns in the Hollowmaw, when Seren walked past. Not like a lamp being lit.

It happened the way ice breaks on a river in spring.

A crack. Somewhere deep. A sound you feel more than hear. And then another. And then the whole surface going at once, breaking, heaving, groaning, and the dark water underneath showing through, moving, *alive*—

Her mother's face broke.

All of it. Every part of it. At once.

And under it, so bright and so warm and so enormous that Wren Ashdown, twenty-five, four months gone, kneeling on a step in a dark city under a sky full of moths, was nine years old again, and not cold at all—

"*Wrennie*," said Maren Ashdown.

Her voice was a croak. Four days unused.

"*Oh, Wrennie. You came back.*"

---

Wren couldn't speak.

She put her face down on her mother's knees. On the lantern. On their hands. And cried. The way she'd cried on this step five years ago, when she came home from the Greyspine with a smith. The way she never cried. The way Lex said she never cried, and was always wrong.

Her mother's hand came up. Off the lantern. Slowly. Shaking. And went into her hair. Into her fox-red braid. And held on.

"*What happened*?" said Maren. Faintly. "*Wren. What happened? I was—I was waiting. On the step. For you. And then there was a light. Up the hill. And it was so warm. And I thought it was you.*" Her hand tightened. "*I thought it was you coming home.*"

"*It wasn't me*."

"*No*." Her mother looked down at her. With her own eyes. Green. Wren's eyes. Clear. Wet. "*No. This is you. That was*—" She stopped. Frowned. "*I don't know what that was.*"

---

Elowen was beside them.

Kneeling. With Oathkeeper across her knees. And her hard grey face doing something Wren had never seen it do.

"*Wren*," she said. Very quietly. "*Wren. Look up.*"

Wren looked up.

The moths were still coming. Down out of the glitter. Slowly. A great grey-gold slide. Toward the lantern in her mother's hands.

And every one that reached it—

Fell.

Not burned. Not crumpled. Not like Lex's black heat.

They reached the lantern. And pressed against it. And drank. And got nothing. And pressed again. And got nothing.

And then they just—*stopped*. Hung in the air. Wings still. Confused. Starving. In front of a light they could see and couldn't drink.

And fell. Slowly. Like leaves. Onto the step. Onto the street. Into the gutter.

And didn't get up.

---

"*It's given*," said Wren. Hoarse. Staring at them. "*It's hers. I gave it to her. They can't drink it.*" She looked at her mother. "*And she was waiting for me. And I came. So it's not waiting anymore.*"

"*It's kept*," said Pim.

Everyone looked at him.

He was standing on the step behind them. Small. Solemn. Looking at the lantern with his grey-violet eyes.

"*The promise*," said Pim. "*She lit it for you to come back. And you came back. So it's kept.*" He looked at the moths, falling. "*Like Master Antho's six. In the drowned city. Like the lamp-lady's.*"

He looked at Wren.

"*They can only drink the waiting*," said Pim. "*When the waiting's done, there's nothing left to drink.*"

---

Tam took longer.

He was twelve. Wren didn't know him as well. She'd been gone so much. Five years of mountains and Wellworlds and the bottom of the world. He'd been seven when she left. He was twelve now. A stranger. Thin and gangly and freckled, with a voice that was starting to crack.

She didn't know what his warm thing was.

Robbie did.

"*The kite*," said Robbie. Kneeling on the step beside his brother. Fierce. With his gap-toothed face set. "*It's the kite. Last spring. You made it, Tam. Out of Mam's old petticoat and some willow. And it wouldn't fly. And you tried for a week. And it wouldn't. And then Wren came home.*"

Wren went still.

"*And she looked at it*," said Robbie. "*And she said, let me try. And she held it up. And the wind came. Her wind. And it went up. So high. Over the river. Over the walls. And you were holding the string.*" His voice cracked. "*And you laughed, Tam. You laughed like a donkey. You laughed so loud the widow Abel came out.*"

He took the dark lantern Wren held out to him. The second one. From the window. Another tin one. Lit it from their mother's. Closed it. Put it in his brother's slack hands.

"*Tam*," said Robbie Ashdown. Nine. Gap-toothed. Furious. "*Tam, you idiot. The kite flew. Wren made it fly. You were holding the string. Look at me.*"

And Tam blinked.

---

They stayed on the step for a long time.

All four of them. Wren and her mother and her two brothers. Squashed together. On a step made for one. With two lit lanterns in their laps and moths falling round them like autumn leaves.

Elowen kept watch. And Ilse and Ivo, who had dropped their illusions and were sitting in the gutter holding hands and crying, both of them, and not ashamed. And Pim, who stood at the bottom of the step with his hands in his pockets, watching the moths fall.

Up and down Fennel Street, the dimmed stood in their doorways. Facing north. Swaying. With their dark lanterns held up.

The cobbler. The widow Abel and her cat. The Fenwicks' apprentice.

Not one of them turned.

---

"*It has to be someone who loves them*," said Wren.

Later. When her mother was leaning against her shoulder, drinking water from a cup Ilse had found, and Tam was sitting with his head on his knees, shaking, and Robbie was holding his hand.

"*It has to be someone who knows*," she said. "*What the warm thing is. What was taken. I couldn't do it for the widow Abel. I don't know her warm thing. Pim couldn't do it for Mam.*" She looked at the street. At the dimmed. At the hundreds of them, the thousands, all over Highcrest. "*It has to be family. Or a friend. Someone who listened.*"

"*And a lamp*," said Elowen.

"*And a lamp*. *Given. Not waiting. Put in their hands.*" Wren looked at the tin lantern in her mother's lap. "*And their name. And the warm thing, said out loud. Given back.*"

"*Every one of them*," said Ilse. From the gutter. Very quietly. "*Every single person in this city. Someone has to come and find them. And sit with them. And remember.*"

"*Yes*."

"*Thousands*."

"*Yes*," said Wren Ashdown. "*Thousands.*"

She looked up the hill. At the Spire. At the gold light in every window.

"*Then we'll need thousands*," she said.

---

And then the moths stopped falling.

Wren felt it. A change. In the air. In the glitter overhead. The great slow turning of it, over the whole of Highcrest, going still. All at once. Like a flock of starlings in the moment before it turns.

She looked up.

Something was coming down out of it.

Big. Heavy. Broad-shouldered. With wings. Grey-gold. Ragged. Strong. Beating. Carrying a man the size of a bear down out of the glitter over Fennel Street as easily as a hawk.

A miner's pick in one hand.

His eyes were lit.

---

Silas Hask came down in the middle of Fennel Street.

Lightly. On his toes. Like a moth on a window. His great wings folding. The dimmed in the doorways on either side of him didn't turn. Didn't look. Didn't see.

He looked at the step.

At the green door. At the four people squashed onto the step. At the two lit lanterns. At the moths lying dead in the street round them like leaves.

At Wren.

He looked at her for a long time. With his gold eyes. At her face. At her hand. Flat. On her stomach.

"*She said you'd come*," said Silas Hask.

Elowen was on her feet. Oathkeeper up. Between him and the step.

Hask didn't look at her.

"*She said*," he said, "*the tracker will come for her mother. They always come for their mothers. She said, Silas, when the tracker comes, bring her to me.*"

He looked at the lanterns. At the dead moths.

"*She didn't say anything about this*," said Hask. Slowly. "*She didn't say anything about people waking up.*"

He looked back at Wren.

"*How did you do that*?" he said.

---

Wren stood up.

She didn't know why. Elowen was in front of her. Pim was beside her. She should have stayed sitting. She should have kept her hand on her mother's and her eyes on the ground and let the knight with the oath-sword deal with the winged man with the pick.

She stood up.

And looked at him. Past Elowen's shoulder. At his heavy weathered face. At his lit eyes. At his chest, rising and falling, deep and easy, with no rattle in it at all.

"*I gave it back*," said Wren. "*What she took. I knew what it was. I gave it back.*"

"*What was it*?"

"*Me*." She looked at her mother. "*Coming home. Through the snow. When I was nine.*"

Hask didn't say anything.

"*Everyone she's taken*," said Wren. "*Every one. Somebody knows. What was taken. Somebody who loves them.*" She looked at him. "*Somebody could give it back.*"

Silas Hask looked at her for a long time.

And Wren saw something in his face. Behind the gold. Deep. Far back. Like a man standing a long way back from a window in a dark room.

"*Who's yours*?" she said.

He went very still.

"*Your warm thing*," said Wren. "*Before. Before her. Before the wings and the sip and the breathing. Your sister. Your da. Your mam. Who knew what it was? Who'd have come for you?*"

Hask didn't answer.

He looked at the lantern in Maren Ashdown's lap. Lit. Given. With dead moths round it.

"*Nobody*," said Silas Hask. "*They're all dead.*"

---

He lifted his pick.

Not at her. At the sky. At the glitter. He held it up. Over his head. Like a man holding up a lamp.

And the glitter answered.

It came down. All of it. The whole great grey-gold ceiling over Fennel Street. Not slowly now. Not drifting. *Falling*. Like a wall. Like a wave breaking.

"*She wants you*," said Hask. Very quietly. Under the roar of wings. "*Tracker. Not your mam. Not your brothers. You.*" He looked at her hand. On her stomach. "*And what you're carrying.*"

"*Wren*!" Elowen was shouting. "*Wren, GO*—"

Wren didn't go.

She stood on her mother's step on Fennel Street, with the moths coming down like a wall and Silas Hask in front of her with his pick held up and his gold eyes on her stomach.

And opened her mouth.

And *whistled*.

---

Two notes. High. Sharp. A bird call. The one she'd used in the Greyspine five years ago, on the way up to the Hollowmaw, to tell a clumsy smith *stop. Danger. Come.*

And she put the wind in it.

All of it. Everything she had. Gale, Commanding. The First Breath, the last of it, that had lived in her lungs since the eye of the storm. She put it in two notes and sent them up. Out of Fennel Street. Out of the Lower Town. Out of the glitter. Over the walls of Highcrest. North. Over the downs. Over the Vale.

To Ashbourne.

To a forge. To a long hall with twenty anvils. To a man sitting by the great hearth with a map of the Blightmarch and a cup of cold tea, thinking about sand.

*Stop. Danger. Come*.

*Hammer. Come*.

---

The moths hit.

And Silas Hask, standing in the middle of Fennel Street with his pick held up like a lamp, looked at the tracker on the step. Whistling. With the wind pouring out of her. With her hair coming loose and her green eyes blazing and one hand flat on her stomach.

And for one moment, one breath, before the wall of moths came down between them—

He lowered the pick.

Just a little. An inch.

As if he'd heard a bird call from somewhere very far off. From a long time ago. From a grey town in a grey valley, where a little girl with grey on her lips used to whistle at the window when he came home from the pits.

*Stop. Danger. Come*.

And then the moths came down.

And Wren couldn't see him anymore.
