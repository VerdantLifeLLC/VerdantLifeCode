# Borrowed Years

Arion Vey had kept records all his life.

It was the Warden in him. Or the scholar. Or simply the man. Two hundred and thirty-three years of ledgers. Of lists. Of small neat columns of figures in small neat handwriting, in books bound in grey cloth that filled three shelves of the secret room in the Spire, behind the portraits. Weather. Harvests. The price of bread in Highcrest in every year of nine kings. The number of children Kindled at every Stone in Caldor, and their classes, and their Ceilings. The day each of his sons was born, and the hour, and how long Seren had laboured, and what she'd said, which was not repeatable.

He'd kept a record of himself, too. Not much of one. There'd never been much to record. Two hundred years of the same face in the same mirror. *No change*, he'd written, once a year, on his birthday, at midwinter. *No change. No change. No change.* Two hundred lines of it. Like a man writing the same word over and over until it stops meaning anything.

He'd started a new book the morning after the wedding.

Grey cloth. Like the others. And on the first page, in his small neat hand: *Hairs, white: 20. Lines, eyes: 4 each side. Lines, mouth: 2. Grip (right): firm. Grip (left): firm. Stairs to the cave mouth: 211 steps, no rest.*

He had filled forty pages since.

---

"*Ten years*," said Arion. "*In one night. I think.*"

He was sitting in the big chair at the head of the long table in the library of the Lantern Keep. Wrapped in a blanket. With his grey book open in front of him and his spectacles on his nose. He hadn't needed spectacles a week ago. He did now. Seraphine had given him a spare pair, and he'd put them on, and the world had come back into focus, and he'd laughed out loud. *So this is what it's like*, he'd said. *Everyone else has been doing this for nine hundred years.*

His sons were sitting on either side of the table. Lex on his left. Antho on his right. With their wives beside them. And Odile at the end, and Pim under the table with the cat, and Ivy Moss perched on a stool by the shelves with her crutch across her knees, very quiet, listening.

Nobody had said anything since he started.

"*I can't be certain*," said Arion. "*Not about the night. I was asleep for most of it, and then I was on the floor.*" He turned a page. "*But the record's quite clear. On the evening before last I was sixty-one, or near enough. Grey hair. Lines here and here. Grip firm. Stairs, two hundred and eleven, one rest.*" He looked at the next page. "*Yesterday morning I was seventy. Seventy-one, perhaps. White hair. Grip weak. Stairs*—" He smiled. Slightly. "*I didn't try the stairs. Lex carried me.*"

"*Father*—"

"*Let me finish*." Arion held up one thin hand. It shook. He looked at it with interest. "*Please. I've been waiting two hundred years to give this report. I'd like to give it properly.*"

Lex shut his mouth.

---

He laid it out for them.

The way he'd lay out a report for the Queen's council. Calmly. In order. Without hurrying and without leaving anything out. He'd learned that, in two hundred years. That the worst news was easiest to hear when it was given plainly. When the person giving it wasn't afraid of it.

He wasn't afraid of it. That was the strange thing. He kept checking. The way you check a tooth with your tongue to see if it still hurts. He wasn't afraid.

*The Root held me*, he told them. *From the day I took it. Thirty-one years old, at the Great Stone, in my first year as a Warden-Architect. It held me the way a dam holds a river. It needed me. A Root has to have a holder, and Aurelian had already chosen his vessel, and it wasn't me, and so the net held on to me instead. As long as it needed me.*

*Two hundred and two years*.

*And then my son reforged the net. And gave the Root away. And there was nothing to hold me anymore.*

He looked at the grey book.

*For the first year and a half*, he said, *it came slowly. A grey hair. A line. A little stiffness in the morning. I was very happy. I thought, I'll have twenty years. Thirty. I'll grow old like an ordinary man.*

*And then, after the wedding, it began to come faster*.

He turned the pages. Showing them. Columns of numbers. Small and neat. Going down the pages like water down a slope.

*A year a month*, he said. *Then a year a fortnight. Then a year a week. And now*—

He put his finger on the last page.

*Ten years in a night*.

---

"*It's a curve*," said Seraphine.

She was leaning forward. Over the book. With her own spectacles pushed up on her head and her eyes moving down the columns the way they moved down a page of Wellscript. Fast. Hungry. Her face had gone very pale.

"*It's not a line*," she said. "*It's a curve. It's getting steeper.*"

"*Yes*," said Arion.

"*Like water*," said Seraphine. "*When a dam breaks. It doesn't come out all at once. It comes out a little, at first. Through the crack. And the crack gets wider. And more comes through. And the crack gets wider still, and*—"

She stopped.

"*And then it all comes*," said Arion gently. "*Yes. That's what I think too.*"

"*How long*?" said Antho.

---

The library was very quiet.

Arion looked at his elder son. Sitting on his right. With his long black hair tied back and his clear grey eyes, the same grey as Arion's own, fixed on him. Steady. Not looking away.

*He always looks*, thought Arion. *Even when it hurts. He's always looked. Since he was nine and sat on the kitchen table asking me why the stars didn't fall.*

"*I don't know exactly*," he said. "*I'm not good enough at mathematics. Seraphine is better.*"

"*Arion*—" said Seraphine.

"*I'd rather you said it*," said Arion. "*If you can. I'd rather hear it from you than work it out alone in the night.*"

Seraphine looked at him. For a long moment. Behind her spectacles her eyes were bright and wet.

Then she looked down at the book. And pulled it toward her. And took the pencil from behind her ear. And began, very quietly, to write.

Nobody said anything while she did it. Nobody moved. Even Pim, under the table, was still.

It took her four minutes.

She put the pencil down.

"*If it goes on like this*," said Seraphine Ilvane. Very steadily. Not looking up. "*If the curve doesn't change. If nothing changes.*" She swallowed. "*A season. Perhaps two. Into the autumn.*"

She looked up.

"*Not past Lantern Night*," she said.

---

Lex stood up.

His chair went over behind him. He didn't notice. He stood at the long table with his big scarred hands flat on the wood and his face gone white and then red and then white again, and the air around him began to shake, and the lamps on the table flared, and the pages of the grey book stirred.

"*No*," said Lex.

"*Lex*."

"*No*. *There's something*—" His hands were pressing into the table. Hard. The oak under them was beginning to smoke. "*There's something we can do. There has to be. There's always something. We went to the bottom of the world. We reforged the net. We*—"

"*We gave away the Root*," said Arion.

His son stopped.

"*You gave it away*," said Arion. Gently. "*At the Heart. I was there. I watched you. And Antho gave his. And I gave mine.*" He looked up at Lex. "*It was the right thing to do. It was the best thing anyone has done in nine hundred years. I'd do it again. I'd do it a thousand times.*"

"*If I hadn't*—"

"*If you hadn't*," said his father, "*I'd be forty-five forever. In a tower. Holding a net that held me. Watching everyone I love grow old and die.*" He held out his hand. Thin. Shaking. Brown-spotted. "*Lex. Look at me. Look at my hand.*"

Lex looked.

"*I'm old*," said Arion Vey. "*I've wanted this for two hundred years. I used to stand in front of the mirror in the Spire every morning and pray for a grey hair. One. Just one. So I'd know I was a man and not a—a fixture. A piece of furniture in the net.*" He smiled. "*I've got hundreds now. I've got so many I've stopped counting. Do you know what that's like? For me? To stop counting something?*"

His son's face crumpled.

He came round the table. And knelt. On the floor of the library. Beside his father's chair. And put his head down on his father's knees, on the blanket, the way he hadn't done since he was four. The night before the seal. With a horseshoe nail in his fist.

Arion put his thin old hand on his son's dark hair.

And held it there.

---

He went back to Highcrest three days later.

They argued, of course. All of them. Even Pim, who came out from under the table and stood in front of the door of the library with his arms spread, small and fierce and silent, until Arion bent down, slowly, and talked to him for a long time in a low voice, and Pim went away and sat on the stairs and cried.

*You can barely walk*, said Lex.

*I can walk. I can't do stairs. Highcrest is flat*.

*Highcrest is built on a hill*.

*The castle has a lift. Lysandra put it in for her grandfather*.

*The Queen*—

*The Queen*, said Arion, *has a woman in a house on the Crown Terrace telling her council that the thing my wife is bringing home is the Unmaker. And half her council is listening. And the woman knows things about my wife's family that my wife never told me. And she asked my son how he knew his mother's name.* He looked at Lex. *I'm the Queen's advisor. That's my work. It's the only work I have left that I'm any good at. I'm going to do it.*

He looked round at them.

*I'm old*, he said. *I'm not finished*.

---

Vharuun carried him.

The storm-mother came down onto the slope of the Hollowmaw at dawn, vast and blue and gentle, and lowered her head to the ground, and Lex lifted his father into a chair that Brannoc had made, the night before, with leather straps and a back and a little roof of oiled canvas against the rain, and strapped it to the dragon's neck between two great ridges of blue scale.

*It's a throne*, said Antho, looking at it.

*It's a cart-seat*, said Brannoc. Who had come up the mountain on purpose. Grumbling. *With straps.*

*It's lovely*, said Arion. And meant it.

Brannoc looked at him. The old smith. Sixty-three. Bald and soot-bearded and enormous. Who had punched him in the face, five years ago, *for Seren*, and then sat up all night drinking with him and given him her letter.

He was older than Arion now. Looked it. Just about.

Not for long.

"*Look after yourself*," said Brannoc Holt. Gruffly.

"*You too*."

"*I'm going to outlive you*," said Brannoc. "*Did you think of that? Two hundred and thirty-three years, and I'm going to outlive you. A smith from Ashbourne.*" His beard twitched. "*Seren'd laugh.*"

"*Seren would laugh*," Arion agreed.

Brannoc looked at him for a moment longer. Then he reached up, with his enormous scarred hand, and took Arion's thin one. And held it. Hard. The way he'd held it at the bottom of a bottle in the back room of the Crooked Kettle.

"*Don't you dare*," said Brannoc. Very low. "*Don't you dare go before she gets home. You hear me? Not after all this. You hold on.*"

Arion didn't say anything.

He couldn't.

---

Highcrest was dark.

He'd known it would be. He'd been in the Hall of Lanterns when Lysandra gave the order. But knowing a thing and seeing it were different.

He came in at dusk. On Vharuun's neck, in Brannoc's chair, over the walls, with the city spread out below him going grey and then blue and then black. The city he'd lived in for two hundred years. Through nine kings and one Queen.

He'd never once seen it dark.

Not even in the long night under the lid, when the Key was turned and the world went black. Even then, Highcrest had lit candles. Thousands of them. In every window. *Against* the dark.

Now there was nothing. Black roofs. Black windows. The watch-fires on the walls. A few candles, here and there, behind thick curtains, the way people used to keep lights in the old days, in the plague years, so no one would see.

And on the north side, high up, on the Crown Terrace, a tall grey house. With Keepers on its doors.

And in one of its upper windows, very faint, a light.

Not a lamp. Not a candle.

Two lights. Small. Gold. Close together.

Looking out.

---

Lysandra came to see him that night.

In his rooms in the Spire. The ones he'd lived in for two hundred years. On the top floor, under the roof, with the long windows looking north over the city, and the shelves of grey books, and the narrow bed, and the secret door behind the third portrait that led to the room where all the other portraits were.

She came alone. Without Elowen. In a plain cloak with the hood up. She came in without knocking, the way she had since she was nine and her grandfather had sent her to him for lessons in history and she'd sat on his floor and argued with him about the Treaty of Scald.

She stopped in the doorway.

And looked at him.

He was in the chair by the window. With a blanket on his knees and his grey book in his lap and his spectacles on. He'd been writing. *Stairs, Spire, ninety-four steps, lift to the sixth floor, four rests. Hands, cold. Eyes, tired.*

He looked up.

"*Majesty*," said Arion.

The Queen of Caldor stood in the doorway of his room with her hood up and her hand on the frame and looked at her old teacher. For a long time.

Then she came across the room. And knelt down in front of his chair. In her cloak. On the floor. The way she'd sat on his floor when she was nine.

"*Oh, Arion*," said Lysandra. Very softly.

"*I know*."

"*Oh, my dear*."

"*It's all right*," said Arion. "*I promise you. It's all right.*"

The Queen put her face in her hands.

---

She told him about the council.

Later. When she'd stopped crying, and he'd made her tea, slowly, with his shaking hands, and she'd sat in the other chair by the window with her feet tucked under her and the cup in both hands, the way she'd sat at nine.

"*Harlowe's been to see her*," said Lysandra. "*Three times. And Cressa Moore. And half the old families on the north side. The ones who lost their Sanctions.*" She stared into her tea. "*She has a salon. In the dark. Every evening. They go and sit in her drawing room with the shutters closed and no candles, and she talks to them.*"

"*About what*?"

"*About what happens*," said the Queen, "*when people rise without limits.*"

Arion was quiet.

"*She's very good*," said Lysandra. "*She doesn't argue. She doesn't push. She just tells them stories. About the old kingdoms. Before the net. About what it was like when anyone could rise as high as they wanted.*" She turned the cup. "*About sorcerer-kings who were shepherds' sons. About wars between them that burned whole countries. About the Sundering.*" She looked up. "*About how the strong always rise. And how they always end up on top of the weak. And how nobody ever stops them.*"

"*And then*?"

"*And then she says, of course, it's different now. You're wiser than we were. You'll manage it better.*" The Queen's mouth twisted. "*And everyone goes home and lies awake.*"

She set the cup down.

"*There's a man called Corwin Hask*," she said. "*From Coldwater. A miner. Level seventy-four. In two years.*"

"*I know*. *Lex met him.*"

"*He's not the only one*. *There are eleven Uncapped over level sixty now. Fifty over fifty. Hundreds over forty.*" Lysandra looked out of the dark window. At the dark city. "*Arion, there are farm-boys in the Vale who are stronger than my knights. There are fishwives in the Saltreach who could take Elowen. There's a girl in the Sunreach, Ishara wrote, who Kindled last autumn as a [Sand-Dancer] and is level forty-one and has already killed two men in duels.*" She swallowed. "*And it's been a year and a half.*"

"*What do the people say*?"

"*The people*," said the Queen of Caldor, "*are delighted. The people are rising. The people have never been happier in their lives.*" She looked at him. "*It's the rest of us who are lying awake.*"

---

"*She wants me to accept her offer*," said Lysandra. "*The council does. Half of it. They want me to let her help us bind the thing that's coming up.*"

"*And you*?"

"*I want to know what you think*." She looked at him. "*I always want to know what you think. I've wanted to know what you think since I was nine years old and you told me the Treaty of Scald was a crime and I asked you why nobody had ever said so and you said* because nobody ever asked.*"*

Arion smiled.

"*I think she's lying*," he said.

"*About what*?"

"*I don't know yet*." He looked at the grey book in his lap. "*That's the trouble. Everything she's said is true. I've checked. Seraphine's checked. Ember's checked; Lex says it has the whole of my memory up to the seal, and it went through all of it, and couldn't find a single false word.*" He shook his head. "*She hasn't lied. Not once. But she's lying.*"

"*How can you tell*?"

He thought about it.

"*Because she sat on the floor*," said Arion Vey.

The Queen stared at him.

"*She sat on the floor*," said Arion. "*In the Hall of Lanterns. In front of the whole council. Lower than everyone. So nobody would forget who was asking.*" He looked at the dark window. "*I've been at court for two hundred years, Lysandra. Through nine kings. I've watched a great many people ask for things.*" He was quiet. "*The ones who sit on the floor are always the ones who want the throne.*"

---

He went to see her the next evening.

Alone. Lysandra didn't like it. Elowen liked it even less. Lex, when he heard, sent a raven from Ashbourne so furious that Quill had refused to carry it and a Keeper's bird had to be found.

He went anyway.

He was the Queen's advisor. He was two hundred and thirty-three years old. He'd spoken to every king and lord and High Warden in Caldor for two centuries, and to a hollow thing wearing his ancestor's face at the bottom of the world, and to a man who'd betrayed him at Fort Caldris and then died to save him at the Rift.

He wanted to look at her.

---

The tall grey house on the Crown Terrace had been Varric Sorn's.

Arion had been inside it once before. A year and a half ago. On the night of the masks. When the Ceiling Party tried to take the throne and Isaura Kell tore Lampshades off people with her Inspection and a man in ash-grey put a Severance chain round Arion's own throat.

The ballroom was shut up now. The masks were gone. The Keepers at the door, four of them, in grey, looked at him with something close to horror as he came up the steps on a stick, slowly, one at a time, and one of them, a young woman, tried to take his arm.

"*Thank you*," said Arion. "*No.*"

He went in.

---

She was in the drawing room.

It was dark. Of course it was. The shutters were closed. There were no candles. No lamps. No fire in the grate. Only the faint grey of the evening coming in round the edges of the shutters, and the dark furniture, and the dark pictures on the walls.

And two lights.

Small. Gold. Close together. In a high-backed chair by the cold fireplace.

"*Warden-Architect*," said Maelis. "*How lovely. Do sit down.*"

---

He sat down.

There was a chair opposite hers. He found it in the dark with his stick, and lowered himself into it, slowly, an inch at a time, the way his knees let him. It took a long time. She didn't help. She didn't look away. She watched him do it with her lit eyes, the way you'd watch something precious being unwrapped.

"*You're older*," she said. When he was settled. "*Than in the Hall. A great deal older.*"

"*Yes*."

"*How much*?"

"*Ten years*. *In one night.*"

She was quiet for a moment.

"*Oh*," said Maelis. Softly. "*Oh, I'm sorry. I truly am.*" And she sounded it. She sounded so sorry that something in his chest went warm and loose for a moment before he caught it and made it stop. "*The dam's broken. Hasn't it. The Root's gone, and the dam's broken, and it's all coming at once.*"

"*Yes*."

"*How long*?"

"*A season*. *Perhaps two.*"

She didn't say anything for a while.

He couldn't see her face. Only the eyes. Two soft gold lights in the dark. Like windows. Like lamps. Like the light in the window of a cottage at the end of a long lane in the Vale, on a winter night, coming home.

He didn't look away from them.

He'd decided that on the steps. He was too old to look at her hands. Too old and too tired and too curious. He'd been looking at people's faces for two hundred years. He wanted to look at hers.

---

"*You know who I am*," said Maelis.

"*I know what you said in the Hall*."

"*That's who I am*." The lights tilted. "*I didn't lie.*"

"*I know*," said Arion. "*That's what's so interesting about you.*"

She laughed.

It was a lovely laugh. Low and warm and surprised. It filled the dark room. And something about it, some note in it, made the hair stand up on the back of his old neck.

"*Oh, I like you*," said Maelis. "*I thought I might. I've been listening to you for months, you know. Through my moths. Every night. At the bottom of the mountain.*" Her voice went soft. "*Good evening, Seren. Good evening. Good evening.*"

He didn't move.

"*She doesn't remember you*," said Maelis. Gently. "*Does she.*"

---

He'd known she'd say it. He'd known on the steps. He'd known in the Hall, when she leaned down to Lex and said *your father says it every night*. He'd prepared for it. He'd lain awake in his narrow bed in the Spire last night and thought about exactly what he'd say.

He didn't say any of it.

He sat in the dark and looked at the two gold lights and didn't say anything at all.

"*She paid you away*," said Maelis. "*To the old man in the boat. To cross his river. Every memory. Every day. The steps of your Spire, in the rain.*" Her voice was very kind. "*And then she didn't even cross. She turned round. For your son. And went back into the dark. And now she's coming home.*"

A pause.

"*To a stranger*," said Maelis. "*An old man. With white hair and a stick and a voice she half-remembers from somewhere, and doesn't know why.*"

---

"*What do you want*?" said Arion.

His voice came out steady. He was proud of that, afterward. Steady and dry and quiet. A Warden-Architect's voice. A voice for council chambers.

"*To help*," said Maelis.

"*Help*."

"*You're running out of time*." The gold lights didn't waver. "*A season. Perhaps two. She's still in the deep. She's walking, Warden-Architect. With something the size of a mountain following her. She won't be home before midsummer.*" A pause. "*You'll be eighty by then. Ninety. You won't be able to walk down the passage to the door to meet her.*"

He didn't answer.

"*I can give you time*," said Maelis.

---

The room was very quiet.

"*A little light*," said Maelis. Softly. "*From my own hands. The way I gave it to my household, nine hundred years ago. A sip.*" The gold lights moved. Closer. She was leaning forward in the dark. "*Not enough to make you one of them. Not enough to make you mine. Just enough to hold the water back. To mend the dam a little.*"

"*How much*?"

"*Ten years*. *Twenty. As much as you like.*" A pause. "*Enough to be young when she comes home.*"

He couldn't breathe.

"*She doesn't remember an old man*," said Maelis. Very gently. "*She remembers a voice. Through the Root, in the dark. Three years of nights. A young man's voice. That's what she fell in love with, the second time. Not this.*" The lights didn't move. "*Let her come home to that. Let her walk up out of the dark and through the door and see a young man standing there. With dark hair. And grey eyes. And a voice she knows.*"

A long pause.

"*Let her fall in love with you a third time*," said the Moth Queen. "*Properly. With her eyes open.*"

---

He sat in the dark for a long time.

He thought about a woman on the steps of the Spire. In the rain. Small and fierce and freckled. With her hair tied back with a strip of blue cloth and flour on her cheek, because she'd come up to Highcrest to sell bread at the market and been caught in the downpour, and she'd stood on the steps of the Spire under the arch to keep dry and laughed at the lanterns. All the lanterns, in all the windows of Highcrest, glowing gold in the rain. She'd laughed and said, *are you all afraid of the dark up here?*

And he'd told her the story of the Nine. As a joke.

And she'd stopped laughing.

He couldn't remember what she'd said next. He'd tried. For years. He'd sat in the secret room behind the portraits and closed his eyes and tried to hear it. It was the one thing he couldn't remember, of that whole night. Her face. Her hair. The rain. The lanterns. Every word of the story he told her.

And then a blank. Like a page torn out.

And then she was laughing again, and saying *buy me a cup of something hot, Warden, I'm soaked*.

---

"*Why*?" said Arion.

The gold lights tilted.

"*Why would you do that*?" said Arion. "*For me. What do you want for it?*"

"*Nothing*."

"*Everyone pays*. *That's the river's law. That's every law.*"

Maelis was quiet.

"*I'd like to be your friend*," she said at last. Softly. "*That's all. I've been very lonely, Warden-Architect. For a very long time. In a grey country with no sun. With my brothers and sisters asleep and my people coming apart into dust one by one.*" Her voice was very low. "*And now I've come home. And I find a man who's lived two hundred years and is dying of it. A man who's been young too long and old too short. Who knows what it is to watch everyone you love grow old while you stay the same.*"

The lights moved closer.

"*I know what that is*," said Maelis. "*I've known it for nine hundred years.*"

"*And when she comes home*," said Arion. "*My wife. With the thing that's following her.*"

"*Then you'll be young*," said Maelis. "*And strong. And you'll be able to stand beside her. And help her.*" A pause. "*And when she understands what she's bringing home, you'll be able to help her let it go.*"

---

He didn't answer for a long time.

He sat in the dark drawing room of Varric Sorn's house, with his stick across his knees and his old heart beating slow and hard and uneven, the way it did now, and looked into the two gold lights.

*Let her fall in love with you a third time*.

*With her eyes open*.

He thought about his grey book. Forty pages. Columns of figures going down like water down a slope.

He thought about the clay lamp. Leaning.

He thought about Brannoc, on the slope of the Hollowmaw, holding his hand. *Don't you dare go before she gets home*.

He thought about the face in the mirror. White hair. Lines like plough-furrows. An old man. *His* old man. The one he'd waited two hundred years to see.

"*I'll think about it*," said Arion Vey.

---

He went home slowly.

Down the steps. On his stick. One at a time. Past the four Keepers, who watched him with white faces. Along the Crown Terrace in the dark. Down the long slope to the Spire. Into the lift. Up. Along the corridor. Into his rooms.

He sat down in the chair by the window.

He didn't light a candle.

He sat in the dark for a long time with his grey book in his lap and his hands folded on it and his stick against the arm of the chair. Looking out at the dark city.

On the outside of his window, on the glass, in the dark, there was something.

He didn't need a light to see it. He knew what it was. Large. Grey-gold. Wings spread. With two rings on them, like eyes. Looking in.

It didn't say anything.

It didn't need to.

---

At some point, very late, he opened the grey book.

And found the pencil. And by the light of the moth's faint glow on the glass, which was the only light there was, he wrote one more line. Under the columns. Under the numbers going down like water down a slope.

Small. Neat. Shaking only a little.

*Offered: twenty years. Price: not stated.*

He looked at it for a long time.

Then, underneath, smaller:

*I would give anything to see her face when she sees mine*.

And then, under that, so small it was almost nothing, almost a scratch on the page:

*Anything?*

He closed the book.

And sat in the dark with it in his lap. And didn't sleep. And the moth stayed on his window until dawn.
