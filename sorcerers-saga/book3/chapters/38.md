# What the Light Costs

They buried Fenn at the rim.

At the place where the trench came round from the east and met the ruins. Where the first lantern had been set down, on the morning of Lantern Night, by an old hewer from Coldwater who'd said *for the little one, from Absalom Ware, that was my wife's*.

The lantern was still there. Still lit. They dug the grave beside it.

Roole did most of the digging. One-armed. With his other arm still in its sling. He wouldn't let anyone help. He dug for three hours in the cold winter sun with his hatchet face set like stone, and when it was deep enough he climbed out and stood at the edge of it and looked down for a long time.

Then he went and got Fenn's staff. The Keeper's staff. Plain ash. That Fenn had held crosswise at the south edge all night, between the families and the Mothwing with knives.

He laid it in the grave first. And then they laid Fenn on top of it.

"*He straightened every leaning Stone in the west*," said Roole. Standing at the head of the grave. In his Keepers' grey. To the hundreds of Keepers standing round it in rows. "*With his own hands. Every one. The summer after the Heart.*" His voice didn't change. "*He said someone should. He said it was the least we could do. After all the years we spent pushing children down.*"

He looked at the grave.

"*He didn't talk much*," said Commander Roole. "*When he did, he said exactly what he meant.*"

He was quiet for a long moment.

"*So will I*," he said. "*He was my friend. I'll miss him every day.*"

And then he picked up the spade again. And filled it in.

---

There were twenty-three graves along the rim, in the end.

The eleven Ashari weren't buried. Grandmother Asha wouldn't have it. *We're fire-women*, she said. *We go back to the fire.* And the two hundred old women in red who were left sat in a ring round the eleven who weren't, at the east rim, where the moths had come thickest. And put their hands flat on the dust. And the dust went dark and hot and rippling.

And then bright.

And then there were eleven small heaps of white ash on the grey ground, and a smell like a forge at dawn, and two hundred old women singing in a language Lex didn't know.

Grandmother Asha didn't sing. She sat at the edge of the ring with her black eyes very bright and her toothless mouth pressed shut.

Lex sat beside her.

"*Two hundred and eleven years*," she said, after a long time. "*I waited for someone to ask us for fire.*" She looked at the heaps of ash. "*My sister was one of those. Ruhaya. She was older than me. She said she'd been waiting two hundred and forty.*"

"*I'm sorry*."

"*Don't be*." Grandmother Asha looked at him. With her crow's eyes. "*She'd have waited forever. She'd have died in the deep desert of nothing, of old age, waiting.*" She turned back to the ash. "*She died making a room for something coming home. That's a fire-woman's death. That's the best kind.*"

---

The little one sent flowers.

That was what people said. Afterward. In the taverns, in the Vale, in the Saltreach, in the market in Highcrest. *The little one sent flowers to the graves.*

It wasn't quite true. Lex knew. He'd felt it. Through the Stone. Through the ground. It hadn't *sent* anything. It didn't do that. It didn't make things. It made *room*.

But every grave along the rim, every one of the twenty-three, had flowers on it by the next morning. Small white ones. Snowdrops. In winter. Pushing up through the turned earth.

Because there was room for them now.

---

Kell had no grave.

She had a stone.

Brannoc made it. Out of a block of the black Gloam-stone from the rubble of Duskhold, the last of it, before the grass covered it all. He cut it square. And smooth. And set it at the edge of the pool. Beside the stick.

And on it, in his big careful hand, the way he'd cut the first rune he ever struck, six years ago, the night before Thornhollow:

*ISAURA KELL*
*She held on*

Lex had asked him why just that. Why not *High Warden*. Why not *Warden by gift*. Why not something about Dorran, or the deep, or the stick.

Brannoc had looked at him for a long time.

"*Because that's what she'd want*," he said. "*Lad. She was a Warden. Wardens don't want speeches.*" He brushed the black dust off the stone with his enormous hand. "*She'd want people to walk past and read it and know exactly what she did. That's all.*"

---

And in the grey book, after the Vhessans, after the twenty-three from the rim, on a new page—

Lex wrote it. Himself. In his own big clumsy careful hand. Sitting by the pool on the first night of winter with the book on his knee and Lark asleep in his shirt.

*Isaura Kell. Of Highcrest. She was High Warden. She signed forty-one warrants for unlatticed sorcery and remembered every name.*

*She was carried once. By a man she'd branded.*

*She sat with a frightened thing in the dark until it got home.*

*She held on*.

And then, under it, he turned the page. And left it blank.

For later.

---

Highcrest woke up on the second day of winter.

Lex didn't see it. He heard about it. From Elowen, who went back with the Queen. From Hollis Penn, who went back to his bakery and found the ovens cold and the dough grey and the cat asleep on the flour sack. From Maren Ashdown, who went back to Fennel Street with Tam and Robbie and sat on the step in the thin winter sun, and the widow Abel came out of her house next door with her cat in her arms and sat down beside her and said *Maren, what in the Lady's name happened*, and Maren laughed and couldn't stop.

Every one of them.

Everyone the Moth Queen had touched. Everyone who'd walked two hundred miles behind her with a dark lantern. Every Keeper at Greyfall. Every shepherd in the Greyspine. Every warm thing she'd taken in nine hundred years, pouring out of her down the point of a hewer's pick on the rubble of Duskhold, into the breach, into the little one.

And the little one, rising through the door with its whole vast soft heart, had taken every warm thing that came pouring down into it.

And given it back.

---

Odile came to the pool on the fourth day.

From the Keep. On foot. A hundred and fifty miles. She said she'd wanted to walk it. She said she'd wanted to see the circle from the ground, the way the people who dug it saw it.

She stood at the edge of the pool and looked down into it for a long time.

"*It gave it back*," she said. To Antho. Who was sitting on the grey stone rim with his legs dangling over the dark. "*Master Antho. The thing she took. The first night. At the Keep. In the snow.*"

"*I know*," said Antho. "*I felt it go back. All of them. All at once.*"

"*No*. *You don't understand.*" Odile sat down beside him. On the rim. "*I had it already. Pim gave it back. In the snow. He made room for it and it came back.*" She looked at the pool. "*And then on Lantern Night, at the Keep, at midnight, I was standing at the door with the students, and I felt it come back again.*"

Antho looked at her.

"*Twice*," said Odile Marsh. "*I've got it twice now. The first time I came up the mountain. When you opened the cave door. When you said, thank goodness, I've no idea what I'm doing.*" She was smiling. Crying. "*It's twice as warm.*"

---

Silas Hask lived.

Lex hadn't been sure he would. None of them had. The grey came back into him on the rubble the moment the warm thing came home. All of it. Twenty-six years of coal-damp and Gloam. His father's cough. His mother's. His sister's.

He'd sat on the black stone at the foot of the gate-tower all night, coughing, with Kit's arms round his neck and the *HOME* lantern in his lap. Grey on his lips. Breathing like a broken bellows.

And at dawn, when the little one lay down in the circle—

It made room.

---

"*Not a cure*," said Seraphine. Kneeling beside him on the grass by the pool, with her hand out, listening. "*It can't cure. You know that. It doesn't heal.*"

"*I know*," said Hask. Hoarsely. Between coughs.

"*But it's made room*," said Seraphine. "*In your lungs. Where the grey is. It hasn't taken the grey out. It's made more room round it. So there's space to breathe.*" She looked at him. "*Can you feel it?*"

Silas Hask breathed in.

Slowly. Carefully. The way a man breathes who's been afraid to for twenty-six years.

It caught. Halfway. A rattle. A catch. The grey.

And then it went past. Further. Deeper. More than halfway.

Not all the way down.

But further than it had gone in years.

He sat on the grass by the pool and breathed. In. Out. In. With his big scarred hands flat on the ground and his eyes shut.

"*Not all the way*," he said at last. Very quietly.

"*No*."

"*Enough*," said Silas Hask. And opened his eyes. Brown. Ordinary. Wet. "*It's enough.*"

---

His wings had fallen off.

In the night. On the rubble. While he sat coughing with Kit. Lex hadn't seen it happen. Kit had. Kit said they'd just—*dropped*. Like dead leaves off a tree in autumn. Grey-gold. Dry. And crumbled into dust on the black stone, and the wind took the dust, and there was nothing left but two long scars down his back, under the torn leather, where they'd come through.

"*Doesn't matter*," said Hask. When Lex asked. "*Never liked flying. Hewers don't go up. We go down.*"

He was still Level eighty-eight. The net said so. Delvebreaker. Epic. The levels were his. He'd earned them, every one, the wrong way and the right way, and the net didn't take back what it gave.

But the line under it had changed.

The Queen came to see him on the sixth day.

At the pool. In her plain blue dress with her silver circlet and Elowen Dunmore behind her with Oathkeeper drawn. She stood on the grass in front of the big coughing man sitting on the grey stone rim beside Kell's stick, and looked at him for a long time.

Hask stood up.

Slowly. Coughing. And bowed. Badly. He'd clearly never bowed to anyone in his life.

"*Majesty*," he said.

"*You broke cores*," said Lysandra. "*Twenty-nine of them. And left the shafts open. And she came down them.*"

"*Yes, Majesty*."

"*You carried Seren Vey out of a window. In the night. While her husband lay on the floor with his head split open.*"

"*Yes, Majesty*."

"*You stood on Fennel Street and tried to take a pregnant woman to the Moth Queen. And you stood at the top of the rubble on Lantern Night and lifted your pick to break the wind round her while she was giving birth.*"

Hask didn't look away.

"*Yes, Majesty*," he said.

The Queen looked at him.

"*And then you put the pick down*," she said. "*And stood in the gap. And held it out. And the Moth Queen flew onto it.*"

"*Yes*," said Silas Hask. Very quietly. "*That too.*"

---

Lysandra was quiet for a long time.

"*I sentenced Varric Sorn to ten years as schoolmaster in Fen Hollow*," she said at last. "*Teaching the children of the people Dorran Vask capped. Because he'd wanted to put them on a list.*" She looked at Hask. "*It was the cruelest thing I could think of. And the kindest.*"

Hask didn't say anything.

"*So I've been thinking*," said the Queen of Caldor, "*what would be the cruelest and the kindest thing I could do to you.*"

She looked past him. At the pool. At the stick on the rim. At the black stone with two words on it. At a small pale girl on a crutch, sitting on the other side of the pool, very still, watching.

"*Someone has to keep it*," said Lysandra. "*The room. Ivy Moss is its keeper. The net says so. But she's fourteen. And she can barely walk. And there'll be people. Uncapped. Thieves. Fools. Moths, maybe, someday, from the grey.*" She looked back at Hask. "*People who'll want to kneel down at the edge of it. And cup their hands.*"

Hask went very still.

"*Isaura Kell stood in front of it*," said the Queen. "*With a stick. In the deep. For six months. And then she held on to the Moth Queen's wings until she fell.*" She nodded at the stick on the rim. "*That's hers. Pim says the little one made it for her.*"

She looked at him.

"*Pick it up*," said Lysandra.

---

Silas Hask looked at the stick for a long time.

Long. Plain. Dark. Like a shepherd's crook without the crook.

"*I'm the one who broke the cores*," he said. Hoarsely. "*Majesty. I'm the one who made the holes. I'm the one she came down.*"

"*I know*."

"*It screamed*. *Every time. I felt it. I told the smith it was rock settling.*" His voice cracked. "*It screamed and I went up three levels and the cough got better and I did it again.*"

"*I know*," said the Queen. "*That's why.*"

She looked at him with her sharp dark eyes.

"*Nobody else in the world knows exactly what it sounds like when someone hurts it*," said Lysandra. "*Except Antho Vey. And he has a door to keep.*"

---

He picked up the stick.

His enormous scarred miner's hand closed round it. Round the plain dark wood. And held it. For a long time. Very still.

And from the pool, from the grass, from everywhere, soft and young and very small—

*[...the big one...]*

Hask flinched.

*[...the big one who broke my hands...]*

He stood by the pool with the stick in his fist and his face grey and didn't move.

*[...he's holding the Warden's stick...]*

*[...brother...is he going to stand in front of me...]*

Antho was sitting on the rim. On the other side. With his legs dangling over the dark. He looked across the pool at Silas Hask.

"*Are you*?" he said.

Hask looked at him.

Then he looked down into the pool. At the dark. At the full. At the bottom of it, very far down, three little lights. Curled together. Sitting down.

"*I'm sorry*," said Silas Hask. To the pool. Out loud. Hoarse. "*I'm sorry. I didn't*—" He stopped. Coughed. Tried again. "*I knew. I knew it was alive. I knew it hurt. I did it anyway. Because I could breathe.*"

The pool was quiet.

"*I'll stand in front of you*," said Silas Hask. "*If you'll let me. Every day. Till I can't. With her stick.*" His voice was very quiet. "*Nobody kneels at your edge. Nobody cups their hands. Not while I'm breathing.*"

A long silence.

*[...you said sorry...]*

Hask didn't move.

*[...brother...he said sorry...]*

"*He did*," said Antho Vey.

*[...nobody ever...]*

*[...except me...]*

The grass round Hask's boots stirred. Just a little. Like a cat brushing past.

*[...all right...]* said Mal. *[...all right, big one...you can stand in front of me...]*

---

Kit stayed with him.

And Brigid, and the Tull brothers, and old Absalom, who came up from the rim with his helmet-lamp and his pick and sat down on the grass beside Hask without a word and stayed there.

The Coldwater crew.

They built a house. On the slope above the pool. Where Duskhold's gate had been. Out of the last of the black rubble, before the grass took it all. Small. Plain. With one window. Facing the pool.

And in the window, a lantern.

Iron and glass. *ARDENT* on the pane. *HOME* on the base.

Lit.

Not waiting for anyone.

They were all already there.

---

The shafts closed on the eighth day.

All twenty-nine. The broken ones. The wounds. The holes the Uncapped had left when they shattered cores, and the moths had come down like smoke down a chimney.

Lex felt them go. Sitting by the pool at dawn. Through the Stone. One by one. All over Caldor. In tavern cellars and inn yards and village greens. A tavern-keeper in the Saltreach who'd been charging a copper a look came down to his cellar in the morning and found nothing but a floor. Grey stone. With a puddle where the drip landed.

*Drawn back*, Seraphine said. *It's home now. It doesn't need to reach up anymore. It's drawing its hands back. All of them. Even the cut ones.* She listened. *Like a hand coming back to rest in a lap.*

No more Delves opened after that.

Not unclassified ones. The old ones were still there. The Frostvault, and Qel'Varran, and the Delve in the Bitter Hills that was nothing but a well-shaft with iron rungs. The net's Delves. Tidy. Predictable. Ordered.

But no more round black holes in the grass, breathing warm air that smelled of the Hollowmaw, humming the Lantern Song flat.

The little one had stopped shedding.

It was home.

---

The Ferryman came on the twelfth day.

Lex didn't see him come. Nobody did. He was just there. At dawn. On the grass beside the pool. Tall. Thin. Grey-robed. Leaning on his long pole. With the lantern on top of it lit.

Eyeless.

Looking down into the pool.

Ivy Moss saw him first. She'd been sitting on the rim since before dawn, the way she did every morning now, telling Mal things. She looked up and saw a tall grey eyeless man standing on the grass beside her and didn't scream. She was a Hollowkeeper. She'd been Kindled beside an open way. She'd stopped screaming at things.

She said, very politely, "*Good morning.*"

"*Good morning*," said the Ferryman.

---

They fetched Antho. And Lex. And their mother.

And their father, in the wheeled chair that Brannoc had made, pushed down the slope by Pim over the new grass very slowly and very carefully, with a blanket over his knees and the grey book on top of it.

The Ferryman turned his eyeless face toward them as they came.

"*Anthoren Vey*," he said.

"*Ferryman*."

"*You opened a door*." The eyeless face didn't change. "*I felt it. In the river. All rivers are my water, a little.*" He was quiet. "*You opened it by asking. I have not felt a door opened by asking since before the world was woven.*"

"*My father showed me the edges*."

The Ferryman turned his face. Slowly. Toward the old man in the wheeled chair.

"*Arion Vey*," he said.

"*Ferryman*," said Arion. Faintly.

"*I told your son you would come to my shore*," said the Ferryman. "*Before long. I said old men always do.*" He tilted his head. "*You have not.*"

"*Not yet*," said Arion Vey.

"*No*." The Ferryman looked at him for a long time. "*Not yet. The little one has made you room. I can see it. A little. Ordinary room. A year. Perhaps two.*" He was quiet. "*I will wait. I am very patient. I have been here since before the world was made.*"

---

And then he turned his face toward Seren.

She was standing behind the wheeled chair. With her hand on her husband's shoulder. Small. Barefoot, still; she'd refused shoes since the Blightmarch turned green. She said she wanted to feel the grass.

"*Seren Vey*," said the Ferryman.

"*Ferryman*."

"*You paid me*," said the Ferryman. "*Thirteen years ago. To cross. The memory you would least wish to lose.*" The eyeless face was very still. "*A man. All of him. Every day. The steps of the Spire, in the rain.*"

"*I know*."

"*And then you did not cross*. *You turned round. For your son.*" He was quiet. "*I told him once, I do not give refunds. The river takes what it is owed.*"

Seren didn't say anything.

"*That is still true*," said the Ferryman. "*I do not give refunds.*"

He reached into his grey robe.

"*But I may give a gift*," he said.

---

He held out his hand.

Long. Grey. Thin. Empty.

And in it, very faint, so faint Lex could barely see it in the dawn, something. Not a thing. A *glimmer*. Like light on water. Like the reflection of a lamp in a wet window. Like rain.

"*The river runs clean*," said the Ferryman. "*The Tithe is ended. The hunger is home and has a name. The one who drank it dry knelt on a hill and was given a lamp, and was warm for the first time in nine hundred years, and is gone.*" He looked at Seren with his eyeless face. "*There is one fewer of the Nine in the grey. I carried them out. I have kept that payment nine hundred years.*"

He held the glimmer out. Toward her.

"*I am lighter this morning*," said the Ferryman. "*I find I would like to give something back.*"

---

"*One*," he said. "*Only one. Not all of him. The river would not allow it. A love for a love; that is the price, and no one has paid it.*" His eyeless face was very gentle. "*But one memory. As a gift. Not a refund. For opening a door by asking. For giving a moth a lamp.*"

Seren stared at the glimmer in his hand.

"*Which one*?" she said. In a whisper.

"*The first*," said the Ferryman. "*The steps. The rain.*"

---

She didn't take it.

Not at first. She stood behind the wheeled chair with her hand on Arion's shoulder and looked at the glimmer in the Ferryman's long grey hand for a long time.

Then she looked down. At her husband. At the white wisp of his hair. At the grey book on his knee.

"*If I take it*," she said. Slowly. "*I'll remember him. Young. On the steps.*" She swallowed. "*I'll know what he looked like. When he looked at me. The first time.*"

"*Yes*."

"*And then I'll look down*," said Seren. "*And he'll be this.*" Her hand tightened on his shoulder. "*And I'll know the difference. I'll know what I lost.*"

The Ferryman didn't answer.

His father didn't say anything. He sat in the wheeled chair with his eyes on the pool and his hands on the grey book and didn't say anything at all.

But Lex saw his hands. On the book. Shaking.

---

"*Take it*," said Arion Vey.

His wife looked down at him.

"*Take it*," said her husband. Very faintly. Not looking at her. Looking at the pool. "*Please. I've been carrying it for thirteen years. Alone. The steps. The rain. You laughing at the lanterns.*" His voice was a thread. "*I'd like someone else to have it. Just one. Just the first.*"

He looked up at her.

"*So it's not just mine*," he said. "*So when I go, it's still somewhere.*"

---

Seren took it.

She reached out. Past the wheeled chair. And put her square freckled hand in the Ferryman's long grey one. Over the glimmer.

And closed her fingers.

---

Lex saw her face.

He'd seen it do a lot of things. In the gold room. In the cavern. On the rubble with a lamp held out to a Sovereign.

He'd never seen it do this.

It went still. Utterly. Her eyes wide. Looking at nothing. At the pool. At the dawn. At something nobody else could see.

And then it *lit*.

From inside. Like a window. Like a lamp. Like the face of a girl of nineteen standing under the arch of a tower in the rain with flour on her cheek, laughing at the lanterns, who has just turned round and seen a young man in a grey coat coming round the corner, very casually, as if he just happened to be passing.

Looking at her.

As if she were the first light he'd seen in two hundred years.

---

She knelt.

Slowly. On the grass. Beside the wheeled chair. And took her husband's face in both her hands. His old face. His walnut face. A hundred years in a drawer. White wisp of hair. Grey eyes.

And looked at it.

For a long, long time.

"*You did*," said Seren Vey. In a whisper. Wet. Shaking. Laughing. "*You did look like a librarian telling off a choir.*"

His father's face broke.

"*I told you*," he said. "*Page four.*"

"*You had ink on your cuff*," said his wife. "*Here.*" She touched his wrist. "*And your hair was too long. And you were so tall you had to stoop under the arch. And you pretended you were just passing.*"

"*I was just passing*."

"*You were not*." She was crying. "*You came round that corner three times. I saw you. The first two times you turned back.*"

Arion Vey laughed.

The real laugh. The wide startled one. Cracking out of him like ice off a roof. Shaking the wheeled chair. Shaking the grey book off his knee onto the grass.

"*I never told anyone that*," he said. "*Not in the letter. Not in the book. I never told anyone I turned back twice.*"

"*You didn't have to*," said Seren Harrow. "*I was there.*"

---

The Ferryman was gone.

Lex didn't see him go. Nobody did. When he looked up, there was only the grass, and the pool, and the dawn. And a small pale girl on a crutch sitting on the grey stone rim, looking at the place where the tall grey man had been.

"*He said*," said Ivy Moss, very quietly, to no one in particular, "*he said thank you. To Mal. On the way out. He said, thank you for the room.*"

---

Brannoc met Lark on the fifteenth day.

He'd been at the pool every day. Every day since Lantern Night. Cutting Kell's stone, and then helping the Coldwater crew with their house, and then sitting on the grass by the pool with Hedda, doing nothing, which he said was the first time in sixty-three years he'd done nothing and he found he was very good at it.

He hadn't asked to hold her.

Lex had seen him looking. From across the pool. From the slope. From the doorway of the tent. The way he'd looked at Lex, the first night, at eleven, standing in the ashes at Harrow.

He hadn't asked.

So on the fifteenth day, Lex walked across the grass to where the old smith was sitting by the pool with his knees creaking and his grey beard full of crumbs from Mother Orla's bacon, and sat down beside him.

And put Lark in his arms.

---

Brannoc didn't move.

He sat on the grass by the pool with the baby in his enormous scarred arms, in the crook of his elbow, like a loaf, and didn't move at all. For a long time.

Lark looked up at him. With her dark eyes. Not unfocused anymore. Fifteen days. Looking. At a great grey bald face with a soot-stained beard and wet eyes, very close.

And reached up.

And grabbed his beard.

"*Ah*," said Brannoc Holt. Very softly. "*Ah. There you are.*"

He didn't take her hand away. He let her hold on. His beard in her square fist, too big for the rest of her.

Then he reached into his coat. With his free hand. Slowly. And took something out.

Old. Black. Square-headed. Small.

A horseshoe nail.

"*There you go, lass*," said Brannoc. And put it in her other hand. And closed her fingers round it. "*That's your first bit of iron.*"

Lex couldn't breathe.

"*Your da had one*," said Brannoc. To the baby. Not looking at Lex. "*When he was two. Sat on my woodpile with his thumb in his mouth while his mam and his brother watched the sparks. I gave him a nail.*" His voice was very rough. "*He had it in his fist the night his da sealed him. Did you know that? Arion told me. He said the boy was holding a nail.*"

He looked down at the baby with her fist round the nail.

"*Smiths*," said Brannoc Holt. "*All of you. Every one. From the first day.*"

---

Antho went back to the Keep on the twentieth day.

With Seraphine. And Pim. And Odile. On Ashka. Who'd been sleeping on the slope above the pool for twenty days, curled round the Coldwater crew's house like a cat round a hearth, with her great golden eye on Mal.

He came to say goodbye. At the pool. At dawn.

"*Someone has to keep the door*," he said. "*The Hollowmaw. There's still a door there. Still lamps. Still a thousand lanterns.*" He looked at the pool. "*And Mal's got a keeper here. And a big man with a stick.*"

"*You'll come back*."

"*Every month*," said Antho. "*To sit with it. To tell it things.*" He smiled. The real one. The one that was exactly like Lex's. "*It likes the one about the miller's cat. I'm going to learn some new ones.*"

He looked at his brother.

And opened his panel. Right there. By the pool. The way people did now, sometimes, with no Wardens watching. The way a man might pull up his sleeve to show you a scar.

---

Lex went home on the twenty-third day.

To Harrow. With Wren. And Lark. On Vharuun's neck, in Brannoc's chair, with the baby inside his shirt and Wren behind him with her arms round his waist and her chin on his shoulder.

Over the circle. Over a hundred miles of soft new green with a pool in the middle and a ring of small gold lights round the rim, still burning, given. Over the edge of it. Over the downs. Over the Vale.

Home.

The cottage was there. In the clearing. In the birch wood. By the stream. Grey Vale stone and new oak beams. With a door that stuck in wet weather because he'd hung it himself in a hurry and never planed it.

And a window on the east side. Deep and wide. With a sill broad enough to sit on.

Empty.

He'd taken the lantern. The *ARDENT* one. *HOME*. Given it to Kit, on the rubble, on Lantern Night, for Silas Hask. It was in the window of a small black house on a slope above a pool now.

The window at Harrow was dark.

---

He made a new one.

That night. At the little forge he'd built in the lean-to behind the cottage. Iron and glass. Small. Plain.

He thought about what to cut on it.

Not *HOME*. Not *ARDENT*. Not anything waiting. Not a light left on for someone who wasn't here.

He thought about it for a long time. Standing at the anvil in the dark with the hammer in his hand and the little lantern in the tongs and the fire saying good evening.

And then he cut one word. On the base. Where only someone who picked it up would see.

*HERE*.

---

He put it in the window.

And lit it. With his own fire.

And Wren came and stood beside him. With Lark asleep on her shoulder. And looked at it. At the little gold flame behind the glass. Facing out. Facing the road.

"*Who's it for*?" she said.

"*Nobody*," said Lex.

She looked at him.

"*Everybody's home*," said Lex Vey. "*Mam's home. Father's home. Antho's home. Mal's home.*" He looked at the baby on her shoulder. "*She's home. You're home.*"

He looked at the lantern.

"*It's not waiting for anyone*," he said. "*It's just—here. Lit. For whoever's in the room.*"

Wren looked at the little lantern for a long time.

Then she leaned against him. With the baby between them. And said, very softly, into his shoulder, "*Idiot.*"

But she didn't move.

---

And on the cord round his neck, under his shirt, against his chest—

The little silver bell with the crooked clapper didn't ring.

Not that night. Not the next. Not for a long time.

Because nobody was sad.
