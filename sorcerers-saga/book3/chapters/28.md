# The Breach He Made

Arion Vey went back to Duskhold on a litter, with a bandage round his head and his wife's lamp in his son's hands.

He hadn't asked to go. He hadn't asked for anything, the first three days after. He'd lain in the narrow bed in the little stone room off the library, the one Antho had given Seren, the one that still smelled faintly of her, of lamp-oil and old wool and the deep. With his eyes open. Looking at the high small window. At the sky.

Not talking.

They thought it was the fall. Seraphine did. She came and sat with him and felt his head and looked into his eyes with a candle and asked him questions. *What's your name. What year is it. Who's the Queen.* He answered them all. Correctly. In a whisper. And then turned his face back to the window.

It wasn't the fall.

---

On the fourth morning he said to Antho, "*Take me to the breach.*"

His son was sitting by the bed. He'd been sitting there, on and off, for four days. With the clay lamp in his lap. Never putting it down. Not to eat. Not to sleep. He slept sitting up, against the wall, with the lamp in both hands, and when he woke he looked at it first, before anything, to see that it was still burning.

He looked up now. Red-eyed.

"*Father*. *You can't travel.*"

"*I can lie down*," said Arion. "*On a litter. On a dragon. I can lie down anywhere.*" He turned his head on the pillow. Slowly. It hurt. Everything hurt now. "*Lex is going to make a room in the Blightmarch. You're going to open the door. I'm the only one alive who knows where the edges are.*"

"*Father*—"

"*She took your mother*," said Arion Vey. "*Up the hill. To my rooms. She's sitting in my chair reading my books with my wife beside her.*" His voice was very quiet. "*And I've been lying here for three days looking at a window.*"

He looked at his son.

"*I'd like to do something*," he said. "*Before I can't.*"

---

They took him on Ashka.

Lex wasn't there. Lex was already in the Blightmarch. He'd gone the day after, with Brannoc and Tilda and Grandmother Asha and forty students and two hundred Ashari fire-women in red and every smith from every forge in Caldor who could hear fire. They'd gone east. To start.

To make room.

So it was Antho who carried him. Down the long passage. Past the lanterns. Five hundred and six of them dark again, with dust on their panes. Six hundred and seven lit. Antho counted them under his breath as he walked. Arion heard him.

And at the cave mouth, Ashka was waiting. With a litter strapped to her back between two ridges of blue scale. Brannoc's work again. Leather and ash-wood and oiled canvas.

And Seraphine was waiting. With a pack and three lamps and her notebook. And Pim. Small and silent in his blue coat. Who looked at Arion on the litter. And came and stood beside it. And put his thin hand on the edge.

And didn't let go the whole way.

---

The Blightmarch went on forever.

Arion had forgotten.

He'd been here before. Many times. Twenty-one years ago, when it was green. When it was the eastern march of Vhessa, rolling hills and birch woods and little grey towns round the mouths of mines. Coldwater. Ashpit. Greyford. Kell's Hollow. Names in a ledger. He'd ridden through them on his way to the Great Stone, with Corvin beside him, talking about nothing, about the weather, about the price of bread.

He'd been here five years ago. When it was dead. Gloam-grey. Silent. With his sons. With Duskhold rising out of the middle of it like a black tooth.

He'd never seen it from the air.

Grey. That was all. From horizon to horizon. Not Gloam-grey; the Gloam was gone. Just grey. Dust-grey. Ash-grey. Hills with nothing on them. Valleys with nothing in them. Rivers that had stopped. Woods that were only stumps, black and broken, standing in rows like the teeth of a comb. Little grey towns with no roofs.

No birds. No sheep. No smoke. No sound.

A hundred miles of it.

And in the middle, far off, black and broken and falling, like the bones of something enormous that had died there—

Duskhold.

---

He made them put him down at the edge.

Not at the ruins. Not yet. At the edge of the Blightmarch. Where the grey began. On a low hill, the last green one, looking east.

He wanted to look at it.

They set the litter down on the grass. Antho and Seraphine. And propped him up, with a folded blanket behind his back, so he could see. And sat down beside him. One on each side. And Pim sat at his feet.

And they looked.

---

"*Eleven thousand*," said Arion.

Nobody said anything.

"*In the first year*," said Arion. "*I wrote it down. In the grey book. That autumn. Eleven thousand, estimated.*" His voice was very steady. "*And then a line. And then the price of bread in Highcrest.*"

Antho was very still beside him.

"*Lex told me*," said Arion. "*Yesterday. Before he went east. He sat by the bed and told me what Hask said. In the street. In Highcrest.*" He looked at the grey. "*Eleven thousand. In a column. Between a line and the price of bread.*"

"*Father*—"

"*It's true*," said Arion Vey. "*That's the thing. It's true. I did write it. I do remember writing it. I remember dipping the pen.*" He looked at his hands. Thin. Shaking. Brown-spotted. "*I remember thinking, I'll come back to this. I'll find out the names. I'll write them all down. Properly. Each one.*"

He was quiet.

"*I never did*," he said.

---

The wind came over the grey. Thin. Cold. Smelling of nothing.

"*I was going to*," said Arion. "*I meant to. And then I went to Avaloria to hold the breaches. And then I was in the crystal for fifteen years. And then I woke up, and there was Duskhold, and you two, and the Queen, and the festival, and the Key, and*—" He stopped. "*There was always something. Something more important. Something I could fix.*"

He looked at the grey.

"*I couldn't fix them*," he said. "*So I didn't look at them.*"

---

"*Do you know what he said*?" said Arion. "*Hask. To Lex. In the street.*"

"*Which of you do I owe*," said Antho. Quietly.

"*Which of you do I owe*." Arion nodded. Slowly. "*Your father, who killed them. You, who set me free. Her, who gave me breath.*" He looked at his son. "*Do you know what Lex said back?*"

Antho shook his head.

"*Nobody*," said Arion. "*He said, you don't owe anyone. That's what free means. Nobody gets to say what you owe them.*" His mouth moved. Not quite a smile. "*He's a better man than me. Your brother. He always was. Even when he hated me.*"

"*He doesn't hate you*."

"*I know*. *That's what makes it worse.*" Arion closed his eyes. "*I owe them, Antho. Hask's sister. Nell. Eight. Who whistled at the window. I owe every one of them. Eleven thousand. More. I owe them their names.*" He opened his eyes. "*And I've got a season. Perhaps less. And I'm lying on a hill on a litter looking at the grey I made.*"

---

Seraphine was writing.

He heard the pencil. Quiet. Steady. Beside him. He turned his head.

She was sitting on the grass with her notebook on her knees. Not looking at him. Writing.

"*What are you doing*?"

"*Writing down Coldwater*," said Seraphine. Without looking up.

He stared at her.

"*I've got the records*," she said. "*The Keepers' records. From when Lex Unbound the Coldwater Stone. Two summers ago. Four thousand and twelve names. The living.*" Her pencil moved. "*And the Kindling registers. From before. From the Order. Every child Kindled at Coldwater for forty years. With their classes and their Ceilings.*" She turned a page. "*And the parish books. Births and deaths. Seventy years of them. The priest kept them in a box under the altar and the Gloam never got in.*"

She looked up.

"*If I cross them*," said Seraphine Ilvane, "*the living against the Kindled against the parish. The ones who were born and Kindled and aren't on the living list.*"

She didn't finish.

She didn't need to.

---

Arion looked at her for a long time.

At his son's wife. With her ink-stained fingers and her crooked spectacles and her notebook on her knees. Who had sat in the cold-room four nights ago with two iron cups and held the dark alone. Who had been a Warden archivist once, and fled with stolen records, and been framed by his oldest friend, and nearly killed proving her innocence.

Who had always been good at gaps. At the spaces between letters. At the pages someone's torn out.

"*How long*?" he said.

"*For Coldwater*?" She bit her pencil. "*A week. If I don't sleep.*"

"*And the rest*?"

Seraphine looked at the grey. At the hundred miles of it. At the little grey towns with no roofs. Ashpit. Greyford. Kell's Hollow.

"*Longer*," she said.

"*I can help*," said Arion.

She looked at him.

"*I can still write*," said Arion Vey. He held up his shaking hand. "*Badly. Slowly. But I can write. I've been writing every day for two hundred years.*" He looked at the notebook. "*I'd like to write them. Myself. In my own hand. In the grey book. Where the line is.*"

He swallowed.

"*Between the line and the price of bread*," he said. "*I'd like to fill it in.*"

---

They went to the ruins in the afternoon.

Ashka carried the litter. Low. Slow. Over the grey. With Antho and Seraphine walking below, and Pim riding on the dragon's neck, very straight, with his hand on the litter's edge.

Duskhold had been a citadel. Morvane's. Grown out of the Gloam-stone over fifteen years like a tumor out of the grey, black and smooth and windowless, with no doors. A great hall in the middle of it with a pit in the floor where the breach had been.

It was falling now.

Five years without the Gloam. The black stone was cracking. Crumbling. The great smooth walls had split and slumped and fallen in, and the towers were stumps, and the whole thing lay in the middle of the grey like the skeleton of a beast, ribs and spine and skull, with the wind going through it.

And all round it, as far as he could see, people.

---

Arion sat up on the litter.

He didn't mean to. It hurt. He did it anyway.

The grey plain round the ruins was *covered*. Tents. Hundreds. Wagons. Fires. Forges. Real forges, with bellows and anvils and hearths, set up on the bare grey ground in rows, like a market. Smoke going up from all of them. And between them, everywhere, moving, working, shouting—

People. Thousands.

He saw the Ashen Banners, two hundred of them, digging. With spades. In a long line. Cutting a trench into the grey ground that went away east further than he could see. He saw Keepers in grey coats, carrying sacks. He saw the red robes of the Ashari, two hundred old women, sitting in a ring on the ground with their hands flat on the dust, and the dust in front of them going dark and hot and rippling.

He saw students from the Ashbourne Forge with hammers. Smiths from every forge in Caldor. Sunreach riders on golden horses, dragging sledges. Farmers from the Vale with ox-carts. Miners from Coldwater—from *Coldwater*—with picks.

And in the middle of it all, on a hill of black rubble that had been Duskhold's great gate, a big man with dark curly hair badly cut and a grey-gold handprint on his face, standing with a map in his hands, shouting at someone.

And beside him, a bigger one. Bald. Grey-bearded. Shouting back.

---

"*He's been at it four days*," said Antho. Quietly. Beside the litter. "*Lex. He hasn't slept. Brannoc says he's going to fall over.*"

"*Brannoc says that about everyone*."

"*Brannoc's usually right*."

Arion looked at the grey plain. At the trench. At the forges. At the thousands.

"*How*?" he said. "*How did he get so many? In four days?*"

Antho was quiet for a moment.

"*He asked*," he said.

Arion looked at him.

"*He went to Ashbourne*," said Antho. "*And stood in the forge yard. And said, I need to make a room for something that's coming home. It's very big. I can't do it alone.*" He looked at the plain. "*And they came. Brannoc's students. And then the other forges. And then the Banners. And then the Ashari. And then*—" He shook his head. "*And then everyone. The Vale. The Saltreach. Coldwater. People he Unbound. People he's never met.*"

"*Just because he asked*."

"*Just because he asked*," said Antho Vey. "*Nobody's ever asked them for anything before. Not like that. Not as if it mattered whether they said yes.*"

---

They carried him into the ruins at dusk.

Into the great hall. What was left of it. The roof was gone. The walls were fallen. The black floor was cracked and heaped with rubble and open to the sky.

And in the middle of it, where the floor dipped down, where five years ago there had been a pit, a chasm, a breach—

A line.

Thin. Dark. Long. Running across the black floor from one side of the fallen hall to the other. Like a scar. Like a seam. Like a crack in a crucible that's been mended and still shows.

The first wound.

---

They set the litter down beside it.

And Arion looked at it for a long time.

He remembered kneeling here. Twenty-one years ago. On this floor. Except it hadn't been this floor then. It had been grass. A hillside. With the Great Stone of Vhessa standing on top of it, tall and grey and humming with the net. He'd knelt in the grass at the foot of the Stone with Corvin beside him and put his hands flat on the ground and reached into the net.

And pulled.

*Like a rotten sail in a gale*. That's what he'd told his sons. Five years ago. In this hall. With eleven thousand of the Quiet watching him. *It tore. Right here. Under my hands.*

And Corvin had been standing beside him.

And it found Corvin first.

---

"*He was my friend*," said Arion.

Nobody asked who.

"*Corvin*," he said. "*Morvane. Before. He was a Warden-Commander. He liked dogs. He was terrible at cards. He thought we were coming here to investigate a fault.*" He looked at the dark line on the floor. "*I didn't tell him. I didn't tell anyone. I was going to fix it. All of it. In one night. And then I'd tell them. Afterward. When it was done.*"

He put his hand out. Over the edge of the litter. Toward the line. He couldn't reach it.

Antho knelt. And took his father's hand. And put it on the floor. On the line. On the scar.

It was warm.

Arion hadn't expected that. He'd expected cold. The breach had been cold. The Gloam had been cold, like wet ash, like the bottom of a well.

It was warm.

"*It's not dead*," said Antho. Quietly. "*Is it. The breach. It's closed. But it's not dead.*"

"*No*," said Arion. Slowly. With his hand on the warm black line. "*No. It's—*" He stopped.

He could feel it.

Not with the Root. He'd given that away. Not with any Well; he'd never Listened properly. He'd been a Listener's husband for eleven years and learned a little, nothing like his sons.

With his hands.

With two hundred years of knowing where things were thin. Where the net was worn. Where a thing was likely to tear if you pulled on it too hard, too fast.

---

"*It goes down*," said Arion.

He shut his eyes.

"*All the way*," he said. "*To the deep. It always did. That's why I came here. The net was thinnest here. It always had been. The world was worn through here, like the elbow of an old coat.*" His fingers moved on the line. Slowly. Feeling. "*And when I tore it, I tore it all the way down. And when we closed it, we closed the top. The skin. The world's side.*"

He opened his eyes.

"*Not the bottom*," he said. "*The bottom's still open. Under. It's a shaft, Antho. Like the ones the Uncapped made. Only older. Wider. Deeper. Going down from here to the bottom of the world.*" He looked at his son. "*Straight down. To where the little one is.*"

Antho went very still.

"*That's why it's warm*," said Arion. "*It's not the breach. It's the little one. Underneath. Close. Pressing up against the bottom of it.*" His voice dropped. "*It's been pressing up against it since the Heart. Since it started to rise. It knows. It's always known. This is the way up.*"

---

He showed Antho the edges.

It took all night.

He couldn't move. He couldn't sit up for more than a few minutes. He lay on the litter beside the line with his hand on it, and Antho lay beside him on the black floor with the clay lamp between them and his own hand on the line, and Arion talked.

Where it was thick. Where they'd stitched it, five years ago, three of the blood, with the Root and the Stone and the Shade. Where the stitches were tightest. Where they'd pulled.

Where it was thin. Where the old tear had gone deepest. Where the edges had never quite met, even after the mend, and something had been left. A gap. A seam. A place where a door could be.

"*Here*," said Arion. At two in the morning. His voice a thread. His hand on a place in the line, near the middle, where the black was a little wider. "*Here. This is where I knelt. This is where my hands were.*" He swallowed. "*This is where it tore first.*"

"*And this is where I open it*," said Antho.

"*This is where you open it*."

Antho put his hand over his father's. On the line. On the place.

"*How*?" he said.

---

Arion was quiet for a long time.

The stars were out. Very bright. Over the open roof of the fallen hall. The fires of the camp outside were a red glow over the broken walls. Somewhere, far off, someone was still hammering. One anvil. Slow. Steady. In the dark.

"*I tore it trying to free everyone at once*," said Arion Vey.

His voice was very quiet.

"*In one stroke. With all my Authority. Pulling. As hard as I could. As fast as I could.*" He looked at the line. "*Because I was angry. Because I'd seen the Ceilings. Every grey band round every gold thread. Millions of them. And I couldn't bear it.*" He was quiet. "*And because I thought I knew best. I thought I could do it alone. Without asking anyone. Without telling anyone. Not Corvin. Not Seren. Not the net.*"

He turned his head on the litter. And looked at his son.

"*Don't do it like that*," he said.

Antho didn't answer.

"*Don't pull*," said his father. "*Don't decide. Don't take. You know how. You did it in the deep. At the Hollowmaw door. Kestrel told you.*"

"*Ask*," said Antho.

"*Ask*." Arion's hand tightened under his son's. On the line. "*Ask it to open. Ask the little one to come up. Ask the net to let it through.*" His voice cracked. "*Ask everyone. Lex. Seraphine. Brannoc. Every smith out there. Every old woman in red. Every miner from Coldwater with a pick.*"

He looked at the stars.

"*I tore it alone*," said Arion Vey. "*You open it with everyone.*"

---

They lay there until dawn.

Father and son. On the black floor of a fallen hall. With their hands on a scar. And a clay lamp between them. And a boy asleep at the foot of the litter with his blue coat for a pillow.

Antho slept, a little. Toward morning. With his hand still on the line.

Arion didn't.

He lay on the litter and looked at the stars going out, one by one, as the sky went grey. And thought about a man who liked dogs and was terrible at cards. And a girl of eight who whistled at a window. And eleven thousand names, in a column, between a line and the price of bread.

And his wife. Sitting in his chair at the top of the Spire. In his rooms. With a woman with lit eyes reading his books.

*She's sitting in my chair*.

*She's reading my books*.

*She's looking at my wife*.

---

He thought about the offer.

He'd thought about it every night since the dark drawing room on the Crown Terrace. Every night. In the Spire. In the Keep. On the litter. *A little light. From my own hands. Twenty years. As much as you like.*

*Enough to be young when she comes home*.

He'd written it in the grey book. *Offered: twenty years. Price: not stated.* And under it. *I would give anything to see her face when she sees mine.* And under that, so small it was almost nothing. *Anything?*

She'd come home.

She'd seen his face. Old. White. Furrowed. In a chair. And she'd knelt down and put her hands on it and listened, and said *you have a nice voice*.

She hadn't needed him young.

He'd been wrong. He'd been so wrong. He'd sat in that dark room and wanted it, wanted it so badly, to be young for her. And she'd come home and not needed it at all. She'd fallen asleep on his shoulder. She'd eaten burnt porridge with him. She'd read his letter by a window and found the one thing in it he'd never been able to remember.

*No change*, he'd written.

He'd meant it.

---

And now she was up the hill.

In the Spire. In his rooms. With the woman with lit eyes. Who never lied. Who only chose where to stand.

And he was on a litter. On a black floor. In a fallen hall. Eighty-three. Eighty-four, maybe, by now. With a bandage round his head and a season left. Perhaps less.

He couldn't walk.

He couldn't hold a cup.

He couldn't climb the ninety-four steps of the Spire, or the six floors in the lift, or the last stair to the top, to the door of his own room, to the chair by the window, where his wife was sitting.

*Not like this*, he thought.

*Not old*.

---

He turned his head.

Very slowly. On the litter. And looked at his son. Asleep. On the black floor. With his long black hair loose and his face, in sleep, younger than it ever was awake. With his hand on the scar. And the clay lamp between them. Lit.

*Given*, Seren had said. *Not waiting. Yours.*

He thought about the moths at the Keep. The Mothwing with its hand on his face. *Nothing there*, he'd said. *She took it already. Every warm thing I had was her.*

*And she gave it all away*.

*There's nothing left in me for you to drink*.

He'd been proud of that. Lying on the kitchen floor with his head split open. He'd been proud.

*There's nothing left in me for her to drink*, he thought now. Slowly. Looking at the stars going out. *Nothing she wants. Nothing she can use. I'm an old man with nothing warm in him that isn't already given away.*

*So what would she want with me*?

*What would she want, from a man with nothing left*?

---

He knew the answer.

He'd known it in the dark drawing room. He'd known it in the way she looked at him. With her lit eyes. As if he were the loveliest thing she'd ever seen.

*I'd like to be your friend*, she'd said. *I've been very lonely.*

He'd thought she was lying. And then he'd remembered she never lied.

*She means it*, he thought now. Slowly. Looking at the stars going out. *She does want a friend. A Vey. Her own. One who's lived too long and is dying of it, the way she should have been dying for nine hundred years.*

*She wants me in her household*.

*Drinking from her hands. A sip at a time. Like Orrin. Like the Mothwing. Like Hask. Young forever, and hers forever, and never able to stop going back to her for more.*

*A Vey on a string*.

He lay very still.

*And she'd let me in*, he thought. *Wouldn't she. Into her house. Into my own rooms. Up the stairs I can't climb. To the chair by the window where my wife is sitting.*

*She'd let me walk right in. Young. Strong. Grateful.*

*Her friend*.

---

The sun came up over the grey.

Red. Then gold. Then white. Over the hundred miles of dust and stumps and roofless towns. Over the camp. Over the trench. Over the forges and the fires and the thousands of people asleep on the bare ground.

Over the fallen hall. And the scar on the floor. And an old man on a litter with his hand on it, and his son asleep beside him.

Arion Vey reached for the grey book.

It was on the litter beside him. Where it always was. With the pencil tucked into the binding. He got it open. Slowly. With his shaking hands. To the page. *Offered: twenty years. Price: not stated.*

And under it. *I would give anything to see her face when she sees mine.*

And under that. *Anything?*

He looked at it for a long time.

Then he crossed out the first line. Slowly. One long straight stroke. From corner to corner. The way his wife's mother had crossed out a moth in the lid of a box.

And under it, very small, very neat, shaking only a little, he wrote:

*Price: known. Her household drinks from her hands.*

And under that:

*So will I. Once. Long enough to climb the stairs.*

And under that, smallest of all:

*Then I'll give it back*.

---

He closed the book.

And put it back on the litter. And put his hand back on the scar. On the warm black line. On the place where he'd knelt twenty-one years ago and pulled.

And lay there in the sunrise.

And beside him, his son stirred. And opened his eyes. And looked at the lamp first. Before anything. To see that it was still burning.

It was.

"*Morning*," said Antho. Hoarse. Sleepy.

"*Good morning*," said his father.

And smiled at him. The real smile. The wide startled one. The one he'd smiled on the slope of the Hollowmaw, a year ago, when Lex said *you look old*.

Antho blinked.

"*What*?" he said.

"*Nothing*," said Arion Vey. "*I was just thinking. You'll be very good at it. The door.*" He looked at the line under their hands. "*You've always been good at doors. Better than me. Much better.*"

He shut his eyes.

"*Wake me when your brother comes*," he said. "*I want to tell him about the edges.*"

And he didn't sleep.

He lay with his eyes shut and his hand on the scar and the grey book on the litter beside him, and the sun climbing over the grey country he'd made.

Counting.

Not hairs. Not lines. Not stairs.

Days.
