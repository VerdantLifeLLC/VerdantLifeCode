@@PART Part One: A Light in the Window
# Two Weddings and a Cow

Lex Vey had forged a Lockward out of the bones of the world, and he could not tie his own collar.

"*Hold still*," said Brannoc.

"*I am holding still*."

"*You're holding still like a horse holds still when there's a wasp in the stable*." The old smith's enormous hands, black-nailed, burn-scarred, steady as an anvil, took the two ends of the cloth from Lex's fingers and did something to them that Lex couldn't follow. Over. Under. Through. Tug. "*There.*"

Lex looked down at it. It was a knot. A good one. Neat and flat and square, at the base of his throat, holding the high collar of the best shirt he had ever owned closed over the burn scars on his collarbone.

"*How did you do that*?"

"*Same way you do a weld*." Brannoc stood back and looked him up and down with his head on one side. "*Don't think about it. Just do it before the iron cools.*"

"*I've never seen you wear a collar in your life*."

"*Wore one once*." Brannoc picked a thread off Lex's shoulder. "*To my own wedding. Thirty-eight years ago. In the chapel at Ashbourne, with Sister Agathe's aunt doing the words, and her aunt's cat asleep on the altar.*" His soot-black beard twitched. "*My Mattie tied it for me. Swore at me the whole time. Said I had a neck like a bull and the sense of a turnip.*"

Lex looked at him.

Brannoc Holt never talked about his wife. Not in the thirteen years Lex had lived in his house. There was a little painted wooden box on the shelf above the forge-loft bed with a lock of brown hair in it, and that was all Lex had ever known.

"*She'd have liked you*," said Brannoc. Gruffly. To the shirt. "*She'd have liked the girl better. But she'd have liked you.*"

"*Everyone likes Wren better*."

"*That's because she's better*," said Brannoc. And then he put both of his enormous hands on Lex's shoulders and held them, hard, the way he'd held them on the night of the fire, when Lex was eleven and couldn't stop shaking. "*You did good, lad*," he said. Very quietly. "*You did good.*"

Lex couldn't say anything.

So he didn't. And Brannoc didn't need him to. He just squeezed Lex's shoulders once, and let go, and turned round, and blew his nose very loudly into a rag that had been used for oiling tongs.

---

*[He's right*,] said Ember. *[You did.]*

Lex stood in the little stone room off the library of the Lantern Keep, which had been Antho's study, and then Seraphine's study, and then a storeroom for Teo's fish-buckets, and was now, for one morning, the place where the groom was being kept out of everybody's way.

The other groom was being kept in the cold-room. With the fish. Nobody had asked him if he minded.

*Ember*.

*[Yes*?]

*Are you crying*?

*[I am a fragment of a sorcerer's mind, pressed into a seal like a maker's mark and currently residing in a metaphorical hearth at the center of your soul*,] said Ember. *[I do not have eyes. I cannot cry.]*

*You're crying*.

*[A little*,] said Ember. *[Shut up.]*

Lex smiled.

It came out wobbly. Everything about him was wobbly this morning. His hands. His knees. His voice, when he'd said good morning to Pim on the stairs, which had come out an octave too high and made Pim laugh so hard he'd had to sit down.

He hadn't slept.

None of them had. Not after midnight. Not after their father had knelt down by the fire and woken them and said *she sent a message*, in a voice Lex had never heard him use before. Not after they'd gone down, all of them, and stood in front of the black door and read nothing, because the words were gone, and Antho had put his hand on the glass and listened, and said *there's something with her. And it's a big shape.*

*Not alone*.

Lex had lain awake on the slope for the rest of the night with Wren asleep against his back and the little silver bell on its cord round his neck, and listened to it. Waiting for it to ring again.

It hadn't.

*Ember*.

*[Still here*.]

*What do you think she meant*?

The coal in his hearth was quiet for a long time.

*[I don't know*,] said Ember at last. Honestly. The way it always was now, since the Heart, since it had gone down into his hearth and stayed there and started writing. *[I've been thinking about it all night. In between composing a very moving speech I will not be permitted to deliver.]* A pause. *[Whatever it is, it's coming slowly. Antho said far down. Very far. You won't be getting married with it in the room.]*

*That's not very comforting*.

*[It wasn't meant to be comforting*,] said Ember. *[It was meant to be true. I've given up being comforting. It never worked. You always saw through it.]*

Lex breathed out.

*[Today*,] said Ember. More gently. *[Just today. Let it be today. She'd want that. She sent the message so you'd know she was coming, not so you'd spend your wedding staring at a door.]*

*You don't know that*.

*[No*,] said Ember. *[But I know her. A little. In the way I know anything. Through him.]* It was quiet. *[She'd want you to have today.]*

---

There was a knock on the door.

It opened before he could answer. Antho came in.

Lex stared at him.

His brother was wearing black. Of course he was. A long coat, plain and fitted, with high collar and silver buttons, over a shirt so white it hurt. His long black hair was tied back at the nape of his neck. His beard was trimmed close. His grey eyes were clear and calm and very, very tired.

He looked like a duke. Or a poet. Or a very well-dressed undertaker.

He was holding a fish.

A large silver fish. By the tail. It was still damp.

"*It got out*," said Antho.

"*What*?"

"*The fish. Teo's fish. The wedding present.*" Antho held it up. It flapped once, weakly, in a disgruntled sort of way. "*It got out of its barrel. I don't know how. It doesn't have legs. I found it in the passage outside the cold-room. Flapping. Towards the door.*"

"*The black door*?"

"*The front door*." Antho looked at it. "*I think it wants to go outside.*"

"*It's a fish*."

"*I'm aware it's a fish*." His brother's mouth did the thing it did. The almost-smile. The one that was exactly like Lex's, which nobody had ever been able to explain. "*I've been holding it for ten minutes. I don't know what to do with it. If I put it back it'll just get out again. If I let it go it'll die. If I give it to Odile she'll name it.*"

"*Give it to Teo*."

"*Teo's doing Seraphine's hair*."

Lex looked at him.

"*He's very good at it*," said Antho. "*It's the Tide. Hair's mostly water, apparently. He made it curl.*"

---

They put the fish in a bucket.

It took both of them. It was a large fish, and slippery, and had opinions. By the time it was in the bucket, in a corner of Seraphine's old study, glaring at them, Lex's best shirt had a long wet streak down the front and Antho had scales in his beard.

They stood there and looked at each other.

And started laughing.

Lex couldn't stop. He leaned against the wall and laughed until his ribs hurt and his eyes ran, and Antho sat down on a crate of fish-food and put his face in his hands and shook, and neither of them could have said why, except that they hadn't slept and their mother was coming home and they were getting married in an hour in front of a door at the bottom of a mountain, and there was a fish in a bucket, and somehow it was all one thing.

"*Brannoc's going to kill me*," said Lex. When he could. Looking down at the shirt. "*He tied the collar.*"

"*Brannoc's going to cry*," said Antho. Wiping his eyes. "*He's been crying since breakfast. Hedda had to take him for a walk.*"

"*He's not crying*."

"*He's crying*," said Antho. "*He told Pim it was the onions. There weren't any onions. Pim checked.*"

---

He changed the shirt. He had a second one. Wren had made him bring it, because she knew him.

Brannoc tied the collar again. He didn't say anything about the first one. He just looked at the wet heap of linen on the floor, and at the bucket in the corner, and at the fish in the bucket glaring, and sighed the long weary sigh of a man who has been raising Vey boys for thirteen years and has given up expecting anything.

Then Arion came to fetch them.

He stood in the doorway in a plain grey coat. The same one he'd worn to the masquerade at Sorn's house, a year and a half ago, when he was still forty-five and always would be. It hung a little looser on him now. He was thinner. Greyer. Eleven grey hairs had become twenty since the morning before. Lex could see them. At the temples. Over the ears. And the bold one at the front, curling like a question mark.

His eyes were red. He'd been crying too. Everybody had.

"*It's time*," said Arion.

He looked at his sons. Both of them. Standing in a storeroom full of fish-food, in their wedding clothes.

He didn't say anything else. He didn't need to. He just looked. For a long moment. The way you look at something you didn't think you'd live to see, and are trying to remember properly, so that you'll have it later, when you need it.

Then he cleared his throat.

"*Your mother*," said Arion Vey, "*would have said you both need a haircut.*"

---

The Hollowmaw was a mountain with a cave in it, and the cave went down.

A long passage. Sloping. Turning. Down through the living rock of the Greyspine, under the snow and the ice and the wind, into the dark. And all the way down, on both sides of the passage, in niches cut into the stone and on shelves and ledges and iron brackets hammered into the walls, lanterns.

One thousand one hundred and eleven of them.

Antho had lit the first one in the summer after Duskhold, with his own hands, in the cave where he'd lived alone for eight years. Then a second. Then a hundred. Then a thousand. People brought them. From all over Caldor. From the Sunreach. From the twelve villages. Old lanterns and new. Tin and iron and brass and glass. Paper ones, from the Saltreach. A great carved stone one, from the Ironbacks, that had taken four men to carry. Every one of them burning. Every one of them lit for someone who hadn't come home.

The Lantern Keep.

Lex walked down the passage between his brother and his father, past all of them, the way he'd walked down it a dozen times before. But it was different today. Today there were people on both sides of the passage, pressed against the walls between the lanterns. Hundreds of them. Watching. Smiling. Crying. Reaching out to touch his sleeve, or Antho's, as they went by.

Mother Orla, from the Crooked Kettle, with her hand over her mouth. Pell, in his ranger's green, grinning like an idiot. Hollis Penn, the baker, with flour on his good coat. Corin Bell and his sister Mella. Roole and Fenn in their Keepers' grey. Benno, the boy standard-bearer, with the black and grey banner of the Ashen Banners held up straight and his young face set like stone, the way Teodra Hale's had been.

Tilda Crane. With Brannoc's hammer on her back. Who saw Lex looking and went bright red and waved, frantically, with both hands.

Bess Rook. From Fen Hollow. Eighty-one years old. Who had walked three hundred miles with a stick. Who caught Lex's sleeve as he passed and pulled him down, with surprising strength, and kissed him on the cheek, and said, very loudly, so the whole passage heard: "*You're the big smith who made the lights come on.*"

"*Yes*," said Lex, red to the ears.

"*Well, don't muck it up*," said Bess Rook. And let him go.

---

The passage opened out at the bottom into a cavern.

Round. High. Shaped like the inside of a bell. With walls of black stone that glittered faintly in the lantern-light, and a floor of black stone worn smooth by ten thousand years of nothing and three years of footsteps.

And at the far end, set into the wall, a door.

Black glass. Tall. Smooth. Sealed. A door to nowhere. A door to the dark under the world. With a narrow niche cut into the stone at its left side, at the height of a woman's hand.

Three lamps in the niche.

A small brown clay one, round and plain, with a chip in its spout and a thumbprint in its base. A squat iron one, with twenty Hush-runes on its glass. And a new one. Small. Iron. Lex's. With a single rune on the glass, which said *ARDENT*.

All three burning. Small and gold and steady. Straight up.

The cavern was full of lanterns too. On every ledge. In every crack. And full of people, standing in a great crowd that filled the floor from wall to wall and pressed up the slope of the passage behind them. The Queen of Caldor, in blue, with a silver circlet. Queen Ishara of the Sunreach, in gold, with a hawk on her shoulder. Elowen Dunmore, in her armour, with Oathkeeper drawn and point-down on the stone in front of her. Kestrel Varn, with her hair burning quietly gold. Ilse and Ivo Marrow, in matching green, holding hands.

And in front of the door, waiting, with a book in her hands and a lantern hanging from a hook on her staff, Sister Agathe of the Ashbourne chapel.

She was very old now. Small and stooped and wrinkled, in the grey robe of the Lady of Lanterns, with her white hair in a knot. She had baptised Lex. She had buried half of Ashbourne. She had stood in the chapel doorway on the night of the gloamhounds and shouted at them until they went away.

She looked at the two of them coming toward her. Through her thick spectacles. With her mouth set in a thin line.

"*Late*," said Sister Agathe.

"*There was a fish*," said Lex.

"*I don't want to know*," said Sister Agathe.

---

They stood in front of the door.

Lex on the left. Antho on the right. Their father a little behind them, between them, with his hands clasped behind his back. Brannoc behind Lex. Hedda Vorne behind Antho, because Antho had asked her to, and Hedda had said *what on earth for* and then gone very quiet and said *all right*.

And Pim.

Pim stood in front of all of them. Right in front of the door. Twelve years old, or thirteen, or fourteen; nobody knew for certain, least of all Pim. Small and thin and solemn, with a mop of black hair that refused to lie flat and the grey-violet eyes that went dark when he was Listening to Shade. He was wearing a coat Seraphine had made him, blue, with brass buttons, of which he was enormously proud.

He was holding a velvet cushion.

On the cushion were two rings. One black iron, plain, made from a hundred-year-old nail. One silver, plain, with a single rune on the inside, which also said *ARDENT*.

Pim had been practicing holding the cushion for six days. He had held it at breakfast. He had held it at lunch. He had held it in his sleep, Odile reported, with his arms out straight, and had woken in a panic at two in the morning shouting that he'd dropped it.

He held it now as if it were made of glass, and the rings were made of glass, and the air was made of glass, and if he breathed it would all shatter.

"*Breathe*, *Pim*," murmured Antho.

Pim breathed. Very carefully. Through his nose.

---

Then the music started.

It wasn't much. A fiddle. A bone flute. A little Sunreach drum, tapped softly with the fingers. Ilse played the fiddle. Ishara's drummer played the drum. And the bone flute—

Lex turned his head.

It was his father. Arion Vey. Standing behind them with Antho's old bone flute at his lips. The one Antho had played for eight years in a cave to keep the Murmur away. Playing *The Lantern Song*.

Not well. His long fingers were stiff. He'd never been a musician. He missed a note in the second line, and found it again, and went on.

*Little light, little light, burning low.*

The crowd at the back of the cavern parted.

And Wren came down the passage.

---

She came on her mother's arm.

Maren Ashdown was small and thin and had been ill for most of Wren's life. Grey-lung, from the Gloam in the old mines at Coldwater, that the physics of Highcrest couldn't cure. She'd been getting better since Duskhold, five years ago, when the Gloam went out of the world. Slowly. A little each year. She walked with a stick now, instead of being carried. She had color in her cheeks.

She walked down the long passage of the Lantern Keep with her stick in one hand and her daughter on her other arm, and she held her head up like a queen.

And Wren—

Lex had seen Wren Ashdown in a lot of things. Mud. Snow. Blood, her own and other people's. A borrowed green gown in a wrecked ballroom with no shoes on. Nothing at all, once, in a hot spring in the Greyspine, by accident, which had been the most embarrassing day of his life.

He had never seen her in this.

It was green. Of course it was green. Dark green, like pine-needles, like the forests above Thistledown in winter. Plain. Long. With narrow sleeves and a high neck and no lace and no jewels. She'd made it herself. With Seraphine's help, and Ilse's, and a great deal of swearing. She'd had a hood on the cloak, and the hood was down, and her fox-red hair was loose. All of it. Down her back. He'd never seen it loose in public. She always braided it. Practical.

There were small white flowers in it. Snowdrops. From the slope outside. Pim had picked them that morning, in the dark, in the snow.

She had her bow on her back.

Of course she did.

She came down the passage toward him with her mother on her arm and her bow on her back and snowdrops in her hair, and she was looking straight at him, with her green eyes very bright, and she was trying not to laugh.

Lex couldn't breathe.

*[Breathe*,] said Ember. Very softly.

He didn't.

---

And behind her, the second bride.

Seraphine Ilvane came on the Queen's arm.

Lysandra had asked. She'd come to the Keep a month ago, on her own, with no guard but Elowen, and sat in the library with Seraphine for an afternoon, and come out with her eyes red. *Her father's dead*, she'd said to Antho afterward. *Drowned. When she was eleven. And she said there was nobody else. And I said I'd be honored.*

The Queen of Caldor walked down the passage in her blue dress with her silver circlet on her brow and the Royal Archivist of Caldor on her arm.

Seraphine was in grey. Silver-grey, like the inside of a shell, with long sleeves to hide the scars on her wrists from the Pale Wastes, where the Tide had healed her. Her dark hair was up, and curled, and full of tiny silver pins. Teo had done it. Teo stood at the side of the passage, dripping slightly, looking extremely proud.

She was wearing her spectacles.

She'd tried not to. She'd told Lex, in the library, a week ago, very seriously, that brides didn't wear spectacles. That it wasn't done. That there were books about it.

*Then how will you see him*? Lex had said.

She'd stared at him. And then gone very pink. And worn them.

She was holding a little notebook. A small one, bound in leather, the size of her palm. In her other hand, against her chest. Lex knew what was in it. Antho didn't.

She came down the passage on the Queen's arm and looked at Antho through her spectacles. And Antho, standing beside Lex in his black coat with fish-scales still in his beard, made a small sound. Like a man who has been hit very gently in the chest.

---

They stood in front of the black door. Four of them. In a row.

Sister Agathe looked at them over her book.

"*Well*," she said. "*Here we all are.*"

And she began.

---

Lex didn't hear most of it.

He heard Sister Agathe's thin old voice going on, about the Lady of Lanterns, and the light in the window, and the long road home. He heard Pim breathing very carefully through his nose. He heard the lanterns hiss, a thousand of them, very softly, all round. He heard Brannoc behind him, crying. Not quietly.

But mostly he heard Wren.

Not her voice. She wasn't saying anything. Just—her. Beside him. The small sounds of her. Her breath. The creak of her bow-string against the green cloth. The way she shifted her weight from one foot to the other, the way she always did when she had to stand still for too long. He'd have known those sounds anywhere. In the dark. In his sleep. In the bottom of a Wellworld with fire coming out of his mouth.

He'd heard them every day for five years.

He wanted to hear them every day for fifty more.

---

"*The vows*," said Sister Agathe. "*If there are vows. I'm told there are vows.*" She peered at Lex. "*Short ones, I hope. I'm ninety-one.*"

Lex turned to face Wren.

He'd written it down. Three times. He'd burned all three. Ember had offered to help, and he'd said no, and Ember had said *[good. I'd have made it rhyme.]*

He opened his mouth.

And nothing came out.

Wren looked up at him. Her green eyes very bright. Her mouth twitching.

"*Hammer*," she whispered. "*You've forgotten it.*"

"*I've forgotten it*," he agreed.

The cavern rippled with laughter. Soft. Fond. Brannoc laughed, through his crying. Kestrel laughed so loud Ishara's hawk flapped.

Lex looked down at Wren. At the snowdrops in her hair. At the bow on her back.

*[Just say it*,] said Ember. *[Whatever it is. Say it the way you'd say it to her at the fire.]*

So he did.

"*I was a Common smith, Level nine, Ceiling twenty-four*," said Lex. "*When I met you. You charged me two silver to walk me up a mountain, and you said I walked like a bear in a china shop.*"

"*You did*," said Wren.

"*I still do*." He swallowed. "*And then I lost everything. My class. My fire. Everything the Stone said I was. And I was nothing. A man with a hammer and no numbers. And you stayed.*" His voice cracked. "*You chose me with nothing. Before anyone else did. Before I did.*"

The cavern was very quiet.

"*I don't know how to say it better than that*," said Lex. "*I'm not good at words. I'm good at making things. So I made you a ring out of a nail from the first forge I ever worked in. And I put a word on the inside.*" He looked at her. "*That's the vow. That's all of it. It's on the inside. Where you'll see it every time you put it on.*"

He took the black iron ring off Pim's cushion. Pim exhaled, enormously, like a bellows.

He held it out.

Wren looked at it. Then at him.

Then she held out her left hand. And he put it on her finger. And she turned it, once, with her thumb, the way she'd turned it on the roof of the Spire at dawn, and he knew she was feeling the rune on the inside, against her skin.

*STAY*.

---

"*My turn*," said Wren.

She didn't have anything written down. She'd told him she wouldn't. *If I write it down I'll read it out like a shopping list*, she'd said. *I'll just say it. And if it's terrible, it's terrible.*

She looked up at him.

"*I've been hungry*," said Wren Ashdown. Quietly. "*Properly hungry. When I was little. The winter Mam got sick, and there was nothing in the house, and I went out into the snow with Da's old bow that I couldn't draw, because I thought if I didn't, she'd die.*" She swallowed. "*I hate waste. You know I do. Food. Time. People. I hate it more than anything.*"

"*I know*."

"*So I'm not going to waste this*," said Wren. Her voice wobbled. She steadied it. "*Whatever it is. However long. I'm not going to waste one day of it. Not one.*" She lifted her chin. "*And if you go off alone again, I'll find you. I'm a tracker. It's my job. I'll find you wherever you go. And I'll be very, very angry. And I'll bring you home.*"

Somebody in the crowd sniffed loudly. It was Elowen Dunmore.

Wren took the silver ring off the cushion—no. Wait. That was Seraphine's. Pim jerked the cushion back so fast both rings bounced. The whole cavern gasped. Lex lunged. Antho lunged.

Pim caught them.

Both of them. One in each hand. Out of the air. With the lightning reflexes of a boy who had grown up on the streets of Kessick and the cliffs of the Greyspine.

There was a long silence.

Pim looked down at the two rings in his two fists. Then up at the four of them. His thin face had gone white.

"*Sorry*," said Pim. In a very small voice.

And then the whole cavern was laughing, the Queen and the knights and the dragons' riders and the bakers and the smiths, and Pim's thin face cracked into a grin, wide and fierce and astonished, and he put the rings back on the cushion very carefully, both of them, and stood up straight.

Wren hadn't got a ring for Lex.

He hadn't wanted one. He'd said so. *I'll lose it. It'll melt. I work in a forge.*

She reached up instead. And took hold of the cord round his neck. The one with Malachi's little silver bell on it. And pulled him down. And kissed him.

In front of everyone. In front of the door. In front of the Queen of Caldor and a ninety-one-year-old Sister of the Lady of Lanterns, who said, very loudly, "*I haven't got to that bit yet.*"

---

Antho's vows were numbers.

Lex had known they would be.

His brother turned to Seraphine in front of the black door, with his long hands clasped in front of him so that nobody would see them shaking. And looked at her. Through her spectacles. For a long moment.

"*Eleven*," said Antho Vey.

Seraphine blinked.

"*Eleven times*," said Antho. "*That you've saved my life. I've counted. Fort Caldris, when my Clarity was at six and you sang. The watchtower. The Pale Wastes. The night in the Barrows, which you don't know about, because I never told you how close it was. The Wellworld of Tide. The Highcrest singing. The white mountain. Four more that you'd say don't count, but they do.*" He swallowed. "*Eleven.*"

He looked at the floor.

"*I've saved yours four times*," he said. "*That's not enough. I'm behind. I'll be behind for the rest of my life.*" He looked up again. "*I'd like to spend it catching up.*"

He took the silver ring off the cushion. Pim held it very still.

"*I counted the lanterns this morning*," said Antho. "*Like I always do. One thousand one hundred and thirteen.*" He held the ring out. "*I'd like to count something else now. With you. Every day. Whatever you like. Books. Stars. Days.*" His voice went very quiet. "*I'm not good at much, Seraphine. But I'm very good at counting.*"

Seraphine was crying.

Silently. Behind her spectacles. With the tears running down and dripping off her chin onto her silver-grey dress.

She held out her hand. He put the ring on it. *ARDENT*, on the inside. A lantern's word.

Then she opened the little leather notebook.

"*I made a list*," said Seraphine Ilvane. Her voice was steady, which was a miracle, because her hands weren't. "*Of reasons. I'm a scholar. We make lists.*" She turned a page. "*It's annotated.*"

"*How long is it*?" said Sister Agathe. With deep suspicion.

"*Four hundred and twelve entries*."

"*Child*."

"*I'll only read the first one*," said Seraphine. Hastily. She looked down at the page. Then up at Antho. Then she closed the book.

"*Reason one*," she said. "*He reads to the bats.*"

Antho stared at her.

"*In the cold-room*," said Seraphine. "*At night. When he thinks nobody's listening. Every night. For three years.*" Her voice cracked. "*He reads them history. Because he says they've been in the dark so long they don't know what happened.*"

There was a pause.

"*That's the first one*," said Seraphine. "*The other four hundred and eleven are footnotes.*"

And Antho Vey, Threshold Warden, Tier IV, the man who had stood in the doorway at the bottom of the world and shut out the hunger with his own body, put his face in his hands. In front of everyone. And laughed. And cried. Both. At once.

---

That was when the lamps leaned.

Lex saw it first, because he was nearest. Out of the corner of his eye. In the niche beside the black door.

The clay lamp. The little brown one, with the chip in its spout and Seren's thumbprint in the base.

Its flame bent.

Not much. Not like last night. Just a little. A hair. Toward the black glass. The way a candle flame bends toward an open window. Toward a draft. Toward someone breathing very softly on the other side of a door.

And then the iron one. And then the new one, the *ARDENT* one, Lex's. All three of them. Leaning. Gently. Together.

As if someone very far away had stopped walking. And turned. And was listening.

The cavern went quiet.

Not everyone saw it. But enough did. Arion did. Lex heard his father make a sound behind him. Small. Like a man who has been holding his breath for a year and a half.

Antho took his hands away from his face. And looked at the lamps. And then at the door.

"*Mam*," he said. Very softly.

The flames held there. Leaning. For a long moment. Three small gold lights, bent toward the black glass.

And then they straightened. Slowly. One by one. And burned straight up again.

Sister Agathe looked at the lamps over her spectacles. And then at the two brothers. And then at their father. And her thin old face did something it almost never did.

"*Well*," said Sister Agathe. Very gently. "*That's everyone, then.*"

She closed her book.

"*By the light of the Lady of Lanterns*," she said, "*who keeps the door for the ones who go, and the lamp for the ones who come home: you're married. All four of you. Gods help you.*" She sniffed. "*Now you can kiss them. Properly. In the right order.*"

---

The feast went on until the stars came out, and then until the stars went round.

It was held on the slope outside the cave mouth, in the snow, around twenty fires. There were tables made of planks and barrels. There was Mother Orla's bacon, which she had brought from Ashbourne in a cart, wrapped in straw. There was Hollis Penn's bread, a wagon of it, in every shape: loaves and rolls and plaits and a great round one shaped like a lantern, which Hollis Penn carried round himself from fire to fire, so that everyone could see it before they ate it. There were Sunreach dates and figs from Ishara's riders, and smoked fish from the Saltreach, and something purple in a pot that Hedda had made, which nobody could identify and everybody was afraid to ask about.

There was wine. There was a great deal of wine.

There was dancing.

Lex danced with Wren. Badly. He danced like a bear in a china shop. She told him so. He trod on her foot. She trod on his, harder, on purpose. They laughed so much they had to stop.

He danced with Maren Ashdown, very slowly, holding her stick for her. He danced with Bess Rook, who led. He danced with Tilda Crane, who was so nervous she forgot which hand was which and spun the wrong way into Pell. He danced with Queen Ishara of the Sunreach, which was terrifying, because she was taller than him and danced like a sword-fight.

He did not dance with Queen Lysandra of Caldor, because she was dancing with Elowen Dunmore. Both of them. Together. In the snow by the biggest fire. And laughing.

Nobody said anything about it. Nobody needed to.

The Marrow Players did a play.

It was about a wedding. A dragon's wedding, Ilse said. With a lot of illusion. The dragon was made of light, and enormous, and wore a veil, and the groom was a very small, very nervous mouse who kept falling off the dragon's head. Ivo played the mouse. He fell off the cart four times. On the fifth, Ashka, sprawled on the ridge above the fires with her mother, lifted her great blue head and *caught* him, gently, in her jaws, and set him back on the cart.

The crowd went wild. Ivo bowed. Shakily. With dragon-spit in his hair.

Prudence the cow got loose during the curtain call and walked straight through the illusion of the dragon, which flickered and went out. She stopped in front of the cart and looked at Ilse with deep disapproval and mooed. Once. Very loudly.

Then she went and found Correspondence the goat, who was eating Hedda's purple thing out of the pot, and headbutted him into a snowdrift.

Tamsin Varn chased her up the slope shouting her name. Kestrel laughed so hard she set fire to the tablecloth.

---

Later, much later, Lex sat on a rock a little way up the slope with his brother.

The fires were burning low. The music had gone soft. Most of the guests were asleep, or nearly. Rolled in blankets by the fires. Curled in the tents. Brannoc was snoring under a table with his head on a sack of flour and Hedda's shawl over him. Pim was asleep on Arion's lap, by the fire at the cave mouth, with his blue coat buttoned to the chin. Arion wasn't asleep. He was sitting very still, so as not to wake the boy, looking at the fire.

Wren and Seraphine were by the next fire down. Talking. Heads together. Laughing about something. Every so often one of them looked up the slope at the rock where their husbands were sitting, and said something, and the other one laughed harder.

"*They're talking about us*," said Antho.

"*They're definitely talking about us*."

"*Wren told her about the hot spring*."

Lex choked. "*How do you know about the hot spring*?"

"*Everyone knows about the hot spring*," said Antho. "*Ashka told Vharuun. Vharuun told me. In images. It was very vivid.*"

Lex put his face in his hands.

His brother laughed. Softly. The real laugh. The one that had taken years to come back.

Then it stopped.

Lex looked up.

Antho was rubbing his temple. With two fingers. Slowly. The way he'd done in the old days, in the cave, when the Murmur was rising. His clear grey eyes were narrowed. As if he was listening to something very far off. Or trying not to.

"*Antho*?"

"*Mm*."

"*You all right*?"

His brother took his hand away from his temple. Looked at it. Then at Lex. And smiled.

"*I'm fine*," said Antho. "*Long day. Long night. Too much of Hedda's purple thing.*" He shook his head. "*It's nothing.*"

Lex looked at him.

He knew that smile. He'd known it for five years. It was the smile Antho used when he'd decided something was his to carry. Alone. For now.

He opened his mouth to say something.

And Ember said, very quietly, in his hearth: *[Lex. Not tonight.]*

So he didn't.

He sat on the rock beside his brother on the night of their wedding and looked down at the fires and the sleeping people and the two dragons on the ridge and their father with a boy asleep in his lap. And he let it be tonight. Just tonight.

He'd ask tomorrow.

He woke an hour before dawn.

He didn't know why. The fire was down to embers. The slope was quiet. The stars were very bright, and very cold, and the snow on the Greyspine glittered under them like salt.

Something had woken him. A sound. Or the absence of one.

He lay still and listened.

Nothing. Wind. The fire ticking as it cooled. Wren breathing. Far off, down the slope, Prudence the cow lowing once in her sleep.

He sat up.

The mouth of the Hollowmaw was a gold arch in the dark. The lanterns inside it glowing. One thousand one hundred and thirteen of them, all the way down. The light spilled out onto the snow in a long soft fan.

Antho was standing in it.

His brother. Alone. In his black wedding coat, with his long hair loose now, and the Lantern in one hand with its shutter closed. Standing just inside the cave mouth. Very still. Looking at something on the wall.

Lex got up. Quietly. So as not to wake Wren. He went up the slope through the snow, and stopped beside his brother, and looked where Antho was looking.

At a lantern.

One of the first ones. Just inside the mouth of the cave. Old tin, battered, with a horn pane instead of glass. Lex knew it. It had come from Fenmoor. From the first Gloam-freed village. A woman had brought it, the summer after Duskhold, for her husband, who hadn't come back from the Quiet.

It was dark.

Not out. Not empty. Lex could see the oil in its little tin well, still half full. The wick was trimmed. It hadn't blown out; there was no wind in the cave, there never was.

It was just *dark*. As if the light had been taken out of it. Lifted out, neatly, the way you'd lift a pearl out of an oyster.

And on the horn pane, on the outside, there was dust.

Fine. Soft. Grey-gold. Like the powder off a moth's wing.

"*Antho*," said Lex.

His brother didn't answer. He was looking past the lantern. Into the dark of the passage. Down. Toward the bell-shaped cavern, and the black door, and the three lamps.

Lex looked too.

Something was moving in the dark between the lanterns. Low. Slow. Drifting. Grey-gold, catching the light as it passed each one. The size of a man's hand. Wings. Two pairs. Patterned with rings and whorls. And on each wing, an eye. A dark painted eye, ringed in gold, that seemed to look back at them as it turned.

A moth.

It drifted down the passage, from lantern to lantern. It didn't land. It didn't stop. It just passed close to each one, close enough to brush it with the edge of a wing.

And each one it brushed flickered. Just for a moment. As if something had touched the flame and tasted it.

"*There are no moths*," said Antho. Very quietly. "*Not this high. Not in winter. Not in the Greyspine.*"

They watched it go. Down and down. Until it was a speck of grey-gold in the far-off glow at the bottom of the passage. Until it was gone.

Toward the door.

Toward the three lamps.

Lex turned and looked at the dark lantern by the cave mouth. The Fenmoor one. With the dust on its pane.

And his brother, beside him, with the Lantern in his hand and his clear grey eyes very wide, did what he always did.

He counted.

"*One thousand one hundred and twelve*," said Antho Vey.
