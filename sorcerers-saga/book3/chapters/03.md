# The Delve of Might-Have-Been

There were no stairs.

Lex had been in a lot of Delves. Eleven, he'd counted once, for Seraphine, who'd wanted it for a footnote. The Frostvault, which had stairs cut into blue ice. The Drowned Archive, which had a spiral ramp going down into black water. A Delve in the Bitter Hills that was nothing but a well-shaft with iron rungs. A Delve in the Sunreach that you went into by walking backward through a mirror.

They all had a way in. A threshold. A first step.

This one had a hole. A round flat perfect dark, like a pool of ink, in the grass beside the Ashbourne Stone. No edge. No lip. No sides.

"*So we jump*," said Wren.

"*We don't know how far down it goes*."

"*So we jump carefully*."

Lex looked at her. She was standing at the edge of the dark with her bow over her shoulder and her knives in her belt and the wind curling round her boots, impatient, the way it always did when she was about to go somewhere it couldn't follow.

"*I'll go first*," said Lex.

"*You'll go first because you're heaviest and you'll hit the bottom first and tell us if there is one*," said Wren. "*Not because you're being noble. Say it.*"

"*I'm going first because I'm heaviest*."

"*Good*." She kissed him. Quick. Hard. "*Don't die. We've only been married three weeks. I haven't finished being annoyed with you about the door.*"

"*I'll fix the door*."

"*You've said that for five months*."

He stepped off the edge.

---

He didn't fall.

He went *through*. That was the only way he could describe it afterward, to Seraphine, who wrote it down. Not down. Through. Like walking through a curtain in a dark room. A soft cool pressure all over him at once, and a sense of the world turning over, gently, the way a page turns.

And then he was standing up.

On a floor. Smooth. Black. Glassy. Like the floor of the cavern at the bottom of the Hollowmaw. Faintly warm under his boots.

In a corridor.

It was long. He couldn't see the end of it. It went away in both directions, straight, into a soft grey dimness that wasn't quite dark and wasn't quite light. The walls were the same black glass as the floor. The ceiling was too high to see.

And on both sides of the corridor, as far as he could see in either direction: doors.

Hundreds of them. Wooden doors and iron doors and painted doors and doors of plain planks with rope handles. Tall doors and short ones. A red door with a brass knocker shaped like a fish. A low round green door like the door of a hill-farm. A door that was just a curtain, blue, stirring very slightly as if someone had just walked through.

Every one of them was closed.

And under every one, along the bottom, a line of light.

---

Wren arrived behind him. Lightly. On her toes. She looked round once and her hand went to her bow.

Then Tilda, who landed badly and sat down hard and said a word Brannoc had certainly taught her.

Then Pell. Who landed perfectly, because he was a Ranger and landing perfectly was apparently a thing Rangers did, and looked round, and said, "*Oh. Oh, I don't like this.*"

"*Nobody likes this*," said Wren.

Lex read it.

*The doors you didn't open*.

He looked at the nearest one. On his left. An ordinary door. Oak, with an iron latch, worn smooth by hands. The kind of door you saw in every house in the Vale. He knew it. He'd known it all his life.

It was the door of the cottage at Harrow.

The old one. The one that burned.

---

"*Don't*," said Wren.

He hadn't moved. He realized he'd been about to. His hand had come up. Toward the latch.

"*Don't touch them*," said Wren. Her voice was very tight. "*Any of them. Look.*"

He looked where she was looking.

Across the corridor. A door. Plain planks. Grey with age. With a horseshoe nailed over it, the wrong way up, the way they did it in Thistledown, so the luck wouldn't run out.

"*That's ours*," said Wren. "*Mam's. The house in Thistledown. Where I grew up.*" She swallowed. "*I can hear her through it. Coughing.*"

Lex could hear it too. Faint. Behind the grey planks. A woman's cough. The deep wet rattling cough that Maren Ashdown had had every winter of Wren's childhood. The one that had eased, slowly, year by year, since Duskhold.

It wasn't stopping now. Behind that door, it was getting worse.

"*It's the one where you didn't come*," said Wren. Very quietly. "*Isn't it. The one where you never came up the road to Thistledown. Where nobody went to Duskhold. Where the Gloam stayed in the mines.*"

She didn't touch it. She stood very still in the middle of the corridor, with her bow in her hands, and listened to her mother cough behind a door that wasn't there.

Then she turned her back on it.

"*Let's go*," said Wren Ashdown.

---

They walked.

Down the corridor. In a line. Lex first, then Wren, then Tilda, then Pell at the back with an arrow on his string. Past the doors. Not looking. Trying not to look.

It was hard.

They all had them. Every one of them. Doors they knew. Doors they'd walked past, once, a long time ago, and not gone through. Pell's was a mill door, with flour on the step, and behind it the clack-clack-clack of a mill-wheel and a man's voice, older, Pell's own voice, singing a miller's song. *The life where I took the class the Stone gave me*, Pell said, white-faced. *The life where I stayed a miller.* Tilda's was the door of a farmhouse in the Ironback foothills, which she'd left at fourteen, and behind it a girl's voice, her own, much younger, crying.

They didn't stop.

Lex didn't let them.

*[This is cruel*,] said Ember. Very quietly. In his hearth. *[Whoever made this, it's cruel.]*

*I don't think it means to be*.

*[How can you tell*?]

*I don't know*, thought Lex. *It doesn't feel cruel. It feels*—

He didn't have the word. He reached for it, the way he'd reach for a tool.

*Lost*, he thought. *It feels lost. Like a child who's gone into every room of a house looking for someone, and opened every door, and left them all open.*

---

The first of the Unfinished came out of a door they'd passed.

Tilda saw it first. She turned, because she'd heard something, and screamed. Short. Sharp. And swung Brannoc's hammer up into her hands in one movement, the way Lex had taught her.

It was a wolf.

Or the idea of one. Drawn in lines. Charcoal-black lines on the grey air, like a sketch on a page, the kind of sketch Seraphine did in the margins of her notebooks when she was thinking. A long back. A narrow head. Four legs. A tail. No fur, no flesh, no eyes. Just the lines. Moving.

It came out through the oak door of a house Lex didn't know, as if the door wasn't there, and stood in the corridor, and turned its blank lined head toward them.

And then more. From other doors. Up and down the corridor. Wolves and dogs and something that might have been a horse. A man made of lines, walking stiffly. A bird, flapping, that never quite finished being a bird; one wing kept trying to be a hand.

"*Back to back*," said Lex.

They were already doing it. Five years. Four people who'd fought together in the Greyspine and the Glass Desert and the Wellworlds and the long night under the lid. They turned into a ring in the middle of the corridor without thinking, Lex and Tilda facing one way, Wren and Pell the other.

The lined wolf came at Lex.

He hit it.

He didn't use Anvilsong. He didn't need to. He put his fire into his fist and struck it in the chest the way he'd strike a bar of iron on an anvil, hard and clean, with Flame Commanding behind it, and he felt the fire go out of him into the lines and—

Through.

Straight through. The fire went through the wolf the way a hammer goes through smoke. The charcoal lines wavered. Bent. Came back. And the wolf closed its blank jaws on his wrist.

---

It didn't bite. It had no teeth. It had lines where teeth would be.

It was *cold*.

Not ice-cold. Not the cold of the Greyspine in winter. A different cold. An *empty* cold. Like putting your hand into a room where nobody has lived for a hundred years. It went up his wrist into his arm. And where it went, he felt himself go—

Thin.

He looked down. At his own hand, in the lined wolf's mouth.

The edges of it were blurring. His fingers. His knuckles. The burn-scars on the back of his hand. Going soft at the edges, like a drawing someone has rubbed with their thumb. Going *lined*.

It was taking his shape.

Not eating him. Not hurting him. Just—borrowing. The way a child borrows a coat that's too big for it. Reaching into him for the lines it didn't have.

"*Lex*!"

Wren's arrow went through the wolf's head. Straight through. Like his fire. The lines wavered and came back.

Tilda swung Brannoc's hammer. It went through the wolf's back. The lines bent around it like water.

Nothing worked. Nothing *touched* it. And his hand was getting thinner, and his wrist, and the cold was in his elbow now, and he could feel it reaching further, reaching for the rest of him, patient and lost and *wanting*—

*Almost*, said the net.

*Almost*.

---

He thought: *it's not finished*.

He didn't know where the thought came from. It came up out of his hands, the way most of his thoughts did. Out of the part of him that had stood at an anvil every day since he was eleven.

*It's not finished. It's a sketch. A pattern. A mould with no metal in it.*

*You can't break a pattern. There's nothing there to break.*

*You can only*—

He stopped trying to hit it.

He put his other hand on the wolf's lined back. Flat. Gently. The way you'd put your hand on a horse that was frightened. And instead of pushing fire into it, he *listened* to it.

It was very simple. That was the first thing. Very simple and very empty. It wanted to be a wolf. That was all. It had been trying to be a wolf for a long time, and it didn't know how, and nobody had ever shown it, and it had found these lines somewhere, these shapes, the shapes of wolves people had *nearly* seen, and it had put them on like a coat and gone looking for the rest.

*All right*, thought Lex. *All right. Here.*

And he did what he would have done at the forge. With a bar of iron that didn't know what it wanted to be. He gave it a shape.

---

Not his own shape. That was what it had been taking. He gave it a wolf's.

He reached into the Stone in him, the Well he Commanded, and drew up iron. Real iron. From the ground under Ashbourne, from the old ore-seams the miners had worked for three hundred years. Not much. A handful. And he poured it into the lines. Into the pattern. Gently. Filling it. The way you pour metal into a mould.

He felt it take.

The lines thickened. Went solid. Went dark. Went grey. The charcoal sketch of a wolf filled up with iron from the nose to the tail, all at once, the way a cup fills when you hold it under a pump.

And then it was a wolf.

An iron wolf. Small. The size of a dog. Rough-cast and grey and plain, with no eyes, standing in the corridor on four iron legs with Lex's hand still in its mouth.

It let go.

It looked at him. With no eyes. Its iron head on one side.

Then it sat down. Very carefully. On the black glass floor. And was still.

Finished.

He looked at his hand.

It was his hand again. Solid. Scarred. Sharp-edged. The cold was going out of his elbow, slowly, like water draining from a sink.

He looked up.

Wren was staring at him. Tilda was staring at him. Pell had an arrow halfway to his ear and was staring at him over it.

The rest of the Unfinished had stopped too. All of them. Up and down the corridor. The lined dogs and the lined horse and the lined man and the bird that kept trying to be a hand. Standing still. Turned toward the iron wolf sitting on the floor. As if they were looking at it. As if they *could* look.

"*It wants to be finished*," said Lex. Hoarsely. "*That's all. They all do. They're not hunting us. They're borrowing. They want shapes.*"

"*So you*—" Tilda's eyes were enormous. "*So you made it into a*—"

"*I finished it*." He looked at her. "*Tilda. Can you hear the fire?*"

"*Yes*. *Always.*"

"*Then you can do this*." He nodded at the lined dog nearest her. "*Don't hit it. Put your hand on it. Listen. Find out what it wants to be. Then give it something to be it with.*"

Tilda Crane looked at the lined dog. And at Brannoc's hammer, in her hands. And at Lex.

She set the hammer down.

She put her small scarred hand on the dog's lined back.

---

She made it out of fire.

Not iron. Fire. Lex wouldn't have thought of it. A dog of fire, red and gold and flickering, the size of a sheepdog, curling up at her feet on the black glass floor like a hearth-cat. Warm. Bright. Finished.

Tilda sat down very suddenly next to it.

"*Oh*," she said. "*Oh, Master Lex.*"

*[It*,] said Ember. Slowly. *[The net said* it. *Who's* it*?]*

*The Delve*, thought Lex.

*[Delves aren't* it*. Delves are places. The net doesn't call them* it*. In nine hundred years of records, the net has never once said a Delve was surprised.]*

Lex didn't answer.

He was looking at the end of the corridor. Where, a moment ago, there had been nothing but grey dimness going on forever.

There was a door there now.

The last door. At the very end. Plain black. No handle. No latch. No light underneath it.

It stood open.

---

The second floor was forges.

Lex knew it the moment he stepped through. He smelled it before he saw it. Coal-smoke and hot iron and quench-water and leather, the smell he'd grown up in, the smell that was more like home than any house.

A long room. Low-roofed. Stone-walled. With a hearth at the far end, and an anvil in front of the hearth, and the coals glowing red.

Brannoc's forge.

The old one. The little one. The one with a single anvil, before the long hall, before the twenty hearths, before any of it. Exactly as it had been when Lex was eleven. The bench under the window with the tongs hung over it. The quench-barrel with the dent in its side. Slag the cat asleep on the woodpile. The little painted wooden box on the shelf over the door.

And at the anvil, working, a man.

Not Brannoc.

---

Lex stopped.

The man at the anvil was big. Bigger than Lex. Bald. Soot-bearded. He was working a bar of iron with a hammer Lex knew, swinging it in a rhythm Lex knew, humming a hymn off-key that Lex knew.

But it wasn't Brannoc. It was Brannoc's shape. Brannoc's size. Brannoc's hammer. With nobody in it.

Lines.

Charcoal-black lines on the dim air, drawing a smith at an anvil. Over and over. The hammer rising. Falling. Rising. Falling. Ringing on iron that wasn't there.

And beside the anvil, working the bellows, another figure.

Small. Thin. Eleven years old, or near enough. Dark curly hair, badly cut. Lines.

It was him.

Lex. At eleven. The way he'd been the first night, when Brannoc brought him in from the ashes at Harrow and sat him by the forge and gave him the bellows to work, because he couldn't stop shaking, and Brannoc said the work would help.

The lined boy worked the bellows. The lined smith swung the hammer. Over and over. Ringing.

Lex stood in the doorway and couldn't move.

---

*[It's not real*,] said Ember. *[Lex. It's not real. It's the Delve. It's a door you didn't open.]*

*No*, thought Lex. *It's a door I did open.*

He couldn't take his eyes off them.

*This happened. This is real. This is the night Brannoc took me in.* He swallowed. *So why is it here? Why is it a might-have-been?*

And then he understood.

The lined boy at the bellows turned his head. Slowly. Toward the door. Toward Lex.

And his lined face, his eleven-year-old face, went *wrong*.

The lines bent. Shifted. The boy's mouth opened. His eyes, which weren't eyes, which were just two dark loops of charcoal, went wide. And black. All the way black. And his shoulders hunched, and his small hands clenched on the bellows, and from somewhere inside the lines came a sound. A sound Lex hadn't heard in five years.

A whisper. Grinding. Broken.

*hungry... so hungry... little brother smells of warm iron...*

The Murmur.

Coming out of *his* mouth. His eleven-year-old mouth.

---

*The might-have-been where it was me*.

He knew it. All at once. Cold and certain, like a nail driven into a board.

*The life where Father's seal on me failed instead of Antho's. Where I Kindled first. Where it was me in the cave for eight years. Me on the woodpile. Me with the voice.*

*And Antho at the forge*.

He looked at the lined smith at the anvil. And saw it wasn't Brannoc's shape after all. It was taller. Thinner. With long black hair tied back and a beard grown wild and long, long hands.

Antho. At the anvil. Hammering. Humming a hymn off-key. Brannoc's son. The smith. The one who'd stayed.

And Lex at the bellows with black eyes and the hunger in his mouth.

---

He didn't know how long he stood there.

He knew Wren's hand closed on his arm. Hard. He knew she said his name. Three times. He knew Tilda was standing beside him, very white, staring at the lined boy at the bellows with the Murmur coming out of his mouth, and that she knew who it was, because everyone knew who it was. Everyone in Ashbourne had heard the story.

He knew that the lined boy was coming toward him.

Slowly. Leaving the bellows. Walking across the little forge with his black loop-eyes fixed on Lex's face and his hands out. Reaching. The way the lined wolf had reached. For a shape. For lines. For the boy Lex had been, that it didn't have.

*...hungry...little brother...*

And then, under the grinding whisper, so faint he almost didn't hear it, something else.

Small. Young. Frightened. Not grinding at all.

*...sorry...*

*...didn't mean...*

*...only learned how to talk from him...it's the only voice it knows...*

*...sorry...*

---

Lex went down on one knee.

On the stone floor of a forge that didn't exist. In front of a boy made of lines who had his face and his brother's madness. And held out his hands.

Open. Palm up. Empty. The way he'd held them out to Brannoc, at eleven, on the night of the fire, when the big man said *give us your hands, lad, let's see if they're any good for anything*.

"*I know*," said Lex. Quietly. "*I know. It's all right.*"

The lined boy stopped.

"*You learned to talk from him*," said Lex. "*From my brother. Didn't you. When you were—when you were hungry. When you were the other thing. You talked through him. It was the only voice you ever had.*"

The lined boy didn't move. His loop-eyes were black and wide.

"*You don't have to use it anymore*," said Lex. "*You can find your own.*"

He didn't give it iron. Or stone. Or fire. He didn't know what to give it. He didn't know what it wanted to be.

So he gave it the only thing he could think of.

Room.

He opened his hands a little wider. And moved back. One step. On his knees on the stone. Away. Leaving a space. In front of him, between them, on the forge floor, where the lined boy could stand. Or not. Where it could be whatever it wanted. Where nobody was pushing.

*Make room*, it had said.

So he did.

---

The lined boy stood in the space for a long time.

Then the lines went soft. All of them. The black eyes. The hunched shoulders. The grinding mouth. They went soft and faint and loose, the way charcoal goes when you blow on it. And the boy came apart. Gently. Into the dim air. Like smoke going up a chimney.

And the lined smith at the anvil came apart too. And the forge. The bench and the tongs and the quench-barrel and the cat. Into lines. Into smoke. Into nothing.

And where the little forge had been, there was a floor of black glass. And a room. Round. Small. Bare.

And in the middle of the room, on the floor, a pebble.

---

It was black.

Not shiny. Not dull. Just black. The way the round dark on the green had been black. A smooth flat river-pebble, the size of a hen's egg, lying on the glass floor in the middle of the empty room.

It was the only thing there.

The Delve closed.

He felt it go. The whole thing. The round room and the forge-that-wasn't and the corridor of doors and all the small finished things sitting patiently on the black glass floor. Folding up. Softly. Behind them and under them and around them. The way a flower closes at dusk.

In Ashbourne. In the thin spring afternoon. Beside the Stone. With Wren beside him, and Tilda, and Pell. And the whole village in a great ring round the edge of the green, staring.
