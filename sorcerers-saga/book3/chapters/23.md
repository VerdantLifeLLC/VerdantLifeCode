# A Stranger with a Nice Voice

The first thing Seren Vey did, when she woke up in the world, was cry at a window.

Not a sad cry. She'd had thirteen years of sad cries. She knew the difference. This one came up out of her before she was properly awake, while she was still lying in the narrow bed in the little stone room off the library that Antho had given her, with the blanket up to her chin and her feet, for the first time in thirteen years, not hurting.

There was a window. High in the wall. Small. Cut through the rock of the mountain. And through it, the sky.

Blue.

Not Shade's dark. Not stars. Not the soft deep black of the little one, folded round her like a hand. *Blue*. Pale. Early. With one long thin cloud across it, pink at the edges, like a scarf someone had dropped.

She lay in the bed and looked at it and cried until the pillow was wet. And then got up, and put on a dress that Seraphine had found for her, too long in the hem, and went to find out what the world smelled like.

---

It smelled like porridge.

And smoke. And stone. And bat. And a goat, somewhere, very close, which turned out to be in the kitchen, eating a dishcloth.

"*That's Correspondence*," said a small voice.

She looked down.

A boy was sitting on the kitchen step. Thin. Solemn. In a blue coat with brass buttons, buttoned wrong. With a mop of black hair that refused to lie flat and grey-violet eyes that looked at her steadily, the way very few people ever looked at anything.

She knew him. She'd heard him. Through the door. For three years. The quiet one. The one with the room inside.

"*Pim*," she said.

He nodded.

"*Is it always in the kitchen*?"

"*Yes*," said Pim. "*It's Master Malachi's. It walked here from the Wastes with a box on its back.*" He considered the goat. "*It eats everything. It ate one of Master Antho's books. Volume six. He said it was an improvement.*"

Seren laughed. Out loud. In a kitchen. In the morning.

She hadn't done that in thirteen years either.

---

She made the porridge.

They let her. Odile had been going to. Odile had come in, tall and dark and solemn, a Shade-Listener, nineteen, and seen Seren standing at the stove with the pot, and stopped in the doorway with her mouth open. And then closed it. And gone out again. And Seren heard her in the passage, saying to someone in a very low voice, *she's making the porridge. Lady's light. Master Antho's mother is making the porridge.*

It was the first thing she'd cooked in thirteen years.

She burned it.

Not badly. Just at the bottom. She'd forgotten how. Forgotten that you had to stir it. Forgotten that porridge didn't just sit there, the way things sat in the deep, patient, unchanging. She stood at the stove with the wooden spoon in her hand and the smell of burnt oats coming up and laughed until she had to hold on to the shelf.

And Arion said, from the doorway, "*You always burned the porridge.*"

---

She turned round.

He was in the doorway. Sitting. In a chair with wheels on it, that someone, Brannoc probably, had made out of a kitchen chair and two cart wheels. Wrapped in a blanket. With his white head and his walnut face and his grey eyes behind his spectacles.

Looking at her.

She didn't know what to do.

She'd been home three days. She'd seen him every day. Sat with him. Read his letter. Kissed him, once, in a cavern at the bottom of a mountain, in front of everyone, without meaning to, because she'd heard his voice and it was the voice, it was *the voice*, and her body had done it before her head could stop it.

She didn't know him.

That was the truth. That was what she lay awake with. She knew his voice. She knew his letter. Forty pages of it. She knew he took salt in his porridge, because Ember had told her.

She didn't know *him*. What he did with his hands when he was nervous. What he looked like when he was angry. Whether he snored. Whether he'd laugh if she burned the porridge, or frown, or pretend not to notice.

"*Did I*?" she said.

"*Every morning*," said Arion. "*For eight years. At the cottage. You'd put it on and then you'd see something out of the window, a bird or a cloud or the boys doing something they shouldn't, and you'd go and look at it, and come back, and it'd be black at the bottom.*"

"*And what did you do*?"

He smiled. Slowly. The lines round his eyes going deep.

"*I ate it*," said Arion Vey. "*Every morning. Burnt bits and all. I told you it was how I liked it.*"

"*Was it*?"

"*No*," he said. "*It was terrible.*"

---

She brought him a bowl.

Burnt bits and all. She put salt on it without asking. He looked at the salt. Then at her.

"*Ember told me*," she said.

"*Ah*."

"*He tells me things*." She sat down on the bench beside his wheeled chair. With her own bowl. "*When Lex lets him. Little things. That you take salt. That you hum when you're reading. That you tap two fingers on the table when you're thinking.*" She looked at his hand on the arm of the chair. Thin. Brown-spotted. Two fingers. Tapping. Very lightly. "*Like that.*"

He looked down at his hand. It stopped.

"*I didn't know I did that*," he said.

"*Ember knew*."

"*Ember's a piece of me from sixteen years ago*." His mouth twisted. "*He knows things about me I've forgotten. It's very strange. Like having a younger brother who's read your diary.*"

Seren laughed.

He looked at her. When she laughed. She saw him do it. Saw his face change. Go very still. The way a man's face goes when he hears a song he hasn't heard in a long time.

"*What*?" she said.

"*Nothing*." He looked down at his porridge. "*You laugh the same. That's all. Exactly the same.*" He picked up his spoon. With both shaking hands. "*I'd forgotten. Isn't that strange. I remembered everything else. I'd forgotten how you laugh.*"

---

They ate the porridge.

It was terrible. He ate all of it. Burnt bits and all.

---

The days went like that.

Small. Slow. Strange. She'd been in the dark so long she'd forgotten what days were. That they had shapes. Mornings and middles and evenings. That things happened in them. Not big things. Small ones. A goat ate a dishcloth. A boy buttoned his coat wrong. An old man ate burnt porridge and said it was terrible.

She sat with him.

Every day. In the mornings, in the kitchen. In the afternoons, at the cave mouth, in the sun, him in his wheeled chair and her on Pim's rock, with the whole of the Greyspine spread out below them, green now, summer, and the sky going on forever.

And he told her about them.

---

Not all at once. He didn't push. He'd learned that, he said, from Antho. Who had told Lex about the five-skip stone every day for a year, a little at a time, after the Ferryman took it. *You don't pour it all in at once*, Antho had said. *It just runs out the other side. You give them a bit. And let them hold it. And come back tomorrow.*

So he gave her a bit.

The steps of the Spire. The rain. The lanterns. What she'd said. What he'd said back. That was the first day.

The second day, the cup of something hot she'd made him buy her, at a stall in the market, which had turned out to be mulled cider, which he'd never drunk before in two hundred years, which had made him so dizzy he'd walked into a pillar.

The third day, the walk. Down out of Highcrest, three weeks later, on foot, with her, to the Vale. To meet her mother. Who had stood in the door of a cottage at the far end of the Vale, at a place that wasn't a village, that was a fold in the hills where the road gave out, and looked at him. A Warden. A Warden-Architect of the Spire. In his grey coat.

*Who's this*? Wenna Harrow had said.

*This is Arion*, Seren had said. *He's going to stay for supper.*

And Wenna had looked at him for a very long time. With her hedge-witch eyes. And then stood aside. And let him in.

*She never said a word to me*, said Arion. *Not that night. Not for a month. She just watched me. Every time I came. She'd sit by the fire with her mending and watch me.* He smiled. *And then one night, in the winter, I came up the path and there was a lantern in the window.*

Seren went very still.

*She'd never had one*, said Arion. *Not once. In all the time I'd been coming. You'd told me. Harrow women don't leave lights on.* He looked at the mountains. *And then one night there was one. In the window. Small. Clay. For me.*

*She'd made it*, said Arion Vey. *Herself. Out of clay from the stream. With her thumbprint in the bottom.*

---

Seren didn't say anything for a long time.

She was sitting on Pim's rock with her knees pulled up. Looking at the mountains. She'd brought the lamp out with her. She always did. It was in her lap. Small. Brown. Clay. With a chip in the spout.

She turned it over.

The thumbprint in the base.

She'd always thought it was hers. She'd thought she'd made the lamp. At the kitchen table at Harrow, the summer Lex was born. She remembered it. Clay from the stream. Her hands. The wheel.

She held her thumb up to the print.

It was smaller. Narrower. An older hand.

"*She made it*," said Seren. Very quietly. "*Mam. Not me. I made a copy. Later. When she was gone. I made a copy and I broke it, the first time I fired it, and I cried, and I never made another.*" She stared at the lamp. "*I forgot. I forgot that I forgot.*"

Arion didn't say anything.

"*She left a light on*," said Seren. "*For you. She broke the rule. Nine hundred years. And she broke it for you.*"

She looked at him.

"*Why*?"

The old man in the wheeled chair looked back at her. With his grey eyes. Very steady.

"*She said*," said Arion slowly, "*the first night. When I came up the path and saw it. I stopped. On the step. And looked at it. And she was standing in the door behind it. And she said*—"

He stopped.

"*She said, I've been running from the dark for forty years, Warden. Waiting for something to come for us. And it hasn't. And now something's come for my girl instead, and it's you, and you're not the dark.*" He swallowed. "*And then she said, don't make me sorry.*"

Seren put her face in her hands.

---

Brannoc came on the fourth day.

She heard him before she saw him. Everyone did. He came up the mountain on a cart, with Hedda Vorne beside him on the seat, and the cart was full of iron and the horse was full of opinions, and Brannoc was singing.

A hymn. *Lantern-bearer, light the way*. Off-key. Very loud.

She knew that voice.

She'd known it twenty years ago. In the Ashbourne forge. When she brought the boys in to watch the sparks. When Lex was two, and sat on the woodpile with his thumb in his mouth, and Antho was seven, and kept asking questions about the bellows. A big bald man with a soot-black beard, singing hymns off-key at the anvil, who'd given Lex a horseshoe nail to hold and said *there you go, lad, that's your first bit of iron*.

The man who'd taken her son in.

When the cottage burned. When she was gone into the dark and Antho was gone into the mountains and Lex was eleven, standing in the ashes. The man who'd come and found him. And taken him home. And given him the bellows to work, because the work would help.

She'd heard about it through the door. For eight years. Antho had told her. And then for three more years Lex had. Every story. Brannoc's hammer. Brannoc's eggs, cooked on the anvil. Brannoc's knees. Brannoc teaching him to draw a nail. Brannoc at the Hollowmaw with Hedda's barricade. Brannoc crying at weddings.

She'd never seen him since.

---

He stopped the cart at the bottom of the slope.

And stopped singing.

And sat on the cart seat with the reins in his enormous hands and looked up the slope at the small woman standing at the mouth of the cave. For a long time.

He was old. That was the first thing. Not old like Arion. Not *that*. But old. Sixty-three. Bald and brown-spotted and stooped at the shoulders. His soot-black beard gone grey. His knees, when he climbed down off the cart, creaking so loud she could hear them from the top of the slope.

He came up the slope.

Slowly. One step at a time. With Hedda behind him.

He stopped in front of her.

Seren looked up at him. He was so *big*. She'd forgotten. He'd been big at forty. He was bigger now. Or she was smaller. A great grey stooped bear of a man with a smith's forearms and burn-scarred hands and his eyes very wet.

"*Seren*," said Brannoc Holt.

"*Brannoc*."

"*I kept him*," said Brannoc. His voice was very rough. "*Your boy. Lex. I kept him. Like you'd have wanted. I didn't—I never knew what you'd have wanted. I just did what I could.*" He swallowed. "*I taught him to draw a nail. I taught him the forge. I taught him*—"

"*I know*," said Seren.

He stopped.

"*I heard*," she said. "*Through the door. Every story. For eleven years.*" Her voice cracked. "*The eggs on the anvil. The hymns. The night he couldn't stop shaking and you gave him the bellows.*"

The big man's face crumpled.

"*Thank you*," said Seren Vey. "*Brannoc. Thank you for keeping my boy.*"

And Brannoc Holt, sixty-three, the smith of Ashbourne, who had sat at Level 24 for nineteen years and never once complained, put his enormous arms round her and lifted her clean off the ground. And held her. And cried into her hair. Very loudly. While Hedda stood behind him with her arms crossed, saying *oh, for pity's sake*, and wiping her own eyes on her sleeve.

---

He put her down eventually.

And looked at her. Up and down. With his wet eyes.

"*You look old*," said Brannoc.

"*I look old*?" Seren stared at him. "*Brannoc. Look at you. You're a hundred.*"

"*I'm sixty-three*."

"*You look a hundred*."

"*Have you seen your husband*?" said Brannoc Holt.

There was a pause.

And then Seren laughed. Out loud. Helplessly. On the slope of the Hollowmaw, in the summer sun, with tears running down her face. And Brannoc laughed too. Great booming gusts of it, that echoed off the rocks. And Arion, in his wheeled chair at the mouth of the cave, where Pim had pushed him to watch, laughed so hard he started coughing and Odile had to come and thump him on the back.

---

Lex came home that night.

From Highcrest. On Vharuun. With the Queen of Caldor and thirty people behind him and a face like the end of the world.

He told them in the kitchen.

All of it. The glitter over the city. The people turning back. The girl with the little tin lamp. Silas Hask with wings. The Moth Queen on the gate. *Bring me the blood. All of it. Every drop of Harrow. And anything of yours.*

The Spire. Lit. From every window. And Arion's room at the top, brightest of all.

Seren listened.

She sat at the long table with her hands round a cup of tea and didn't say anything until he'd finished. She watched her son tell it. Her big son, with his father's height and her face and a moth's handprint glittering on his cheek. Telling it badly. With his hands. The way he told everything.

When he'd finished, nobody said anything.

Then Arion, in his wheeled chair at the end of the table, said very quietly: "*She's reading my books.*"

Everyone looked at him.

"*The grey ones*," said Arion. "*On the shelves. And the ones in the room behind the third portrait. Two hundred years of them.*" He looked at his hands. "*Every Kindling. Every Ceiling. Every name.*" A pause. "*Every child born to a Vey since I took the Root. When. Where. Who the mother was.*"

"*Why does that matter*?" said Wren.

"*Because she's hunting the blood*," said Seren.

---

She hadn't meant to say it.

It came out of her. The way the pool's voice used to come out of Hesper, nine hundred years ago, in a dry garden. The way the rule came out of her mother, at the end of every summer, in twenty-two cottages.

Everyone was looking at her now.

She put down her tea.

"*I should tell you*," said Seren Vey. "*All of it. What I know. What Mam told me. What her mam told her.*" She looked round the table. At her sons. At their wives. At Kestrel and Seraphine and Brannoc and Hedda and the Queen of Caldor, who was sitting very straight at the far end with her hood down and her eyes red. "*I should have told you before. At the Heart. Through the door. I didn't think—I didn't think it mattered anymore. I thought they were asleep. I thought they'd gone.*"

She looked at Arion.

"*I never told you*," she said. "*Did I. Not once. Not in eight years.*"

"*No*," said her husband. Very gently. "*You never told me anything about your family. Not once. I asked. You'd change the subject. You'd go and look at a bird out of the window and burn the porridge.*"

"*Mam said not to*." She looked at her hands. "*Especially not you. Especially not if I loved you.*"

"*Why*?"

"*Because you were a Warden*," said Seren. "*Because you had the Root. Because if you knew, you'd write it down.*" She looked at him. "*And then it'd be in a book. On a shelf. In a room at the top of a tower.*"

The kitchen was very quiet.

"*And someday*," said Seren Vey, "*someone would come and read it.*"

---

She told them.

All of it. Hesper and the pool. The Nine. The cupped hands. The tenth sister in the doorway with a crown in her hands, who said no and poured back. Antho had seen it. Through the little one. He'd told her. She'd known already. Her mother had told her. And hers. And hers. All the way back.

The Ferryman's shore. The chains. The boat. *Leave a light on for me, little sister.* *No.*

The Harrow women. Twenty-two cottages. Thirty houses. Twenty generations. Never Kindling. Never staying. Never leaving a light on. The moth carved in the lid and crossed out.

The crown.

"*Mam took it*," said Seren. "*Before she died. I was nineteen. I was in Highcrest, selling bread. I came home and she was sick and the box was empty.*" She shook her head. "*She wouldn't tell me where. She said it was where it belonged. She said, you'll know, if you ever need to.*" She was quiet. "*I never needed to. I never knew.*"

"*Why does she want the blood*?" said Lex.

Seren looked at him.

"*Because we never drank*," she said.

---

She'd have to explain it badly. She always explained things badly. She was a hedge-witch, not a scholar. She knew things the way her hands knew bread. Not the way Seraphine knew them, in lines and footnotes.

She did her best.

"*The Veil learned them*," she said. "*The Nine. When it was woven. When Aurelian tied off the edge of the world. It learned what they were. What they tasted like. Everyone who drank from the pool.*" She made a shape with her hands. A ring. "*And it pushes them back. Every time. Like a door that knows your face and won't open.*"

"*But it let Maelis through*," said Kestrel.

"*Because Lex loosened the knot*." Seren looked at her son. "*When you reforged the net. You tied it again kinder. You left a little give. She said so. Just enough for a moth. If she's patient.*" She looked at her hands. "*Not enough for the others. Not enough for a king with a gold crown. Not enough for four of them.*"

"*And the blood*?"

"*The blood never drank*." Seren held up her own hand. Square. Strong. "*Hesper didn't. Not a drop. She poured back instead. The Veil never learned her. Never learned any of us.*" She turned her hand over. "*So it doesn't push us. It doesn't know us. A Harrow can walk up to the edge of the world and put her hand on the Veil and it'll open like a curtain. Like a door that's never seen your face before and has no reason to keep you out.*"

The kitchen was very still.

"*Mam told me*," said Seren. "*When I was small. She said, if you ever have to run, really run, from something with lit eyes, go west. Go to the edge. Put your hand on the grey and walk through. They can't follow you there. Not ever. Because they can't come back.*" She swallowed. "*She said it was the last door. The one only we have the key to.*"

"*And if Maelis gets the blood*," said Seraphine slowly.

"*If she gets the blood*," said Seren, "*and puts it on the Veil, from this side*—" She stopped. "*It opens. Not just for us. For anyone. For anything on the other side of it. The way a door opens for anyone once someone's turned the key.*"

She looked round the table.

"*Four more of them*," said Seren Vey. "*Asleep. In the grey. Waiting. My sister's sister's brothers and sisters. The Gilded King, who drank first and drank most. The Glass Queen. The Iron Crown. The Thorn.*" Her voice was very quiet. "*She needs our blood to open the Veil for them. All four.*"

---

Nobody said anything for a long time.

Then Wren spoke. From beside Lex. Very quietly. With her hand flat on her stomach, under the table, where only Seren could see it.

"*And the baby*," said Wren Ashdown. "*Why does she want the baby?*"

Seren looked at her.

At her daughter-in-law. The tracker. The one with the wind. Who'd stood on a tower at the edge of the world and been looked at by the Moth Queen, and had felt, for the first time, a heart beating under her hand.

She'd known Wren would ask. She'd been waiting for it for four days.

"*Because it's never been anyone's*," said Seren.

Wren didn't move.

"*Every light in the world*," said Seren, "*is someone's. A lantern in a window is for someone. A candle is for someone. Your fire, Lex, is for everyone you love. Antho's lantern is for the lost. Even the lights inside people. The warm bits. The bit she took from Odile. From Kell. They're all* for *something. Someone. They've all been given, a little.*"

She looked at Wren's hand. Under the table.

"*A new light*," she said. "*One that's never been born. Never been given. Never been anyone's but its own.*" Her voice was very soft. "*She thinks that's the only light that would fill her. A light so new it's never been poured into anything. She thinks if she drank that, it wouldn't leak.*"

"*Would it*?" whispered Wren.

Seren looked at her daughter-in-law for a long time.

"*I don't know*," she said honestly. "*Nobody's ever let her try.*"

---

She sat with Arion that night. By the kitchen window. The small high one that looked east.

It was late. Everyone else was asleep. Or pretending. The fire was down to embers. The goat was asleep under the table with a sock. The lamp, the clay one, Wenna's, was on the windowsill. Lit.

She'd put it there. She hadn't asked anyone. She'd just picked it up, after the others had gone to bed, and carried it to the window, and set it on the sill. Facing out. Facing the dark. Facing east, toward the Vale, toward Harrow.

A light in the window.

Arion had watched her do it. From his wheeled chair. Not saying anything.

"*Mam would kill me*," said Seren. Looking at it.

"*Your mam broke the rule too*."

"*For you*." She sat down on the bench under the window. Beside his chair. "*She broke it for you. Because you weren't the dark.*" She looked at the little flame. "*I'm breaking it for*—"

She stopped.

"*For who*?" said Arion. Very gently.

She didn't answer for a long time.

"*For the little one*," she said at last. "*For Kell. Sitting with it in the dark. Telling it the one about the miller's cat.*" She swallowed. "*For everyone at Highcrest standing in the street holding up their dark lanterns. For Hollis Penn and his loaf.*" Her voice cracked. "*For me. For the girl on the steps of the Spire in the rain, who said don't ever leave a light on for me, Warden, and you said too late, I've already lit one.*"

She looked at him.

"*I don't remember her*," said Seren Vey. "*That girl. I don't remember being her. I've read about her. Forty pages. Page four's my favorite.*" She tried to smile. "*But I'd like to be her again. If I can. If there's time.*"

---

He didn't say anything.

He reached out. Slowly. With his thin shaking brown-spotted hand. And took hers. Off the bench. Into his lap.

And held it.

That was all. He didn't pull her close. He didn't kiss her. He didn't tell her anything. He held her square strong freckled hand in his old thin one, on the blanket over his knees, by the window, with the lamp on the sill.

She sat there. Looking at the lamp.

"*Tell me about the night Lex was born*," she said.

He looked at her.

"*You were there*," said Seren. "*I know you were. I can feel it. Someone held my hand all night. Long fingers. Cool.*" She looked down at their hands. "*I can't see who it was.*"

Arion Vey was quiet for a long moment.

"*It was snowing*," he said. "*The midwife from Ashbourne couldn't get through. The road was blocked. Your mother had been dead a year. Antho was five and asleep in the loft. It was just us.*" His thumb moved. Very slightly. On the back of her hand. "*You swore at me for nine hours. You said things I didn't know you knew. You said I had a neck like a bull and the sense of a turnip.*"

"*I did not*."

"*You did*. *It's on page twelve.*" He was smiling. "*And then, at four in the morning, he came. All at once. Huge. Purple. Furious. Screaming like a kettle.*" He looked at the lamp. "*And you held him. And looked at him. And said, oh. Oh, he's got big hands.*"

She laughed. Wet.

"*And then*," said Arion, "*you looked up at me and said*—"

He stopped.

She waited.

He didn't go on. She looked at him. And saw his eyes were shut. And his white head had dropped a little. Toward his chest.

Not asleep. Not quite.

Tired. So tired. An old man at midnight by a window who had told the same story eleven times to himself in the dark over a year and a half and finally, finally got to tell it to her.

"*Arion*," she said softly.

"*Mm*."

"*What did I say*?"

He opened his eyes. Just a little. Grey. Clear. Looking at her.

"*You said, he's going to be trouble*," whispered Arion Vey. "*Just like his father.*"

And his eyes closed again.

And his hand stayed round hers.

---

She sat with him until dawn.

Not moving. With her hand in his. With the lamp on the sill and the fire going out and the goat snoring under the table. Watching his face. His old furrowed walnut face, asleep. Learning it. The way you learn a face. Line by line. Like reading.

*Neck like a bull*, she thought. *Sense of a turnip.*

*He's going to be trouble*.

*Just like his father*.

She didn't remember saying any of it.

But she knew she had. The way she knew which way was up, at the bottom of the world. The way she knew Ember's voice. The way she knew, at the very edge of hearing, when a little silver bell with a crooked clapper rang, far off, somewhere, for someone who was sad.

At dawn, the sky outside the window went grey. Then pink. Then blue.

And Seren Vey, sitting by the window with her husband's hand in hers, put her head down on his shoulder. Very gently. On the blanket. The way you'd put your head down on someone's shoulder that you'd been doing it on for thirty years.

And fell asleep.

---

When she woke, the grey book was open on his knee.

He must have reached for it. Slowly. With his free hand. Without waking her. And found the pencil. And written, very small, very neat, shaking only a little, at the bottom of the last page, under all the numbers going down like water down a slope:

*She fell asleep on my shoulder*.

*No change*.

She read it twice.

She didn't understand it. *No change*. She looked at it for a long time, with her head still on his shoulder, trying to work out what it meant.

And then she turned back. Through the grey book. Through the forty pages of columns. Through the hairs and the lines and the stairs and the grip. Back. Past the first page. To the inside of the cover.

Where he'd pasted a page. Old. Yellow. Torn out of some other, older book. And on it, in faded ink, in the same small neat hand, two hundred lines.

One a year. On his birthday. At midwinter. For two hundred years.

*No change. No change. No change. No change.*

*No change*.

Seren Vey closed the book.

And put it back on his knee. Very gently. And put her head back down on his shoulder. And didn't sleep again.

She didn't need to.

She was awake.
