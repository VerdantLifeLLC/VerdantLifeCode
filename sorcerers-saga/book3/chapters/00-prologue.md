# Prologue: The Moth at the Window

There was a country at the end of the world where nothing had moved for nine hundred years.

It had no name anymore. It had had one, once. A long and beautiful name, in a language with eleven words for *crown* and none for *enough*. But the people who spoke that language had stopped speaking it a long time ago, and the name had worn away the way names do when nobody says them. Like a coin rubbed smooth in a pocket.

The dust called it the Outer Court. The dust had nothing better to do.

It lay beyond the Sundered Lands, past the last broken road, past the place where the Veil hung across the edge of the world like a curtain of grey silk that nobody could see through and nobody could pass. A country of grey-gold dust and dead gardens. Of fountains that had been dry since before the Lattice was woven. Of palaces made of glass and bone and gold, standing in the dust with their doors open, because there was no one left to keep out and no one left to keep in.

There was no sun there. There was no moon. There was a dim grey-gold light that came from nowhere, the color of a candle seen through a dirty window, and it never changed. Not at morning. Not at night. There was no morning. There was no night.

There was only waiting.

---

In the tallest of the glass palaces, in a hall with a ceiling like a frozen waterfall, five cocoons hung from the rafters.

They were made of silk. Grey-gold silk, thick as a man's arm, wound round and round and round, each one longer than a coffin and twice as wide. They hung from the high glass beams on ropes of the same silk, swaying very slightly, though there was no wind. There had been no wind in the Outer Court for nine centuries.

Under each cocoon, on the glass floor, lay a crown.

Not on a cushion. Not on a stand. Just lying there in the dust, where it had fallen. A crown of gold leaves. A crown of glass. A crown of black iron, plain and heavy as a manacle. A crown of thorns that had once been a crown of roses.

And under the fifth cocoon, the one at the end, nearest the tall glass windows: a crown of silver wire, so fine it looked like frost. Shaped like wings. Folded.

The dust lay over everything. The crowns. The floor. The cocoons. Thick and soft and grey-gold, like the powder on a moth's wing.

Nothing moved.

Nothing had moved for nine hundred years.

---

And then, very far away, on the other side of the Veil, on the other side of the world, at the bottom of a mountain, in front of a door of black glass—

A man lit a lamp.

A small iron lamp. Squat and round. With one rune on its glass.

He set it down beside two others. And lit it with his own fire. And stood back.

Three lamps. Side by side. In front of a door.

A light left on.

---

The fifth cocoon moved.

Just a tremor. A shiver, running through the grey-gold silk from top to bottom, the way a sleeper twitches when someone says their name in another room.

The dust on it slid. A little. A soft grey-gold whisper, pouring off the silk onto the glass floor.

Then nothing.

Then the tremor again. Stronger.

Something inside the cocoon was breathing.

---

She woke the way she always had, slowly and then all at once.

First the dark. The close warm silk-dark of the cocoon, pressing on her eyelids, on her mouth. The smell of dust. Old dust. Her own dust.

Then the hunger.

It had been there the whole time she slept. Nine hundred years of it. A dull ache at the center of her, low and steady, like a fire banked down to embers in the bottom of a stove. She had slept through it the way you sleep through a toothache, by going down so deep that the ache was only a rumor.

It wasn't a rumor now.

It was awake when she was. Sharper than she was. It had woken her, in fact. It had smelled something. Far off. And it had shaken her the way a dog shakes its sleeping master when it hears a step on the path.

She lay in the dark and breathed and let the hunger tell her what it smelled.

*Light*.

Somewhere. Far away. On the other side of the Veil.

Not the dim nothing-light of the Court. Not the grey-gold glow that came from nowhere. A real light. Small and gold and warm and *chosen*. Lit on purpose. By a hand. For someone who wasn't there.

A light left on.

She knew that kind of light. She'd known it all her long life. It was the sweetest light there was. Sweeter than sunlight. Sweeter than fire. Sunlight was only the sun doing what the sun did. Fire was only fire. But a lamp in a window, left burning for someone who hadn't come home—

That was a *promise*. And a promise could be eaten.

She opened her eyes in the dark.

---

The cocoon split.

Not all at once. One long seam, from top to bottom, opening the way a seed-pod opens, with a soft dry tearing sound like paper. Grey-gold light came in through the seam. Dust poured out. A great deal of dust.

And then a hand.

Long. Pale. Grey-gold, like the dust. The fingers very fine and very long, with too many joints, or with joints in the wrong places. It hooked over the edge of the split silk and held.

Then another.

Then she pulled herself out, and dropped, and landed on the glass floor of the hall in a crouch, among the dust, with her head bowed and her hair falling round her face.

She stayed like that for a while. Breathing.

Then she stood up.

---

She was tall. Taller than a man. Thin as a candle, and straight, and pale in a way that wasn't the paleness of skin but the paleness of ash, or of the underside of a leaf. Her hair was long and fine and the color of old silver. It fell to her knees.

Her face was beautiful. That was the first thing anyone noticed about her, and it was meant to be. High cheekbones and a long jaw and a wide, soft, sorrowful mouth. A face from a painting. A face from a church window.

Her eyes were the second thing.

They were lit.

Not reflecting light. *Lit*. From inside. Soft and gold and warm, like two windows in a house at the end of a lane on a winter night. The kind of windows you see from a long way off, walking home in the cold, and think: *there. Someone's in. Someone's waiting.*

That was what made people come to her. Nine hundred years ago, and before that. It wasn't her face. It was her eyes. People looked into them and saw a light left on, and walked toward it, the way the lost always do.

And then she was very kind to them, for as long as it took.

On her back, folded close against her spine like a cloak, she had wings.

They were made of silk and dust. Grey-gold. Enormous. Folded, they hung from her shoulders to the floor and trailed behind her a little, like a train. Open, they would have filled the hall. They were patterned, very faintly, in rings and eyes and whorls, darker gold on paler, like the wings of the great night-moths that came to the windows of farmhouses in late summer and beat themselves soft against the glass.

Every time she moved, they shed. A little. A soft gold powder, sifting down onto the glass floor behind her.

She looked down at the crown at her feet. Silver wire. Fine as frost. Shaped like folded wings.

She didn't pick it up.

"*Not yet*," said Maelis of the Crowned, to no one. Her voice was low and soft and a little hoarse, the way a voice is after nine hundred years of not being used. "*Not yet, I think.*"

---

She walked.

Down the hall. Past the other four cocoons, hanging still and silent from the glass beams. She stopped under each one and looked up at it for a while. Not long. Just long enough to be polite.

Gold leaves on the floor. *Oszric*. Her eldest brother. The Gilded King. Who had gone first to the pool, and drunk first, and drunk most. Who had told them all, at the end, when the net came down over the world like a fisherman's cast and the old Ferryman's boat bumped against the shore, that they would be back. *A thousand years at the most. The net will rot. Nets always rot. Then we'll come home and take it back, and everything in it.*

The cocoon didn't stir.

Glass on the floor. *Ysenne*. Who had loved mirrors. Who could not sleep without seeing her own face. Who had wept on the Ferryman's boat because there was no reflection in the grey water.

Black iron. *Caddoc*. Who had never laughed in his life, as far as anyone could tell, and who had sat in the bottom of the boat with his arms crossed and his jaw set and said nothing at all, the whole way out.

Thorns. *Her*. The one whose name none of them said anymore, because she had asked them not to, at the end, when the roses died.

Maelis looked up at the four of them. Hanging in the grey-gold dim. Breathing so slowly you couldn't see it. So slowly you'd think they were dead.

They weren't dead. That was the whole point. That was what they'd drunk for.

"*Sleep*," said Maelis softly. "*I'll go and look. I always was the one who went first to the light.*"

She smiled. It was a lovely smile. Sad and fond and patient.

"*I'll bring you something back*."

---

She went out through the tall glass doors of the palace into the Outer Court.

The dust lay everywhere. Over the dead gardens. Over the dry fountains. Over the broad white road that ran from the palace doors, straight as a ruler, across the grey-gold plain, toward the edge of the world.

There were shapes in the dust. Along the road. On either side. Hundreds of them.

They looked like heaps. Low mounds of grey-gold, scattered in no order, the way snow drifts against fences. But when she came down the palace steps, they began to move.

One. Then ten. Then all of them.

They sat up out of the dust. Slowly. Shedding it. And unfolded.

People. Or what had been people. Thin and pale and grey-gold, all of them, with long hands and lit eyes, not as bright as hers, nowhere near as bright, just a faint dull glow like the last coal in a grate. And on their backs, small wings. Dust-wings. Furled and ragged and dry.

Her Mothwing.

Her household. Her court. Her servants and soldiers and stewards. The people who had followed her to the pool nine hundred years ago, and drunk a little, a very little, from her own cupped hands, and lived. And kept living. Long after they wanted to. Long after there was anything to live on.

They knelt in the dust as she came down the steps. Row on row of them. With their heads bowed and their small dry wings trembling.

At the front, nearest the steps, an old man. Or the shape of one. Bent and grey and thin as a rake, with a steward's chain of office round his neck, gone black with age. His eyes were barely lit at all. Two dim sparks, like the ends of cigars in a dark room.

"*Majesty*," he said. His voice was a whisper. Dry as a page. "*Majesty. You wake.*"

"*Orrin*." She looked down at him. Fondly. "*You're still here.*"

"*Where else would I go, Majesty*?"

"*Nowhere*," she agreed. "*There's nowhere else. That's rather the point of an exile.*" She tilted her head. Listening. "*Orrin. Can you smell it?*"

The old steward lifted his head. His dim eyes flickered.

"*I can't smell anything anymore, Majesty*," he said. "*Not for three hundred years.*"

"*Light*," said Maelis.

A ripple ran through the kneeling Mothwing. Row on row. A soft dry rustle, like a wind through dead leaves, though there was no wind. Their small wings stirred.

"*Light*," said Maelis again. Softly. Savoring it. "*On the other side. Someone has left a light on.*"

---

She walked to the edge of the world.

It took a long time. The broad white road ran across the plain for miles, and there was nothing on it, and nothing changed. The grey-gold light stayed the same. The dust stayed the same. Her Mothwing followed her in a long ragged line, shedding, rustling, their dim eyes fixed on her back.

She didn't hurry. She'd waited nine hundred years. She could wait an afternoon. If there had been afternoons.

At the end of the road, there was a wall.

Not stone. Not glass. Not anything you could touch.

Grey.

A curtain of grey, hanging from nowhere, falling to nowhere, stretching away to the left and right as far as eyes could see. Not quite still. It moved, very slightly, the way silk moves in a draft. You could see light through it, dimly. Or the memory of light. Colors that might have been fields, or sky, or nothing at all.

The Veil.

The edge of the net. The last knot in the great weaving, where Aurelian Vey had tied off the world and left everything outside it *outside*.

She stopped in front of it.

She'd stood here before. Many times. In the first hundred years. When she still believed Oszric that the net would rot. She'd come to the end of the road and stood in front of the Veil and pressed her long hands flat against it, and pushed. And it had pushed back. Not hard. Not angrily. Just firmly. The way a door pushes back when it's locked.

*No*, it had said. Not in words. *Not you. Not ever.*

After the first hundred years she'd stopped coming. After the second hundred, she'd gone to sleep.

---

She lifted one long pale hand and laid it flat on the grey.

And it gave.

Not much. Not enough. But it *gave*. The way cloth gives under a finger when the threads have gone loose. The way a knot gives when someone, somewhere, has pulled out the rope that held it tight.

She held very still.

She could feel it. Through her palm. Through the grey silk of the Veil. Something had happened, on the other side. Something enormous. The whole great weaving of the world had been *changed*. Not torn. Not rotted. Changed. Unknotted, ring by ring, and knotted again, differently. Looser. Kinder. With no hand at the center holding all the threads.

*No Root*, she thought. *There's no Root.*

*Someone took the net apart and put it back together, and didn't keep the center for themselves.*

*Who would do that*?

She laughed. Softly. With her hand flat on the Veil.

*A fool*, she thought. *A good, kind, generous fool.*

*I do love a fool.*

---

And under the change, underneath it, deep down, so faint she nearly missed it—

Something else.

She went very still. Her lit eyes went wide.

She knew that. She knew that taste. She'd tasted it once, nine hundred years ago, on her knees at the edge of a black pool in a hall of glass, with her brother on one side of her and her sister on the other and her hands cupped full of dark.

The Hollow.

Not the hunger. The hunger was gone. She could feel that it was gone. The great starving emptiness that had followed them to the shore, screaming, as the Ferryman pushed off. The thing that their drinking had left behind.

Gone.

And in its place—

*Young*.

Something young. Something new. Rising. Slowly. From very far down. Soft and dark and enormous and *full*.

*It's grown back*.

Maelis of the Crowned stood at the edge of the world with her hand on the Veil and her heart, which had not beaten fast in nine centuries, beating fast.

*It's grown back. The Well. The seventh Well. The one we drank. It's healed. It's whole. It's full again. And it's coming up.*

*Toward the light*.

---

"*Orrin*," she said. Without turning round.

"*Majesty*."

"*Wake them*." She didn't take her hand off the Veil. "*No. Don't. Let them sleep.*" She smiled. "*I'll go first. I'll go and look. When there's something to bring them, I'll bring it.*"

"*Majesty*." The old steward's dry voice shook. "*Majesty, the Veil. It won't let us. It never has. Not us. Not the Crowned. Not anyone who drank.*"

"*It's loose now*," said Maelis.

"*Loose*?"

"*Someone untied the knot*." She pressed her palm against the grey. "*They tied it again. But they tied it with a kinder hand. They left a little give.*" She tilted her head, considering. "*Not enough for Oszric. Not enough for a crown. Not yet.*" She looked down at herself. Thin and pale and tall. Grey-gold. Shedding dust. "*But enough for a moth. If she's patient. If she goes very slowly. If she folds her wings.*"

She folded her wings.

Tight. Close against her spine. Until they were barely a cloak, barely a shadow, a fold of grey-gold silk down her back.

And she leaned against the Veil the way a moth leans against a window. Gently. Patiently. With her whole weight. Toward the light on the other side.

---

It took a long time.

It took a long time, and it hurt. The Veil didn't want her. It knew her. Nine hundred years hadn't changed that. The old kind careful knot that Aurelian Vey had tied at the edge of the world still remembered every one of the nine who had knelt at the black pool and drunk, and it pressed against her, *no, not you*, the way it always had.

But it was looser now. And she was very, very patient.

She went through it the way water goes through a cloth. One thread at a time. Leaving dust on every one.

Behind her, her Mothwing pressed against the grey too. A hundred of them. Two hundred. Leaning. Their small wings folded. Following her into the loose place she'd made, the way moths follow each other through a gap in a shutter.

Not all of them made it. The Veil was not as loose for them as for her. Some of them pressed against it and pressed and pressed, and their dry dim bodies came apart into dust against the grey, and the dust fell, and lay on the road with the rest.

She didn't look back.

She never did.

---

On the far side of the Veil it was night.

A real night. Cold. Black. With *stars*.

She came out of the grey into the Sundered Lands, on her knees, on hard frozen ground, in a wind that cut like a knife. And knelt there. And lifted her face. And looked up.

Stars.

Thousands of them. Hard and white and glittering, scattered across a black sky. And a moon. Thin. A paring. Low over the broken hills to the east.

She hadn't seen a star in nine hundred years.

She knelt on the frozen ground and looked at them for a long time.

They weren't what she wanted. They were too far. Too cold. Nobody had lit them. Nobody was waiting under them. They were only stars.

But they were *light*.

Her lit eyes brimmed. Gold. And something ran down her pale cheeks, slow and glittering, that might have been tears and might have been dust.

---

The net found her almost at once.

She felt it come. A touch. Silver. Light as a spider's thread. Brushing across her the way a blind man's fingers brush across a stranger's face, trying to learn its shape.

A panel opened in the dark in front of her.

She'd never seen one before. She looked at it with great interest.

*Not of this net*.

Maelis of the Crowned looked at it for a long moment. And then laughed. Low and soft and delighted, kneeling on the frozen ground at the edge of the Sundered Lands, under the stars, with her Mothwing crawling out of the Veil behind her one by one.

"*No*," she agreed. "*No, I'm not.*"

She lifted one long pale finger and touched the panel. Very gently. The way you'd touch a soap bubble.

And left a smudge of grey-gold dust on it.

The panel flickered. Went dim.

And went out.

---

She stood up.

She looked east. Across the Sundered Lands. Across the broken hills and the frozen ground and the black ruins of the old Umbral Rift, toward a world she had not seen since before the Lattice was woven.

It was dark. It was enormous. She couldn't see the end of it.

But far, far off, very faint, at the very edge of seeing, she could feel it. The thing that had woken her. Small and gold and warm.

A light left on.

*Three*, she thought. *Three lamps, in front of a door. At the bottom of a mountain. For someone who isn't home.*

*And behind the door, rising, the Well we drank.*

*And beside the Well, coming home, someone who smells of my sister.*

She smiled.

"*Come, Orrin*," said Maelis, the Moth Queen. "*Come, all of you. It's a long way, and I'm hungry, and someone has very kindly left the light on.*"

She opened her wings.

They filled the night. Grey and gold and enormous, patterned with rings and eyes and whorls, shedding dust that glittered in the starlight like frost.

And she flew east.

Toward the light.
