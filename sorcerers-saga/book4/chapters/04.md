# The Kept

Antho counted the lanterns every night.

He'd done it for three years. Since before Lex wove the net. Since before the door at the bottom of the passage was a door at all, when it was only a black glass seal at the end of a long cold tunnel in the mountain, with his mother on the other side of it.

He walked down the passage at dusk with the Keeper's Lantern in his hand and counted.

One. Two. Three. All the way down. Iron lanterns and tin ones and clay ones and one made out of a turnip that a child from Millbridge had carved and filled with tallow and that Seraphine re-carved every autumn when the old one rotted. On shelves cut into the rock. On hooks. On the floor, in rows, along both walls. Each one lit. Each one for someone. Someone's daughter. Someone's father. Someone who'd gone into the dark and not come back, or come back changed, or come back and gone again.

One thousand, one hundred and thirteen.

He'd stopped at the last one. At the bottom of the passage. In front of the door. A black door of glass, set in the rock, with nothing behind it now but the dark of the mountain and, far down, the place where a Well had once lain starving.

There was a small brown clay lamp on the floor in front of it. Chipped at the spout. With a thumbprint in the base.

He didn't count that one. It wasn't for anyone. It was just his.

"*One thousand, one hundred and thirteen*," said Antho Vey. To the door. To the dark. To nobody.

And turned round. And went back up.

---

Seraphine was waiting at the top.

She was sitting on the step where the passage came up into the great hall of the Keep, with her knees pulled up and a book open on them and an ink-pot balanced on the stone beside her, writing. She didn't look up as he came.

"*How many*?" she said.

"*One thousand, one hundred and thirteen*."

"*Same as yesterday*."

"*Same as yesterday*."

She wrote something. A short line. At the bottom of the page.

"*What's that*?"

"*Reason five hundred and sixty-two*," said Seraphine Ilvane. Not looking up. "*He counts the lanterns every night even though he knows exactly how many there are, because he says if he ever stops counting he'll stop seeing them, and if he stops seeing them he'll stop seeing the people they're for.*"

He sat down on the step beside her.

"*I never said that*."

"*You said it to Pim*," said Seraphine. "*Last week. When he asked why. I was on the stairs.*" She blotted the line. Carefully. With a corner of her sleeve. "*You were very wise and very boring about it. It was extremely attractive.*"

---

She'd been keeping the list for three years. Since before the wedding. Since before he'd asked her, at the bottom of the world, with his voice shaking, whether she'd marry a man who'd been mad for eight years and might go mad again.

*Reasons*, she'd said. *To marry you. In case I ever forget. In case you ever ask me why.*

There had been four hundred and twelve on the wedding day. She'd read some of them out. In the middle of the vows. In front of the black door, in front of the Queen, in front of everyone. Number one hundred and six had been *he plays the flute badly but he plays it anyway, every night, so the dark will know someone's listening*. Number three hundred and forty had been *he once spent six hours trying to get a goat out of a well and when he got it out it bit him and he apologized to it*.

Lex had laughed so hard he'd had to sit down.

The list had gone past five hundred on Lantern Night. On the rubble. While Antho held a door open for a Well with his whole heart and very nearly didn't come back.

She'd never told him what number five hundred was.

He'd never asked.

---

"*You've got that look*," said Seraphine.

"*What look*?"

"*The one where you're listening to something I can't hear*." She closed the book. Turned on the step to face him. Her spectacles had slid down her nose. She pushed them up. "*Is it Mal?*"

"*No*." He looked out across the hall. At the long tables. At the great fire. At the students eating their supper. Odile at the head of one table, cutting bread with a Shade-knife she wasn't supposed to use for bread. Teo dripping on the end of another, the way he always dripped, because he was a Tide Speaker and water followed him round like a dog. The Millbridge twins arguing about something. Pim, at the end of the bench by the fire, alone, small, silent, eating his soup. "*It's not anything. It's just—*"

He didn't know what it was.

It had been there all day. Since the morning. A pressure. Faint. At the very edge of him. Not a sound. Not a voice. A *draught*. The feeling you get in a big old house when somebody, somewhere, has left a door open, and the air is moving in a way it shouldn't, and you can't tell where it's coming from.

He was a Threshold Warden. He felt doors. That was what he was.

And somewhere, a long way off, a door had opened that shouldn't have.

---

"*How's Bet*?" he said.

Seraphine's face changed.

"*Not long*," she said. Quietly. "*Tonight. Tomorrow. I sat with her this afternoon. She asked me to read to her and she fell asleep in the middle of a sentence and I sat there and listened to her breathe.*" She looked down at the closed book on her knees. "*It catches. Every few breaths. And then it starts again.*"

"*And Fennick*?"

"*Won't leave her*." Seraphine's mouth twisted. "*He hasn't slept for three days. He sits by the bed and holds her hand and talks. About the sheep. About their boy. About the first time he saw her, at a hiring fair in Thistledown, forty-one years ago, in a yellow dress.*" She swallowed. "*He's told me about the yellow dress four times. He doesn't know he's told me.*"

Antho didn't say anything.

They'd come up the mountain a week ago. An old shepherd and his wife. From the high crofts above Thistledown. On foot, the last of it, because the cart couldn't manage the path. She'd been walking with a stick, slowly, leaning on him. They'd come to light a lantern for their son. Wat Dale. Twenty-two. Who'd gone down to work the Coldwater seams eleven years ago for the money and come up grey and coughing and died in the spring.

She'd lit it herself. Her hands shaking. On a shelf a third of the way down the passage. *For Wat. From his mam.*

And then she'd sat down on the step at the top of the passage, very carefully, and said, *I don't think I can go down again, love*.

She hadn't been able to. They'd given her the little room off the east stair. With the window. Where you could see the mountains.

She'd been dying ever since.

---

"*I'll go and sit with them*," said Antho. "*Later. When the students are in bed.*"

"*Take your flute*."

"*She doesn't like my flute*."

"*Nobody likes your flute*," said Seraphine. "*That's not the point. Fennick likes it. He told me. He said it makes him feel like someone's keeping watch.*" She stood up. Gathered her book and her ink-pot. Looked down at him. "*Reason five hundred and sixty-three. He goes and sits with dying strangers in the middle of the night and plays them a song they don't like because it's better than silence.*"

"*That's two in one night*."

"*It's been a good night*," said Seraphine.

---

He heard the knock at midnight.

He was in the little room off the east stair. Sitting on the floor with his back to the wall, under the window, with his bone flute in his lap. Not playing. He'd played. An hour of it. The Lantern Song, every verse, slowly, the way his mother used to sing it. Bet had slept through it. Fennick hadn't. Fennick had sat by the bed with his wife's hand in both of his and his eyes shut, listening, and when Antho stopped, he'd said, very quietly, *thank you, Master Vey*.

Now it was quiet.

Bet's breathing. In. Catch. Silence. In again.

Fennick's. Slow. Heavy. The breathing of a man who has been awake for three days and is very nearly not.

And then the knock.

---

It came from below. From the great door of the Keep. The outer one. Oak and iron, three hands thick, that opened onto the mountain path and the long drop to the valley.

*Knock*.

Not loud. Not hard. Three soft taps. Courteous. Patient. The way a well-brought-up visitor knocks at a house where they know someone is ill.

*Knock. Knock. Knock*.

Antho was on his feet before the third.

---

He felt it on the stairs.

The draught. The one he'd felt all day. Not far off now. Not at the edge of him. *Here*. On the other side of the great door. Pressing on it. Not hard. Just leaning. The way a cat leans on a door it wants to come through.

He'd felt a lot of things on the other side of doors.

He'd felt the hunger at the bottom of the world. He'd felt his mother. He'd felt a Moth Queen, tall and grey-gold and courteous, standing in the snow outside this very door last spring, asking very politely if she might come in.

This wasn't any of those.

This was *thin*. That was all he could think. Thin and dry and old. Not a person. Not quite. Not a thing. A shape, held together by something that wasn't flesh. Like smoke that had decided to stand up. Like a draught that had learned to knock.

He stopped at the bottom of the stair. In the great hall. In the dark. The fire was down to embers. The tables were empty. The students were asleep.

*Knock. Knock. Knock*.

"*Who's there*?" said Antho.

---

"*A servant*," said a voice.

It came through the oak. Through the iron. Through the three hands' thickness of door. Dry. Soft. Hissing. Like sand poured slowly out of a sack.

"*Only a servant, master*," it said. "*A steward. My name is Orrin. I have a gift.*"

"*I don't want it*."

"*It isn't for you*," said the voice. Gently. "*Forgive me. It isn't for anyone in particular. I carry it from house to house. I knock. I ask.*" A pause. "*May I come in?*"

Antho didn't move.

He knew that question. He'd heard it before. In the snow. In a voice like warm honey. *May I come in?*

*Don't ever let a moth in*, his mother had said. Through the door. A year ago. *Don't ever.*

"*No*," said Antho.

---

The voice on the other side of the door laughed.

Softly. Not unkindly. Like an old man laughing at a child who has said something very serious and very sweet.

"*Of course not*," it said. "*Of course. You're a Harrow. I forgot. She warned us. Hesper. Nine hundred years ago. On the shingle.*" A sigh, like dust settling. "*No light in any window. No moth through any door.*"

"*You're not a moth*."

"*No*," agreed Orrin. "*I'm dust. Mostly. Dust doesn't need to be let in.*"

And came in.

---

Not through the door.

*Under* it.

Antho saw it. In the dark. In the faint red light of the embers. A thin grey line of dust, sifting in under the great oak door the way snow sifts in under a door in a blizzard. Soft. Silent. Spreading across the flagstones in a thin pale fan.

And then rising.

---

It stood up in the middle of the great hall like smoke rising off a doused fire.

Tall. Thin. Bent. A shape made of grey-gold dust, its edges smoking, shifting, never quite still. Arms like bundles of dead twigs. A long narrow head. A suggestion of a coat. A chain round its neck, black. And two eyes. Dim. Barely lit. Two sparks in the dust, like the ends of pipes in a dark room.

It bowed.

Low. Courteous. Old-fashioned. One twig-arm folded across its middle and the other held out to the side.

"*Master Vey*," whispered Orrin. "*The warden. The door. His Majesty has heard of you.*"

Antho threw a Void Bolt at it.

---

He didn't think about it. He didn't plan it. His hand came up and the dark came out of it, the way it had come out of him ten thousand times, a fist of black nothing hurled across the hall at the shape in the dust.

It went through.

Of course it did. The dust scattered. Blew apart round the bolt in a glittering cloud, like flour thrown in the air. The bolt went on and hit the far wall and left a scorch on the stone the size of a dinner plate.

And the dust came back together.

Slowly. Unhurried. Grain by grain. Settling back into the thin bent shape, the long head, the twig arms, the two dim sparks.

"*Oh, master*," it whispered. Sadly. "*Your brother tried that too. With fire. This morning. At the edge of the world.*" The sparks flickered. "*I'm only dust. You can't hurt dust. You can only move it about.*"

"*Then I'll move it*," said Antho.

And called the wind.

---

Gale came to him easier than it came to Lex. It always had. He'd been Speaking it for a year. He didn't have to ask. He just had to open his hand.

He opened his hand.

The air in the great hall moved. All of it. All at once. A gust, out of nowhere, out of everywhere, sweeping across the flagstones, through the tables, up into the high dark beams. The embers in the hearth flared bright red and roared. Papers flew off the long tables. A chair went over.

The dust went with it.

Up. Away. Whirling into the beams like smoke up a chimney. Scattering into every corner of the hall. Spreading thin. Thinner. Until there was nothing left of the shape at all, just a faint grey haze in the air, glittering in the red of the embers.

Antho stood in the middle of the hall with his hand open and his breath going hard.

And from everywhere—from the beams, from the corners, from the haze in the air—very soft, very patient, like sand sifting through the cracks of a floor—

"*That's better*," whispered Orrin. "*Now everyone can hear me.*"

---

He understood too late.

The dust was everywhere. He'd put it everywhere. He'd blown it up into the beams and the corners and the cracks in the stone, and the beams ran into the walls, and the walls ran up into the floors above, into the stairwells and the corridors and the rooms.

Into every room in the Keep.

And from every room, at once, very softly, the voice began to speak.

---

"*Forgive me*," it said.

Antho heard it. In the hall. In the stairwell behind him. In the corridors above, faint, echoing. He heard a door open somewhere on the second floor. A student's voice, sleepy, frightened. *Who's there?*

"*Forgive me for waking you*," whispered Orrin. To everyone. To the whole Keep. Gently. So gently. "*I won't stay long. I only want to say one thing. I carry a gift from my master, and I offer it to anyone who wants it.*"

"*Stop*," said Antho.

"*My master is very old*," said the voice. "*Older than your world. He has sat by a great many beds. He has watched a great many people he loved grow thin, and grey, and slow. He has held their hands while their breath caught. And caught. And stopped.*"

"*Stop it*."

"*And he could not bear it*," whispered Orrin. "*So he found a way. So that nobody he loved would ever have to go. So nobody would ever have to sit by a bed again.*" A pause. Soft as dust settling. "*He keeps them.*"

Antho ran for the stairs.

---

He went up them three at a time. The Keeper's Lantern in his fist, blazing. The voice coming out of the walls all round him, soft and kind and patient.

"*Not dead*," it said. "*Not changed. Not hurt. Kept. Exactly as they are. Exactly as you love them. Forever.*" A sigh. "*No more fear. No more pain. No more wasting. No more watching the breath catch.*"

He was on the second floor. The students were in the corridor. Half a dozen of them. In their nightshirts. White-faced. Odile at the front, with her Shade-knife in her hand and her jaw set. The Millbridge twins clinging to each other. Teo dripping.

"*Master Antho*—"

"*Back in your rooms*," said Antho. "*Shut the doors. Don't listen.*"

"*It's in the walls*—"

"*I know where it is*. *Don't listen to it.*"

"*I carry a pinch of my master's gold*," whispered the voice. From the walls. From the ceiling. From the cracks round the doors. "*Only a pinch. On the tongue. That's all it takes.*" Gently. "*For anyone who's sitting by a bed tonight. For anyone who's holding a hand and listening to the breath catch. You need only come down to the courtyard and ask.*"

He was past the students. Running. Up again. To the third floor. To the east stair.

To the little room with the window.

---

The door was open.

He stopped in it.

Bet was asleep. In the bed under the window. Her thin face grey on the pillow. Her breathing slow. In. Catch. Silence.

In again.

Fennick was awake.

He was sitting by the bed. Where he'd been sitting for three days. With his wife's hand in both of his. His big shepherd's hands, brown and cracked and gentle, wrapped round her thin white one. He was looking at the wall.

At a crack in the wall. Beside the window. A thin dark line in the old stone, where the mortar had worn.

A little grey dust was sifting out of it. Very slowly. Like sand in an hourglass.

"*Forever*," the crack was saying. Softly. Only to him. "*She'd never have to go, old man. You'd never have to watch.*"

Fennick's face was wet.

"*Fennick*," said Antho.

The old shepherd didn't look round.

---

"*Don't listen to it*," said Antho. From the doorway. Very quietly. "*Please. Fennick. It's not what it sounds like.*"

"*What does it sound like*?" said Fennick Dale.

His voice was hoarse. Three days of not sleeping. Three days of talking about sheep and a yellow dress.

"*It sounds like she wouldn't die*," he said.

"*I know*."

"*It sounds like I wouldn't have to sit here and listen to her stop*." He was still looking at the crack. At the dust. "*I've been listening for three days, Master Vey. Every breath. Waiting for the one that doesn't start again.*" He swallowed. "*Do you know what that's like?*"

Antho didn't answer.

He did know. He'd sat on a rubble-heap a few months ago with an old man's hand in his and felt it go slack.

"*It's a lie*," he said. "*Whatever it is. Whatever it does. It's not what it sounds like.*"

"*How do you know*?"

"*Because it came in under the door*," said Antho Vey. "*Things that tell the truth knock and wait.*"

---

Fennick looked at him then.

For a long moment. With his red tired eyes. The eyes of a man who has been very kind his whole life to sheep and dogs and a wife in a yellow dress, and has been given nothing back for it, in the end, but a dead son and a dying wife and a narrow bed in a cold stone room on a mountain.

"*No*," said a voice from the bed.

They both turned.

---

Bet was awake.

Her eyes were open. Pale blue. Faded. Clouded at the edges. Looking at the crack in the wall. At the dust.

She'd heard.

"*No*," said Bet Dale. Very faintly. A whisper. Barely a breath. "*No, thank you.*"

The crack went quiet.

"*Bet*—" Fennick's voice broke. "*Bet, love, you heard*—"

"*I heard*." Her hand moved, very slightly, in both of his. "*I'm not deaf, Fen. I'm dying. It's different.*" A ghost of a smile. Her lips were grey. "*No, thank you. Tell it.*"

"*It says you'd never*—"

"*I know what it says*." Her eyes went to his face. Slowly. As if it took all the strength she had left. "*I've had sixty-three years, Fen. I've had you. I've had Wat. I've had the high crofts and the sheep and that yellow dress.*" Another breath. Catch. Silence. In again. "*I'm not greedy.*"

He was crying. Openly. His head bent over her hand.

"*I don't want to be kept*," said Bet Dale. To the crack in the wall. To the dust. To whatever was listening. "*I want to go and see my boy.*"

---

She closed her eyes.

Fennick held her hand.

And the crack in the wall, very softly, very patiently, like sand settling, said nothing at all.

---

Antho stayed till dawn.

He sat on the floor with his back to the wall and the Keeper's Lantern beside him, blazing, and his bone flute in his lap, and watched the crack. Nothing came out of it. Nothing moved.

The voice in the walls had gone quiet.

He'd gone down once, in the dark hours, to check the courtyard. Seraphine had come with him. White-faced, with her spectacles crooked and a Hush-cup in her hand. They'd stood in the door of the great hall and looked out at the courtyard under the cold stars. Empty. The flagstones grey. The well-head. The mounting block. The gate.

Nothing.

No thin grey shape. No dim sparks. Nobody waiting.

"*It's gone*," said Seraphine.

"*It's not gone*," said Antho. "*It's waiting.*"

He went back up to the little room with the window. And sat down. And watched the crack.

---

He must have slept.

He didn't mean to. He'd been awake since the knock. He'd been awake, mostly, for a long time before that. He closed his eyes for a moment, just a moment, with the lantern beside him and the flute in his lap and the slow sound of Bet's breathing.

In. Catch. Silence. In again.

And when he opened them, it was grey light. And the breathing had stopped.

---

He knew before he looked.

Not because it was quiet. It wasn't the kind of quiet he'd been waiting for. The quiet after the last breath, when the room changes. When something goes out of it. When there's one fewer person in it than there was.

He knew that quiet. He'd been in a lot of rooms.

This wasn't that.

This was *full*.

---

Fennick was standing by the window.

He'd got up. He was standing with his back to the bed and his hands at his sides, looking out at the mountains in the grey dawn. Very still. His shoulders very straight.

There was a small silk pouch on the windowsill. Open. Tied with a gold cord. Empty.

And on the bed—

---

Bet Dale was gold.

She lay on her back under the grey wool blanket with her head on the pillow and her hands folded on her chest, the way Fennick must have folded them. Her thin face turned a little toward the window. Her mouth a little open. Caught in the middle of a breath.

Gold.

All of her. Her grey hair on the pillow, every strand of it, gold. Her eyelashes. Her eyebrows. The fine lines round her eyes and her mouth. The liver-spots on the backs of her folded hands. The thin gold chain of her wedding ring, which had been gold already, and now was simply part of her finger, part of her hand, part of the rest of her.

The blanket where it touched her was gold. Up to the line of her shoulders. As if the gold had crept a little way out of her and stopped.

And her eyes.

They were open.

They were looking at the window.

At the old man standing in it with his back to her.

And as Antho stood up, slowly, with his heart going like a hammer, they moved.

Slowly. So slowly. The way the hand of a clock moves. Across. Down. To him.

---

"*She was going*," said Fennick Dale.

He didn't turn round. He spoke to the window. To the mountains.

"*An hour ago. Before the light. Her breath went and it didn't come back for a long time. Longer than ever.*" His voice was very calm. "*And then it came back. And then it went again. And I thought, this is it. This is the one.*" He swallowed. "*And I couldn't.*"

Antho didn't say anything.

"*It was on the windowsill*," said Fennick. "*When I looked up. Just sitting there. Like it had been waiting.*" His hands at his sides opened and closed. "*She said no. I know she said no. I heard her.*" A long silence. "*I just couldn't, Master Vey. I couldn't sit there and listen to her stop.*"

He turned round.

He looked at the bed. At his wife. At the gold face on the pillow, mid-breath.

At her eyes. Which moved. Slowly. To him.

"*She's not dead*," said Fennick Dale. "*Is she.*"

"*No*."

"*She'll never die*."

"*No*."

The old shepherd came and knelt by the bed. And reached out. And took her hand. Her folded gold hand. In both of his.

And stopped.

---

Antho saw his face.

Saw it understand. All at once. The way a man understands, stepping off a ledge in the dark, that there was no step there.

The hand was cold.

Not dead-cold. Not the cold of a body. The cold of *metal*. Of a coin. Of a gold ring left on a windowsill in winter. Hard. Smooth. Perfect. It didn't move in his. It didn't give. It didn't curl round his fingers the way it had for forty-one years, since a hiring fair in Thistledown, since a yellow dress.

It was just a gold hand. Folded. Kept.

Fennick held it.

And his wife's eyes, on the pillow, in her gold face, looked at him.

And couldn't blink.

And couldn't cry.

And couldn't say anything at all.

---

"*Bet*," said Fennick Dale. "*Bet, love.*"

Nothing.

"*Bet, say something. Please. Bet.*" His voice was going. Breaking up. Like ice on a pond. "*I'm sorry. I'm sorry. I'm sorry. I'll—I'll take it back, I'll find him, I'll make him take it back, I'll*—"

Antho knelt down beside him.

He didn't know what else to do. He knelt down on the cold stone floor beside the old shepherd and put his hand on the bed. On the blanket. On the gold edge of it, where it touched her shoulder.

And reached.

---

He was a Threshold Warden.

That was what he was. It was the only thing the net had ever called him that felt true. He felt doors. Every door. Not just the oak-and-iron kind. The other kinds. The door between waking and sleeping. The door between one breath and the next. The door at the back of a person's eyes, where they keep the things they don't say.

And the last one.

Everyone had it. He'd felt it in every dying person he'd ever sat with. In the old woman at the bottom of the passage last winter. In the miner from Coldwater, in the summer. In the old man on the rubble with his hand going slack in Antho's.

A door. At the very end. Open a crack. With light coming round it. Not frightening. Just—*there*. Waiting. The way a door at the end of a hall waits, at night, with a lamp lit behind it, for whoever's coming home.

He'd felt it in Bet. Last night. Clearer than he'd ever felt it in anyone. Wide open. Warm. With a young man's voice on the other side of it, very faint, saying *Mam*.

He reached for it now.

And it wasn't there.

---

Not closed. Not locked. Not barred. Not anything he could knock on, or open, or ask.

*Gone*.

As if it had never been. As if someone had come in the night with a trowel and a hod of gold and bricked it up, so smooth and so perfect you couldn't even see where it had been. There was no seam. No crack. No keyhole. Just gold. Solid. All the way through.

No door.

No way out.

He knelt by the bed with his hand on the blanket and felt the Clarity in him drop. Not far. Not dangerously. A tremor. A lurch. The way a floor lurches under your feet when you realize the stair you were reaching for in the dark isn't there.

He'd never felt anything like it.

Not in the Hollow. Not at the bottom of the world. Not in eight mad years with a hunger whispering in the back of his head.

*He's taken her door*, he thought. *Her last one. The one everyone gets. The one her boy is waiting behind.*

*He's taken it and he's bricked it up and she'll never, ever, ever get through.*

---

Pim was in the doorway.

Antho didn't know how long he'd been there. Nobody ever did, with Pim. He was just there. Small. Thin. Fourteen, now, or near it, with his hair sticking up and his nightshirt on and his bare feet on the cold stone. Looking at the bed.

At the gold woman on it.

He didn't say anything. He hardly ever did. He'd been silent for twelve years and talking for three and he still said as little as he could, as if words were coins and he'd been poor too long to spend them easily.

He came into the room.

He knelt down on Fennick's other side. By the bed. And looked at Bet's gold face for a long time.

And then he did the thing.

---

Antho had seen him do it before.

In the snow, outside the Keep, last spring, when the moths came and Odile went dim and cold and empty. On the rubble on Lantern Night. A dozen times since. In small ways. A frightened student. A crying child at the door.

Pim got quiet.

Not just not-talking. That was always. Something *more*. Something that came out of him like warmth comes out of a hearth. A stillness. A hush. Spreading. Out of his small thin body into the room. Into the air. Into the cold stone and the grey light and the old man kneeling by the bed with his face in his hands.

A *room*. That was what Seraphine called it. *He makes room. With his silence. Like the Hollow does. He's got a little piece of it in him. He made it himself, out of being hurt, a long time ago.*

He made one now.

Round the bed. Round the gold woman on it. Soft. Quiet. Small. Pim's whole self, poured out into the air like water poured round a stone.

Asking.

*Is there anywhere*, the silence said. *Is there any room. Anywhere at all. Just a little. Just enough.*

---

And for a moment—

For one moment—

Antho felt it give.

Not the door. The door was gone. But *something*. Somewhere deep in the gold. A hair's-breadth. A crack too small to see. As if the gold, solid all the way through, perfect, full, had been asked very gently by a quiet boy if it could spare just the tiniest space.

And had found, somewhere, that it could.

---

A tear ran out of Bet Dale's eye.

Out of the corner of it. Out of the gold. Slowly. So slowly. Not gold. *Wet*. A real tear. Clear. Salt. Catching the grey dawn light from the window.

It ran down her gold cheek.

It fell onto the pillow. And soaked in. A small dark spot on the gold-edged wool.

And that was all.

The crack closed. Whatever it had been. Antho felt it close. Felt the gold settle back. Perfect. Full. Solid all the way through.

Pim knelt by the bed with his small face very white and his thin hands flat on the blanket.

"*She's crying*," he said.

---

Fennick looked up.

He saw the tear. The dark spot on the pillow. The wet track down his wife's gold cheek.

And made a sound Antho would hear for a long time afterward. In the night. In the passage. Every time he counted the lanterns.

Not a sob. Not a word.

The sound a man makes when he understands exactly what he's done.

---

Antho found the dust on the wall at sunrise.

He went up. Onto the battlements. Above the great door. Where the wind came off the mountains, cold and clean. He didn't know why. He just needed to be outside. Away from the little room with the window. Away from the gold.

It was there.

Sitting on the parapet. Thin. Grey. Bent. Its twig-legs dangling over the drop. Its dim sparks of eyes looking out at the sunrise over the Greyspine, pink and gold and cold.

"*Beautiful*," whispered Orrin. "*Isn't it. We don't have those. In the Court. No sun.*" The sparks flickered. "*I'd forgotten.*"

Antho didn't throw anything at it.

He stood on the battlements with the wind in his face and his hands shaking at his sides.

"*She said no*," he said.

"*She did*." The dust didn't look round. "*He said yes. For her.*" A soft sound, like a sigh. "*They usually do. In the end. The ones by the bed. It's not the dying who want to be kept, Master Vey. It's the ones who have to watch.*"

---

"*Why*?" said Antho.

The dust turned its head. The long narrow shape. The two sparks.

"*Why what*?"

"*Why her. Why here. Why any of it.*" His voice was shaking. He didn't let it stop him. "*You want Harrow blood. You want the Key. You want—whatever you want. Why come up a mountain and take an old woman's door?*"

Orrin looked at him for a long moment.

"*Because he asked me to*," it said simply. "*His Majesty. He said, offer it. Everyone who's ever sat by a bed. He said, they'll come. They always come.*" The sparks dimmed. Brightened. "*He wants your world to know, master. Before he comes. He wants every old man holding his wife's hand in the night to know there's another way.*"

"*It's not a way*. *It's a*—"

"*It's a kindness*," whispered Orrin. "*To him. It's the only kindness he knows.*"

It stood up. On the parapet. Thin and tall and bent against the pink sky.

"*He kept us all*," it said. "*Nine thousand. In the first years. When we started to come apart. He walked out among us with his hands and he kept us.*" The sparks were very dim now. "*He didn't keep me. I asked him not to. I wanted to keep serving.*" A pause. "*Sometimes I'm sorry.*"

---

It bowed.

The old bow. Low. Courteous. One twig-arm across its middle.

"*His Majesty sends his regards to your mother*," whispered Orrin. "*And asks after the child. Your brother's child. Born on the rubble. On the night his sister died.*"

Antho went cold all the way through.

"*He'd like to meet her*," said the dust. "*Very much.*"

And let go.

---

It went up off the parapet like smoke off a snuffed candle. A thin grey stream. Glittering in the dawn. Up into the cold clean wind off the mountains.

And east.

Down the valley. Over the foothills. Toward the low country, toward the shires, toward a long green fold in the hills where a river ran through a village with a forge in it, and beyond the village a birch wood, and beyond the birch wood a clearing by a stream.

Antho stood on the battlements and watched it go until he couldn't see it anymore.

Then he went down. Fast. Three steps at a time. Shouting for Ashka.

---

Seraphine caught him at the bottom of the passage stair.

She was white. Her spectacles crooked. She had her book in her hand, and her pen, and she wasn't writing. She caught his arm as he came past.

"*Antho*."

"*I have to go. Lex. The baby. It's going east*—"

"*Antho, look*."

She pointed. Down the passage. Down the long cold tunnel in the mountain. At the lanterns.

---

They were all lit.

One thousand, one hundred and thirteen. Iron and tin and clay and one carved out of a turnip. On shelves and on hooks and in rows along the floor. All burning. All flickering. Small gold flames, moving the way flames move, alive, in the draught that always came up the passage from the dark below.

All but one.

A third of the way down. On a shelf cut into the rock.

It was still lit. It was still burning. Its flame was still there, small and bright, inside the little tin lantern.

But it wasn't moving.

It stood perfectly still inside the glass. Not flickering. Not bending in the draught. A small gold teardrop of flame, every edge of it sharp and clear and fixed, like a flame in a painting. Like a flame carved out of metal.

And the lantern round it was gold. And the shelf under it. And a hand's breadth of the rock.

Antho walked down the passage toward it. Slowly. His heart going.

He didn't need to read the tag tied to the handle. He knew whose it was. He'd watched an old woman light it, a week ago, with her hands shaking.

*For Wat. From his mam.*

---

The flame didn't move.

It would never move. It would never go out. It would burn there, gold and still and perfect, a third of the way down the passage at the Lantern Keep, for as long as there was a passage, and a Keep, and a world.

Kept.

Antho stood in front of it for a long time.

Then he turned round and went back up the passage. Past one thousand, one hundred and twelve small flames, flickering in the draught.

He didn't count them.

He'd count them tonight. When he got back. If he got back.

He'd count the gold one too.
