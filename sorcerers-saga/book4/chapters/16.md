# The Night of Glass

The cellar smelled of apples and earth and old stone.

Wren sat on the floor in the dark with her back against the cold wall and her daughter on her chest and listened.

She couldn't see anything. Not her hand. Not the baby. Not Seren, a yard away, sitting against the barrel where the apples used to be. Black. Total. The kind of dark you only get underground, the kind that presses on your open eyes like a hand.

She could hear.

Lark breathing. Small. Fast. Warm against her collarbone. Asleep. She'd gone down the ladder fussing and Wren had fed her in the dark and she'd gone quiet and heavy and slept.

Seren breathing. Slower. Steadier. A hedge-witch's breathing. In for four, out for four, the way you breathe at a door when you're listening for what's on the other side of it.

Above them, the trapdoor. And on the trapdoor, a weight. Enormous. Still. The boards creaking a little every time it shifted.

Hask.

And above Hask, very faint, through the floor, through the boards, through the black heat Lex had poured into every crack—

Nothing.

---

"*Tell her a story*," said Wren. Very quietly. Into the dark.

"*She's asleep*."

"*Tell it anyway*."

A pause. Then Seren's voice. Low. Just above a whisper.

"*There was a miller*," said Seren Harrow, "*who had a cat.*"

Wren closed her eyes. It made no difference.

"*And the cat was very lazy*," said Seren. "*It slept all day in the flour sacks. It never caught a single mouse. The miller's wife said get rid of it. The miller said, it's a good cat, it just hasn't found its mouse yet.*"

"*I don't know this one*."

"*Nobody does*. *I made it up. In the dark. For the little one.*" A breath. "*I told it four hundred and eleven times. It liked the bit where the cat falls in the millpond.*"

Wren almost laughed.

"*Go on, then*," she said. "*Get to the millpond.*"

---

Seren got to the millpond.

And then past it. The cat climbing out, wet and furious. The miller laughing. The wife saying I told you so. The cat sulking in the flour sacks for a week and coming out white from nose to tail like a ghost and frightening every mouse in the mill so badly they all moved to the next village.

*So the cat found its mouse after all*, said Seren. *It just had to be a ghost first.*

Wren didn't know when she'd started crying. In the dark. Silently. With her daughter asleep on her chest.

She didn't stop.

"*It's a terrible story*," she said.

"*I know*," said Seren. "*It's the best one I've got.*"

---

And then Lark woke up.

All at once. The way she always did. A jerk against Wren's chest. A small sharp intake of breath.

Wren felt her lift her head. Wobbling. In the dark.

And look.

---

Wren didn't know how she knew. She couldn't see. But she felt it. Felt the baby's head turn, slowly, against her collarbone. Away from Wren. Away from Seren. Up.

Toward the trapdoor.

Toward the cracks round the edges of it. The thin lines where the boards didn't quite meet the frame. Black. Sealed with black heat.

And in one of them—

A glint.

---

So small. So faint. A single point of light. Gold. No bigger than a grain of sand. Caught in the crack between the trapdoor and the frame, high up, by the hinge.

Glowing.

Not reflecting. There was nothing to reflect. There was no light in the cellar. It was *glowing*. On its own. Faint and gold and warm, like the last spark in a dead fire.

"*Seren*," said Wren.

"*I see it*."

The glint moved.

It slid. Out of the crack. Fell. Turning over and over in the dark, glittering, a tiny gold spark drifting down through the black air of the cellar the way a snowflake drifts down past a window.

It landed on the earth floor between them.

And lay there. Glowing.

And in it—

---

Wren leaned forward.

She didn't mean to. She couldn't help it. She leaned forward in the dark with the baby on her chest until her face was a foot from the floor, from the glowing grain, and looked.

There was a face in it.

Tiny. So tiny she could barely see it. Pale. White hair. Grey eyes. A face like the edge of a knife.

Looking up at her.

Smiling.

"*There you are*," said Ysenne.

---

Her voice came out of the grain. So small it was barely a sound. Like a fly at a window. Like a wet finger round the rim of a thimble.

"*In the dark*," said the Glass Queen. "*Under the floor. With the apples.*" The tiny face tilted. "*Did you think I needed light, Mistwalker? I don't need light. I only need something that shines.*" A pause. "*Oz's gold shines on its own. Did you know? It always has. Even in the dark. Even at the bottom of a cellar.*"

Wren didn't move.

"*Every grain*," said Ysenne. Softly. "*Every single grain of my brother's gold. A little mirror. A little window. A little door.*" The tiny face glittered. "*And Orrin's brought so many.*"

---

Above them, the trapdoor creaked.

Hask. Shifting. And then a sound. A grunt. Short. Hard. The sound a man makes when something lands on the back of his neck.

"*Hask*—"

"*It's coming through*." His voice. Through the boards. Rough. Tight. "*The floor. Up here. It's coming in under the door. Through the cracks. It's everywhere.*" A pause. "*It's on me.*"

"*Get off the trapdoor*—"

"*No*."

"*Hask, it's on you*—"

"*I said no*." The boards creaked. "*Nobody comes down this ladder. Not while I'm*—"

He stopped.

Wren heard him stop. Heard the silence where the rest of the sentence should have been.

And then, very quietly, through the boards—

"*Huh*," said Silas Hask.

---

"*What*?" said Wren. "*What is it? Hask, what*—"

"*It won't stick*."

She stared up into the dark.

"*It's landing on me*," said Hask. Slowly. Wonderingly. "*On my neck. My arms. My back. I can feel it. Little cold specks.*" A pause. "*And then it slides off. Like rain off oilcloth.*"

"*Why*?"

"*I don't know*." The boards creaked. "*I think*—" Another pause. Longer. "*I think it knows me. I think it remembers.*"

*She was in me*, he'd said. At the pool. *For a whole season. Her light. In my chest. I had her wings.*

"*It thinks I'm one of theirs*," said Silas Hask. Very quietly. "*It won't keep what it thinks it's already got.*"

---

More grains were falling.

Wren saw them. Glowing. Drifting down out of the cracks round the trapdoor like gold snow. Two. Five. Ten. A dozen. Turning over and over in the black air. Landing on the earth floor. On the old apple barrel. On the bottom rung of the ladder.

And where they landed, the gold spread.

Slowly. From each grain. Outward. Like ink in water. The earth floor round each grain going gold. Hard. Shining. The barrel-staves. The ladder rung. Little pools of gold, spreading, meeting, joining.

Glowing.

And in every one of them, a face.

---

"*Wren*," said Seren.

Wren was already moving.

She stood up. With the baby on her chest. In the black cellar with the gold falling round her like snow and the faces looking up out of the floor.

And breathed.

---

Not fog. Fog wouldn't help. Fog was for glass, for windows, for still surfaces you could breathe on and blur.

This was dust.

*Dust can't land on what's moving*, she thought. She'd thought it on the roofs at Ashbourne, with the gold coming down. *It can't settle. It can't stick. It needs still air.*

*So I won't be still*.

She called the wind.

---

There wasn't room for it. That was the trouble. The cellar was small. Low-roofed. Ten feet by twelve. The wind came when she called it, it always came, but it came into a box. It hit the walls and the ceiling and the floor and came back at her. Tumbling. Confused. Like a bird caught in a room.

She held it anyway.

She spun it. Round her. Round the baby. Round Seren, who'd come and pressed close against her side. A tight fast whirl of air, filling the cellar from wall to wall. Lifting the dust off the floor. Lifting the grains out of the air before they could land. Spinning them.

And the cellar *lit up*.

---

Gold.

Thousands of grains. Tens of thousands. Lifted off the floor, off the barrel, off the ladder, out of the air. Whirling round them in the dark. Glowing. Every one of them. A storm of tiny gold sparks, spinning, turning, lit from inside, filling the black cellar with light.

And in every spark, a face.

Tens of thousands of faces. Tiny. Pale. White-haired. Grey-eyed. Whirling round them in a golden storm. All of them looking at the baby.

All of them laughing.

---

And Lark laughed back.

Wren felt it before she heard it. Felt the baby on her chest go stiff with delight. Felt her square fists fly out. And then the laugh. Loud. Astonished. Delighted. The laugh from the roofs at Ashbourne. The laugh of someone who has just found the best thing in the world.

Wind. Gold. Lights. Spinning.

Lark laughed at the storm like it was a toy someone had hung over her cradle.

---

The faces stopped laughing.

Wren saw it. All of them. All at once. Tens of thousands of tiny pale faces in the whirling gold going still.

Looking at the baby.

"*Oh*," said Ysenne. From everywhere. From every spark. A hundred thousand tiny voices, all the same. Very small. Very quiet. "*Oh, she's laughing.*"

"*She likes the wind*," said Wren. Through her teeth. Holding it. Holding the whirl. Her arms shaking.

"*She's laughing at me*," said the Glass Queen. Wondering. "*Nobody's ever*—" She stopped. "*She's not afraid. Why isn't she afraid?*"

"*Because she's five months old*," said Seren. Pressed against Wren's side. Her voice very steady. "*And you're very pretty. And very shiny. And spinning.*" A pause. "*You're the best thing she's ever seen.*"

The sparks went very still in the air. Just for a moment. Hanging. Not spinning.

And then Wren lost the wind.

---

It was too much. Too small a room. Too long. Her arms were shaking and her lungs were burning and the wind had been bouncing off the walls for two minutes like a bird in a box and it got away from her.

Not all at once. A gust. Sideways. Out of the whirl. Slamming into the cellar wall and coming back wrong.

And the gold came through the gap.

---

It landed on her arm.

Her left. The one holding the baby. On the back of her hand. A spatter of it. Ten grains. Twenty.

She felt it. Cold. Like sleet.

And then she felt it *go in*.

---

She'd watched it happen to Tilda. On the green. She'd watched the gold creep up a girl's arm under the skin like a tide over sand.

She hadn't known what it felt like.

It felt like nothing.

That was the worst thing. It didn't hurt. It didn't burn. It was cold for a moment, and then it wasn't anything at all. A patch on the back of her hand that just—stopped. Stopped being cold. Stopped being warm. Stopped being hers.

She looked down.

In the glow of the whirling gold she could see it. The back of her hand. Gold. A patch the size of a coin. Spreading. Slowly. Toward her knuckles. Toward her wrist.

Toward the baby's back, where her hand lay flat.

---

"*OUT*," said Seren.

---

She'd never heard her mother-in-law shout. Not once. Seren didn't shout. Harrow women didn't.

She shouted now.

"*HASK*. *OPEN IT. NOW.*"

The trapdoor flew up.

Light. Not much. Grey. The faint red of the banked fire in the kitchen above, smothered in black heat. And dust. Gold dust. Pouring down through the open trapdoor like sand through the neck of an hourglass.

And Hask's face in the square of it. His head. His shoulders. Covered in gold. Glittering. Sliding off him. Like rain off oilcloth.

His hand came down. Enormous. Scarred. With a line of grey-black at the wrist.

"*Give her to me*," said Silas Hask.

---

Wren didn't think.

She held the baby up.

Her daughter. Five months old. Laughing. With her fists out and her face lit gold by the storm. She held her up through the whirling dust toward the hand of a man who'd once stood on Fennel Street in Highcrest and tried to carry her to the Moth Queen.

Who'd broken the cores. Who'd made the holes. Who'd worn the wings.

Whose skin the gold slid off like rain.

His hand closed round the baby.

Gently. So gently. The way he'd closed it round Kell's stick. One finger at a time.

And lifted her up. Out of the cellar. Out of the storm.

Into the dark.

---

Seren went next.

Up the ladder. Fast. Barefoot. Her hands on the rungs, which were gold now, some of them, hard and cold and shining. She didn't stop. She didn't look down.

Wren went last.

With her hand.

She didn't look at it. She climbed with it. It didn't hurt. It didn't feel like anything. It was just there, at the end of her arm, cold and stiff and wrong, and she gripped the rungs with it anyway and climbed.

She came up out of the cellar into the kitchen.

---

It was dark.

Black heat. She could feel it. Rolling off the walls. Pressing on her face like the heat from an oven. Lex's black heat, in every crack, in every board, eating every scrap of light.

And in it, the dust.

Glittering. Everywhere. In the air. On the floor. On the table. Glowing faint and gold in the black, because Oszric's gold glowed on its own. Swirling. Drifting. Settling.

Wherever it settled, the gold spread.

The table was gold. Half of it. The near end. The chairs. A gold cup on the gold table. The bench by the hearth. The edge of the hearthstone.

The hornlight.

On the sill of the boarded east window. The little iron lantern with its four panes of scraped horn. Unlit. Gold. All of it. The iron, the horn, the new wick inside. Gold. Shining. Still.

Kept.

---

And in the middle of the kitchen, in the black, with his hands out and his face lit from below by a faint red glow that didn't give off any light—

Lex.

---

He was holding it.

All of it. The whole kitchen. The black heat rolling off him in waves, the air round him so hot it shimmered. Eating the light. Keeping the dust dark. Every grain that came near him going dim, going black, its little face going out like a candle snuffed.

But only near him.

Only in a ring round him, a few yards across. Beyond that, the dust glittered. Glowed. Settled. Spread.

His face was grey. His hands were shaking. His eyes, in the faint red not-light, were very bright.

He saw her come up out of the trapdoor.

He saw her hand.

"*Go*," he said.

---

"*Lex*—"

"*Across*." His voice was hoarse. "*To Father's. Antho's there. The Lantern. Pim.*" His hands were shaking harder. "*Pim makes rooms. The dust can't get into his rooms.*"

"*Your hand*—"

"*Your hand*," said Lex. "*Wren. Your hand.*"

She held it up.

She hadn't meant to. She held it up in the faint gold glow of the dust, and they both looked at it.

The gold had reached her knuckles. It was creeping down her fingers. Toward the nails. And back. Toward the wrist.

"*It hasn't set*," said Lex.

He took one hand off the black heat.

---

The ring round him shrank.

Wren felt it. The dark pulling in. The glittering dust pressing closer. Settling on the floor a yard from his feet. Spreading.

He didn't look at it.

He took her hand. Her gold hand. In his. And shut his eyes.

And listened.

---

She felt it.

Not in the gold. There was nothing in the gold. Above it. Past it. At the wrist. At the edge, where the gold met her skin, where it was still moving, still creeping, still filling.

A *gap*.

Small. A hair. A breath. Opening. Just ahead of the gold. Between the edge of it and the rest of her. A space with nothing in it. A room.

And the gold came to the room.

And stopped.

---

She felt it stop. Felt it hesitate. Felt it sit at the edge of the room in her wrist and not know what to do. Like water at the edge of a step. Like a tide that has come up to a wall.

And then go back.

Slowly. From the wrist. Down the back of her hand. Toward the knuckles. Draining. Pink coming back behind it. Freckled. Hers.

And stopping.

At the knuckles.

The backs of her fingers stayed gold. From the knuckle to the nail. All four. Set. Finished. Full.

Her thumb was pink.

---

"*I'm sorry*," said Lex. Hoarsely. Letting go. "*I'm sorry. The fingers were*—"

"*It's my left*," said Wren.

He looked at her.

"*I'm right-handed*," said Wren Ashdown. "*I draw the bow with my right. I throw the knife with my right. I hold her with my left.*" She looked at her hand. At the four gold fingers. "*I can still hold her.*"

She could. She flexed it. The thumb moved. The palm moved. The fingers didn't. They stayed curled, gold, stiff, like the fingers of a statue. But the hand was a hand. It could cup. It could hold.

It could hold a baby's back.

"*Go*," said Lex. "*Wren. Please. Go.*"

She went.

---

Hask had the baby.

He was standing by the door. The kitchen door. With Lark in the crook of his good arm, held against his chest, against the scarred leather of his jerkin. The gold sliding off his shoulders. Off his head. Pooling round his boots.

Not touching her.

He'd curled himself round her. His enormous shoulders hunched. His head bent. His bandaged arm up over her like a roof. The dust came down on him and slid off and fell to the floor and didn't touch the baby at all.

She was looking up at him. In the gold glow. With her grey-green eyes wide.

Not laughing now. Not crying. Just looking. At the great scarred grey face bent over her. With wet on its cheeks.

"*She's all right*," said Hask. Hoarsely. "*Not a speck. Not one.*" He looked at Wren. "*I've got her.*"

Wren held out her arms.

He gave her back. Slowly. One finger at a time.

---

Seren opened the door.

---

The clearing was gold.

Wren stood in the doorway and couldn't breathe.

The fog was there. Her fog. White. Thick. Rolling. She'd laid it down at dusk and held it all night from the cellar without even thinking, the way you hold your breath. It was still there. Pressing against the cottage. Swallowing the birch wood.

But the dust was in it.

Glittering. Glowing. Hanging in the white like sparks in smoke. Billions of them. The whole clearing full of tiny gold lights, drifting, turning, settling.

And where they'd settled—

The grass was gold. In patches. Spreading. Joining. Gold grass, every blade. The woodpile was gold. The lean-to. The cart, with its tailboard down, where Brannoc and Hedda had sat the night before with their arms round each other. Gold.

A birch tree at the edge of the clearing was gold. The whole of it. Trunk and branches and every new leaf, shining in the fog like a thing made by a jeweller.

And every leaf a mirror.

---

She saw them.

In the gold. In every leaf of the gold birch. In every blade of the gold grass. In the gold wood of the cart and the gold boards of the lean-to.

Faces.

Pale. Tiny. Thousands. Turning. Watching.

"*There*," said Ysenne. From everywhere. "*There. You see? Everything shines, in the end. Everything.*"

---

And there was a voice under hers.

Dry. Soft. Hissing. Like sand poured slowly out of a sack. Coming out of the fog. Out of the dust. Out of every grain.

"*Forgive me*," said Orrin. "*I'm sorry. I'm so sorry.*"

He was in the fog. Somewhere. Everywhere. A thin bent shape made of dust, walking through the white in the middle of his own storm with his twig-arms held out to either side.

Scattering.

---

The others were fighting.

She saw them in flashes. Through the fog. Through the gold.

Kestrel. At the edge of the clearing. On her knees. With her hands flat on the ground and her red hair wild and her face white. Holding her part of the ring of sand. The black heat rolling off her in waves. The dust round her going dark.

But only round her.

Grandmother Asha and her two old women in red. Further round. In a row. Holding hands. Singing in their own language. The sand round them so hot the fog boiled off it in steam.

But the gold was coming over the ring. Between them. Where the heat was thinnest.

Elowen and her knights. On the track. Swords out. Swinging at nothing. At the dust. At the faces in the gold leaves. Because there was nothing else to swing at.

And Teo.

---

Teo was in the stream.

Up to his knees. In the water. With his arms out and his head thrown back and his whole gangling dripping body shaking.

Keeping it moving.

The whole stream. From the birch wood to the bend. Rippling. Churning. Splashing. Never still. Never still enough to show a face. The dust landing on it and being swept away. Carried off downstream. Tumbled. Broken up. Never settling.

Wren could see it costing him. His face was grey. His lips were blue. Water was running off him in sheets.

He wasn't stopping.

"*GO*," he shouted. At them. Across the gold clearing. Through the fog. "*CROSS. NOW. I CAN'T HOLD IT MUCH*—"

---

They ran.

Seren first. Barefoot. Across the gold grass. Wren didn't know if it would keep her. She didn't stop to find out. She ran behind her mother-in-law with her daughter clutched to her chest in her gold-fingered hand and her other hand out, calling the wind, spinning it round them, round the baby, a tight bright whirl that blew the dust away before it could land.

Hask behind. With his pick out. With the gold sliding off him.

To the stream.

To the stepping stones.

---

Seren was on the first stone.

The second.

Wren was on the first. With the baby. The water rushing round the stones. Rippling. Churning. Teo's water. Never still.

The third.

And Teo fell.

---

She didn't see it. She heard it. A cry. Short. Cut off. And then a splash. And then—

Nothing.

No splash. No rush. No churn.

*Silence*.

The stream stopped moving.

---

She looked down.

The water round the stepping stones. Under her feet. All along the stream. From the birch wood to the bend. Black. Smooth. Still.

Perfectly still.

And the dust came down on it.

---

It didn't sink. It didn't get carried away. It landed on the still black water like snow on a pond. And spread. From every grain. Outward. Gold. Joining. Meeting. A skin of gold across the surface of the stream. Hard. Smooth.

Shining.

A mirror.

A mirror the length of the stream, gold, flat, still, perfect.

And in it—

---

Not tiny.

Not a thousand faces in a thousand leaves.

One face. One figure. Huge. Filling the whole gold length of the stream. Lying in it the way a reflection lies in a pond. Long. Pale. White-gowned. White-haired. With a narrow band of glass on her brow.

Looking up at Wren.

From under her feet.

Smiling.

---

"*Oh*," said Ysenne. "*Oh, that's better.*"

And sat up.

---

Out of the stream.

Out of the gold. Out of the mirror. The way a woman sits up out of a bath. Her head first. Her white hair streaming, not wet, glittering. Her shoulders. Her arms. Her long white gown pouring off her like water and not like water.

Glass.

All of her. Not flesh. Not a reflection anymore. *Glass*. Clear and cold and faintly glittering, like frost on a window, like ice over deep water. You could see through her. You could see the fog behind her, the gold birch, the far bank. Bent. Distorted. As if through a lens.

She was half out of the stream. From the waist up. Her lower half still in the gold, still a reflection, still flat.

She looked at her hands.

Long. Clear. Glittering. Real.

"*Hands*," said the Glass Queen. Wonderingly. "*Oh, I've got hands. In the air. In the world.*" She turned them over. "*It's been so long. Nine hundred years. Nothing but glass.*" She breathed in. Wren saw her breathe in. Saw her glass chest rise. "*Oh. Oh, the air. It smells of something. What does it smell of?*"

"*Rain*," said Seren.

She was on the third stone. Turned round. Facing the Glass Queen across the gold water.

---

Ysenne looked at her.

"*Rain*," she said. Slowly. "*Yes. I remember rain.*" She tilted her head. Her glass crown caught the gold glow. "*Hesper's girl. Hello.*"

"*Go back*," said Seren.

"*I've only just come out*."

"*Go back*," said Seren Harrow. "*Into the glass. Into the stream. Into the gold. Go back to your brother and tell him no.*"

"*Everyone keeps telling me to tell him no*," said Ysenne. "*Nobody ever tells him themselves.*" She smiled. "*Except you. You told him. Through Rosie's rose. He laughed so hard, Orrin said, the cocoons shook.*"

---

And she turned her head.

Slowly. Her long glass neck turning. Her ice-grey eyes moving.

To Wren.

On the first stone. With the wind round her. With the baby on her chest. With her gold-fingered hand on the baby's back.

"*And there she is*," said the Glass Queen.

---

Wren didn't move.

She stood on the stepping stone with the gold water round her feet and the Glass Queen sitting up out of it two yards away, and didn't move.

Lark was looking at her.

At Ysenne. With her grey-green eyes. Wide. Not laughing. Not afraid. Looking.

The way she'd looked at her in the loft window. The first night. With milk on her chin.

The way nobody looked at the Glass Queen. Not at the glass. Not at the reflection. At *her*.

"*Hello*," said Ysenne. To the baby. Very softly. "*Hello. You looked at me. Do you remember? In the window. You were the first one in nine hundred years.*"

Lark frowned at her.

"*He wants you*," said the Glass Queen. "*My brother. He wants to put a crown on you and keep you. Forever.*" Her glass face was very still. "*He thinks it would be nice. He thinks it would be good for the Court. Something new.*"

She held out her hand.

Long. Clear. Glittering. Real.

"*Come here*," said Ysenne. "*Just to look. I won't hurt you. I only want to look.*"

---

And Lark reached back.

---

Wren felt it. The baby's arm, coming out of the sling. Her square fist. Opening. Toward the glass hand. Toward the glittering. Toward the long clear fingers reaching out of the gold water.

Opening and closing.

The way she reached for the nail.

"*No*," said Wren.

She stepped back.

On the stepping stone. One step. Backward. Toward the bank. Away from the glass hand.

And her heel came down on gold.

---

The edge of the stone. Where the dust had settled. Where the gold had spread. Smooth. Hard. Slick as ice.

Her foot went.

She felt it go. Felt the stone slide out from under her. Felt her weight go back. Felt the baby on her chest lurch.

Felt herself fall.

---

The wind caught her.

Not her wind. She'd lost hers. She'd lost it the moment her foot went. It wasn't hers.

It came from behind. From the bank. From the cottage. A gust. Hard. Sudden. Cold. Slamming into her back and holding her. Holding her up. Like a hand. Like a wall.

And a voice.

"*Not today*."

---

Antho.

On the bank. Behind her. In the doorway of the blue-door cottage, at the top of the oak ramp. With his Lantern in one hand, blazing, and his other hand out. Open. The wind pouring out of it.

Gale. Speaking.

His black hair wild. His face white. His eyes—

His eyes were the eyes of the man who'd stood in every kind of door.

"*Wren*," said Antho Vey. "*Come here.*"

---

She came.

Off the stepping stone. Not onto the next one. Onto the wind. Antho's wind. It caught her and carried her. Across the gold water. Over the last two stones. Up the bank. She hit the oak ramp running with the baby against her chest and Seren beside her and Hask behind, and Antho stepped back from the door, and they went through it.

Into the blue-door cottage.

Into the dark.

---

Antho slammed the door.

Wren stood in the middle of the room and breathed.

It was dark. Black heat. Boards over every window. And in the middle of it, Antho's Lantern. The Keeper's Lantern. On the table. Blazing. Gold. The only light.

And round the Lantern, round the table, round the bed under the west window—

*Quiet*.

---

She felt it. The moment she came through the door. Like stepping into a room where someone has been sleeping. Like walking into the hush of a chapel. Soft. Still. Close. A *room*. Inside the room.

Pim.

He was sitting on the floor by the bed. Cross-legged. With his small thin hands open on his knees and his eyes shut and his face very white.

Making room.

The dust didn't come in. Wren could see it. Through the cracks round the door, round the boarded windows. Glittering. Sifting. Drifting into the dark of the cottage.

And stopping.

At the edge of Pim's room. A yard from the walls. Hanging in the air like flies at a pane. Unable to come further. Unable to settle. Because there was nothing to settle *on*. Because the room Pim made was the one thing in the world the gold couldn't fill.

Because it was empty on purpose.

---

"*Is she*—" said a voice. From the bed. Thin. Dry. A thread.

Arion.

Propped on his pillows. Under the boarded west window. His white hair wild. His grey eyes very bright in the Lantern-light.

"*She's all right*," said Seren. Kneeling by the bed. Taking his hand. "*She's all right. Not a speck. Hask had her.*"

"*Wren's hand*—"

"*It's my left*," said Wren. From the middle of the room. With the baby. Breathing. "*I hold her with my left.*"

---

Antho was at the door.

With his back against it. With his hand flat on the wood. Listening. The way his mother listened at doors.

"*She's still out there*," he said. Very quietly.

Nobody answered.

"*In the stream*," said Antho. "*In the gold. Half out. I can feel her.*" He shut his eyes. "*She's not trying the door. She's not trying anything.*" A pause. "*She's waiting.*"

"*For what*?" said Hask.

Antho opened his eyes.

He was looking at the table. At the Lantern.

The Keeper's Lantern. That Lex had forged for him six years ago in Malachi's valley, with HUSH ×12 and SHELTER cut into its iron. That he'd carried every day since. That had held the Murmur off for six years. That burned now on the table in the middle of Pim's room, gold, blazing, the only light.

Glass.

Four panes. Clear. Clean. Shining in its own light.

---

Wren saw it.

They all saw it.

In the pane facing the bed. Small. Pale. Growing.

A face.

---

"*Oh, warden*," said Ysenne. From the glass of the Lantern. From inside Pim's room. From the one thing in the cottage that shone. "*You brought a light.*"

Antho went white.

"*Every room needs a light*," said the Glass Queen. Softly. "*Doesn't it. Even his.*" The tiny face in the pane tilted toward Pim. Toward the boy on the floor with his eyes shut. "*Even the quiet ones. Even the empty ones.*" A smile. "*Especially those.*"

Pim didn't open his eyes.

But Wren saw his hands shake. On his knees.

"*He can keep the dust out*," said Ysenne. "*He's very good. He's the best I've ever seen. Better than Hes.*" The face in the glass was growing. Filling the pane. "*But he can't keep me out. Not if you've brought me in yourselves.*"

And a hand came out of the Lantern.

---

Long. Clear. Glittering. Glass.

Reaching out of the pane the way a hand reaches out of water. Fingers first. Then the wrist. Then the forearm. Thin. Fine. Impossible. Coming out of a pane of glass no bigger than a playing card.

Into the room.

Toward the bed.

Toward the old man on the pillows, and the woman kneeling beside him, and—behind them, against the wall, where Wren had backed without knowing she'd moved—

The baby.

---

"*Antho*," said Arion.

Very quietly. From the pillows. Not frightened. Not loud.

The voice of a man who had been Warden-Architect of the Spire for two hundred years and had designed every mirror-ward in it.

"*Put the Lantern down on the floor*," said Arion Vey. "*Glass side up. And then hand me my stick.*"
