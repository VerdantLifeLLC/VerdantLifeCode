# The Gilded Girl

Pim had been listening to her for four days.

Nobody else could hear it. He knew that. He'd known it since the first day, on the white road, near the beginning, when the others walked on and he stopped and looked at a small gold girl at the side of the road with a wooden spoon in her hand.

Her eyes had been moving fast.

Everyone else's moved slow. Like clock-hands. Like the shadow of a post going round on a summer day. Hers darted. Pim knew that kind of looking. He'd done it himself. For twelve years. In a cellar in Millbridge with a man called Kessick upstairs.

Fast eyes. Looking for the door.

He'd looked back. Twice. He couldn't help it.

And the second time, something had happened.

---

He hadn't meant to.

He never meant to. It just came out of him sometimes. The quiet. When he looked at something that was frightened. It came out of him the way warmth comes out of a hearth, without the hearth deciding.

He'd looked back at the gold girl, the second time, and the quiet had come out of him. Just a little. Just a breath of it. Across the road. Into her.

And he'd felt it *catch*.

Somewhere deep in the gold. A hair's-breadth. A crack too small to see. The way it had caught in Bet Dale, at the Keep, in the spring, when a tear ran out of a gold eye.

And then Antho had said *Pim, thirteen cups*, and he'd turned round, and walked on.

And behind him, very faint, so faint nobody but him could ever have heard it—

*Scrape*.

---

He'd heard it every Pim since.

Walking down the white road between the kept with the basket on his back. In the night, sitting in the dust holding the room. Under the hum. Under the one low endless note that came out of nine thousand gold throats.

*Scrape*.

Slow. Very slow. An hour apart. More. A sound like a stone being dragged a finger's width across another stone.

Behind them. Far behind. Getting no further behind.

Following.

---

He didn't tell anyone.

He didn't tell anyone things. He'd learned that very young. Things you told people got used against you. Things you kept were yours. He'd been silent for twelve years and talking for three, and he still kept almost everything.

But he'd drawn her.

On the fourth night. In the dust, behind the last of the kept, in the room he was holding with Antho's hand on his knee holding the edge. With the pencil Ivy had given him and the back of a page from Seraphine's notebook. A girl in a grey dress too big for her. A wooden spoon. Big eyes.

And a boy, beside her, holding out his hand.

Then he'd waited till Antho slept.

---

It was easy to go.

That was the strange thing. He'd thought it would be hard. He'd thought he'd have to creep.

He just stood up. In the middle of the room. And the room stayed. Because Antho was holding the edge of it. Because Antho had learned, in four days, to hold the edge of a room the way you'd hold the edge of a door, without thinking, even asleep.

Pim looked at him.

Asleep on his side in the dust. With his black hair over his face and his hand still out, flat on the ground, where Pim's knee had been. Still holding.

*He'll be cross*, thought Pim.

He folded the drawing. And put it on the dust beside the basket. And put a stone on it so it wouldn't blow away, though there was no wind, though nothing had blown away in the Court for nine hundred years.

And went.

---

He made a room for himself.

Small. Just him. Round him like a coat. He'd been doing it his whole life. In the cellar in Millbridge. In the snow outside the Lantern Keep. It was the easiest thing in the world. Easier than talking. Easier than breathing.

The Court pressed on it. Heavy. Full. Like water pressing on a bubble.

It didn't get in.

He walked back down the white road. East. Toward the door. Between the kept. With his room round him like a coat and his hand on the pocket over his heart, where Ivy's cat was.

The kept watched him go.

Thousands of gold eyes. Moving slowly. Following.

He didn't look at them. He listened.

*Scrape*.

---

He found her in the second hour.

Further back than he'd thought. Not as far back as she should have been. She should have been four Pims back. At the beginning. Near the door. Where she'd stood for nine hundred years.

She wasn't.

She was *here*.

On the white road. Not at the side of it. On it. In the middle of the stone. Small. Gold. In her grey dress too big for her, gold now, every fold. With her wooden spoon in her hand, gold. With one foot forward and one foot back. Mid-step.

Walking.

Behind her, on the white stone, a line of marks. Small. Scraped. Gold dust ground into the white. Footprints. Going back down the road, into the dim, as far as Pim could see.

Four days of them.

---

He stopped.

A few feet away. In the middle of the road. With his room round him.

She didn't move.

She couldn't. Not really. Not the way people move. She'd been gold for nine hundred years. She was gold all the way through, solid, full.

Except somewhere. Deep. In the crack he'd made when he looked back.

Her eyes were on him.

Fast. Darting. His face. The pocket over his heart. His face.

And then, very slowly, so slowly he could barely see it, her front foot came down.

*Scrape*.

A finger's width. On the white stone.

Toward him.

---

Pim sat down.

He didn't know why. It seemed right. He sat down cross-legged on the white road in front of her, the way he sat down in front of frightened students at the Keep, the way he'd sat in front of the hole in the cold-room, the way he'd sat on the rim of the pool beside Ivy.

He looked up at her.

She was very small. Smaller than him. Seven. Eight. With a round face and a snub nose and her hair scraped back under a cap. A kitchen girl. The kind who scrubbed pans and turned spits and got cuffed when the bread burned.

He knew that kind.

He'd been that kind. In a different kitchen. With a different man.

"*Hello*," said Pim.

---

Her eyes stopped darting.

They stopped. On his face. And stayed there.

He'd never said hello to anyone first. Not in his whole life. People said hello to him and he didn't answer, or nodded, or looked at them with his grey-violet eyes until they went away. He'd said hello to Antho, once, the first winter, after a month. Antho had nearly dropped his soup.

He said it again.

"*Hello*. *I'm Pim.*"

Nothing. Of course nothing. She was gold.

But her eyes didn't move off his face.

---

He didn't know what to do.

He sat on the white road and looked at her and didn't know. He'd come all this way back. Two hours. In the night that wasn't night. Leaving Antho holding a room alone. Leaving the cups. Leaving everyone.

And he didn't know what to do.

So he did the only thing he knew.

He got quiet.

---

Not the coat. Not the little room round himself that he'd worn walking. *More*.

He let it out. Slowly. The way you'd let out a long breath. Out of his chest. Out of the place deep in him where he'd made a room a long time ago, out of being hurt, and lived in it. Out into the air. Out into the white road. Out round the gold girl standing on it with one foot forward and her eyes on his face.

A room. Round both of them.

Soft. Close. Still. The way the cellar in Millbridge had been still, at night, when Kessick was asleep and nobody was going to come down the stairs for hours and hours.

The safe kind of still.

And then he leaned it in.

---

Into her.

Not pushing. You couldn't push gold. He'd learned that at the Keep, with Bet Dale. It was full. It was solid. There was nothing to push into.

He didn't push.

He *asked*.

*Is there anywhere*, the quiet said. Very softly. *Is there any room. Anywhere at all. Just a little. Just enough.*

*You've been walking for four days. You walked out of your place. You walked after me. There must be a crack. There must be.*

*Show me*.

---

And she did.

---

He felt it. Deep. So deep. In the middle of the gold. Where the heart would be, if gold had a heart.

A crack.

Not the hair's-breadth from before. Bigger. *Wider*. Four days of walking had worked it. Four days of dragging a gold foot a finger's width across white stone, every hour, toward a boy who'd looked back. It had worked it wider. Like a crack in ice that someone keeps stepping on.

There was room in it.

Not much. Not enough to move. Not enough to breathe.

Enough to say one thing.

---

Pim poured into it.

He didn't mean to. He couldn't help it. He found the room in her and the quiet in him went into it the way water goes into a cupped hand, without deciding, without asking, because that was where it went.

All of it.

He felt it go. Out of him. Into her. His room. His quiet. The thing he'd made out of being hurt and lived in for twelve years.

Into a crack in a gold girl at the side of the road.

And the girl's mouth moved.

---

Not much.

Her lips. Gold. Parted a little, the way they'd been parted for nine hundred years, as if she'd been about to say something when the King put his hand on her.

They moved.

Closed. Opened. A finger's width. Slow as a flower.

And a sound came out.

Not the hum. Not the one low note. Something else. Small. Cracked. Rusty. The sound of a voice that hadn't been used in nine hundred years, trying.

"*...mam...*"

---

Pim didn't move.

He sat on the white road with his whole quiet poured out into a gold girl and his hands open on his knees and looked up at her.

"*...mam...*" she said again.

Barely a breath.

And a tear ran out of her eye.

Out of the corner of it. Out of the gold. Wet. Clear. Salt. It ran down her gold cheek and fell off her gold chin and landed on the white stone between them.

And didn't go gold.

It lay there. On the white road. A small wet spot. Clear.

The only wet thing in the Court.

---

"*Where*?" said Pim.

He didn't know he was going to ask. It came out of him. Very quiet.

"*Where's your mam*?"

The girl's eyes moved.

Not fast now. Slow. So slow. Off his face. Down. Down the road. Back the way she'd come. Back toward the door.

And stopped.

Pim turned round. And looked.

At the white road going back into the dim. At the rows of gold on either side. Cooks and grooms and maids and gardeners. Thousands of them.

He couldn't tell which one.

He looked back at the girl.

"*I'll find her*," he said.

---

He'd never promised anything in his life.

Promises were like hellos. Like telling things. They got used against you. Kessick used to promise. *I won't hit you if you're quiet*. *I won't hit you if you're good*.

Pim had stopped believing in promises when he was five.

He promised now.

"*I'll find her*," he said again. Out loud. On the white road. To a gold girl with a tear on her chin. "*Not now. I can't now. There's not enough room.*" He swallowed. "*But there's a pool. In a green field. With a girl who draws cats. It's full of room. It's still little.*"

The gold girl's eyes were on his face.

"*When it's big*," said Pim. "*When there's enough. I'll come back. I'll find your mam. I'll find everyone's.*"

He'd never said so many words in a row in his life.

"*I promise*," said Pim.

---

And the hum changed.

---

He felt it first. In the road. In the dust. In the air. The one low note that had been going on for four days, for nine hundred years, out of nine thousand gold throats.

It *shifted*.

Not louder. Not higher. It just—moved. Like a sleeper turning over. Like a field of barley when the wind first touches it.

And he heard it.

Not a note. A word.

---

The kept on either side of the road. The ones nearest. A washerwoman with a gold basket. A man with a gold rake. A boy with a gold hoop. Their mouths didn't move. They couldn't. They were gold all the way through, full, with no room at all.

But the hum coming out of them had *shape* now. Because a girl had said one word, out loud, on the white road, through a crack a boy had made. And the word had gone into the hum like a stone into a pond.

And the hum had remembered what it was.

He heard it. All round. Low. Soft. Worn smooth by nine hundred years, like a step worn hollow by feet, like a coin rubbed smooth in a pocket.

But a word.

One word.

The only word they had left.

*...home...*

---

Pim sat on the white road and listened to nine thousand people say *home*.

He didn't cry. He never cried. He'd stopped crying when he was five, at the same time as he stopped believing in promises.

He found he was crying anyway.

---

*Footsteps*.

Behind him. On the white road. Running. Heavy. Fast. Coming from the west.

He didn't turn round. He knew who it was.

They stopped. A few yards behind him. He heard breathing. Hard. Fast. The breathing of a man who's been running for two hours in a country where there's no room to breathe.

"*Pim*," said Antho.

---

He didn't sound cross.

Pim had thought he would. He'd thought about it, walking back. He'd thought, *he'll be cross, he'll be so cross, he'll shout*.

Antho never shouted. Not at Pim. Not once in four years. But Pim had thought, this time.

He didn't shout.

He came round. Slowly. Into the room. Into what was left of it. Pim felt him come in. Felt him feel it, the way he felt every door. And stop.

He was looking at the girl.

At her gold face. At her gold mouth, open a finger's width. At the tear on her chin.

At the small wet spot on the white stone.

"*Oh*," said Antho Vey. Very quietly. "*Oh, Pim.*"

---

He knelt down.

On the white road. Beside Pim. In front of the gold girl. He didn't say anything else for a long time.

Then he put his hand out. Not touching her. Just near. A foot from her gold chest. Where the heart would be.

And shut his eyes.

Pim watched his face. He knew what Antho was doing. Feeling. For the door. The last one. The one everyone gets.

The one the King took.

Antho's face changed.

---

"*There's a crack*," he said.

Very quiet. Not opening his eyes.

"*Where the door should be*," said Antho. "*Where it was. Before.*" His voice was shaking. "*It's bricked up. Gold. Solid. Like all of them. Like Bet.*" A pause. "*But there's a crack. In the gold. Right where the door used to be.*"

Pim didn't say anything.

"*You made a crack*," said Antho. Opening his eyes. Looking at him. "*In the place where her door was. You made a crack in it.*"

"*She did*," said Pim.

Antho looked at him.

"*She did*," said Pim again. "*Walking. Four days. After me.*" He looked at the footprints on the white stone, going back into the dim. "*I only looked back.*"

---

Antho didn't say anything for a long time.

He knelt on the white road with his hand out toward the gold girl and looked at the scraped footprints going back toward the door. Four days of them. A finger's width every hour.

"*Not enough*," he said at last.

"*No*."

"*It's not a door*. *It's a crack where one was.*" He took his hand back. "*She can't get through it. Not yet. Nobody could. There's no room on the other side.*"

"*I know*."

"*But there's a crack*," said Antho Vey. "*There's a crack.*" He looked at Pim. His face was wet. "*Lex says everything's a crack first.*"

"*I promised her*," said Pim.

Antho went still.

"*I promised*," said Pim. "*I said I'd come back. When the pool's big. When there's room. I said I'd find her mam.*" He looked at the girl. "*I've never promised anything.*"

Antho looked at him for a long time.

"*Then we'll come back*," he said.

---

"*Smith says nineteen days*," said Pim.

"*Eleven cups*," said Antho. "*Sixteen days, now. You've been gone two hours and I've been running after you for two.*" He didn't say it crossly. He just said it. "*That's not counting walking back.*"

"*I'm sorry*."

"*Don't be*," said Antho. "*I'd have done it. I've done worse. I went into the Hollow on my own once to keep a promise to a grey girl.*" He looked at the gold girl. At the tear on her chin. "*It was the right thing.*"

"*Was it*?"

"*It was the right thing and it cost two cups*," said Antho. "*Most right things cost something. You just have to decide if you'll pay.*"

---

Pim stood up.

He felt it as he did. Something in him. Thinner. He'd poured his quiet into the girl. All of it. Into the crack. And some of it had stayed there.

He could feel it. In her. A little room. His. Inside her gold. Small and quiet and still.

He'd left it there.

He didn't want it back.

"*Ilda*," he said.

Antho looked at him.

"*Her name*," said Pim. "*Ilda.*" He didn't know how he knew. He'd known since the tear. Since the word. Since the room. It had come back to him out of the crack the way a smell comes back to you on the wind. "*She's Ilda. She turned the spit in the King's kitchen. Her mam was the bread-woman. She was eight.*"

"*How do you know*?"

Pim didn't answer.

He looked at the girl. At Ilda. At her gold eyes on his face.

"*Goodbye, Ilda*," he said.

And her eyes followed him. All the way. As he turned. As he walked back up the white road beside Antho. West. Toward the palaces.

Until he couldn't see her anymore.

And behind them, very faint—

*Scrape*.

---

They walked back in silence.

Two hours. Between the kept. With the hum all round them. Pim could hear it now. He couldn't stop hearing it. Nine thousand voices, all saying the same word through throats that couldn't open.

*...home...*

He didn't know if Antho could hear it. He thought so. Antho's face was very still. The still face. The one he got when he was holding a door.

They didn't talk.

Halfway back, Antho put his hand on Pim's shoulder. And left it there. And they walked like that.

---

He saw the face in the gold an hour from the camp.

In a gold washerwoman's basket. In the curve of the gold linen piled in it, which shone, the way all the gold shone, dully, in the grey-gold light.

A face. Small. Pale. White-haired. Grey-eyed.

Looking at him.

---

He stopped.

Antho stopped too. And saw.

"*Don't*," said Antho. Very quietly. "*Pim. Don't look at it. Keep walking.*"

Pim didn't keep walking.

The face in the gold linen was looking at him. Not at Antho. At him. Very still. Very steady.

"*Room-maker*," said Ysenne.

---

Her voice was very small. Like a fly at a window. Like a wet finger round the rim of a thimble.

"*You made her cry*," said the Glass Queen. "*The little cook. Ilda. The spit-girl.*" The tiny face tilted. "*I've watched her for nine hundred years. Every day. In every shiny thing on that road. She's never cried. Not once.*"

Pim didn't say anything.

"*Nobody's ever made any of them cry*," said Ysenne. "*Not Oz. Not Mae. Not me.*" A pause. "*I tried. Once. In the first century. I sat in a spoon in her hand and talked to her for a year. I told her every story I knew. She never even blinked.*"

"*You didn't make room*," said Pim.

The face went still.

"*You talked*," said Pim. "*You filled it up. With stories.*" He looked at the tiny pale face in the gold linen. "*She didn't need filling. She was full. She needed room.*"

---

The Glass Queen didn't answer for a long time.

"*Room*," she said at last. Slowly. As if tasting the word. "*Yes. That's what the little man with the hammer said. In his note. The scribe read it to me. Unwrite it when there's someone who can make room.*"

"*Pim*," said Antho. "*Come away.*"

"*He means you*," said Ysenne. Not to Antho. To Pim. "*Doesn't he. The little man. He means things like you. Things that make room.*" The tiny face was very close to the surface of the gold linen now. Pressed against it, almost. Like a face pressed against a window. "*I've never seen one. Not in nine hundred years. Not in all the glass in two worlds.*"

"*Pim*—"

"*Come and see my house*," said the Glass Queen.

---

The gold linen moved.

Not the face. The *gold*. The surface of it. The shine. It went bright. Brighter. A gleam spreading out across the curve of gold cloth in the gold basket on the gold washerwoman's hip, like sun on water.

And a hand came out of it.

---

Pim had seen it before. At Harrow. Out of the Lantern. Out of the stream.

Long. Clear. Glittering. Glass. Fingers first. Then the wrist. Then the arm. Thin. Fine. Impossible. Reaching out of a curve of gold no bigger than a dinner plate.

Toward him.

---

He didn't move.

He should have. He knew that. Antho was shouting. Antho was reaching for him. Antho's hand was coming down on his shoulder to pull him back.

He didn't move.

Because he was empty.

That was the thing. That was what he understood, afterward, lying on the floor of a room made of glass. He'd poured his quiet into a gold girl on the white road. All of it. Into the crack. He'd left some of it there.

He'd come back without enough.

Not enough to make a room. Not enough to hold one. Not enough to stand in front of a glass hand reaching out of a gold basket and be the one thing in the world that couldn't be filled.

He was just a boy.

---

The glass hand closed round his wrist.

Cold. Like ice. Like the bottom of a river.

"*I won't hurt you*," said Ysenne. Very softly. Very close. "*I promise. I only want to look.*"

And pulled.

---

He felt himself go into the gold.

Not through it. *Into* it. Like falling into still water. Like going through a window that wasn't open. His wrist first. His arm. His shoulder. Cold. Bright. Glittering. The world going flat round him. Going thin. Going *shiny*.

He heard Antho shout his name.

He felt Antho's hand on his other arm. Gripping. Pulling. Hard.

And slipping.

---

The last thing he saw before the glass closed over him was the washerwoman's face.

Gold. Still. Kept. With her basket on her hip.

Her eyes were on him.

Moving. Slowly. Following.

And her mouth—

Her mouth didn't move. It couldn't. She was full. All the way through.

But the hum coming out of her, as he went into the gold, as the glass closed over his head, as Antho's hand slipped off his arm—

The hum had shape.

*...home...*

---

And then there was only glass.

And light.

And a face. Everywhere. In every surface. Pale. White-haired. Grey-eyed.

Smiling.

"*There*," said the Glass Queen. From everywhere. From a thousand mirrors. "*There you are. Welcome to my house, room-maker.*"

A pause.

"*I've been so lonely*," she said.
