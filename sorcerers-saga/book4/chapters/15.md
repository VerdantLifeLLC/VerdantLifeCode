# Midsummer Eve

Lark got her first tooth on the ninth day before midsummer, and the first thing she did with it was bite her father.

"*Ow*," said Lex.

"*Ah*," said Lark. With great satisfaction.

She'd been chewing on his knuckle. She'd been chewing on everything for a fortnight. Her fists, his fingers, Wren's braid, the edge of the table, the horseshoe nail on its red ribbon, which Wren had had to lower within reach because Lark had screamed at it for an hour. Chewing and drooling and glaring and chewing.

And then, on the ninth morning before midsummer, sitting on his knee at the table by the east window while he ate his porridge, she'd clamped down on his knuckle with her gums, the way she did, and there'd been something in them.

Small. Sharp. Hard as a nail-point.

He held up his hand. There was a mark on the knuckle. A tiny white dent.

"*She bit me*," he said.

"*She's a smith's daughter*," said Wren. From the other side of the table. Not looking up from her own porridge. "*She's testing your temper.*"

"*She drew blood*."

"*She did not draw blood*."

"*Nearly*."

Wren looked up. At the knuckle. At the baby, sitting on Lex's knee with drool on her chin and a look on her face like a cat that has just knocked something off a shelf and is waiting to see what you'll do about it.

Wren started to laugh.

---

It was a good morning.

He'd think that, later. Over and over. The way Wren thought it about the morning at Old Mag's. That it had been a good morning. Grey. Warm. The windows soaped white and the hornlight glowing soft and gold on the sill behind its four panes of horn. The stream loud outside. Wren laughing. Lark with drool on her chin and a new tooth and a look of triumph.

Nine days.

*At midsummer*, Orrin had said. In Old Mag's kitchen. *On the shortest night. When the windows are open for the heat.*

*His Majesty's sister will come. In every window. In every glass. For the child.*

---

*[You're doing it again*,] said Ember.

*Doing what*?

*[Counting*.] A pause. *[Nine days. You've been counting since she said it. Nine. Ten. Eleven. Twelve. You count when you're frightened. You counted iron rings down a gallery.]*

*I'm not frightened*.

*[You're a smith with a tooth-mark in his knuckle and a Glass Queen coming through his windows in nine days*,] said Ember. *[If you're not frightened I'm going to start worrying about you.]*

---

The hornlight went out on the eighth day.

Lex came down the ladder from the loft at dawn and it was dark. The east window. The sill. The little iron lantern with its four panes of scraped horn, that he'd lit with his own fire on the first night home from the Blightmarch and that had never once gone out.

Dark.

He stood at the bottom of the ladder and stared at it.

Then he heard the chewing.

---

The goat was under the table.

Correspondence. Malachi's goat. White and brown and bony, with yellow eyes and a beard and a bell on her collar that didn't ring, because she'd eaten the clapper years ago. She'd come to Harrow with Seren in the winter, from the Keep, and lived in the lean-to, and roamed. She roamed everywhere. She'd eaten half a bridle. She'd eaten a tapestry in the Hall of Lanterns. She'd eaten, Antho swore, a whole volume of Founders' records at the Keep, including the index.

She was under the table now. Chewing. Thoughtfully. With her yellow eyes half shut.

There was a little bit of something hanging out of the corner of her mouth. Pale. Fibrous. Charred at the end.

Lex looked at the hornlight.

The little iron door on the side was open. The goat must have nosed it. And inside, where the wick should have been, the wick he'd twisted himself from cotton and soaked in tallow and lit with his own fire on the first night home—

Nothing.

---

"*She ate the wick*," said Wren.

She'd come down the ladder behind him. She was standing on the bottom rung in his shirt with Lark on her hip, looking at the goat.

"*She ate the wick*," said Lex. "*While it was lit.*"

"*How do you eat a wick while it's lit*?"

"*Ask her*."

Correspondence opened one yellow eye. Looked at them. Chewed.

"*She's Malachi's goat*," said Wren. Slowly. "*He had her for fifty years. In the Wastes. In a cave. With a fire in it.*" She looked at the goat. "*She's probably eaten worse.*"

"*She's eaten my lantern*."

"*She's eaten the wick of your lantern*," said Wren. "*You've got more wick. You've got more fire.*" She shifted Lark on her hip. "*You're a Worldforger. Light it again.*"

---

He lit it again.

New wick. Twisted cotton. Soaked in tallow. He set it in the hornlight and held his hand over it and let the fire come up, small, gold, the way it came every morning when he said good morning to the hearth.

It caught.

It burned. Soft. Gold. Blurred behind the horn.

It was the same. It looked the same. A little gold glow in the east window, like a lantern in a fog.

He knew it wasn't.

He stood in front of it for a long time.

*[It's just a wick*,] said Ember. Quietly.

*It's the first one. The one from the Blightmarch. The one I lit the first night home.*

*[It's a wick, Lex. It's twisted cotton and tallow. It's been burning for six months. It was going to go out eventually.]* A pause. *[Things do.]*

He looked at the little gold glow.

*[That's not an omen*,] said Ember. *[It's a goat.]*

*I know*.

*[You don't believe in omens*.]

*I know*.

*[Then stop looking at it like it's one*,] said Ember. Gently.

---

He didn't tell his father.

He went across the stream that afternoon and sat by the bed and his father asked him about the preparations, and he told him. The boards for the windows. The black heat. The cellar.

He didn't tell him about the wick.

---

His father was in bed most days now.

Ilsa said that was all right. Ilsa said it was the hip, still, and the tiredness, and he'd be up again soon. For an hour. In the chair. At the window.

Lex didn't believe her.

He didn't think she believed herself.

His father lay on the narrow bed under the west window with the curtain drawn back and his white wisp of hair on the pillow and the grey book on the blanket by his hand. He was thinner. Lex could see it in his wrists. In his neck. In the way the bones of his face stood out, sharp, under the walnut skin, like the frame of a house when the plaster's coming off.

His eyes were the same.

Grey. Sharp. Turning from the window as Lex came in.

"*Good afternoon*," said Arion Vey.

---

"*There's a box under the bed*," said his father. When Lex had told him about the boards and the black heat and the cellar.

Lex looked at him.

"*You know the one*," said Arion. "*Would you?*"

Lex reached under the bed.

The box came out heavier than before. He could feel it. He set it on his knees and opened the lid, and it sighed.

Letters. Folded. Sealed with grey wax. A line on each.

He counted them. He couldn't help it.

Eleven.

*For Lark. On her eleventh birthday*.

---

"*Eleven*," said Lex.

"*My hand's been bad*," said his father. "*I'd hoped for more by now.*" He looked at the box. "*Ten more. I'll try. One a week, if I can manage.*"

"*It's nine days to midsummer*."

"*I know*."

"*And then we're going*. *Across. Through the crack.*"

"*I know*," said his father. "*I've been counting too.*"

---

Lex closed the box.

It sighed. He sat with it on his knees and didn't say anything.

"*You want to ask me something*," said his father.

"*No*."

"*You've wanted to ask me something since the pool*," said Arion. "*Since the Knot. Since Elric read the last of the notes.*" His grey eyes were very steady. "*You've been sitting on it for weeks. Like a hen on an egg.*"

Lex looked at the box.

"*Whoever turns it last*," he said.

"*Goes with what it unwrites*."

"*Elric's going to do it*."

"*Yes*."

"*You knew*. *You worked it out. Before he said.*"

"*I worked it out*," said his father. "*He's my friend. I know how he thinks. I know how he's always thought.*" A faint smile. "*First, Lex. He wants to go first. He's wanted to go first his whole life.*"

"*And you're going to let him*."

His father didn't answer for a long time.

---

"*It's not mine to let*," said Arion at last.

Lex looked up.

"*It's not mine*," said his father again. "*It's not yours. It's his.*" He turned his head on the pillow. Toward the window. Toward the birch wood. "*He gave back his threads at the lid and went into the Knot and wrapped himself round the Key, and he's been holding it for three years in the dark, alone, counting threads, so nobody could ever turn it again.*" His voice was very quiet. "*If he wants to be the one who turns it the last time, that's his. He's paid for it. He's paid for it ten times over.*"

"*He'll die*."

"*He's already dead*, *Lex.*" His father looked back at him. "*Mostly. He's been dead for three years. He's a thread. He said so himself.*" A pause. "*He's asking to finish. That's all. To be good for something.*"

Lex didn't say anything.

"*I've been asking the same thing*," said Arion Vey. "*All year.*"

---

Lex put the box back under the bed.

Carefully. On this side. Where his father could reach it if he leaned. He sat back up and looked at the old man on the pillow and found he couldn't say anything at all.

"*When we come back*," he said at last. Hoarsely. "*From across. With the crown.*"

"*Yes*."

"*Be here*."

His father looked at him.

"*I'll try*," said Arion.

"*Don't try*. *Be here.*"

"*Lex*." His father's voice was very gentle. "*I don't make promises I can't keep. Your mother taught me that. Page nine.*" He reached out. Slowly. Across the blanket. And found his son's hand. "*I'll try. I'll try very hard. I'll be at the window every evening, looking west, the way I was when your mother went to the river.*" His grip was so weak. "*That's all I can promise. Trying.*"

Lex held his hand.

"*It's not enough*," he said.

"*No*," agreed his father. "*It never is.*"

---

Wren wanted to go.

She told him that night. In the loft. In the dark. With the baby asleep in her cradle under the window and the new wick burning in the hornlight downstairs, and the stream loud outside.

"*I'm coming*," she said.

He'd known she would say it. He'd been waiting for it for weeks.

"*Across*," she said. "*Through the crack. With you.*"

"*Wren*—"

"*Don't*." She was sitting up in the bed with her knees drawn up and her arms round them. He couldn't see her face. Just the shape of her against the soaped grey of the little window. "*Don't you dare say I can't. I can. I'm a Mistwalker. I'm Level sixty-nine. I tracked Mothwing across the Sundered Lands while I was carrying her. I stood on the wind for a hundred miles on Lantern Night while she was trying to get out.*"

"*I know*."

"*I can run again*," said Wren. Very quietly. "*I ran. At Ashbourne. Over the roofs. With the gold coming down.*" Her voice cracked. "*I can do it, Lex. I can do it again. I can go.*"

---

He didn't say anything for a long time.

He lay on his back in the dark beside her and looked at the slope of the roof and thought about it.

He thought about her on the roofs at Ashbourne, with the baby on her chest, laughing. With a whirlwind round her. With the gold coming down. Running.

He thought about her on the wind above the Blightmarch on Lantern Night, holding a hundred miles of air still.

He thought about her in a chair in the dark at three in the morning, not moving, with a Glass Queen in the window telling her she'd stopped.

"*I know you can*," he said.

She went still.

"*I know you can go*," said Lex. "*I know you can run. I know you'd be better at it than me. You'd find the tower in a day. You'd find the stair. You'd carry the crown back over your head on the wind.*" He swallowed. "*I know.*"

"*Then*—"

"*Who fogs the glass*?"

---

She didn't answer.

"*She's coming*," said Lex. "*At midsummer. In every window. In every glass. For Lark.*" He turned his head on the pillow. Toward her. Toward the shape of her in the dark. "*And then we cross. And then she'll still be there. In every window. For as long as we're gone.*"

"*Lex*—"

"*Fog stops her*," he said. "*Your fog. Your breath. You stopped her the first night. You stopped her in the Hall of Lanterns. You're the only one who can stop her without burning the house down.*" His voice was shaking. "*Black heat works, but Kestrel can't hold it all night. I can't. Asha's two hundred and eleven.*"

Wren was very still.

"*If you come*," said Lex, "*who's here when she comes through the window for our daughter?*"

---

She didn't say anything for a long time.

He heard her breathing. In the dark. Fast. Then slower. Then very slow.

"*I hate you*," said Wren.

"*I know*."

"*I hate you so much*."

"*I know*."

"*You're right*," said Wren Ashdown. "*And I hate you.*" She put her face down on her knees. "*I hate that you're right. I hate that it's me. I hate that I'm the one who has to stay and breathe on windows while you go and walk into a country with no sun and find a crown in a forest of thorns.*"

"*It's not breathing on windows*."

"*It is*," said Wren. Into her knees. "*It's exactly breathing on windows. It's the most boring heroic thing anyone's ever done.*"

---

He sat up.

He put his arms round her. She let him. She was shaking. Not crying. Shaking. The way she shook on rooftops before a jump, sometimes, when she'd measured the gap and knew she could make it and was afraid anyway.

"*I'd swap*," he said. Into her hair.

She lifted her head.

"*If I could*," said Lex. "*If I could fog glass. I'd stay. I'd stay and breathe on windows every night and you could go.*" He swallowed. "*I'd rather. I'd rather stay here with her than go anywhere.*"

She looked at him in the dark.

"*Liar*," she said.

"*I'm not*."

"*You want to see it*," said Wren. "*The country. The tower. The thorns. You want to see what's on the other side. You've always wanted to see what's on the other side of everything.*" Her hands were in his shirt. Holding on. "*You went down a hole under a coal mine because you wanted to see what was at the bottom.*"

"*That's not*—"

"*I know you*," said Wren. "*I married you. I know exactly what you want.*" She pulled his head down. Her forehead against his. "*And I want you to want it. I want you to go and see. I want you to come back and tell me.*" Her voice cracked. "*I just wanted to be the one who went, for once.*"

---

They didn't sleep much.

They lay in the dark with the baby breathing in her cradle and the stream running and talked. About nothing. About everything. About the first time they met, on the moors above Thistledown, when she'd laughed at him for not knowing what a hare-track looked like. About the rooftops at Highcrest. About the black iron ring on her finger, which he'd forged from a nail of the old Ashbourne forge.

About Lark.

About what she'd be like. At one. At two. At eleven. At twenty-one.

"*She'll be a smith*," said Lex.

"*She'll be a Mistwalker*."

"*She bit me*. *With her first tooth.*"

"*She laughed at the wind*," said Wren. "*In the gold. Over the whole village.*"

"*She can be both*," said Lex.

Wren was quiet for a moment.

"*She can be anything*," she said. "*That's the point. That's the whole point.*" She turned her face into his shoulder. "*That's what you made, Lex. When you took the Ceilings off. That's what you made for her. She can be anything she likes.*"

He didn't answer.

He was thinking about a gold crown on a small dark head. About a child who'd be five months old forever.

---

They came on the seventh day.

All of them. To Harrow. Up the track through the birch wood. Lex hadn't asked them. He hadn't had to.

Antho first. On Ashka. With Seraphine behind him, her spectacles on, and Pim behind her, small and silent in his blue coat with the brass buttons. And a basket strapped to Ashka's side, wrapped in straw, that Pim wouldn't let anyone else touch.

Twelve Hush-cups. Each with an iron lid that sighed when you set it on. Each one heavy with nothing.

Hask next. On foot, from the Blightmarch, two days' walk, because he said he didn't trust dragons and he wanted to see the country. With his pick on his back and his right arm out of its sling now, bandaged, with a line of grey-black showing at the wrist where the iron had gone in. He came up the track at dusk and stood at the edge of the clearing and looked at the two cottages and the stream and the hornlight in the window, and didn't say anything.

"*Kit's got the stick*," he said, when Lex asked. "*And Ivy's got the pool. And Brigid's got Kit.*" He shrugged. "*Nobody kneels.*"

---

Kestrel came on the sixth day. On a borrowed horse. With her red hair cropped short and her amber eyes bright and a sack over her shoulder that clanked.

"*Mirrors*," she said. When Wren asked. "*Every mirror between here and Highcrest I could find. I broke them all.*" She dumped the sack on the grass. It clanked again. "*I thought you could use the frames. For something. I don't know. Lex'll think of something.*"

"*Why did you break them*?"

"*Because I could*," said Kestrel Varn. "*Because she's in them.*" She grinned. A wild bright grin. "*And because it was very, very satisfying.*"

---

Grandmother Asha came on the fifth day. On a camel. With two more old women in red behind her, on two more camels, and a look on her dried-apple face like a general reviewing troops.

"*Sand*," she said. Climbing down. Slowly. Swearing in a language Lex didn't know. "*Three of us. That's six hours of sand a night, smith. Maybe seven. If nobody falls asleep.*" She looked at the cottages. At the birch wood. At the soaped windows. "*It's not a very big house. We can cover it.*"

"*Thank you*."

"*Don't thank me*," said Grandmother Asha. "*Thank the camels. I hate camels. They hate me. We've come two hundred miles hating each other.*"

---

Brannoc and Hedda came on the fourth day. In the cart. With the travelling forge in the back and a load of oak planks and a box of nails and Teo Brask, dripping, on the seat between them.

"*Boards*," said Brannoc. Climbing down. Looking at the windows. "*For every pane. Inside and out. Nailed.*" He held up his hands. The fingertips shone gold in the evening light. "*And I'll hold the nails. I can't hit my thumb anymore. Can't feel it if I do.*"

Teo slid off the cart. He was seventeen now. Tall and gangling and always, always wet. Water followed him. It dripped off his hair and his sleeves and his nose. It pooled round his boots. It came up out of the ground where he stood.

"*Water reflects*," said Teo. When Lex looked at him. "*Barrels. Buckets. The stream. Puddles. Dew.*" He wiped his nose on his sleeve. It didn't help. "*I can keep it moving. All of it. All night. Ripples. So it never goes still enough to show anything.*" He shrugged. "*Seraphine says a reflection needs still water. So I won't let it be still.*"

---

And Lysandra sent Elowen.

On the third day. On a grey warhorse. With Oathkeeper on her hip and four knights behind her and a letter from the Queen sealed in blue wax.

Lex opened it on the step.

It was very short.

*Vey. I'd come myself. They won't let me. I'm practicing being stern about it in a mirror with no glass in it. — L.*

And under it, smaller, in a different ink, as if added at the last moment—

*Bring her home safe. All of them.*

---

The night before midsummer eve, they sat outside.

All of them. In the clearing. Between the two cottages. On the grass by the stream. With a fire Lex lit with his own hands, and food Seren and Hedda had been cooking all afternoon, and Mother Orla's plum brandy, which Brannoc had brought a whole cask of and which nobody but Brannoc and Grandmother Asha could drink without coughing.

Lex sat with his back against the oak ramp of the blue-door cottage and looked at them.

---

Antho, with Seraphine leaning against his shoulder. Her spectacles off, folded, face down on her knee. His bone flute in his lap. He wasn't playing. He was looking at the fire.

Pim, beside them, cross-legged on the grass. Small. Silent. With the basket of Hush-cups beside him and one hand resting on its lid. He hadn't let go of it all evening.

Hask, a little apart, on a stump at the edge of the firelight. Enormous. Quiet. With his pick across his knees and his bandaged arm resting on it, looking at the stream.

Kestrel, flat on her back on the grass with her hands behind her head, telling Teo a story about a Warden she'd set on fire in the Sunreach, which might have been true.

Teo, dripping, listening, laughing.

Grandmother Asha and her two old women in red, sitting in a row on a log with their hands folded in their laps, like crows on a fence. Passing the plum brandy between them. Arguing in their own language.

Brannoc and Hedda, side by side on the cart's tailboard. Brannoc's arm round Hedda's shoulders. His gold fingertips catching the firelight.

Elowen, standing. Even now. At the edge of the clearing. With Oathkeeper on her hip. Watching the dark.

And Wren.

Sitting on the grass beside him. With Lark asleep in her lap. Her red hair in a braid over one shoulder. Her face in the firelight very still.

---

And behind him, through the blue door, through the open curtain, his father.

In bed. Under the west window. Propped on three pillows so he could see. His mother sitting on the edge of the bed beside him with her hand in his.

The door was open. So he could hear.

---

*[Look at them*,] said Ember. Very quietly.

*I'm looking*.

*[All of them*.] A pause. *[Do you remember when it was just you? In the Ashbourne forge? At nineteen? With a Common class and a Ceiling of twenty-four and nobody but Brannoc?]*

*I remember*.

*[And me*,] said Ember. *[You had me. From the first day. From the moment the seal cracked.]* Another pause. *[I was the first one. Before any of them.]*

Lex looked at the fire.

*[I wanted to say that*,] said Ember. *[Before. I don't know why. I just wanted to say it.]*

*Before what*?

Ember didn't answer.

---

Antho started playing.

Lex didn't see him decide to. He just looked up and his brother had the bone flute to his lips, and the first notes were coming out of it, thin and breathy and a little off, the way they always were. Antho had played it every night for eight years in a cave in the Greyspine to hold the dark off, and he still couldn't play it very well.

The Lantern Song.

Slowly. The way their mother used to sing it.

Nobody said anything.

---

And then his mother started to sing.

From the bed. Behind the curtain. Through the open door. Very softly. Barely above a whisper. The words to the tune his brother was playing.

*If the dark comes calling, love, don't you fear.*
*There's a light in the window, and I'm right here—*

And Wren joined her. On the grass. With the baby in her lap. Very quietly.

And then Seraphine. Who couldn't sing. Who sang anyway.

And then, from the cart's tailboard, in a voice like a landslide, off-key, Brannoc.

---

Lex didn't sing.

He sat with his back against the oak ramp and listened. To his brother's flute. To his mother's voice from the bed. To his wife, and his brother's wife, and the old smith who'd pulled him out of the ashes. To Kestrel, who didn't know the words and hummed. To Teo, dripping. To three old women in red on a log, who didn't know the song at all and were singing something else entirely, in their own language, in harmony, and it somehow fitted.

To Hask, on his stump. Who wasn't singing. Whose lips were moving.

He looked at the fire. At the faces round it. At the baby asleep in Wren's lap with drool on her chin and one square fist curled against her mouth.

And thought, *this*. *This is what he wants to keep*.

*This is what he'd keep, if he could. Exactly like this. Forever. Every face. Every voice. The fire. The song.*

*And it would never be sung again*.

---

When the song was done, nobody said anything for a long time.

Then his father spoke. From the bed. Through the open door. Thin. Dry. A thread.

"*Thank you*," said Arion Vey. "*That was the best I've ever heard it.*"

"*You've heard it a thousand times*," said Seren. Softly.

"*Yes*," said his father. "*And that was the best.*"

---

Midsummer eve came grey and hot.

They boarded the windows in the morning. Brannoc and Hask and Lex. Every pane. Inside and out. Oak planks, nailed tight, with Brannoc holding the nails in his gold fingers and Hask driving them with his pick-hammer in two blows each, and Lex sealing the cracks between the planks with black heat so not a sliver of light got through.

Both cottages. Every window.

Teo walked the clearing with his hands out. The stream. The water butt by the lean-to. The trough. A bucket someone had left by the woodpile. Every surface of every still water rippling as he passed. Shivering. Never quite still.

Kestrel and the three old women in red walked the edges of the clearing. Slowly. In a circle. With their hands out. Laying down sand. Black heat. A ring of it, round both cottages, round the stream, invisible, a hand's width above the grass. Lex could feel it. Like walking past an oven.

Elowen and her knights took the track.

Wren stood in the middle of the clearing with her eyes shut and her hands open and breathed.

---

The fog came up at dusk.

Out of the stream. Out of the grass. Out of the birch wood. Soft and white and thick. Rolling across the clearing in slow waves, the way it rolled across the high moors above Thistledown at dawn. Swallowing the cottages. Swallowing the woodpile and the lean-to and the cart. Swallowing everyone.

Wren's fog.

Lex stood in it and could barely see his own hand.

"*Can you see*?" he said.

"*Yes*," said Wren. Somewhere close. Somewhere in the white. "*I can always see in mine.*"

---

Lark went into the cellar at dark.

Under the cottage. Under the kitchen floor. Through the trapdoor and down the ladder. The cellar where they'd found Wenna's box, a year and a half ago, with a moth carved in the lid and crossed out.

No windows. No glass. No water. No light.

Wren took her down. And Seren. They'd decided that together. Two women. One who could fog glass and one who could say no. In the dark, with the baby, with the trapdoor shut over their heads and Hask sitting on it.

His father wouldn't go.

"*I can't do ladders*," he said. From the bed in the blue-door cottage. With the windows boarded and the fog pressing against the planks outside. "*And somebody has to be in this house. Somebody has to keep it.*"

"*You can't keep anything*," said Seren. "*You can't get out of bed.*"

"*I've kept a great many things from a great many beds*," said Arion Vey. "*I held the whole net from a crystal for sixteen years. I can hold a cottage for one night.*" He looked at her. "*Go, love. Go with the baby. That's where you need to be.*"

She didn't move.

"*Seren*," said her husband. Very gently. "*Go.*"

---

Antho stayed with him.

With his Lantern. With Pim. In the blue-door cottage, in the dark, with the windows boarded and his father in the bed and the black heat in the walls.

Lex went across the stream.

To his own cottage. To the kitchen. To the trapdoor in the floor with Silas Hask sitting on it like a boulder. To the east window, boarded. To the hornlight on the sill, unlit now, because Grandmother Asha had said *no light, smith, not one, not even a horn one, not tonight*.

He stood in the dark kitchen.

He could hear the fog against the planks. A soft wet pressing. He could hear the stream, rippling, never quite still. He could hear, faint, under the floor, his wife's voice. Singing. Very low. To the baby.

*If the dark comes calling, love, don't you fear*.

---

He waited.

They all waited. In the dark. In the fog. In the heat.

It was very hot. Midsummer. The shortest night. The windows would have been open, in any other year. Every window in Caldor would be open tonight. People sleeping on top of their blankets. Children on the floor by the door for the draught.

Every window in Caldor.

*In every window*, Orrin had said. *In every glass*.

---

Midnight.

He felt it. Through the Stone. A shift. A change. The way you feel the air change before a storm breaks.

Then nothing.

Then—

*Tap*.

---

On the window.

The east window. The boarded one. Behind him. On the glass, behind the planks.

*Tap*.

Small. Light. Like a fingernail.

*Tap. Tap*.

He turned round.

The planks were dark. Black heat in every crack. Not a sliver of light. He couldn't see the glass behind them. He couldn't see anything.

*Tap*.

And then a sound he'd never heard before.

---

A hiss.

Soft. Wet. Everywhere. From the window behind the planks. From the window in the loft above his head. From the little window by the door. From across the stream, faint, from the windows of the blue-door cottage.

From every pane of glass at Harrow. All at once.

The sound of soap running.

Of something on the inside of the glass going wet. Going soft. Melting. Running down the panes in thin streams, the way frost runs down a window when the sun gets to it. As if a hand were pressed against every pane at once, from the other side, cold, and the soap was thawing under it.

---

And then the hiss stopped.

And from behind the planks of the east window, from the glass, clear now, black now, washed clean—

A voice.

Small. Far. Like a wet finger round the rim of a glass.

"*Smith*," said Ysenne. "*You boarded them. How sweet.*" A pause. "*Did you think I needed the windows?*"

Lex didn't move.

"*Look down*," said the Glass Queen.

---

He looked down.

At the floor. At the dark kitchen floor. At the flags, grey, in the black.

And at the thing that was shining on them.

A fleck. A speck. A single grain of something. Gold. Lying on the flagstone by his bare foot. Catching what little light there was. Gleaming.

And beside it, another.

And another.

Coming in under the door. A thin gold line of them. Sifting. Glittering. Spreading across the floor like sand across a beach. Grain after grain after grain.

Gold dust.

And in every grain, if he looked closely, if he bent down—

A tiny pale face.
