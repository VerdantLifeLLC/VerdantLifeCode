# The Harrow Songs

Seren Vey had been courting her husband for three months, and she was fairly sure he'd noticed.

She'd brought him pies. Six. One a fortnight, more or less, depending on whether the apples held out. She'd brought him a blanket she'd knitted herself, badly, in a blue that didn't match the door. She'd read him his own grey book every morning and his own forty-page letter every night, and laughed at the right places, and some of the wrong ones. She'd sat with him at the west window at sunset with her hand on the arm of his chair, next to his, not quite touching.

She'd done all the things her mother told her never to do.

*Don't stay*, Wenna used to say. At the end of every summer. Packing. *Don't let them get used to you. Don't let them learn your face.*

She'd let him learn her face. She'd let him learn it twice.

The first time, she didn't remember.

This time she was paying attention.

---

She was at the window when the dragon came.

She was always at the window in the mornings. After the porridge and before the reading. He liked to sit and look at the birch wood for a while when he woke, before he could talk properly, and she liked to sit beside him and look at it too. It was a quiet time. A grey time. The stream running. The birches dripping. Her hand on the arm of his chair, on *HOME*, next to his.

She saw it first. A shape in the grey sky to the north. Small and dark and coming fast. Blue-grey. Long-winged.

"*Ashka*," said Arion.

He'd seen it too. He didn't miss much. Even now. Even with his eyes in a face that was a hundred years in a drawer.

"*Antho*," said Seren.

She was up and out of the blue door before the dragon landed.

---

He came down off Ashka's neck in the clearing before the dragon had properly stopped, and stumbled, and caught himself, and stood there.

He was white. His black hair wild. His eyes very dark. He looked the way he'd looked at the bottom of the world, coming up out of the Hollow with a door held open behind him. The way he'd looked at nineteen, at sixteen, at every age she'd never seen him and had dreamed about in the dark for thirteen years.

"*Mam*," he said.

"*What's happened*?"

"*Where's Lark*?"

"*With Wren. In the house. They're fine. What's happened?*"

He shut his eyes. Just for a moment. Standing in the wet clearing with his dragon steaming behind him.

Then he opened them.

"*There's a man made of dust*," said Antho. "*He knocked on the Keep door last night. He took an old woman's door.*" His voice cracked. "*And then he asked after the baby.*"

---

They brought everyone into the blue-door cottage.

She didn't decide it. It just happened. Antho went across the stream to the other house and came back with Wren, and Wren had Lark in a sling against her chest and a knife on her belt and a look on her face like a hawk on a fence post. And Seren put the kettle on, because that was what you did, and Arion moved his chair back from the window a little so there was room at the table, and they all sat down.

And Antho told them.

About the knock. About the dust under the door. About the voice in the walls. About an old shepherd and his wife and a little silk pouch on a windowsill. About a gold face on a pillow. About a tear.

About a door that wasn't there anymore.

Seren listened with her hands flat on the table.

She didn't say anything until he'd finished. Then she got up. And went to the hearth. And knelt down in front of it. And poked the fire with a stick, for a long time, very hard, until the sparks went up the chimney in a roar.

---

"*Mam*," said Antho. Behind her.

"*I'm fine*."

"*You're not*."

"*No*." She poked the fire. "*No. I'm not.*"

She sat back on her heels. Looked at the flames.

"*My mother used to tell a story*," she said. "*At the end of summer. When we were packing. About a king who couldn't bear to lose anything. Who kept everything he ever loved in a gold room with no doors.*" She swallowed. "*I thought it was a story. I thought it was just a story to frighten children into not being greedy.*"

"*It was him*," said Wren.

"*It was him*." Seren turned round. On her knees. On the hearthstone. And looked at them all. Her son. Her son's wife. Her granddaughter, asleep in a sling, one square fist curled against Wren's collarbone. Her husband, in his chair, with his hands on the arms. "*It was always him. All of it. Every story she ever told. I just didn't know they were true.*"

---

Lark woke up at noon and wanted feeding, and Wren took her into the curtained corner by the bed, and Antho went out to see to Ashka, and for a little while it was just the two of them again.

Seren and the old man at the window.

She sat down in her chair beside his. She didn't pick up the grey book. She just sat.

He didn't say anything either. He was good at that. She'd noticed it in the first week. Most people, when you were sad, talked at you. Filled up the quiet. As if quiet were a hole they had to stop up before you fell in.

He didn't. He just sat in his chair with his spotted hands on the arms and let the quiet be quiet. And waited. As if he had all the time in the world.

He hadn't. They both knew he hadn't.

He waited anyway.

---

"*Sing me the old verses*," he said.

She looked at him.

"*The ones your mother sang*," said Arion. "*At the end of summer. You said them this morning. Gold for the eldest. Glass for the vain.*" His grey eyes were very steady. "*I've never heard them. Not all of them. I heard you sing the Lantern Song a thousand times, at the cottage, to the boys.*" A pause. "*I'm told I did. I'm told it was every night. But never those.*"

"*I wasn't allowed*."

"*I know*."

"*She said never*. *Not to anyone. Not even my own babies.*" Seren looked out of the window. At the birch wood. "*She said they were the kind of thing that got you found.*"

He didn't say anything.

He just waited.

---

She sang them.

Quietly. Barely above a whisper. The way her mother had sung them, the last night of summer, every summer, with the cottage half packed round them and the fire low and a little girl sitting on the floor with her knees pulled up, listening.

*Gold for the eldest, who feared to grow old,*
*Glass for the vain one, who loved to be told,*
*Iron for the soldier who never once laughed,*
*Thorns for the gardener who listened—and drank.*

She stopped.

"*It doesn't rhyme*," said Arion. Softly.

"*It's not supposed to*." She was looking at the birch wood. "*I asked Mam. When I was seven. I said, laughed and drank don't rhyme, Mam, you've got it wrong.*" Her mouth twisted. "*She said it was meant to go wrong there. Right there. On that word. So you'd always remember. So you'd never forget that's where it went wrong.*"

"*She listened*," said Arion. "*Rhosyn.*"

"*She listened. And she drank anyway.*" Seren looked down at her hands. "*And the song breaks, every time. Every time you sing it. Right in the middle. Like a dropped stitch.*"

She sang the rest.

*Silver for the moth, who went toward the light,*
*And nothing, nothing, for the tenth, who stood in the door all night.*

---

The fire ticked.

From behind the curtain, faint, the small wet sounds of a baby feeding. Wren's voice, low, murmuring something.

Arion didn't say anything for a long time.

"*Nothing for the tenth*," he said at last.

"*She didn't take a crown*." Seren turned her hands over in her lap. Square. Freckled. Strong. "*She put it down. On the stone. At the edge of the pool. And poured back. Mam said that was the whole point of the song. Nine of them got something. Gold and glass and iron and thorns and silver.*" She was quiet. "*And she got nothing. And she was the only one who got to keep being a person.*"

---

"*But there was a crown*," said Arion.

She looked at him.

"*In the box*," he said. "*In the cellar. Lex told me. The moth carved in the lid and crossed out. And the hollow in the bottom. Seven points.*" His grey eyes were very sharp. "*Your mother had it. Your mother's mother had it. All the way back. Twenty generations of women who never took the crown, carrying a crown about in a box.*"

"*Hesper's*," said Seren. "*Malachi went back for it. He gave it to her on the shingle. He said someone had to keep it. He said it was the only one that could say no.*"

"*And then*?"

"*And then they kept it*," said Seren. "*For nine hundred years. In a box. Under the floor. Moving it from cottage to cottage. Never wearing it. Never showing it to anyone. Never*—"

She stopped.

"*And then your mother took it somewhere*," said Arion. Very gently.

---

She didn't answer.

She sat in her chair and looked out at the birch wood and felt something move in her. Deep down. Under thirteen years of dark and three months of pies. Something she hadn't let herself look at for a long time.

*I've taken the crown where it belongs*.

"*She went away*," said Seren.

Arion waited.

"*The last summer*," said Seren. "*The summer before she died. I was nineteen. I'd just come back from Highcrest. With—*" She stopped. Looked at him. At his walnut face. At the grey eyes in it. "*With rain in my hair. Mam said. In the letter. With my face lit up like a Lantern Night window.*"

"*You'd met me*," said Arion. Very quietly.

"*I'd met you*." She swallowed. "*I don't remember it. I remember the steps. The rain. You coming round the corner. I remember that now. But I don't remember coming home. I don't remember telling her.*"

"*You told her*," said Arion. "*She threw a pan at me. The first time I came. You'd brought me to meet her. She looked at me and she looked at you and she picked up a frying pan and threw it at my head.*" A ghost of a smile. "*She missed. I think she meant to.*"

Seren stared at him.

Then she laughed. Despite herself. It came out of her cracked and wet and sudden.

"*That sounds like Mam*," she said.

---

"*She went away*," she said again. When she'd stopped. "*That summer. After the pan. For a month. Alone.*" She frowned. Reaching. Down into the dark. Into the places the Ferryman hadn't taken, because they weren't his. "*She said she had to see someone. She said she'd be back before the apples. She took the box.*"

"*Which way*?"

Seren closed her eyes.

She could see it. She could see it clearly. Not him. Not the Warden-Architect who must have been there, in the background, coming and going, a tall young man in a grey coat with ink on his cuff. He was a shape. A blank. A space with nobody in it.

But her mother she could see.

Standing at the door of the cottage. At Harrow. In the grey of an early morning at the end of summer. With a bundle on her back and a stick in her hand and her square face set.

Turning. Not toward the Vale. Not toward the road to Ashbourne and the east.

*West*.

"*West*," said Seren. Opening her eyes. "*She went west.*"

---

Arion's hands moved on the arms of his chair.

"*And she came back*," he said.

"*She came back*." Seren looked at the birch wood. "*Before the apples. Like she said. She was thin. Thinner than I'd ever seen her. And she didn't have the box.*"

"*Did you ask her*?"

"*No*." Seren's mouth twisted. "*You didn't ask Mam things. Not about the box. Not about the rule. Not about anything.*" She was quiet. "*But there was something else.*"

She hadn't thought about it in thirty years.

She thought about it now.

---

"*I asked her about my father*," said Seren.

Arion went very still.

"*When I was small*," said Seren. "*Once or twice. Who was he. Where was he. Was he dead.*" She looked at her hands. "*Harrow women don't marry. They don't stay. You know that. But there was always someone. For a summer. For a season. Mam never pretended there wasn't.*"

"*What did she say*?"

"*She said he was a whistler*." Seren smiled. A small sad crooked smile. "*That's all. She said he whistled. Better than anyone she ever knew. She said he could whistle a blackbird down out of a tree. She said I had his ears.*" She touched one. Absently. "*She'd smile, when she said it. Every time. A little private smile. Like she was remembering a tune.*"

She stopped.

"*And after*?" said Arion.

"*After that summer*," said Seren, "*I asked her again. Just once. I don't know why. I think—I think I was thinking about you. About staying. About whether she'd ever wanted to stay, for him.*" She swallowed. "*I said, Mam, tell me about the whistler again.*"

She looked at her husband.

"*And she looked at me*," said Seren Harrow, "*like I'd spoken in another language. She said, what whistler, love? And I said, my da. The one with the ears. And she just*—" Her voice broke. "*She just looked at me. For a long time. And then she said, I don't know. I don't know who you mean.*"

---

The fire ticked.

Neither of them said anything.

Behind the curtain, Lark had stopped feeding. Wren was singing to her. Very softly. Not the Lantern Song. Something else. A Thistledown song. About a hare in the snow.

"*She paid him*," said Arion at last.

Seren didn't answer.

"*The Ferryman*," said Arion. "*She went west. To the river. To the shore. And she paid him to take something across.*" His voice was very gentle. "*The memory you would least wish to lose.*"

"*A whistler*," said Seren.

"*A whistler*."

She put her hands over her face.

She didn't cry. She'd done all her crying in the dark. She just sat there with her hands over her face and breathed. In. Out. In. The way you breathe when something you've been carrying for thirty years without knowing it is suddenly lifted off you, and you find out how heavy it was.

"*She did the same thing*," she said through her fingers. "*She did exactly the same thing I did.*"

"*Yes*."

"*She forgot him for a crown*." Seren took her hands away. Her face was dry. White. "*I forgot you for a door.*"

---

Lex came home at dusk.

They heard Vharuun first. The great slow beat of wings over the birch wood. And then he came across the stepping stones and up the oak ramp and in through the blue door, barefoot, with his boots in one hand and his coat over his arm and a look on his face that Seren didn't like at all.

He'd been down.

She could see it. The way he moved. The way he looked at the room, at all of them, as if he'd come back from a long way off and wasn't quite sure they were still there.

"*Well*?" said Wren.

Lex sat down at the table.

He looked at his father.

"*He talked*," he said.

---

He told it. All of it.

The pool. The room Mal made. The black glass. The Key and the Lockward and a grey thread wound round them, frayed and worn and tight. The last thread, running out into the dark. The black iron rings coming closer.

*Clink*.

Lantern Night.

The crown in the road.

And then the other part. The quiet part. That Lex told looking at the table and not at anyone. Quill and his spoons. *Don't tell him I asked.*

*Then bring him. Before he goes. I'd like to say it to his face.*

---

Arion didn't move.

He sat in his chair by the west window with the last of the sunset coming in red behind him and his hands on the arms. On *STEADY* and *HOME*.

Seren watched his face.

She'd been watching it for three months. She'd learned it. The way you learn a new path through a wood. Every fold of it. Every crease. The way his mouth went when he was trying not to laugh. The way his eyes went when he was tired, and pretending not to be. The way the whole of it went still, perfectly still, like water in a bucket, when something hurt him.

It was still now.

"*He said that*," said Arion. "*Exactly that.*"

"*Exactly that*."

"*Bring him*."

"*If he'll come. If the little one can carry him.*"

His father looked out of the window. At the birch wood. At the red light going down behind it.

"*I'll go*," he said.

---

"*You will not*," said Seren.

They all looked at her.

She hadn't known she was going to say it. It came out of her the way the old verses had come out of her mother. Sharp. Certain. From somewhere very deep.

"*You will not*," she said again. "*You can't go down a ladder. You can't go up a hill. Last Tuesday you couldn't lift the teapot.*" Her voice was shaking. She didn't let it stop her. "*You are not going to the bottom of the world in a bubble to say sorry to a man who betrayed you.*"

"*He's not the one who needs to say sorry*," said Arion. Quietly.

She stared at him.

"*Or he is*," said her husband. "*Both of us are. I don't know.*" He looked down at his hands. "*I've had three years too, Seren. I've had two hundred. And I still don't know what I'd say to him.*" A pause. "*I'd like to find out. Before I can't.*"

"*You'll die*."

"*I'm dying anyway*," said Arion Vey. Very gently. "*That's rather the point.*"

---

Nobody said anything.

Seren stood by the hearth with the poker in her hand. She didn't remember picking it up.

"*Not now*," said Lex. Into the silence. "*Not yet. Elric said Lantern Night. That's months.*" He looked at his father. "*We'll work out how. Mal can do it. I think. If we're careful.*" He looked at his mother. "*If you come.*"

Seren looked at him.

"*Mal likes you*," said Lex. "*It calls you the lamp-lady. It'll carry him gentler if you're there.*"

She didn't answer for a long moment.

Then she put the poker down.

"*If I come*," she said.

"*If you come*."

"*If it's careful*."

"*Very careful*."

She looked at her husband. At the old man in the chair. At the face she'd been learning for three months.

"*I'm coming*," said Seren Harrow. "*And if he says one wrong word to you down there I'll throw a pan at him.*"

Arion's mouth twitched.

"*Your mother's daughter*," he said.

---

"*And the crown*," said Lex.

He was looking at her now. Steadily. With her own brown eyes in his father's face.

"*Elric said ask your mother where it went*," he said.

Seren didn't answer straight away.

She looked at Arion. And he looked back at her. And something passed between them, across the table, across the last red light from the window. Something she'd told him that afternoon, and he'd held for her, and was giving back now, so she could say it herself.

"*West*," said Seren. "*She went west. To the Ferryman. With the box. The summer before she died.*"

Lex stared at her.

"*She paid him*," said Seren. "*I don't know what for. To take it somewhere. To put it somewhere.*" She swallowed. "*She paid him with my father. She paid him the only thing she had that she'd have least wanted to lose.*"

---

*[Seren*,] said a voice.

She went still.

She knew it. She'd known it in the dark. In the deep. In a room the little one had folded her into, with a boy who was her son standing in front of her with gold in his eyes, and a voice in the air round him that wasn't his.

*Hello, Seren*, it had said.

She'd known it then. She'd known it before she knew anything. Before she knew the old man in the chair. Before she knew she had a husband at all.

Dry. Gentle. Precise. With an old-fashioned courtesy. And underneath it, faint as a watermark in paper, another voice entirely.

*[Seren*,] said Ember. *[May I say something?]*

---

The others couldn't hear it.

She could tell. Wren was looking at Lex. Antho was looking at the fire. Arion was looking at her. Only Lex had gone still, the way he always went still when the voice in his head spoke, his eyes going a little unfocused, as if he were listening to someone in another room.

And she could hear it. Because she was close. Because she was his mother. Because she'd heard it in the dark.

*Yes*, she thought. Not out loud. She didn't know if it could hear her thinking. It seemed to.

*[I have them*,] said Ember. *[You know I do. I told you in the deep. Everything he was. Up to the night he sealed the boys. Everything he remembered.]* A pause. *[The steps. The rain. The cottage. The pan your mother threw. His side of it. All of it.]*

*I know*.

*[I could give them to you*,] said Ember. Very quietly. *[Not yours. I don't have yours. Yours are in the river. But his. His side of it. Every day of it, up to the night he sealed the boys. You'd know what he saw. What he felt. When he looked at you.]*

She couldn't breathe.

*[I gave you the steps*,] said Ember. *[In the deep. Just that one. You were frightened. You needed something. And then the Ferryman gave you yours, the same day, the same memory, from the other side.]* A pause. *[You have it twice now. His and yours. The only one you have twice.]*

*Yes*.

*[I could give you the rest*,] said Ember. *[Tonight. Now. All of it.]*

---

She looked across the table at her husband.

He was watching her. He couldn't hear Ember. He'd never been able to; Ember was a piece of him, pressed into his son's blood twenty years ago, and you can't hear your own voice in someone else's head. But he could see her face.

He'd always been able to see her face.

"*It's talking to you*," said Arion. Quietly. "*Isn't it.*"

"*Yes*."

"*It's offering*."

"*Yes*."

He looked at her for a long moment. And then he looked at Lex. At his son. At the place behind his son's eyes where a piece of himself lived.

"*Not yet*," said Arion Vey.

---

*[He can't hear me*,] said Ember. Startled. *[How did he*—]

"*Because I know what I'd say*," said Arion. To Lex. To the voice in Lex. "*If I were you. Which I was. Once.*" His mouth twitched. "*You'd offer her everything. You'd want to. You'd want her to have it all at once. So she'd know. So she wouldn't have to guess.*"

Lex's face had gone very strange.

*[...yes*,] said Ember. Faintly.

"*Not yet*," said Arion. "*Please.*"

Seren stared at him.

"*Why*?" she said.

---

He turned in his chair. Slowly. Stiffly. To face her.

The sunset was gone. The window behind him was grey. The fire lit his face from the side, red and gold, every fold of it, every crease. A hundred years in a drawer.

"*Because if it gives you mine*," said Arion, "*you'll know what I felt. On the steps. In the rain. Every day after.*" His voice was a thread. "*And you'll love me because you remember.*"

She didn't say anything.

"*And that would be lovely*," said her husband. "*It would. I've waited thirteen years for you to remember me.*" He looked down at his spotted hands. "*But I've only got a year, love. Perhaps two. And for three months you've been bringing me pies.*"

"*Arion*—"

"*I'd like you to love me because of the pies*," said Arion Vey. "*For a little while. Just a little. Because of the old man in the chair. Not because of the young one on the steps.*" His mouth twisted. "*He had everything. He had you, and the boys, and the cottage, and thirty years. He doesn't need any more.*" He looked up at her. "*I'd like something that's just mine. Just this. Just you, now, choosing. Before it's all memory again.*"

---

She couldn't speak.

She sat at the table with her hands flat on the wood and looked at him. At the old man in the chair. At the face she'd been learning.

*[...I understand*,] said Ember. Very quietly. In the back of Lex's head. In the back of hers. *[I understand. I'll wait.]* A pause. *[I'm good at waiting. I learned it from him.]*

"*It says it'll wait*," said Lex. Hoarsely.

"*Thank you*," said Arion. To his son. To the voice in his son. Very formally. The way you'd thank a colleague. "*I appreciate it.*"

*[He's welcome*,] said Ember.

And then, so faint Seren almost didn't hear it, so faint she thought afterward she might have imagined it—

*[Lex. I don't know how long I'll be able to.]*

Lex's face went white.

*[He's going*,] said Ember. *[I can feel it. Every day. Like a draught. Like a tide going out.]* A pause. *[I'm a piece of him. I don't know what happens to the piece when the rest of it goes.]*

Seren looked at her son.

He didn't say anything. He sat at the table with his hands round his cup and his face white and didn't say anything at all.

---

The knock came at midnight.

Seren was awake. She was always awake at midnight. Thirteen years in the dark had taught her that the hour after midnight was the hour things came to doors.

She was sitting in the chair by the hearth. In the blue-door cottage. With the fire banked and a single candle on the table and her husband asleep behind the curtain, breathing. Slow. Even. Every breath starting again.

Lex and Wren and the baby were across the stream. Antho was asleep on the floor by the door, rolled in a blanket, with his Lantern beside him. He'd insisted.

*Knock*.

Soft. Courteous. Three times.

*Knock. Knock. Knock*.

---

Antho was on his feet. His Lantern in his hand. Blazing.

Seren was already at the door.

"*Mam*—"

"*Hush*."

She didn't open it. She stood in front of it. Barefoot. In her nightdress. With her hand flat on the blue-painted wood. The way her mother used to stand at the end of every summer, at the door of every cottage, with her hand flat on the wood, listening.

"*Who's there*?" said Seren Harrow.

---

"*A servant*," said the voice.

Dry. Soft. Hissing. Like sand poured out of a sack. Coming through the wood. Through the gap under the door.

"*Only a servant, mistress*," it said. "*A steward. My name is Orrin. I have a gift.*"

"*I know who you are*."

"*Of course you do*." A pause. Soft as settling dust. "*Your son told you. The warden. I knocked at his door last night.*" Another pause. "*I'm sorry about the old woman. Truly. It was her husband who chose.*"

"*She said no*."

"*She did*," agreed Orrin. "*So many of them do. And then someone else says yes.*" A faint dry sigh. "*That's the trouble with a gift, mistress. It's never only for one person.*"

---

There was dust coming in under the door.

Seren saw it. A thin pale line of it. Grey-gold. Sifting across the floor.

She didn't step back.

"*What do you want*?" she said.

"*To offer*," whispered Orrin. "*Only to offer. His Majesty sends me with it. To the Warden-Architect.*" The voice grew softer. Gentler. Almost tender. "*He has heard about him. The old man in the chair. He has heard how old. How little time.*"

Behind her, behind the curtain, Seren heard the breathing change.

Arion was awake.

"*His Majesty would consider it an honour*," whispered Orrin, "*to keep him. As he is. Exactly as you love him.*" The dust was spreading across the floor. Slowly. Toward her bare feet. "*You'd never lose him, mistress. Never. Not this year. Not next. Not ever. You could sit beside him at the window every evening for a thousand years.*"

---

Seren didn't move.

She stood at the blue door with her hand flat on the wood and the dust coming toward her bare feet across the floor, and she thought about it.

She let herself. Just for a moment. Just for one breath.

A thousand years. At the window. Every evening. His hands on the arms of the chair. His grey eyes looking out at the birch wood. Never getting older. Never getting slower. Never one morning not waking up.

Never one morning not being there.

She thought about it.

And then she thought about a gold woman on a pillow. With a tear on her cheek. And a door that wasn't there anymore. And a young man's voice on the other side of it, saying *Mam*.

"*No*," said Seren Harrow.

---

"*No*," she said again. Louder. To the door. To the dust. To whatever was on the other side. "*Not him. Not me. Not anyone in this house. Not ever.*"

The dust stopped.

"*He's mine*," said Seren. "*For a year. Maybe two. Every day of it. Every slow cold morning. Every time he can't lift the teapot. Every time his breath catches and I lie there in the dark waiting for it to start again.*" Her voice was shaking. She didn't let it stop her. "*That's mine. All of it. You don't get to keep it. Nobody does.*"

Silence.

"*And when he goes*," said Seren Vey, "*I'll be there. Holding his hand. And I'll let him.*"

---

Behind the curtain, very faintly, an old man said, "*Thank you.*"

She didn't turn round.

The dust on the floor lay very still.

And then, from under the door, from the gap, from everywhere, soft and dry and almost sad—

"*Hesper said that*," whispered Orrin. "*On the shingle. To her sister. Not me. Not ever.*" A pause. "*I was there. I held the cup for the servants that night. I watched her say it.*" Another pause. "*You have her voice, mistress. Exactly. I'd forgotten what it sounded like.*"

Seren didn't answer.

"*His Majesty asks after the child*," said Orrin.

---

Antho moved.

She heard him. Behind her. The Lantern flaring. The dark gathering in his other hand.

"*Don't*," said Seren. Not turning round. "*It's dust, love. It wants you to.*"

He stopped.

"*The child's across the water*," said Orrin. Gently. "*I know. In the little house with the lantern. With her mother, who has a knife, and her father, who has a fire.*" The dust stirred on the floor. "*I won't go there tonight. His Majesty doesn't want her frightened. He wants her well. He wants her whole.*"

"*He can't have her*."

"*He'd like to meet her*," said Orrin. As if she hadn't spoken. "*That's all. For now. When the crack is wider. When the King can come.*" A sigh. "*Tell her father that. The smith. Tell him His Majesty would very much like to meet his daughter.*"

---

The dust went back under the door.

All of it. All at once. Sliding back across the floor like water draining out of a basin. Under the blue wood. Out into the night.

Seren stood with her hand on the door.

Outside, faintly, she heard it go. A soft dry hiss, like wind through grass. Rising. Going east. Not toward the stream. Not toward the other house. East. Down the track. Toward the Ashbourne road.

Toward the village.

"*There are so many beds in a village*," she heard it whisper. Very faint. Very far. Fading. "*So many old men. So many hands.*"

And then it was gone.

---

She stood there a long time.

Antho put the Lantern down. Came and stood beside her. Put his hand on her shoulder. He didn't say anything. He was his father's son. He knew when to let the quiet be quiet.

"*Seren*," said a voice from behind the curtain.

She went.

---

He was lying on his back in the narrow bed under the window, with the blankets up to his chin and his white wisp of hair on the pillow and his grey eyes open in the dark. Looking at her.

She sat down on the edge of the bed.

He took her hand. His was cold. Thin. Spotted. It shook a little.

"*You said no*," said Arion.

"*Of course I said no*."

"*Not of course*." His thumb moved on the back of her hand. Very slowly. "*Fennick Dale said yes. Last night. For a woman he'd loved for forty-one years. Because he couldn't bear to listen to her stop.*" His voice was a thread. "*You've loved me for three months, love. You'd have had every excuse.*"

She looked at him. At his face in the dark. At the shape of it. At the eyes.

"*Not three months*," said Seren.

He went still.

"*I don't remember*," she said. "*I know I don't. I know it's all in the river. Thirty years.*" She swallowed. "*But I said no to him for you, Warden. Just now. At that door. Without thinking.*" She held his hand very tight. "*I'd never have done that in three months. Not for anyone. Not even for pies.*"

Arion didn't say anything.

He lay in the narrow bed and held her hand and looked at her, and his face did something she'd never seen it do. Not still. Not like water in a bucket.

It came apart. Softly. Like a dandelion.

---

She lay down beside him. On top of the blankets. In her nightdress. With her bare feet cold and her head on the edge of his pillow and his hand in hers.

She didn't mean to sleep.

She slept.

---

And dreamed.

For the first time since the dark. For the first time in thirteen years. The way Harrow women dreamed. At the end of summer. With the cottage half packed and the fire low.

She dreamed of a country with no sun.

Grey-gold dust. A white road running straight as a ruler across a plain. Gold people standing in rows along it, in their thousands, very still.

And a long way off the road, at the edge of everything, a forest of black thorns.

Miles of it. Round and round. Hooked and dry and taller than a house.

And in the middle of the thorns, a tower.

Small. Slender. Grown, not built. Grey and cracked and dead.

With a door at the bottom. Shut.

And a window at the top. Dark.

And through the window, very faint, so faint she could barely see it, so faint she might have been imagining it—

A chair.

And on the chair, something small. Something silver. A circle, a little bigger than a man's hand could span.

With seven points.

---

She woke with a start.

It was grey light. The fire was out. Arion was asleep beside her, breathing. Slow. Even. Every breath starting again.

She lay there with her heart going.

*I've taken the crown where it belongs. Where it was always meant to go.*

She knew where it was.

She didn't know how. She'd never been there. She'd never seen it. She'd never heard anyone describe it, not once, not in any song or any story at the end of any summer.

But she knew.

It was in a tower that a girl's sister had grown for her, in a country at the end of the world, in case she ever changed her mind.

And somebody was sitting outside the door of it. In the thorns. Asking.

*Hes?*
