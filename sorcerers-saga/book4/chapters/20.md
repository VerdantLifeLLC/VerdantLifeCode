# Interlude: Wenna's Fare

Thirty-two summers before a Gilded King walked out of the edge of the world, a hedge-witch in a cottage at the far end of the Vale woke up in the night with her heart going like a hare's and knew she had to break the rule.

---

She'd dreamed again.

She always dreamed, at the end of summer. They all did. Her mother had. Her mother's mother. All the way back. Twenty generations of women lying awake in twenty generations of cottages on the last nights of summer, with the packing half done and the fire low, dreaming of a country with no sun.

The dream was always the same.

Grey-gold dust. A white road. A hall with a ceiling like a frozen waterfall. And in the hall, hanging from the rafters on ropes of silk, five cocoons. Grey-gold. Thick as a man's arm. Swaying very slightly, though there was no wind.

Still.

They were always still. That was the point of the dream. That was why you had it. So you'd know. Every summer, at the end, so you'd wake up in the dark and lie there and know, *still*. *Still asleep. Still hanging. Still on the other side.*

And then you'd get up in the morning and finish the packing and move on to the next cottage.

---

This summer they'd moved.

Not swayed. *Moved*. Turned. In their silk. The way a sleeper turns when a dream is going bad. The great grey-gold shapes shifting against their ropes, one after another, slowly, all down the hall. And from inside the first one, the biggest, the one above the crown of gold leaves—

A sound.

Like breathing.

---

Wenna Harrow lay on her back in the dark and looked at the ceiling of the cottage and listened to her own heart.

Across the room, behind the curtain, her daughter was asleep. Nineteen. Square and freckled and stubborn, with her mother's hands and somebody else's ears. Breathing slow.

Smiling. In her sleep. Wenna could hear it. You could hear a smile, if you'd listened to someone sleep for nineteen years.

She'd been smiling in her sleep for two months. Since she came home from Highcrest with rain in her hair and her face lit up like a Lantern Night window.

Since the Warden.

---

He was asleep in the barn.

Wenna had put him there. The first night. She'd looked at him for a long time, standing in her doorway in his grey coat with his ink-stained cuffs and his grey eyes, too tall for the lintel, and she'd stood aside and let him in. And fed him. And not said a single word to him all evening.

And then she'd pointed at the barn.

He'd slept there every night since. Every time he came. Which was every week. Three days down from Highcrest on foot. Three days back. To sit at her table for one evening and look at her daughter like she was the first light he'd seen in two hundred years.

She didn't know about the two hundred years. Not then. She found out later.

She knew he looked at Seren like that, though. She'd known it the first night.

She'd watched him. A month. Every time he came. From the fire. With her mending. Not saying anything. Watching the way he listened when Seren talked. The way he laughed at her jokes, which were terrible. The way he went quiet when she sang.

And then one evening, at the end of the month, he'd stood up from the table.

---

Very straight. Very formal. In his grey coat. Too tall for the room. With his hands at his sides and his face like a closed book.

*Mistress Harrow*, he'd said. *I'd like to ask your permission to marry your daughter*.

And Wenna had looked at him.

And put down her mending.

And picked up the frying pan from the hearth.

And thrown it.

---

She'd missed. Of course she'd missed. She'd meant to. A Harrow woman doesn't throw anything she isn't sure of, and she was sure she wanted to miss.

It had gone past his ear and hit the door with a crash like a bell. He hadn't flinched. He'd stood there, very straight, with his hands at his sides, and looked at her.

*I'll take that as a no*, he'd said.

*Take it however you like*, Wenna had said. *Harrow women don't marry.*

*So Seren tells me*.

*So she's told you*. Wenna had picked up her mending again. *Did she tell you the rest?*

*Some of it*. He hadn't sat down. *The cottages. The moving. The rule.*

*And you still want to marry her*.

The Warden had looked at her daughter. Sitting at the table with her square freckled hands flat on the wood and her face white and her eyes very bright.

*I've wanted to marry her*, he'd said, *since she laughed at the lanterns.*

---

That had been a fortnight ago.

Seren hadn't spoken to her since. Not properly. *Pass the salt*. *The goat's out*. *Goodnight*. She went about the cottage with her jaw set and her eyes bright and her mother's own stubbornness coming off her like heat off a forge.

And the Warden still came. Every week. Three days down, three days back. And sat at the table. And ate what Wenna gave him. And slept in the barn.

And didn't ask again.

Wenna thought that was the cleverest thing he'd done.

---

She lay in the dark and thought about the cocoons.

About the breathing.

About her daughter asleep behind the curtain, smiling. About the Warden asleep in the barn. About the cottage at the far end of the Vale where she'd lived for ten years now. Ten. Longer than any Harrow woman had stayed anywhere in nine hundred years.

She'd meant to move on. When Seren was ten. When she was twelve. When she was fifteen. Every summer, at the end, she'd started packing.

And every summer Seren had looked at her with her square face set and said, *no, Mam. Not this year. I like it here.*

And every summer Wenna had unpacked again.

---

*You're getting soft*, her mother would have said.

Her mother had moved thirty times in thirty years. Her mother had taught her the rule at the end of every summer, packing, with the fire low. *We break the ground. We never take the crown. We never let a moth in.*

*And we never stay*, her mother had added. Every time. *That's the part that isn't in the rule because it doesn't need to be. Because it's the whole of it. Everything else is just reasons.*

Wenna had stayed.

Ten years. In the Vale. Because her daughter liked it. Because she was tired. Because there was an apple tree behind the cottage that gave the best cider apples she'd ever tasted, and a stream, and a birch wood, and nobody had felt a moth this side of the grey for four generations.

And now her daughter was going to stay forever.

With a Warden.

With a Warden-Architect of the Spire, who held the net in his hands, who could look at anyone's blood and know what they were.

---

*If they come*, thought Wenna. Lying in the dark. *If they wake. If they come looking.*

*A family that stays is very easy to find.*

---

She got up before dawn.

Quietly. Barefoot. The way she'd got up before dawn in twenty-two cottages. She went to the corner by the hearth and moved the flagstone, the loose one, and lifted out the box.

Oak. Old. Older than anything else she owned. With a moth carved in the lid and crossed out.

She opened it.

The crown was there.

---

It always was. It had been there her whole life. In the box. Under some floor. Moving from cottage to cottage. She'd seen it perhaps a dozen times. At the end of summer, when her mother opened the box to check. Once, when she was nine, when her mother took it out and let her hold it.

*Don't put it on*, her mother had said. *Not ever. Not even to see.*

*Why do we keep it, then*?

*Because somebody has to*, her mother had said. *Because the boy with the bare feet said so. Because it's the only one that can say no.*

*Say no to what*?

Her mother had taken it back. Put it in the box. Closed the lid.

*I don't know*, she'd said. *Nobody does. Hesper didn't. She just kept it.*

---

Small. Plain. Silver. A circle a little bigger than a man's hand could span. With seven points rising from the rim, at even spaces, like the tines of a fork.

It wasn't beautiful. It never had been. It was the plainest crown Wenna had ever seen or heard of. No jewels. No carving. Not even polish; the silver had gone soft and grey with nine hundred years of lying in the dark.

It was cold.

It was always cold. Even in summer. Even by the fire. You picked it up and it was cold the way a stone from the bottom of a river is cold, all the way through.

She held it in her lap.

And thought about a hall with five cocoons in it, turning in their sleep.

And thought about what her mother had said.

*The only one that can say no*.

---

*There must be a place*, she thought. *For her. For the tenth. In that country. In that hall.*

*Even if she never went. Even if she never wanted to. Somebody must have kept a place.*

*Somebody must have thought, in case*.

---

She knew it was mad.

She sat on the floor of the cottage in the grey dawn with the crown in her lap and knew it was the maddest thing a Harrow woman had ever thought. To take the crown. *Toward* them. Across the grey water. Into the country of the Crowned. And leave it there.

In the middle of them.

So that every night, in the dark, in the hall, while they slept, there'd be one thing in that country that said no.

*Like a hand on a forehead*, she thought. *Like a mother's hand. When you've got a fever and you can't sleep. It doesn't do anything. It just sits there. And says, hush. And you sleep.*

*Maybe they'd sleep*.

*Maybe they'd sleep so soundly they'd never wake*.

*Maybe my girl could stay*.

---

Seren found her there.

Standing in the doorway of the curtained corner, in her nightdress, with her hair down and her face creased with sleep. Looking at her mother on the floor with the box open and the crown in her lap.

She didn't say anything.

She'd been not-speaking to her mother for a fortnight. She didn't break it now.

She just looked. At the crown. At her mother's face.

"*I've got to go away*," said Wenna.

Seren's face went very still.

"*Not moving*," said Wenna. "*Not packing up. Just me. For a while.*" She closed the lid of the box. "*A month. I'll be back before the apples.*"

"*Where*?"

"*West*."

Seren looked at the box.

"*With that*," she said. The first real words in a fortnight.

"*With that*."

"*Why*?"

Wenna looked at her daughter for a long moment.

"*So you can stay*," she said.

---

She told the Warden at breakfast.

He'd come in from the barn with straw in his hair, the way he always did, and sat down at the table, and she'd put a bowl of porridge in front of him, the way she always did. And then, instead of going back to the fire, she'd sat down across from him.

He'd looked at her. Very carefully. With his grey eyes. The way he must look at a ward he wasn't sure of.

"*I'm going away*," said Wenna. "*A month. Maybe less.*"

"*I'm sorry to hear it*."

"*You'll look after her*."

He'd gone very still.

"*While I'm gone*," said Wenna Harrow. "*You'll come down from your Spire. More than once a week. You'll make sure the goat doesn't get into the cider. You'll make sure she eats.*" She looked at him. "*She doesn't eat when she's angry. She's very angry.*"

"*I know*," said the Warden. "*She told me. Through the barn door. At some length.*"

"*Good*." Wenna stood up. "*And when I come back, you can ask me again.*"

He'd looked at her for a long time.

"*Will you throw another pan*?" he'd said.

"*I might*," said Wenna. "*I'll decide when I get back.*"

---

She walked.

West. Out of the Vale. Over the downs. Across the shires. With a stick in her hand and a bundle on her back and the box in the bundle, and her boots, which were old, and her feet, which were older.

She didn't stop.

She'd decided that, the first day. If she stopped, she'd think. If she thought, she'd turn round. If she turned round, she'd go back to the cottage at the far end of the Vale, and put the box back under the flagstone, and pack, and take her daughter, and run.

And her daughter wouldn't come.

So she didn't stop.

---

Past the shires the land went grey.

She'd heard about it. Everyone had. The Sundered Lands. The scar. Where the world had been cut, nine hundred years ago, when the Crowned were sent out. Grey ground. Black stumps. A wind that never stopped.

She went round it. North. Into the mountains. Over a pass between white peaks like teeth. And down the other side, into a country that wasn't grey and wasn't green.

*Thin*. That was the only word. Things had no edges. No weight. The grass was the colour of nothing. The streams slid over their stones without a sound. She put her hand on a tree once, to rest, and felt it give under her palm like wet paper.

Her feet were bleeding.

She didn't stop.

---

Eleven days.

On the twelfth morning she came over the top of a long low hill and saw the river.

Grey. Wide. Flat. Slow. Running north to south across the bottom of a long grey valley. The grey of slate. The grey of a dead man's face.

And on the near bank, on the shingle, a boat.

And beside it, leaning on a long pole with a lantern on top of it, a tall thin figure in grey robes.

Waiting.

---

She'd never seen him. No Harrow woman ever had. Not in life. That was the other rule, the one under the rule. *Never go to the river. Never go to the shore. Never let him see you until it's time.*

She went down the hill.

Her feet left red marks on the grey stones. She didn't look at them. She walked down the long slope to the shingle with the bundle on her back and her stick in her hand, and stopped in front of him.

He turned his head.

He had no eyes.

---

"*Wenna Harrow*," said the Ferryman.

She'd known he would know her name. She hadn't known how it would feel. To be named. By that voice. Quiet and old and gentle, like water over stones.

"*I didn't tell you that*," she said.

"*No*." The eyeless face tilted. "*You never did. None of you ever did. For nine hundred years. Not one Harrow woman ever came to my shore.*" A pause. "*I have known all your names anyway. Every one. I hear them in the water, a little. Whenever one of you is born.*"

"*Then you know why I've come*."

"*I know you have a box*," said the Ferryman.

---

She opened it on the shingle.

She knelt down on the grey stones with her bleeding feet folded under her and set the box in front of her and lifted the lid.

And took out the crown.

The Ferryman went very still.

She saw it. In the long grey body leaning on the pole. In the long grey face. Something going still in him that she didn't think had gone still in a very long time.

"*Hers*," he said.

"*Hesper's*."

"*I saw it on this shingle*." His voice was very quiet. "*Nine hundred years ago. In her hands. The boy with the bare feet had just given it back to her. She was sitting there.*" His long grey hand moved on his pole. Pointing. To a place on the shingle a few yards off. "*There. With her knees up. Holding it in her lap like a bowl.*"

Wenna looked at the place.

Grey stones. Nothing else.

She thought, *she sat there*. *Hesper. At seventeen. With her brothers and sisters gone into the grey. With the crown in her lap.*

*And then she got up, and walked east, and never stopped*.

---

"*I want you to take it across*," said Wenna.

The Ferryman didn't answer.

"*To them*," said Wenna. "*To the Court. To the hall where they sleep.*" Her voice was shaking. She didn't let it stop her. "*There must be a place for her there. For the tenth. Even if she never came. Somebody must have kept a place.*"

"*Why*?"

"*So there's one thing in that country that says no*," said Wenna Harrow. "*Every night. While they sleep.*" She held the crown up. In both hands. Cold. "*They're waking. I've dreamed it. They're turning in their sleep. I don't know what to do. I've only got a song and a rule and this.*" She swallowed. "*So I thought—if I put it in the middle of them—*"

"*It would keep them asleep*," said the Ferryman.

"*I don't know*," said Wenna. "*Maybe. I hope.*"

---

The Ferryman was quiet for a long time.

The river ran. Grey. Slow. A long way off, on the far side, there was nothing. No hills. No sky. Just grey.

"*It can be done*," he said at last.

Wenna let out a breath she hadn't known she was holding.

"*A crown is not a person*," said the Ferryman. "*It has no time to be done. It has no room in it to be pushed against. I can carry it.*" A pause. "*And there is a place.*"

She looked up.

"*There is a tower*," said the Ferryman. "*At the edge of their plain. Far from the palaces. Grown, not built. Out of a white tree.*" His eyeless face turned toward the grey. "*One of them grew it. In the first year. For the tenth. In case she changed her mind.*"

Wenna's hands tightened on the crown.

"*Which one*?"

"*The fourth*," said the Ferryman. "*The one with the roses.*"

*Thorns for the gardener who listened*, thought Wenna. *And drank*.

The song broke there. It always had. It was meant to.

---

"*There is a price*," said the Ferryman.

"*I know*."

"*To cross, or to carry*. *It is the same for everyone.*" His voice was very gentle. "*The memory you would least wish to lose.*"

"*I know*," said Wenna.

She'd known. She'd known for eleven days. Walking. She'd known since she sat on the floor of the cottage at dawn with the crown in her lap.

She'd been thinking about it the whole way.

---

"*My girl*," she said.

The Ferryman didn't move.

"*Seren*," said Wenna. "*My daughter. Nineteen years. Every day.*" Her voice was very steady. She'd decided, on the eleventh day, that it would be. "*The day she was born. The first time she walked. The first time she sang the rule back to me. The day she came home with rain in her hair.*" She swallowed. "*That's what I'd least wish to lose. Take that.*"

"*No*," said the Ferryman.

---

She stared at him.

"*No*," he said again. Very gently. "*That is not the price. Not for you.*"

"*It's what I'd least wish to lose*."

"*It is what you love most*," said the Ferryman. "*That is not the same thing.*"

She didn't understand.

"*Your daughter is not a memory*, *Wenna Harrow*," said the Ferryman. "*She is waiting for you. In a cottage at the far end of a valley, with a goat and a Warden and a barrel of cider.*" His eyeless face turned toward her. "*You will see her face in eleven days. And the next day. And the next. You will make new memories of her every morning for the rest of your life.*" A pause. "*If I took the old ones, you would only make more.*"

"*Then what*—"

"*The memory you would least wish to lose*," said the Ferryman, "*is the one you can never have again.*"

---

The river ran.

Wenna knelt on the shingle with the crown in her lap and her bleeding feet folded under her and looked at the grey water.

*The one you can never have again*.

And she knew.

She'd known all along. She'd known on the eleventh day, walking. She'd known on the first. She'd known, if she was honest, the moment she sat down on the floor of the cottage with the box open.

She'd just hoped he wouldn't ask.

---

He'd come up the road at the end of summer.

Twenty years ago. Nearly twenty-one. To a cottage in the Ironbacks. With a pack on his back full of pots and pans and a little anvil and a bag of rivets. A tinker. Brown as a nut. With a crooked nose and big ears and a laugh you could hear from the next valley.

He'd knocked on the door. She'd opened it. He'd held up a pan. Hers. With a hole in the bottom that she'd been meaning to mend for a month.

*Found it in your hedge*, he'd said. *Thought you might want it back. I can fix that for you. Ten minutes. A copper.*

She hadn't wanted it fixed. She hadn't wanted anyone at her door. She was a Harrow woman. She'd been about to tell him to go away.

And then he'd whistled.

---

Not at her. At a blackbird. In the apple tree by the gate. A long high liquid run of notes, rising and falling, exactly like the bird's own song. Better than the bird's own song. And the blackbird had cocked its head. And looked at him. And come down out of the tree. And sat on the gatepost a yard from his shoulder.

And whistled back.

Wenna had stood in the doorway of the cottage in the Ironbacks with her mouth open.

*How did you do that*? she'd said.

The tinker had grinned at her. A crooked grin. Under a crooked nose.

*I asked nicely*, he'd said.

---

He'd stayed till the end of the summer.

In the barn. Mending things. Everything in the cottage that was broken, and some things that weren't. Whistling. In the mornings, at the anvil. In the evenings, on the step, with the sun going down. Blackbirds and thrushes and larks and a tune of his own that he said had no name, that he said he'd made up on a road somewhere and never finished.

*You'll finish it someday*, she'd said.

*No*, he'd said. *Some tunes you don't finish. You just keep whistling them till you've got somewhere to stop.*

---

He'd gone at the end of summer.

They always did. Harrow women didn't keep them. That was part of the rule too, the part that didn't need saying. He'd packed his little anvil and his rivets and his bag of pots and kissed her at the gate, and gone off down the road. East. Whistling.

She'd stood at the gate and listened till she couldn't hear him anymore.

She'd never told him.

Three months later she'd known. Nine months later there was a girl. Square. Freckled. Stubborn. With her mother's hands.

And his ears.

---

Twenty years.

She'd never seen him again. She'd never looked. You didn't. She'd never said his name out loud, not once, not to anyone. Not even to Seren. Seren had asked, when she was small. *Who was my da? Where is he? Is he dead?*

And Wenna had smiled. Every time. A small private smile. And said, *he was a whistler. The best you ever heard. He could whistle a blackbird down out of a tree.* And touched her daughter's ear. *You've got his ears.*

That was all.

She'd kept the rest.

Twenty years. Every day. Quietly. Without ever saying. The crooked grin. The crooked nose. The blackbird on the gatepost. The tune with no name and no end.

She'd never be able to have it again. He was gone. Down a road somewhere. Twenty years down it. Old now. Married, maybe. Dead, maybe. Whistling somewhere she'd never hear.

She'd never have him again.

She'd only ever have the summer.

---

"*The whistler*," said Wenna Harrow.

The Ferryman didn't say anything.

"*Take the whistler*," she said. "*The summer. All of it.*" Her voice cracked. She didn't let it stop her. "*That's what I'd least wish to lose. That's the one I can never have again.*"

The Ferryman looked at her for a long time.

"*Are you sure*?"

"*No*," said Wenna. "*But take it anyway.*"

---

He held out his hand.

Long. Grey. Thin. Open.

She looked at it.

Then she put the crown in it.

And closed her eyes.

---

She felt it go.

Not like a cut. Not like a theft. Like a tide going out. Slowly. Gently. From the back of her mind. From the place where she'd kept it for twenty years.

A gate. An apple tree. A blackbird.

A crooked grin.

A tune.

Going. Out. Away. Down a long grey river. Into the dark.

She held on. She couldn't help it. She held on to the tune the longest. A long high run of notes, rising, falling, never finished. She held on to it until it was only a feeling. Only a shape. Only the space where a sound had been.

And then that went too.

---

She opened her eyes.

She was kneeling on the shingle. On grey stones. By a grey river. In front of a tall grey man with no eyes, holding a small plain silver crown in his long grey hand.

There was a box open on the stones in front of her. Oak. Old. Empty. With a moth carved in the lid and crossed out.

She looked at it.

There was something missing. She could feel it. A space. Warm. Empty. Like a bed someone has just got out of. Like the place on a gatepost where a bird has been sitting.

She didn't know what it was.

---

"*It is done*," said the Ferryman.

"*What is*?" said Wenna.

He didn't answer.

She sat back on her heels. On the shingle. And looked at the river. Grey. Slow. And found that her hands were shaking. And that her face was wet. And that she didn't know why.

"*I've been crying*," she said. Surprised.

"*Yes*."

"*Why*?"

The eyeless face looked at her for a long moment.

"*Because you paid*," said the Ferryman. "*And it was the right price.*"

---

She closed the empty box.

Slowly. Carefully. The lid going down over the hollow in the bottom. The circle. The seven points. Pressed into the oak by nine hundred years of a crown lying there in the dark.

She didn't put the box in her bundle. She held it in her lap. Like a bowl.

"*Will you*—" She stopped. Tried again. "*Will you put it there? On the chair? In her tower?*"

"*I will*."

"*Will you tell me if it works*?"

"*You will know*," said the Ferryman. "*At the end of summer. When you dream.*"

---

She got up.

Her feet hurt. She hadn't noticed before. She noticed now. Bleeding through her boots. Eleven days. Three hundred miles.

She turned round. East. Up the long grey slope of the valley. Toward the thin country. Toward the white peaks like teeth. Toward the Vale.

Toward a cottage at the far end of it with a goat and a barrel of cider and a tall young Warden sleeping in the barn.

Toward her girl.

She didn't look back.

Harrow women never did.

---

The Ferryman watched her go.

He watched her climb the long grey slope with her stick and her empty box and her bleeding feet. He watched her reach the top. Stop. Stand there for a moment, with her back to him, a small square shape against the grey sky.

He watched her lift her hand to her mouth.

And try to whistle.

Nothing came. She'd never been able to. Harrow women can't.

She stood there a moment longer, with her hand at her mouth, puzzled. As if she'd reached for something on a shelf and found the shelf empty.

Then she lowered her hand. And went on. Over the top of the hill. And was gone.

---

He rowed it across that night.

There was no night, where he was going. But he rowed it across in what would have been the night, on the river that runs between the world and everything else, with the small plain silver crown sitting in the bow of his boat where nine Crowned had sat in chains nine hundred years before.

It was very cold. Colder than any passenger he'd carried.

It didn't speak. Crowns don't.

But it sat in the bow, facing forward, toward the grey. Toward the Court. Toward the country it had never touched. And the Ferryman, who had rowed every soul that ever lived to whatever shore it was owed, felt it the whole way across.

*No*.

Not a word. A refusal. Quiet. Patient. Steady. Like a hand laid flat on a door.

*Not you. Not ever*.

---

The far shore was dust.

He stepped out of the boat onto grey-gold, fine as flour, undisturbed for nine hundred years. He walked across the plain. Away from the white road. Away from the palaces, glittering on the edge of the sky. Away from the nine thousand gold shapes standing in rows along the road, humming one note.

North. To the edge of the plain.

To a tower.

---

Small. Slender. Grown out of a white tree, coaxed up and round on itself into the shape of a tower. Grey now. Cracked. Dead for nine hundred years.

A door at the bottom.

He opened it.

A stair. Winding. Up through the hollow of the dead trunk. He climbed it. With the crown in his long grey hand. With the cold coming off it. With the *no*.

At the top, a room.

A girl's room. Grown out of the wood. A bed, with a coverlet of dead grey leaves. A shelf, with a little carved bear on it, worn smooth. A bowl of dried roses, so old they'd gone to dust in the bottom of it. A window, looking west, toward nothing.

And a chair.

Small. Plain. Grown out of the floor like a root. Facing the window.

Empty.

---

He set the crown on it.

Gently. The way you'd set down a sleeping child. In the middle of the seat. Facing the window.

And stepped back.

And the room went quiet.

Not silent. Quiet. The way a room goes quiet when someone you love walks into it. The way a sick-room goes quiet when the fever breaks.

*No*, said the crown. To the room. To the tower. To the whole grey-gold country round it. Patiently. Steadily. Like a hand on a forehead.

*Hush. Not you. Not ever. Sleep.*

---

She was at the bottom of the stair when he came down.

Thin. Grey. Barefoot. In rotten silk. With a crown of black thorns on her head and her hands pressed together under her chin. Looking up at him.

She'd woken. He could see it. In her green eyes. In the dust still pouring off her shoulders. She'd felt the crown come into the Court the way you feel a cold hand on the back of your neck, and she'd woken from four hundred years of sleep and come running.

"*Is it hers*?" she said.

Her voice was like a stem bending.

"*It is*."

"*Is she coming*?"

"*No*," said the Ferryman. "*She has been dead eight hundred and seventy years. One of her line sent it.*"

The thorn-crowned woman stared at him.

"*She had a line*?"

"*Twenty generations*," said the Ferryman. "*Daughters. All of them. Every one of them kept the crown in a box and never wore it. And never let a moth in.*" A pause. "*The last of them paid me a whistler to bring it here.*"

---

She sat down.

On the bottom step of the stair. In the doorway of the tower she'd grown for her sister. With her thin knees drawn up. And put her face in her hands.

The Ferryman stood over her.

He didn't say anything. He was very patient. He'd been there since before the world was made.

After a long time she lifted her head.

"*Oz mustn't know*," she said.

"*No*."

"*Nobody must know*." She stood up. Slowly. Like something growing. "*Not Oz. Not Ysy. Not Cad. Nobody.*" She looked up the stair. Toward the room at the top. Toward the thing on the chair. "*It has to stay there. And say no. Forever.*"

She knelt down in the dust.

She put her hands flat on the ground. Thin. Grey-green. The fingers like twigs.

And the thorns came.

---

The Ferryman left her there.

On her knees in the dust at the foot of the tower, with her hands on the ground and her face wet, growing thorns. Black. Hooked. Thick as a wrist. Rising round the tower in a ring. Round and round. Taller and taller. A mile of them.

He walked back across the plain to his boat.

Behind him, in the tallest of the glass palaces, in a hall with a ceiling like a frozen waterfall, five cocoons hung from the rafters.

They'd been turning in their sleep. For a summer. Shifting against their ropes. Breathing.

They stopped.

One by one. Down the hall. The great grey-gold shapes going still. Settling. Sinking deeper into the silk. The way a sleeper settles when a cool hand is laid on a hot forehead.

And slept.

---

Wenna Harrow came home before the apples.

Thin. Thinner than her daughter had ever seen her. With her boots worn through and her feet bound in strips of her own skirt. With an empty box under her arm.

Seren was on the step.

She didn't say anything. She stood up. And looked at her mother coming up the path through the birch wood.

And ran.

---

The Warden was in the yard.

He'd been mending the barn door. Badly. With straw in his hair. He put down the hammer and watched them. Mother and daughter. Holding each other on the path in the evening light. Not saying anything.

Wenna looked at him over her daughter's shoulder.

He looked back.

"*Did she eat*?" said Wenna.

"*Not much*," said the Warden. "*She was very angry.*"

"*She still is*," said Seren. Into her mother's shoulder. Crying. "*I'm furious. I'm so angry with you. You went away. You never go away. You never*—"

"*I know*," said Wenna. "*I know, love.*"

---

She dreamed at the end of that summer.

The hall. The cocoons. Five of them. Grey-gold. Hanging from the rafters.

Still.

So still. More still than she'd ever dreamed them. Not swaying. Not turning. Not breathing. Hanging in the hall the way stones hang at the bottom of a pond.

And somewhere, far off, at the edge of the dream, at the edge of the plain, a tower. Small. Grey. In a ring of black thorns.

And in the window at the top, very faint, a gleam of silver.

She woke up smiling.

She didn't know why.

---

At the first snow, she made a lamp.

Out of clay from the stream. At the kitchen table. With her own hands. Small. Round. Brown. Plain. A Harrow lamp. One flame on both sides of a door. For the ones who go.

She pressed her thumb into the base before it dried.

She fired it in the hearth. She filled it with oil. She set it in the window of the cottage at the far end of the Vale. The east window. Facing the road.

And lit it.

---

Seren came in from the yard and stopped dead in the doorway.

She looked at the lamp. In the window. Lit.

Then at her mother.

"*Mam*," she said. "*You've never*—"

"*I know*."

"*Harrow women don't*—"

"*I know what Harrow women don't*," said Wenna. "*I've been one for forty years.*" She went back to the fire. Sat down. Picked up her mending. "*It's not for you.*"

Seren stared at her.

"*It's for the Warden*," said Wenna Harrow. "*He's coming down from his Spire tonight. Three days on foot in the snow. He'll want to see where he's going.*"

---

He came up the path at dusk.

In his grey coat. Too tall. With snow on his shoulders and ink on his cuffs. And stopped. At the edge of the birch wood. Looking at the cottage.

At the lamp in the window.

He stood there for a long time.

Then he came up the path. And knocked. And Wenna opened the door.

He didn't say anything. He looked at her. And past her. At the lamp on the sill.

"*Well*," said Wenna Harrow. "*Ask me again.*"

---

She didn't throw the pan.

---

She wrote the letter at midwinter.

At the kitchen table. By the light of the clay lamp. With her daughter married and gone to Highcrest with her Warden, and the cottage at the far end of the Vale very quiet, and the goat asleep under the table with a sock.

*Seren. My bright girl. My last one.*

*If you're reading this, I'm gone, and you've stayed.*

She wrote it all. The rule. The song. The cottages. The moths. She wrote it slowly, in her small slanting hand, the hand of a woman who'd never stayed anywhere long enough to learn to write slowly.

And near the end, she stopped.

She sat with the pen over the page for a long time.

Then she wrote:

*I've taken the crown where it belongs. Where it was always meant to go.*

She didn't write where. She didn't write how. She didn't write what it cost.

She didn't remember what it cost.

She only knew, somewhere very deep, in a warm empty place like a bed someone has just got out of, that it had been the right price.

---

She died before the spring.

In the cottage at the far end of the Vale. In her bed. With her daughter holding her hand.

At the end, she opened her eyes.

"*Light the lamp for me*," said Wenna Harrow.

And closed them.

---

And on a grey river at the edge of the world, a tall thin man with no eyes rowed a small square woman across to the far shore.

She sat in the bow. Facing back. Toward the world. The way they all do.

And halfway across, she started to whistle.

A long high run of notes. Rising. Falling. A tune with no name and no end. She didn't know what it was. She didn't know where she'd learned it. She stopped, halfway, and frowned, and said so.

And then she started again.

And whistled it all the way to the other side.

As if she'd finally found somewhere to stop.
