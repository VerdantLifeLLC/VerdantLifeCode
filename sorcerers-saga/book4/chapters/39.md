# Epilogue: The Edge of the World

The Gilded King was standing at the edge of the world when it fell.

He'd walked there. Two nights and a day. West, from a cottage with a blue door, through the snow, down a gold road he'd made himself in the summer, across the Vale and the downs and the shires. Not hurrying. Not slow. Walking the way a man walks away from a house where he has seen something he can't stop seeing.

He didn't stop at any wells.

He didn't sit with anyone.

He walked.

---

He had a cup in his hand.

Lopsided. Too thick. With a thumb-print in the clay near the handle where a smith had pressed too hard. Gold now. All of it. And the tea in it, a gold disc, still. He'd carried it the whole way. In his great gold hand. Very carefully. The way you'd carry a bird's egg.

He didn't know why.

He'd never carried anything for anyone. Orrin carried things. The household carried things. He'd been carried, once, in a boat. In chains.

He carried the cup.

---

And against his chest, inside his robe, over his heart, a crown of silver wire. Fine as frost. Shaped like folded wings.

He'd carried that since he woke.

He carried it still.

---

Greyfall was behind him. The east gate. A man standing in it, gold, with snow on his shoulders, with a staff held crosswise. Daxian Roole. Commander of the Keepers of the Stones.

*You're not coming through this gate*.

He'd walked past him. Again. In the dark. In the snow.

He'd stopped. Just for a moment. And looked.

At the gold face. At the eyes, moving. Slowly. Following him.

*Somebody has to stand somewhere*.

He'd thought it was beautiful. In the summer. He'd thought, *you should never stop. You should stand there forever. Exactly like that.*

He'd looked at the eyes, moving. Following him past. Saying something he couldn't hear.

He'd walked on faster.

---

The gold at the foot of the Veil was under snow.

A field. Forty sheep. A boy among them, mid-step, with his crook out and snow on his cap. Further on, a woman sitting on the grey at the edge of the gold. Wrapped in a shawl. With a basket beside her.

She'd come back.

The Keepers had carried her east in the summer. Biting. Kicking. And she'd come back. Alone. In the autumn. When the King had gone. And sat down again at the edge of the gold. And started talking.

She looked up as he passed.

At the biggest man she'd ever seen. Gold. Glowing like metal in a crucible. Walking past her in the snow with a gold cup in his hand.

She didn't say anything.

She went on talking to her son.

He walked past her. And didn't look back.

He wanted to. He didn't.

---

The Veil stood in front of him.

Grey. Hanging from nowhere. Falling to nowhere. Moving very slightly, the way a curtain moves in a room with a window open somewhere behind it.

With a door in it.

Tall. Dark. Wide. Wide enough for a cart. Wide enough for a king. Where two brothers had put their bleeding hands on the edge of the world in the summer, and it hadn't known them, and had opened.

He'd come through it. In the summer. On the day after they went in. Smelling the Harrow on the edges. Laughing.

*Thank you, cousins. I've been waiting so long.*

He stood in front of it now. In the snow. At midnight. On Lantern Night.

And waited.

---

For Cad.

That was the plan. It had always been the plan. Since the hall. Since the four of them woke in their silk and stood under the empty cocoons and he said *the Key*.

Cad would climb the last thread. Ring by ring. To the knot at the bottom of the world. On the night they lit their lanterns. And take the bar out. And take the Key.

And bring it here. Up the thread. To the edge. To him.

And he'd turn it.

And pick up the net.

And keep it.

---

He stood in front of the door at the edge of the world and thought about that.

He'd thought about it for nine hundred years. In the silk. In the dark. In his dreams. The net in his hands. Every thread. Every child. Every old man in every chair.

Safe.

Kept.

Nobody going through the door. Not ever. Not one. In the whole world.

He'd thought about it with his whole heart. It was the only thing he'd ever wanted.

He stood in the snow with a gold cup in his hand and thought about it.

And thought about an old man on three pillows under a boarded window. With his grey eyes. Saying *you should have gone in. He'd have liked you there.*

And thought about a baby with a fist in her mouth. Saying *no*.

---

He felt it at midnight.

Under his feet. Through the snow. Through the cracked dead ground of the Sundered Lands. Through the last thread, running deep down under the world from the foot of the Veil to the knot at the bottom.

A turning.

The Key.

He knew it. He'd have known it anywhere. The little man with the hammer had made it the night he wove the world, and the Crowned had felt it made, in their chains, on the shingle, waiting for the boat.

It was turning.

---

*Cad*, he thought. *Cad's got it. Cad's turning it. Cad's bringing it*—

No.

Cad wouldn't turn it. Cad would bring it. Cad had promised. On the shingle. *I'll go where you go. I'll do what you ask*.

Somebody else was turning it.

---

And then he felt the *no*.

---

It came up the thread.

Out of the bottom of the world. Out of the knot. Up through the dark under the Drowned Country and the Greyspine and the Sundered Lands. Up the last thread. Into the ground under his feet.

Cold. Quiet. Patient. Steady.

Like a hand laid flat on a door.

*Not you. Not ever*.

He knew it.

He'd felt it on the shingle. Nine hundred years ago. In chains. Looking back at the shore. At a girl of seventeen standing on the grey stones with a crown in her hands and her voice shaking.

He'd felt it in the hall. For thirty-two years. In his sleep. A cool hand on a hot forehead. A quiet he never understood.

He felt it now.

Coming up the last thread from the bottom of the world.

"*Hes*," said Oszric of the Crowned.

---

The thread went slack.

He felt it go. Under his feet. Through the ground. The one high note it had been humming since the world was woven—going flat. Going soft. Going grey.

Going.

And the Veil—

---

It fell.

Not like a wall. Walls crash. Walls crack and tumble and break.

It fell like a *cloth*.

The way a cloth falls when someone lets go of the corners. The way a curtain falls when the rope that holds it is cut. From the top. Softly. Slowly. The grey that had hung across the edge of the world for nine hundred years, from nowhere to nowhere, sinking.

Down.

The door in it going with it. The tall dark gap where two brothers had bled. Folding. Crumpling. Sinking into the grey like a mouth closing.

Down.

All along the edge. North and south. As far as he could see. Further. The grey coming down out of the stars like a blind being lowered. Like a sheet drawn off a bed.

He stood in the snow and watched it come down.

---

And there was a wind.

From behind him. From the east. From the world. A great soft rolling sigh of air, pouring west across the Sundered Lands, across the gold, across the woman by the line and her gold son in his gold field. Into the falling grey.

Through it.

Into the grey country.

The first wind in the Outer Court in nine hundred years.

---

And the grey country breathed out.

He felt it. The wind going in. And something coming back. Dust. Old dust. The dust of a closed room. Of a hall nobody had walked through in nine hundred years. Rolling out of the falling grey on a breath of air, across the snow, past him.

East.

Into the world.

And under it, faint—

Roses.

---

The Veil touched the ground.

He felt it. A softness. A settling. Like a cloth laid on a table. Like snow landing on snow.

And then it wasn't there.

---

There was nothing.

Nothing between him and the grey country. No curtain. No edge. No grey from nowhere to nowhere. Just—*ground*. The cracked dead ground of the Sundered Lands, under snow, running west. And then, without any line at all, without any seam, without anything to say *here is where one stops and the other begins*—

Dust.

Grey-gold. Fine as flour. Lying in drifts. Going on. West. Flat. To the edge of everything.

With a road in it.

White. The white of old bone. Broad. Straight as a ruler. Starting at his feet. At the foot of where the Veil had been. Running away west across the grey-gold plain toward a smudge of towers on the edge of the sky.

Glass. Iron. Gold. Bone.

Home.

---

He'd thought he'd feel—

He didn't know what he'd thought he'd feel.

Triumph. That was what he'd thought. For nine hundred years. In the silk. *When the edge comes down. When we walk back into the world. When nothing stands between us and the light.*

He stood in the snow at the edge of the world with a gold cup in his hand and a crown of silver wire against his heart and looked at the white road running home.

He didn't feel triumph.

He felt *cold*.

Right in the middle.

---

Something was moving on the road.

Far off. At the edge of what he could see. A glitter. Clear. Cold. Like frost on a window. Walking. Toward him. Down the white road out of the grey-gold dim.

And behind it, something else. Thin. Dark. Grey-green. Stumbling.

And a sound.

*Chime*. *Chime*.

Like glass touching glass.

---

Ysenne came down the white road with her sister on a chain.

He watched her come. Tall. Thin. Clear. Glass, all of her, glittering, with the grey-gold dim and the white road showing through her, bent, as through a lens. With her white hair and her narrow crown of glass.

With a chain in her hand.

Glass. Fine. Links like icicles. Running back from her clear fingers to a pair of thin grey-green wrists.

Rhosyn walked behind her.

Barely walking. Stumbling. Her rotten silk hanging off her. Her crown of thorns crooked. Her face grey. Not grey-green. *Grey*. The colour of ash. Of the inside of a dead tree.

She'd given everything. In the thorns. Every stem. Every root. To hold a thousand gold servants back from a tower long enough for two Harrow boys to run.

She had nothing left.

She walked behind her sister on a chain of glass with her head down and her bare feet dragging in the dust.

---

They stopped in front of him.

At the foot of the white road. Where the Veil had been. A yard of snow between the grey-gold dust and his gold feet.

Ysenne looked at him.

With her ice-grey eyes. For a long time.

"*Oz*," she said.

"*Ysy*."

"*It's down*," said the Glass Queen. "*The edge.*"

"*Yes*."

"*You didn't turn it*."

"*No*," said Oszric.

She looked at him. At his face. At whatever was in it. She'd always been able to see what was in his face. Since they were children. She'd sit at the mirror and look at him in it, over her shoulder, and tell him what he was thinking.

"*Hes did*," said Ysenne. Very quietly. "*Didn't she. Her crown. On someone's head. Saying no.*"

"*Yes*."

"*I felt it come up the thread*," said the Glass Queen. "*In every piece of glass in the Court. Every pane went cold.*" She looked down at her clear hands. At the chain in them. "*Like a breath on a window.*"

---

"*Where's Cad*?" said Oszric.

Ysenne didn't answer.

She looked past him. East. Into the world. At the snow. At the gold road wandering away across it. At the dark.

"*He fell*," said Rhosyn.

---

The King looked at her.

His youngest sister but one. On a chain. With her head down. With her crown of thorns crooked. With her voice like a stem bending.

"*He fell*," she said again. Not looking up. "*With the thread. When it went. He was standing on it. At the bottom.*" Her thin shoulders were shaking. "*I felt him go. Through the roots. Through the ground. All the way down.*"

"*Rosie*—"

"*He nodded*," said the Thorn-Crowned. "*Before he went. I felt that too. The way he nodded at her on the shingle.*" She lifted her head. Her green eyes were wet. "*He kept his word, Oz. All the way down the thread. To the end.*" A pause. "*And then there wasn't any more thread to keep it on.*"

---

The Gilded King didn't say anything for a long time.

He stood at the edge of the world. In the snow. With his sisters in front of him on the white road. With a gold cup in one hand and his other hand pressed flat against his chest, over his heart, over a crown of silver wire.

*Cad held his hand*, he'd said. In the cottage. To the old man. *Every night. At the end. Not saying anything. He was eleven.*

*And I stood outside the door*.

He'd stood outside every door since.

He'd made nine thousand people stand outside theirs.

And now Cad was gone. Through his own. Into the dark. Nodding.

And he hadn't been there.

---

"*Oz*," said Ysenne.

He looked at her.

"*They're coming*," said the Glass Queen.

She turned. On the white road. And looked back. West. Down it. Into the grey-gold dim.

He looked too.

---

The kept were walking.

All of them. He saw it. Along the white road. On both sides. As far as he could see. Nine thousand of them. Gold. In the grey-gold dust. Cooks and grooms and maids and gardeners. Soldiers with gold spears. Nurses with gold babies. Old men with gold sticks. Children with gold hoops.

Turned.

Every one. Facing east. Facing the edge. Facing where the Veil had been. Facing the world.

And walking.

*Scrape*. *Scrape*. *Scrape*.

A finger's width. A finger's width. Slow. So slow. Gold feet on white stone. Gold feet in grey-gold dust.

Coming.

---

"*Did you*—" he said. "*Ysy, did you tell them*—"

"*No*," said the Glass Queen. "*Orrin didn't. I didn't. Nobody did.*" She looked at them. Nine thousand gold servants walking down the white road toward the edge of the world. "*They just started. When it fell. When the wind came in.*" A pause. "*They felt the wind, Oz. They haven't felt wind in nine hundred years.*"

And under the scraping, the hum.

One note. From nine thousand gold throats. Low. Soft. Worn smooth.

He'd heard it for nine hundred years. In his sleep. In the hall. Walking down the white road past them. He'd never listened.

He listened now.

*...home...*

---

At the front of them, a long way ahead of the rest, small, in a grey dress too big for her—

A girl.

Gold. With a wooden spoon in her hand.

Walking.

Not like the others. Not a finger's width. More. A step. A real step. Slow. Stiff. Scraping. But a step. And another. Down the white road. Out of the grey-gold dim. Toward him.

Toward the edge.

Toward the world.

Her eyes were moving.

Fast.

---

He knew her.

He didn't know how. He hadn't looked at any of them in nine hundred years. He'd walked past them every day and never once looked.

He knew her.

*The spit-girl*. *Ilda. The bread-woman's daughter. She turned the spit in my kitchen. She was eight. She burned her hand on the spit-iron the week before the pool and cried and I gave her a sweet.*

*I kept her in the first year*. *When the household started to come apart. When there was nothing left to drink. She was so frightened. She was coming apart in her mother's arms. I put my hand on her forehead*—

He stopped.

He stood in the snow and watched a gold girl with a wooden spoon walk down the white road toward him.

And she wasn't looking at him.

She was looking past him. Into the world. East. At something he couldn't see.

At a boy, maybe. In a blue coat. Who'd looked back.

---

"*Oz*," said Rhosyn.

He looked at her.

She'd sat down. On the white road. On her chain. In the dust. With her thin knees drawn up and her arms round them. Like a girl on the edge of a cabbage bed.

She was looking at the gold girl.

"*She's going home*," said the Thorn-Crowned. Very softly. "*They all are. The hum. That's what it's saying. That's what it's always been saying.*" She looked up at him. "*The warden told me. In the thorns. Home.*"

"*They are home*," said the Gilded King. "*With me. In the Court. Safe.*"

He'd said it in the cottage. To a man in a bed. *They're safe*.

He'd said it to himself every day for nine hundred years.

He said it now. To the snow. To the dark. To the gold girl walking past him.

It didn't sound true anymore.

---

The girl reached the edge.

Where the Veil had been. Where the grey-gold dust stopped and the snow began, without any line, without any seam.

She stopped.

Just for a moment. At the place. With one gold foot on the dust and one gold foot lifted. Her eyes on the snow in front of her. White. Cold. Melting where her breath would have been, if she'd had breath.

The first snow she'd seen in nine hundred years.

Then her foot came down.

On the snow.

In the world.

*Scrape*.

---

The snow didn't go gold.

He saw it. He stood beside her, a yard off, and saw it. The gold foot on the white snow. And the snow under it—

White.

Not gold. Not kept. *White*. Cold. Wet. Melting a little round the edges of the gold foot. The way snow melts round anything that's been standing in the sun.

She was in the world.

And the world wasn't stopping for her.

---

She walked on.

Past him. East. Into the dark. Into the snow. Toward the Sundered Lands. Toward the gold road. Toward a world full of lanterns that were all, he could see now, very faint, on the edge of the eastern sky, every one, lit.

*Scrape*. *Scrape*.

A gold girl with a wooden spoon. Walking home.

And behind her, down the white road, out of the grey-gold dim, nine thousand more.

---

The dead came last.

He saw them rise. At the edge. Where the Veil had stood. Where they'd lain in drifts for nine hundred years against the foot of the grey, because there was no river for them and no door and nowhere to go.

Bronze. Bone. Copper. Coral.

Four. Rising. Columns of coloured dust. Swaying. In the first wind they'd felt since they came apart.

His cousins. His sisters' husbands. Who'd come apart against the edge on the night the boat came through, because they'd drunk the least and the edge didn't know them and pushed them till they broke.

They rose. And swayed. And turned.

East.

And drifted.

On the wind. Out of the grey country. Into the world. Past him. Past the gold girl. Past the nine thousand. East. Slowly. Like smoke. Like mist. Like the last of a fire going up a chimney.

Toward the lanterns.

---

And behind them, more.

He hadn't known. He'd never looked. *Grey*. Thousands. Rising out of the grey-gold dust all over the plain of the Court, as far as he could see. Not coloured. Not his cousins. Just grey. Columns of grey dust. Swaying.

Everyone who'd ever come apart in the Court.

In nine hundred years. In the first years, before he went out with his hands. The ones he hadn't reached in time. The ones who'd crumbled. The ones who'd lain down in the dust and not got up and become the dust.

He'd thought they were dust.

They were rising.

And drifting. East. On the first wind. Toward the edge that wasn't there anymore.

Toward the lanterns.

Toward the room.

---

*There is a pool in a green field*, the warden had told the four at the edge. In the summer. With his hand out. Rhosyn had heard it. Through the roots. *Full of room. It's still very little.*

*When it's bigger. When it's grown. I'll come back*.

The dead didn't wait.

They drifted east. Thousands of them. On the first wind in nine hundred years. Toward a green circle a hundred miles across, under snow, with a pool in the middle and three small gold lights at the bottom of it.

And a girl on a crutch.

And a boy in a blue coat.

Sitting on the rim. With their feet over the dark.

Waiting.

---

The Gilded King stood at the edge of the world.

In the snow. With a gold cup in his hand and a crown of silver wire against his heart. With his sisters on the white road behind him. One of glass. One on a chain. Sitting in the dust.

With nine thousand gold servants walking past him into the world. Scraping. Humming one word.

With the dead of nine hundred years drifting past him on the wind.

With the lanterns. Every one. On the edge of the eastern sky. Lit. Given.

He stood there a long time.

---

"*Oz*," said Ysenne.

He didn't answer.

"*Oz*. *What do we do?*"

He looked down at the cup in his hand.

Lopsided. Too thick. A thumb-print near the handle. Gold. With a gold disc of tea in it that he'd never be able to taste.

*Take it anyway*, the hedge-witch had said. Kneeling on her floor. *It's yours. I made it for you. If it goes gold, it goes gold. It's still yours.*

*Nobody's ever made me anything*.

He looked east.

At the world. At the snow. At the gold girl with the wooden spoon, getting smaller, walking home. At the lanterns.

At a cottage, two hundred miles off, at the end of a valley, with a blue door. And a lantern in its west window, horn-paned, crooked-hinged, lit, with two words cut into the base where only someone who picked it up would see.

He couldn't see it. He couldn't know.

But he looked anyway.

---

"*We go and see everyone*," said Oszric of the Crowned.

He said it the way he'd said it in the hall. When he woke. With Mae's crown in his hand and the crack open in the Veil and green coming through it.

*Then we'll go and see everyone*.

It didn't sound the same.

He didn't know why. The words were the same. The voice was the same. Deep. Warm. Like a bell.

But something in it had cracked. In the middle. Like a cup with a thumb-print near the handle where somebody pressed too hard.

He stepped across the line.

Off the snow. No. *Onto* it. Out of the grey country. Into the world. Where the Veil had been.

The snow under his foot went gold.

Of course it did. Everything he touched.

He looked at it for a long time.

And walked on anyway.

East.

Toward the lanterns.

---

On the far side of the world, at the end of a valley, in a cottage with a birch wood behind it and a stream in front, Lex Vey woke at dawn and the world was quiet.

Inside.

He lay on his back in the loft and listened to the quiet for a long time.

Then he got up. And went down the ladder. And knelt at the hearth.

---

The fire was banked. A heap of grey ash with a red eye in the middle.

He blew on it. Gently. The way you'd wake someone who'd been up all night.

It woke. Flickered. Caught the kindling. Climbed.

*Good morning*, said the fire.

Not in words. A warmth. A lift. A small bright gladness.

"*Good morning*," said Lex.

He waited.

Nothing.

He'd known there'd be nothing. He'd known it yesterday and the day before. He'd know it tomorrow.

He said it anyway.

*It would be rude not to*.

---

Lark came across the floor.

On her hands and knees. Fast. A furious scuttling lurch, like a crab with a grudge. From wherever Wren had put her down. She arrived at the hearth and sat back on her bottom beside him.

And looked at the fire.

He watched her look at it. With her grey-green eyes. With her fist halfway to her mouth. With her fluff of dark hair standing up like a bird's crest.

She didn't reach for it.

She'd learned. *No*. *Hot*.

She just looked. Very seriously. The way she looked at the horseshoe nail on its ribbon every morning. The way she looked at the lanterns all night on her birthday.

As if she were saying good morning to it.

---

And then she said something.

---

Not *no*. Not her one word.

Something else. Small. Wet. Round. A sound. A babble. The kind of noise a child makes when she's trying a word she's heard and hasn't got the shape of yet.

"*Mo*," said Lark. To the fire. "*Mo. Nn.*"

Lex went very still.

"*Mo-nn*," said his daughter. Frowning at the flames. Working at it. The way he worked at a weld. "*Mmo-ning.*"

---

It wasn't a word.

He knew it wasn't. She was one. She had six teeth and a grip like a vice and one word, and the word was *no*. This was a sound. A babble. A baby copying her father, who knelt at the hearth every morning and said the same thing to the fire, and she'd heard it, and she was trying it.

That was all.

That was all it was.

---

The fire leaned toward her.

He saw it. The flames. Bending. All together. Toward the small square figure sitting on the hearthstone. The way grass leans. The way a dog leans against your legs.

Glad.

The way it was glad for him every morning. Because it was the Flame. Because it had been burning since before there were people to burn for. Because someone had sat down in front of it and said good morning.

Glad.

That was all.

---

And for a moment—

Just a moment—

In the leaning of the flames toward his daughter. In the small bright lift of them. In the warmth coming off the hearth onto her round face.

He thought he heard something.

Not a voice. Not words. Not in the back of his head, where there'd been something for twenty-two years and was nothing now.

*In the fire*.

Small. Dry. Gentle. Precise. With an old-fashioned courtesy.

Glad.

---

He didn't move.

He knelt on the hearthstone beside his daughter and didn't breathe.

The fire burned. The flames stood up straight again. Lark frowned at them. And put her fist in her mouth. And looked at her father.

"*No*," she said. Conversationally.

---

He didn't know.

He'd never know. Not for certain. He'd lie awake at night for a long time, after, wondering. Whether it was anything at all. Whether it was a fire being a fire, and a baby being a baby, and a man who'd lost a voice in his head hearing it where it wasn't, the way you hear a step on the stair in a house where nobody lives anymore.

Probably.

That was probably all it was.

---

But he picked up his daughter.

And sat with her on the hearthstone. In front of the fire. In the grey dawn. In the quiet.

And said it again. Out loud. For her. So she'd learn it. So it would go down. Not in words. In voices.

"*Good morning*," said Lex Vey.

"*Mmo-ning*," said Lark. Through her fist.

And the fire leaned toward both of them.

Glad.

---

*Here ends* The Sorcerer's Crown, *Book Four of the Lattice Chronicles.*
