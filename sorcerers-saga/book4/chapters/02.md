# Gilt

"*You're going*," said Wren.

It wasn't a question. She was standing in the kitchen with Lark on her hip and Roole's note in her other hand, reading it for the third time. Her face had gone very still. The way it went still on a rooftop when she'd seen something move in the street below.

"*I'm going*," said Lex.

"*Today*."

"*Now*."

She looked up from the paper.

"*I'm coming*."

"*Wren*—"

"*Don't*." She didn't raise her voice. She never did, when it mattered. "*Don't you dare. Don't you dare say I can't. I've tracked Mothwing across the Sundered Lands. I've stood at the Veil. I was there when Fenn*—" She stopped. Swallowed. Looked down at the baby on her hip. Who was looking up at her with round brown eyes and one square fist wound tight in the end of her braid.

Lark said, "*Ah*."

Wren closed her eyes.

"*She eats every three hours*," she said. Very quietly. To the ceiling. "*Every three hours. Sometimes two. I can't*—" She opened her eyes again. "*It's a day's flight. Each way. Even on Vharuun. I can't take her to the Veil. And I can't leave her for two days. Not yet. Not now.*" Her voice cracked. Just a little. Just at the edge. "*I can't.*"

"*I know*."

"*I hate it*."

"*I know*."

"*I hate it so much*." She glared at him. Her eyes were wet. "*You go and look at gold sheep and I sit here and—and*—"

"*Feed our daughter*."

"*Don't make it sound noble*," said Wren. "*It's not noble. It's milk.*" She wiped her face with the back of the hand holding the note. Fiercely. "*Go. Go on. Come back tomorrow night or I'll come and find you and I'll bring her and she'll scream the whole way and it'll be your fault.*"

He kissed her.

She let him. She held on to his collar with her free hand. Hard. The way Lark held on to things.

"*If it's him*," she said into his shoulder. "*The gold one. The King.*"

"*It won't be him. He's on the other side.*"

"*If it is*." She pulled back. Looked at him. "*You don't go near it. You don't touch anything. You don't do anything brave and stupid. You look, and you come home, and you tell me, and then we decide.*"

"*Together*."

"*Together*," said Wren Ashdown. "*Or I'll break your nose again. And it won't be a Moth Queen doing it this time.*"

---

Vharuun was in the high pasture above the birch wood, where he slept most days now, curled round a boulder like a cat round a warm stone.

He was enormous. He'd always been enormous. Bronze and copper and old gold, with a ridge of horns down his back like a mountain range and eyes the colour of a forge at full heat. He'd been asleep under the Greyspine for four hundred years before Lex woke him, and he'd made it clear, since, that he considered the waking a favour he was still deciding whether to forgive.

He opened one eye as Lex came up the hill.

*[You want something*,] said Ember. *[He can tell. He has that look. The look of a dragon who's been asked to do something before breakfast.]*

*It's after breakfast*.

*[Not his*.]

Lex stopped in front of the great bronze head. Laid his hand flat on the warm scales between the nostrils. The way he always did. A greeting. A question.

*West*, he thought. *To the Veil. To Greyfall. Today. Please.*

The eye looked at him. Molten gold. Ancient.

Then a picture. Not words. Vharuun didn't do words. Just a picture, very clear, very sharp, dropped into Lex's head like a stone into a well.

A sheep. Roasting.

*Fine*, thought Lex. *Two sheep. When we get there. Roole will pay.*

Vharuun considered this.

Then he uncurled, all of him, all at once, with a sound like a mountainside settling. And lowered one great wing to the grass.

---

They flew all day.

West over the Vale. Over Ashbourne, small and grey and smoking far below, with the long roof of the Forge and the chimneys at each end and the tiny black dot of Quill on his nest of spoons. Over the downs. Over the fields of the western shires, brown and wet and waiting for the plough.

Over the Blightmarch.

Lex looked down. He couldn't not.

A hundred miles of soft green. Where a year ago there had been grey. Dead grass, dead trees, the long scar of the Gloam. Green now. All of it. Pale and new, like the first growth on a burned hillside. And in the middle, far off, a glint. A small dark round glint, like an eye.

The pool.

And beside it, smaller still, a little black house on the slope. With a window. With something in the window, so small at this height it was only a spark.

A lantern. *HOME*.

*[You're smiling again*,] said Ember.

*I'm allowed*.

*[I didn't say you weren't. I'm just noting it. For the record.]* A pause. *[It's very green.]*

*It's very green*, Lex agreed.

---

Then the bare lands.

The Sundered Lands began a little way past the Blightmarch's western edge. He knew them. He'd crossed them twice. Grey cracked ground. Black stumps of trees that had died nine hundred years ago and never fallen, because nothing out here rotted. Broken roads that went nowhere. The bones of villages. And the wind, coming always from the west, always cold, never stopping.

Vharuun flew lower. He didn't like the bare lands. He said so, in pictures. A picture of a fire going out. A picture of a cave with nothing in it.

*I know*, thought Lex. *I don't like them either.*

They flew on. Into the wind. Toward the edge of the world.

---

Greyfall stood on a low ridge at the very end of everything.

It had been a Warden fort once. Then a Keepers' post. Then, briefly, last spring, a ruin, its lanterns eaten, its people dimmed, its gates hanging open in the wind. Roole had rebuilt it in the summer. Stone on stone. With his one good arm and two hundred Keepers.

There were lanterns on the walls again. Lit. Even in the daytime. Roole said he didn't care what it cost in oil.

And beyond the fort, two miles off across the bare grey ground—

The Veil.

---

It never got easier to look at.

Lex had seen it twice before. He'd thought he was ready. He wasn't. You couldn't be.

It was grey. That was all. Just grey. A wall of grey, hanging from nowhere, falling to nowhere, stretching north and south as far as the eye could follow and then further. Not mist. Not cloth. Not stone. Something that the eye couldn't settle on, that kept sliding off, the way your tongue slides off a loose tooth. It moved, very slightly, all the time. Rippling. Like a curtain in a room where someone has left a window open.

The edge of the world.

On the other side, nine hundred years ago, nine people in chains had been rowed across a grey river by a man with no eyes.

On the other side, now, four of them were awake.

*[Lex*,] said Ember. Very quietly. *[Look at the ground.]*

He looked.

---

At the foot of the Veil, the world was gold.

He saw it from a mile off. From the air. A great soft blot of it, spreading out from one point at the very bottom of the grey like spilled paint. Shining. Even in the flat grey light of the Sundered Lands, under the flat grey sky, it shone. Dully. Like old brass. Like a coin at the bottom of a well.

It was round. Roughly. A mile across. Perhaps more.

And it wasn't a colour.

He understood that as Vharuun came down. It wasn't paint. It wasn't light. It wasn't the way the grass looks at sunset. The grass there *was* gold. The stones were gold. The black stumps of the dead trees were gold, every crack in their bark, every splinter. A broken wall of an old farmstead. Gold. A cart-track with ruts in it. Gold ruts.

And in the middle of it, a field.

And in the field, forty small round gold shapes. Heads down. Grazing.

And among them, one tall thin one. Standing.

---

Roole met him at the edge.

He was standing on the grey ground twenty paces back from the line where the gold began, with three Keepers and a woman Lex knew. Ada Fell. Grey cloak. Iron-grey hair. A Keeper's staff. She'd been at the Ashbourne Stone the day Ivy Moss was Kindled.

Roole's arm was out of its sling. He held it a little stiffly, a little close to his side, like a man carrying something he didn't want to drop. His hatchet face was the same as ever. Hard. Plain. Lined. His eyes went to the dragon, and then to Lex, and didn't change at all.

"*Vey*," he said.

"*Roole*."

"*You came fast*."

"*You said come*."

"*I did*." Roole looked past him. At the gold. "*I didn't know who else to say it to.*"

---

They walked to the edge together.

Not to it. Near it. Roole stopped two paces back and put out his stiff arm, not touching Lex, just there, the way you'd put an arm out in front of a child at the edge of a road.

"*Don't step on it*," he said. "*We don't know what it does. We lost a dog the first day. Ran straight onto it after a hare.*" His jaw tightened. "*It's there. Over by the wall. Gold. With the hare.*"

Lex looked.

There. By the broken farmstead wall. Two small gold shapes. One long and low, mid-leap. One small and round, a yard in front of it, ears flat, also mid-leap.

Both of them stopped. In the air. Just above the ground. Like a carving of a chase.

"*It's moving*," said Ada Fell. Quietly. "*The edge. Not fast. A few feet a day. We've been marking it.*" She pointed with her staff. A line of small white stones on the grey ground, laid out in a curve. And another line, a few feet in from it, gold. And another, a few feet further in, gold.

"*It's coming out of there*," said Roole. And nodded. West. Toward the Veil. Toward the foot of the grey, where the gold was brightest. "*There's a crack. You'll want to see it. After.*"

"*After what*?"

Roole didn't answer.

He just looked out across the gold. At the field. At the forty sheep. At the one tall thin shape among them.

And at the other shape. On the near side of the gold. On the grey. Sitting on the ground. A woman.

---

She was sitting very close to the line. Closer than Roole would let anyone stand. Her knees drawn up under her skirt. A shawl round her shoulders. A basket beside her, covered with a cloth.

She was talking.

Lex couldn't hear what she was saying. The wind took it. But he could see her mouth moving. Steadily. Patiently. The way you talk to someone who's sick in bed, when you don't know if they can hear you and you talk anyway, just in case.

She was talking to the boy.

"*Nan Ashby*," said Roole. Very quietly. "*His mother. From the croft at Westerling. It's eight miles.*" He didn't look at Lex. "*She walks it every morning. She sits there all day. She brings him his supper. She leaves it on the line.*" His voice didn't change. It never did. "*She says he's always hungry after a day with the flock.*"

---

Lex walked out to her.

Not onto the gold. Along the edge of it. On the grey. Roole didn't stop him. Roole stood with his stiff arm at his side and watched.

The woman looked up as Lex came. She was younger than he'd thought. Thirty, perhaps. Thin. Brown-haired. With a farm woman's red hands and a face that had cried itself out a long time ago and was now only very, very tired.

She looked at the mark on his cheek. The grey-gold handprint. Everybody did.

Then at his face.

"*You're the smith*," she said. "*The one from the songs. With the little hammer.*"

"*Yes*."

"*Can you get him out*?"

---

He didn't answer.

He couldn't. He didn't know. He stood on the grey ground beside her and looked across the gold at her son.

Twelve. Thin. In a shepherd's smock and a felt cap. Caught in the middle of a step. One foot lifted. One arm flung out. His crook in his fist. His mouth open, as if he'd just that moment opened it to whistle for his dog.

Gold.

All of him. Cap to boots. His smock, every fold of it. His hair, where it stuck out from under the cap. His eyelashes. The dirt under his fingernails. A scab on his knee where the smock had ridden up. A little hole in the toe of one boot. All of it gold. All of it perfect. Like a statue made by someone who loved him very much and had looked at him for a very long time and got every detail exactly right.

And his eyes.

Lex was close enough now. Thirty paces. He could see them.

They were gold too. Iris and white and pupil, all the same dull shining gold.

And they were looking at him.

---

They moved.

Slowly. So slowly. The way the hand of a clock moves. You couldn't see it move. You could only see that it had moved. That a moment ago it had been looking at his mother and now it was looking at Lex.

At Lex's face.

At the mark on his cheek.

And then back. Slowly. To his mother.

Lex felt something go out of his chest. All the air. All at once. As if he'd been kicked.

*[He's awake*,] said Ember. Very small. *[Lex. He's awake in there.]*

*I know*.

*[He can see us*.]

*I know*.

*[How long has he*—]

"*Nine days*," said Nan Ashby. She wasn't looking at Lex. She was looking at her son. "*Nine days, today. It was a Tuesday. He went out with the flock at dawn. There was a wind from the west. Mad Annie at the next croft said it was full of sparks.*" She was very calm. Terribly calm. "*He didn't come home for his supper. So I came out to look.*"

She stopped.

"*His eyes move*," she said. "*Every day. A little. I watch them. I talk to him and I watch them.*" She swallowed. "*They follow me. When I go along the line. They follow me.*"

---

He tried.

He'd known he would. He'd known from the moment he read the note, standing on the stepping stone in the stream. He'd tried to talk himself out of it all the way across the bare lands. He'd promised Wren.

*You don't touch anything*.

He knelt down on the grey ground at the edge of the gold. Close to the line. Close to Nan Ashby. Close enough that he could have reached out and laid his hand on it.

He didn't. Not yet.

He opened himself. Carefully. The way Malachi had taught him, years ago, in a cave in the Wastes. *Listen first. Listen before you ask.*

He listened.

---

Flame first.

He held his hand out over the gold. Not touching. A finger's width above. And let the fire come up in him. Not much. A warmth. The way you'd hold your hand over a pan to see if it was hot.

He let it out.

Heat poured down off his palm onto the gold grass. He could feel it. Could feel the air between them go thick and shimmering. Enough to warm iron. Enough to soften lead. Enough to make gold, real gold, the gold in a ring or a coin, go soft as butter and run.

Nothing happened.

Not *nothing*. Worse than nothing. The heat went down into the gold grass and *stopped*. Didn't sink in. Didn't spread. Didn't warm it at all. As if he'd poured water onto glass. It just sat on the surface, and then slid off, and was gone into the cold air.

He pressed harder. More heat. Real heat. Forge heat. White.

The gold grass didn't change. It stayed exactly the temperature it had been. A cold morning in late winter. A Tuesday. Nine days ago.

*[It's not taking it*,] said Ember. *[Lex. It's not—it won't take heat. It's not hotter or colder. It's just the same. It's the same as it was.]*

*I know*.

*[Gold takes heat. Every metal takes heat. That's what metal is. That's the whole point of metal.]*

*I know*, thought Lex. *It's not metal.*

---

Stone next.

He put his palm flat on the grey ground beside the gold. Not on it. Beside it. And reached down. Into the earth. The way he did every morning, half without thinking, the way you'd reach out in the dark for a wall you knew was there.

The Stone answered. It always did. Grey. Old. Patient. The cracked dead ground of the Sundered Lands, which had been dead for nine hundred years and was used to it.

He reached sideways. Under the line. Into the ground under the gold.

And felt nothing.

Not stone. Not soil. Not anything. Not even absence. He knew absence. He knew the Hollow, the room, the space where new things came in. This wasn't that. This was the opposite of that.

This was *full*.

Full the way a block of iron is full. Solid all the way through. No gaps. No cracks. No grain. No room for anything. Not a breath. Not a seed. Not a thought.

He reached for the Hollow. The new one. The seventh. The thing he'd only learned that autumn, kneeling by his wife with his hand on her belly, feeling his daughter turn over inside her.

*Make room*, he asked it. Very gently. *Here. Just a little.*

And the Hollow, which made room in everything, which had made room for a starving Well and a Moth Queen and an old man's last year, put out its hand.

And found nowhere to put it.

---

He sat back on his heels.

He was shaking. He didn't know when he'd started. His hands. His knees. A deep cold tremor, coming up from somewhere in his middle.

"*It's not metal*," he said. Out loud. To nobody. To Roole, who'd come up behind him. To Nan Ashby, who hadn't moved. "*It's not anything. It's just—*"

He didn't have the word.

*[Kept*,] said Ember.

He looked up.

*[It's kept*,] said Ember. Very softly. *[Like a thing in a jar. Like a fly in amber. Like a flower pressed in a book. It's not dead. It's not changed. It's just kept. Exactly as it was. The moment the dust touched it.]* A pause. *[Forever.]*

Lex looked at the boy. At the gold eyes in the gold face. Slowly. Slowly. Turning to look at his mother.

*He's not dead*, he thought.

*[No*.]

*He's not hurt*.

*[No. Nothing can hurt him. Nothing can touch him. He'll never be hungry. He'll never be cold. He'll never get old. He'll never die.]* Ember's voice was very quiet. *[He'll never take that step, Lex. Or whistle for his dog. Or go home for his supper.]*

---

"*Can you get him out*?" said Nan Ashby again.

Lex looked at her.

She'd turned her face from her son at last. She was looking at Lex. With her tired, cried-out eyes. Not hoping. She'd stopped hoping somewhere around the fourth day. Lex could see it. Just asking. Because she had to ask. Because someone had to, and there was nobody else.

"*I don't know*," said Lex.

She nodded. Slowly. As if that was the answer she'd expected.

"*I'll find out*," said Lex.

She looked at him for a long moment.

"*Everyone says that*," said Nan Ashby.

And turned back to her son. And went on talking. About the hens. About the leak in the byre roof. About Mad Annie, and her cat, and the price of wool in Westerling market. In a low, steady, patient voice. The way you talk to someone who's sick in bed.

Just in case.

---

He stood up.

Roole was beside him. Not looking at him. Looking at the boy.

"*There's something else*," said Roole. "*Next to him. By his foot. You'll want to see it before you see the crack.*"

---

It was a rose.

Lex saw it as he came along the edge of the gold. Saw it and stopped dead. Because it was the only thing for a mile in any direction that wasn't gold or grey.

It was red.

It was growing out of the gold ground beside the boy's lifted foot. Close. So close it almost touched his boot. A thin black stem, hooked with thorns, splitting the gold like a crack splits ice. Curling up into the cold air. Three leaves on it. Green. Actual green. And at the top, open, a single flower.

Dark red. Velvet. Damp. As if it had rained on it. It had not rained in the Sundered Lands for nine hundred years.

"*It came up the first morning*," said Roole. "*The morning we found him. Ada saw it. It's the only thing that's moved, out there. Apart from his eyes.*"

Lex stared at it.

And then he saw the thing tied to the stem. Under the flower. A scrap of something. Grey. Ragged. Fluttering a little in the wind.

"*Has anyone touched it*?"

"*No*," said Roole. "*Nobody's been on the gold.*"

"*Then how*—"

"*I don't know*," said Roole. "*That's why I wrote to you.*"

---

He couldn't reach it.

It was thirty paces in. On the gold. Beside the boy. He stood at the edge and looked at it and couldn't reach it.

He could have stepped on the gold. Maybe. He didn't know what it would do. Maybe nothing. Maybe it only kept things when the dust was fresh. Maybe it was only the dust on the wind that did it, not the ground it left behind.

Maybe he'd step on it, and stop, and stand there forever with one foot lifted beside a shepherd boy, and Wren would come and find him with Lark screaming the whole way.

He didn't step on it.

He called the wind instead.

---

He wasn't good with wind. He never had been. Wren was good with wind. Wren could stand on a rooftop and lean into a gale and walk across it like a bridge. Lex was a smith. He liked things you could hit.

But he'd been Answering the Gale for two years now. It knew him. It humoured him. The way a big dog humours a child.

*Please*, he asked it. *Just a little. Just there. Gently. Like a hand.*

The wind came. Off the Veil. Cold. Thin. Curling round in a little eddy, low over the gold ground. Round the boy's lifted foot. Round the black stem.

And plucked the grey scrap off it.

It came fluttering. Across the gold. Tumbling. Turning over and over in the air, light as a moth, and Lex put out his hand and it landed in his palm.

---

Silk.

Old silk. Grey-gold. So old it was coming apart in his fingers, threads unravelling from the edges. Soft. Dry. It smelled of dust. Old dust. The dust of a closed room.

And of something else. Very faint. Underneath.

Roses.

There were marks on it. Scratched. Not ink. Scratched into the weave with something sharp, so that the threads had been pushed aside to make the lines. Three letters. Square. Plain. Crooked, as if whoever made them had learned their letters a very long time ago and in a different shape.

And a fourth mark after them. A little hook. A curl.

*HES?*

---

He looked at it for a long time.

Roole was looking over his shoulder. Ada Fell beside him. Nobody said anything.

*[Hes*,] said Ember.

*Hesper*.

*[Lex*—]

*I know*.

*[That's her name. The tenth sister. The one who wouldn't drink. Your mother's—]* It stopped. *[Your—]*

*My grandmother's grandmother's grandmother*, thought Lex. *Something like that. Twenty times over.*

*[Someone on the other side is asking for her*.]

Lex turned the scrap of grey silk over in his hand. Very carefully. So it wouldn't fall apart.

*Someone on the other side*, he thought, *is asking if she's still here*.

He looked up. At the rose. At the red of it. The only living colour for a mile. Growing out of gold beside a boy who couldn't move.

*And they sent a flower to ask*.

---

The rose came away when he called it.

He hadn't meant to. He'd only meant to look. He'd reached out with the Stone, with the ground, very gently, toward the place where the black stem went down into the gold. To feel where it went. To feel what it was rooted in.

And the stem let go.

Just like that. As if it had been waiting. As if it had only been holding on until someone came. The roots slid up out of the gold, clean, with a soft dry whisper, and the wind he'd called curled round under it and lifted it, and it came across the gold to him the way the silk had come. Turning. Slowly. And he put out his hand.

He caught it by the stem. Below the thorns.

It was warm.

---

*[Given*,] said Ember. Very quietly. *[Lex. That was given. It wanted to come.]*

*Who to*?

*[I don't know*,] said Ember. *[Not to you. I don't think it was meant for you.]* A pause. *[I think it was meant for her.]*

He looked at the rose in his hand. Dark red. Warm. Smelling, very faintly, of summer.

*[Your mother*,] said Ember. *[I think someone sent your mother a rose.]*

---

"*The crack*," said Roole. Behind him. Quietly. "*Come on. While there's light.*"

---

They went round.

The long way. Along the edge of the gold, north, then west, keeping to the grey. Two miles. Roole first, with his stiff arm. Then Lex, with the rose in one hand and the scrap of silk folded inside his shirt against the bell. Then Ada Fell. The other Keepers stayed with Nan Ashby. Roole said someone always did.

The gold was narrower here. A tongue of it running west, toward the Veil, a hundred yards wide. And at the end of the tongue, at the very foot of the grey, where the gold was brightest—

The crack.

---

It was small.

That was the first thing. He'd imagined it, all the way across the bare lands, and he'd imagined something big. A tear. A rent. A great black wound in the edge of the world.

It wasn't. It was a line. A dark seam, low down, right at the bottom of the Veil where the grey met the ground. No longer than he was tall. No wider than his finger.

You could have missed it. If you didn't know where to look. If it wasn't for the gold.

The gold came out of it.

Slowly. Like sand in an hourglass. A thin trickle of grey-gold dust, sifting out of the dark seam onto the ground in a soft little heap, and the wind picking it up, a few grains at a time, and carrying them east. Lex watched a grain land on a grey stone at the edge of the tongue. Watched the stone go gold. Not all at once. From the point where the grain landed, outward. Like a drop of ink in water. In the time it took to draw a breath.

And then it was a gold stone. And it would be a gold stone forever.

---

And there was something else.

In the crack. In the dark of it. Pushing.

Black. Hooked. Thin as wire. Stems. Dozens of them. Curling out of the seam, lying along the foot of the grey, digging into the cold ground with their hooked thorns. As he watched, one of them moved. Slowly. Pushing deeper into the edge of the crack. Like a root pushing into a crack in a wall.

Widening it.

He couldn't see it widen. It was too slow. But he could feel it. Through the Stone. A tiny groan in the ground. A creak. Like a door being pushed on its hinges by a draught.

*[Thorns*,] said Ember. *[The Thorn-Crowned. Lex. Thorns for the one who listened.]*

Lex looked down at the rose in his hand. At the black stem. At the thorns on it.

The same thorns.

---

"*Don't go closer*," said Roole.

Lex went closer.

Not onto the gold. The gold was thinner here, near the seam; the wind carried most of the dust away east before it settled. There was a strip of grey ground running right up to the foot of the Veil, a few feet from the crack. Lex walked along it. Slowly. Carefully. Until he was standing at the foot of the edge of the world.

The Veil hung in front of him. Grey. Moving. Close enough to touch.

He'd been this close once before. Last spring. With Wren. Looking for Fenn. He'd put his hand out then, the way you put your hand out to a fire, without thinking, and the Veil had pushed. Gently. Firmly. *Not you. Not ever.* The way it pushed everyone.

He put his hand out now.

It didn't push.

---

He stood there with his hand an inch from the grey.

Nothing. No push. No pressure. No *not you*. It hung there in front of his palm, rippling slightly, and did nothing at all. As if he wasn't there. As if he was air.

*[It doesn't know you*,] said Ember. Very quietly. *[Lex. It's not pushing. It doesn't know you.]*

*It pushed me last spring*.

*[Last spring you were the Worldforger. Last spring you'd woven the net. It knew you by that. By the Vey. By the net in you.]* A pause. *[It's looking at the other half now. Since the crack. Since the blood. It's looking at the Harrow.]*

*And it doesn't know the Harrow*.

*[Nobody does*,] said Ember. *[That was the whole point. Nine hundred years of never staying anywhere long enough to be looked at.]*

Lex looked at his hand. An inch from the grey.

He could put it through. He knew it. He could put his hand through the edge of the world the way you put your hand through a curtain. And on the other side—

"*Vey*," said Roole. Behind him. Very sharp. "*Step back.*"

He stepped back.

---

He was glad, later, that he had.

Because as he stepped back, the dust at the mouth of the crack *moved*.

Not the trickle. Not the slow sifting heap. The heap itself. All at once. As if someone had breathed on it from inside. It lifted. It rose up off the ground in a little cloud, grey-gold, glittering, no bigger than a man's head.

And didn't blow away.

The wind was blowing east. Hard. Cold. Off the Veil. The dust should have gone with it. It didn't. It hung in the air in front of the crack, turning slowly, holding together, against the wind.

And then it rose.

And grew.

And became a shape.

---

Thin.

That was all he could think. Thin. Thin as a rake. Thin as a gallows. A tall bent grey shape made of dust, standing in the air at the foot of the Veil, its edges shifting and smoking, its arms like bundles of dead twigs. A long narrow head. A suggestion of a coat. Of a chain round the neck, gone black.

And two eyes.

Dim. Barely lit. Two tiny sparks in the dust. Like the ends of pipes glowing in a dark room.

They looked at Lex.

At his face. At the mark on his cheek.

And the dust-shape bowed.

---

It was a very good bow.

Low. Courteous. Old-fashioned. One twig-arm folded across its middle and the other held out to the side, the way servants bowed in old paintings when the king came into the room. It held it for a long moment.

Then it straightened.

"*Smith*," it whispered.

Its voice was dust. Dry and soft and hissing, like sand poured slowly out of a sack. It came from everywhere and nowhere.

"*His Majesty sends his greetings*," whispered the dust. "*And his condolences.*"

Lex didn't move.

"*For your father*," said the dust. "*He hears your father is very old. He hears it won't be long.*" The dim sparks of its eyes flickered. "*His Majesty is very sorry. He knows what it is to sit by a bed.*"

"*Who are you*?"

"*Nobody*," whispered the dust. "*A steward. A servant. Dust.*" It bowed again. Shorter. "*My name is Orrin. I carry His Majesty's gifts.*"

"*What gifts*?"

The dust-shape didn't answer.

It looked past Lex. East. Toward the gold. Toward the field. Toward the forty gold sheep, and the one tall thin gold shape among them, and the woman sitting on the grey ground beside the line with a basket, talking.

And Lex understood.

---

He moved before he'd decided to.

Fire. All of it. Not the careful heat he'd poured on the gold grass. Not the little warmth over a pan. The real thing. The Flame, Commanding, the way he'd called it on the rubble of Duskhold on Lantern Night, up out of his chest and down his arm and out of his hand in a roar of white and gold.

It hit the dust-shape full in the middle.

And went through it.

The dust scattered. Of course it did. It was dust. It blew apart in a burst of glittering grey-gold, like a dandelion clock under a child's breath, and the fire went on through the place where it had been and hit the Veil behind it.

The Veil didn't care. It took the fire the way a lake takes a dropped stone. A ripple. Then nothing.

And the dust—

The dust came back together.

Ten paces east. On the wind. Laughing.

---

It was a horrible laugh. Dry. Soft. Hissing. Like the sound of a sack of sand being dragged across a floor.

"*Oh, smith*," it whispered. "*Fire. Of course. You're the smith.*" The dim sparks of its eyes were very bright. "*You can't burn dust, smith. You can only make more of it.*"

It bowed one more time. Very low. Very courteous.

"*Give my regards to your mother*," said Orrin. "*And to the child.*"

And let go.

The dust came apart. All of it. All at once. Into a thin glittering stream that went up into the grey sky, high, higher, and caught the wind, and went east. Over the gold. Over the field. Over Roole and Ada Fell standing frozen on the grey ground, and Nan Ashby, who looked up, and the boy, whose gold eyes moved, slowly, to follow it.

East.

Toward the Blightmarch. Toward the shires. Toward the Vale.

---

On the cord round Lex's neck, against his breastbone, under the folded scrap of grey silk—

The little silver bell with the crooked clapper rang.

Once.

Flat.

*Ting*.

---

He stood at the foot of the Veil with a rose in his hand and listened to it fade.

*[Lex*,] said Ember.

*I know*.

*[He said the child*.]

*I know*.

*[He's going east. He's going toward*—]

"*I know*," said Lex. Out loud.

And turned. And ran. Back along the strip of grey. Past Roole. Past Ada Fell. Toward Greyfall. Toward the high pasture behind the fort where a bronze dragon lay curled round a boulder, waiting for two sheep.

He didn't stop for the sheep.

Vharuun forgave him, eventually.
