# The Ferryman's Fare

His mother had never flown on a dragon by choice.

She'd flown on Vharuun to Highcrest because there was no other way to get an old man in a wheeled chair a hundred miles in a morning. She'd sat behind Lex with her arms locked round his waist and her eyes shut and her face pressed into his back, and when they landed she'd climbed down very carefully onto the grass and stood there for a while with her hand on the dragon's flank, breathing.

*I'm a hedge-witch*, she'd said. When Antho asked if she was all right. *We walk. We've always walked. Twenty generations of walking. My feet know where they are. My feet don't know where they are up there.*

She was sitting behind him on Ashka now. Her arms round his waist. Her eyes open.

She'd said she was going to keep them open.

"*It's beautiful*," she said. Into the wind. Into his ear. Her voice tight. "*Isn't it. Everyone keeps saying it's beautiful.*"

"*It is beautiful*."

"*I'm sure it is*." Her arms tightened. "*I'm going to look at it now. Any moment.*"

---

They'd left at dawn.

From the clearing at Harrow. Ashka standing in the wet grass by the stream, steaming, impatient, her blue-grey scales beaded with dew. Antho on her neck. His mother on the oak ramp of the blue-door cottage, barefoot, with a bundle in her arms and her face very still.

And in the doorway behind her, in his chair, with a blanket round his shoulders and his white wisp of hair standing up in the morning draught—

"*Go*," said Arion.

"*Four days*," said Seren. "*Maybe five.*"

"*I know*."

"*Lex will come across. Every morning. And Wren. And Brannoc said he'd come up from the village every evening to—*"

"*I know*," said Arion. "*I was there when you arranged it. I was there when you arranged it again. I believe I was there the third time.*" His mouth twitched. "*Go, love. It's your mother.*"

She didn't move.

She stood on the ramp with her bundle and looked at him. At the old man in the chair. And Antho, on the dragon, saw her face do the thing it did sometimes now. The thing it hadn't done in the first months. A kind of flinch. A kind of catch. As if she were looking at something she'd just noticed was very fragile, and had been holding very carelessly.

"*Don't die while I'm gone*," said Seren Harrow.

"*I'll try very hard not to*," said his father.

"*Promise*."

"*I don't make promises I can't keep*," said Arion Vey. "*You taught me that. Page nine.*" He looked at her. "*I'll be at the window. Every evening. Looking west.*"

She came back up the ramp. And bent. And kissed him. On the forehead. Quickly. Fiercely. Like someone stamping a seal.

"*Bring me back a stone*," said his father. Into her hair. "*From the river. A flat one. For skipping.*"

---

West over the Vale. Over the Greyspine. Over the Teeth.

The great white jagged peaks at the western end of the mountains rose up ahead of them in the morning and Ashka climbed to meet them, beating hard, higher and higher, into the thin cold air where the wind came off the snowfields like knives. His mother made a sound into his back. A small sharp sound. Not a scream. A Harrow woman's sound. The sound of someone who has decided not to scream and is holding it in with her teeth.

And then they were over. And the land fell away on the other side, long and low and grey-green, toward the west.

"*Oh*," said Seren.

He felt her lift her head.

---

The thinned lands had changed again.

He'd been this way a year ago. With Pim. Looking for the same river. It had been coming back then. A faint patchy green on the hills, like wishful thinking. Streams beginning to talk.

It was greener now. Not lush. Not the deep wet green of the Vale. But *green*. Unmistakably. A pale spring green on every hill, in every hollow. Grass. Real grass. And here and there, in the lee of a slope, something darker. Gorse. Heather. A thorn tree, low and twisted, with white blossom on it.

And the streams were singing.

You could hear them from the air. Faint. Silver. Running down off the hills into the long valleys.

"*It's alive*," said his mother. Into his ear. Wondering.

"*It's coming back*," said Antho. "*Since the Tithe stopped. Since the net was rewoven.*" He looked down at the green. "*It was grey for nine hundred years. All of it. The world was worn through here. Like the elbow of a coat.*"

"*And now it's mending*."

"*And now it's mending*."

She was quiet for a while. Her arms round his waist. Her chin on his shoulder. Looking down.

"*I walked through here*," she said. "*Thirteen years ago. To the river. I don't remember it being grey.*" A pause. "*I don't remember it being anything. I don't remember anything about that walk at all.*"

He didn't say anything.

"*I remember the river*," said Seren. "*And the boat. And the price.*" She was very quiet. "*And then I remember turning round.*"

---

They came down at dusk in a fold of the hills, by a stream.

Ashka found a warm hollow out of the wind and curled up in it like a cat and was asleep before they'd got the packs off her. Antho made a fire. His mother made supper. Bread and cheese and two apples and a little flask of something Brannoc had pressed on her at the last moment, which turned out to be Mother Orla's plum brandy and which she took one sip of and then put away, coughing.

They sat by the fire in the dark.

The stream ran. Ashka snored. Overhead, the stars came out. More of them than he ever saw in the Vale. Hard and white and close.

"*You came this way*," said his mother. "*Didn't you. To the river. Before.*"

"*Twice*. *Three times now.*"

"*Lex told me*." She didn't look at him. She was looking at the fire. "*He said you paid. The first time. To cross.*"

Antho didn't answer for a moment.

"*A stone*," he said.

---

He told her.

He'd never told anyone, really. Not all of it. He'd told Lex the truth of it, once, under the stars, on the way back from the Heart. Lex had been there; Lex had been the other half of it. But he'd never told it from the beginning.

A summer evening. A riverbank below the mill at Harrow. A boy of fifteen and a boy of ten. A flat grey stone.

*Low, low, like you're trying to make it fly under a door*.

Five skips. He'd told his brother it was four. Because he was fifteen and Lex was ten and he'd wanted, for once, just once, to be the one who was better at something.

And then the Kindling. And the fire. And eight years of madness. And a grey river and a man with no eyes asking for the memory he'd least wish to lose.

"*It wasn't the fire*," said Antho. "*Or the Kindling. Or Mam going into the dark.*" He looked at his hands. "*I'd have paid any of those gladly. I'd have paid every one.*" He swallowed. "*It was the stone. It was the one good thing I had from before. The last time I was just his brother.*"

His mother didn't say anything.

"*It's a hollow*," said Antho. "*Where it used to be. Warm. Empty. Like a bed someone's just got out of.*" He looked at the fire. "*I can tell you about it. I know what happened. Lex told me, and I've told it back to him a hundred times. But I can't*—" He stopped. "*I can't* see *it.*"

---

She was quiet for a long time.

Then she reached across. And took his hand. His scarred hand, with the old burns on it from the night of the fire, from the night of the Kindling, from eight years of a hunger burning through it.

"*Same*," said Seren Harrow.

He looked at her.

"*Same*," she said again. Very softly. "*A warm hollow. Like a bed someone's just got out of.*" She held his hand. "*I lie in it every night, love. Next to him. I lie in the hollow where thirty years of him used to be. And I know he was there. I know it was warm. I can feel the shape of it.*" Her voice cracked. "*And I can't see a single thing.*"

They sat by the fire with their hands together.

Neither of them said anything else for a long time.

---

The river was silver.

They saw it at noon on the second day. From the top of the last hill. A line across the bottom of the long valley, wide and flat and slow, running north to south. Catching the light. With the sky in it.

And on the near bank, on the grey shingle, a long flat boat. Grey. Low.

And beside it, leaning on a long pole with a lit lantern on the top of it, a tall thin figure in grey robes.

Waiting.

"*He knows we're coming*," said his mother.

"*He always knows*."

"*How*?"

"*All rivers are his water*," said Antho. "*A little. He says.*" He looked down at the figure on the shingle. "*We crossed six streams yesterday.*"

---

The Ferryman turned his head as they came down the shingle.

His mother stopped.

Antho felt her stop. Felt her go still behind him, on the stones, the way she'd gone still on the oak ramp at Harrow. A hedge-witch's stillness. The stillness of a woman listening at a door.

She'd been here before. Thirteen years ago. She'd stood on this shingle and looked at this face. She didn't remember the walk. She didn't remember the land. But she remembered this.

The long thin grey face. Smooth. Old as river-stones. The mouth. The nose.

And where the eyes should be, nothing. Smooth grey skin. As if they'd never been.

"*Seren Vey*," said the Ferryman.

"*Ferryman*."

"*You have the steps*." The eyeless face tilted. Slowly. "*I can feel them in you. Twice. His and yours. Two halves of the same rain.*" A pause. "*It suits you.*"

His mother didn't say anything.

"*Anthoren Vey*," said the Ferryman.

"*Ferryman*."

"*You have come about her mother*," said the Ferryman.

---

They sat on the shingle.

The Ferryman didn't sit. He never did. He stood beside his boat leaning on his long pole with the lantern on top of it, lit, swaying very slightly in a wind that wasn't there, and turned his eyeless face toward the water.

"*Thirty-two summers*," he said. "*By your counting.*"

His mother went still.

"*She came down that hill*," said the Ferryman. "*There. Where you came. On foot. At the end of summer. With a stick, and a bundle on her back, and her feet bleeding through her boots.*" His long grey hand moved on his pole. "*She had walked three hundred miles. She had not stopped. She said, when she reached the shingle, that if she stopped she would turn round.*"

"*Mam*," said Seren. Very quietly.

"*Wenna Harrow*," said the Ferryman. "*She did not tell me her name. I knew it. I have known all of them. Every Harrow woman who ever lived. They never came to my shore, not in life. Not one of them, in nine hundred years.*" A pause. "*Except her.*"

---

"*What did she want*?" said Seren.

The eyeless face turned toward her.

"*She had a box*," said the Ferryman. "*Oak. Old. With a moth carved in the lid and crossed out.*" A pause. "*She opened it on the shingle. There. Where you're sitting. And she took out a crown.*"

Nobody spoke.

"*Small*," said the Ferryman. "*Plain. Silver. Seven points.*" His voice was very quiet. "*I knew it. I had seen it before. On the head of a girl of seventeen, standing on this shingle, watching me row her brothers and sisters into the grey.*" He was quiet. "*She was not wearing it then. She was holding it. The boy with the bare feet had just given it back to her.*"

"*Malachi*," said Antho.

"*Malachi*," agreed the Ferryman. "*He sat with her here till dark.*" The eyeless face turned toward the water. "*And nine hundred years later, her daughter's daughter's daughter, twenty times over, came down that hill with the same crown in a box, and stood where she stood, and asked me to take it across.*"

---

"*Across*," said Seren.

"*To the grey country*," said the Ferryman. "*To the Court. Where I took them.*"

"*Why*?"

The Ferryman didn't answer for a long time.

"*She had dreamed*," he said at last. "*Harrow women dream. At the end of summer. Through the grey. Through the edge. They always have. They see the Court. They see the cocoons. They see the five of them, hanging in the hall, asleep.*" His long grey hand was still on his pole. "*She had dreamed that summer that the cocoons were moving. Turning. In their sleep. The way a sleeper turns when a dream is going bad.*"

"*They were waking*," said Antho.

"*They were stirring*," said the Ferryman. "*Nine hundred years is a long time. Even for those who drank. The hunger was getting sharper. The sleep was getting thinner.*" He was quiet. "*And she had a daughter, that summer, who had come home from the city with rain in her hair. Who had met a man. Who was going to stay.*"

His mother made a small sound.

"*She knew*," said the Ferryman. Very gently. "*She knew you would stay. She knew the line would stop running. After twenty generations. She knew that the next Harrow woman would put down roots, and Kindle her sons, and let the net learn their faces.*" A pause. "*And she knew that if the Crowned woke, and came looking, a family that stayed would be very easy to find.*"

---

"*So she sent it across*," said Seren.

"*She asked me to put it where it belonged*," said the Ferryman.

"*Where*?"

"*She did not know*. *Not exactly.*" The eyeless face turned toward her. "*She said, the chair of the one who refused it. She said, there must be a place for her there. For the tenth. Even if she never came. Somebody must have kept a place.*" A pause. "*She said, put it there. Put it in the middle of them. So there's one crown in that Court that says no. So every night, in the dark, while they sleep, something in that country is saying no to them.*"

Antho stared at him.

"*She thought it would keep them asleep*," he said.

"*She hoped it would*," said the Ferryman. "*She was not sure. She had only a dream and a song and a crown in a box.*" He was quiet. "*She was right. They slept. Thirty years. More soundly than they had in nine hundred.*"

---

"*And was there*?" said Seren. "*A place?*"

The Ferryman was quiet for a long moment.

"*There was a tower*," he said.

His mother's breath went out of her.

"*Small*," said the Ferryman. "*Grown, not built. Out of a white tree, coaxed up and round on itself. At the edge of the plain, far from the palaces. Dead. Grey. Empty.*" A pause. "*The fourth sister grew it. In the first year. When they still had power. For the tenth. In case she changed her mind.*"

"*Rosie*," whispered Seren.

"*Rhosyn*," said the Ferryman. "*Yes.*" He turned his face toward the water. "*There was a room at the top. A girl's room. Grown out of the wood. A bed. A shelf. A window. A chair.*" A long silence. "*I put it on the chair.*"

---

"*She woke*," he said.

They waited.

"*The fourth sister*. *As I came down the stair. She was standing in the doorway at the bottom. Thin. Grey. In her rotten silk. With her crown of thorns.*" His voice was very quiet. "*She had felt it. Through her sleep. Through the silk. A crown that had never touched the pool, carried into the Court by a man she had last seen nine hundred years ago, on a shingle, rowing her away from her sister.*"

"*What did she do*?" said Antho.

"*She looked at me*," said the Ferryman. "*For a long time. And then she looked up the stair.*" A pause. "*And she wept.*"

His mother had her hands over her mouth.

"*She asked me*, *is it hers. And I said, it is. And she asked me, is she coming. And I said, no. She has been dead eight hundred and seventy years. A Harrow woman sent it. One of her line.*" The eyeless face turned. "*And the fourth sister said, she had a line? And I said, twenty generations. And she sat down on the step at the bottom of the tower and put her face in her hands.*"

Nobody spoke.

"*When I left*," said the Ferryman, "*she was growing thorns. Round the tower. Round and round. As fast as she could. Her hands in the dust.*" A pause. "*I asked her why. She said, so Oz never finds it. So nobody ever finds it. So it can sit there and say no forever.*"

---

The river ran.

Silver. Slow. With the sky in it.

His mother sat on the shingle with her hands over her mouth and looked at the water.

"*And Mam paid you*," she said at last. Through her fingers.

"*She paid me*."

"*With what*?"

The Ferryman turned his eyeless face toward her.

"*You know with what*," he said.

---

"*Tell me anyway*."

He was quiet for a long moment.

"*The price is the same for everyone*," said the Ferryman. "*To cross, or to carry. The memory you would least wish to lose.*" His long grey hand was still on his pole. "*She stood on the shingle with the crown in her hands and I asked her. And she did not answer for a long time. And then she said*—"

He stopped.

"*Said what*?"

"*She said, I'll pay you the whistler*," said the Ferryman.

---

His mother didn't move.

She sat on the grey stones with her hands pressed over her mouth and her eyes wide and wet, looking at the eyeless face.

"*A summer*," said the Ferryman. Very quietly. "*Twenty years before. A tinker. On the road. Who came to the cottage door to mend a pan.*" A pause. "*Who could whistle a blackbird down out of a tree. Who stayed for a season, the way men sometimes did, for Harrow women. Who went on down the road at the end of summer, whistling.*" The eyeless face was very still. "*Who never knew he'd left a daughter behind.*"

Seren made a sound.

"*She loved him*," said the Ferryman. "*Not for long. A summer. But all the way down.*" A pause. "*She had loved him every day for twenty years. Quietly. The way Harrow women love. Without ever saying.*" His long grey hand moved on his pole. "*She would have least wished to lose him. So that was the price.*"

"*What was his name*?" said Seren.

---

The Ferryman didn't answer.

"*Please*," said his mother. Her voice was breaking. "*Please. You know it. You have it. It's in your river. His name. His face. His whistle.*" She took her hands away from her mouth. Her face was wet. "*He was my father. I never knew him. Mam never—she couldn't—she didn't*—"

"*I cannot*," said the Ferryman.

"*Please*."

"*I cannot*, *Seren Vey.*" His voice was very gentle. "*It was hers. She paid it. It is mine now. It is not yours to ask for and it is not mine to give.*" A pause. "*The river takes what it is owed. I do not give refunds.*"

She bent her head.

"*But I will tell you one thing*," said the Ferryman.

She looked up.

---

"*I rowed her*," he said. "*Your mother. A year later. When she died. In a cottage in your Vale, with you holding her hand.*"

Seren went very still.

"*She came down to my shore*," said the Ferryman. "*The way they all do, in the end. Old men. Young women. Everyone.*" His eyeless face turned toward the boat. "*And she got in. And sat in the bow. Facing back. Toward the world. The way they all do.*" A pause. "*And I pushed off.*"

He was quiet for a moment.

"*And all the way across*," said the Ferryman, "*she whistled.*"

---

His mother's face broke.

Antho saw it. Saw it go. Not like water in a bucket. Not like a dandelion. Like ice on a pond in spring. All at once. Cracking. From the middle out.

"*She did not know the tune*," said the Ferryman. "*She said so. Halfway across. She stopped whistling and she said, I don't know what this is. I don't know where I learned it.*" He was quiet. "*And then she started again. And she whistled it all the way to the far shore.*"

He turned his eyeless face toward Seren.

"*It was a very good whistle*," said the Ferryman. "*It could have whistled a blackbird down out of a tree.*"

---

They didn't say anything for a long time.

His mother sat on the shingle and cried. Not loudly. Not the way she'd cried in the dark, which Antho had heard, once, through a black glass door, and never wanted to hear again. Quietly. Steadily. With her face in her hands and her shoulders shaking. The way you cry when something you've been carrying for a long time is finally put down.

Antho put his arm round her. She leaned into him. Small. Square. Shaking.

The Ferryman stood beside his boat and waited.

He was very patient. He had been here since before the world was made.

---

"*Can you take us across*?" said Antho at last.

The eyeless face turned toward him.

"*To the Court*," said Antho. "*To the tower. To fetch it.*"

"*No*," said the Ferryman.

"*We'd pay*."

"*It is not the price*." The Ferryman's voice was very quiet. "*I told the tenth sister the same thing. On this shingle. Nine hundred years ago.*" His long grey hand moved on his pole. "*My boat carries two kinds of passenger to the grey country. Those whose time is done. And those who drank.*" A pause. "*You are neither.*"

"*You carried a crown*."

"*A crown is not a person*," said the Ferryman. "*A crown has no time to be done. A crown has no room in it to be pushed against.*" The eyeless face tilted. "*The grey country is the country of those who drank, Anthoren Vey. It was made of their drinking. It is full. All the way through. Like the gold your brother found at the edge.*" A pause. "*Anyone with room in them, it pushes. Slowly. Firmly. The way the edge pushed the four who came apart.*"

"*Hesper would have come apart*," said Antho.

"*She would have*. *In a day. Perhaps two.*" The Ferryman was quiet. "*She was full of room. She was the only one of the Ten who was.*"

---

"*Then how*," said Antho.

The Ferryman turned his eyeless face toward the west. Toward the far side of the river. Toward the grey.

"*There is a crack in the edge*," he said. "*You know there is. Your brother has seen it. A finger wide. Opened by Harrow blood.*" A pause. "*Harrow blood would open it further. The edge does not know it. The edge does not push it.*"

"*And on the other side*?"

"*On the other side, the country pushes*," said the Ferryman. "*As I said. You would come apart. Slowly. Unless—*"

He stopped.

"*Unless*?" said Antho.

The eyeless face turned back toward him.

"*Unless you carried room with you*," said the Ferryman. "*Your own. Enough to stand in. Enough to breathe.*" A pause. "*There is a pool in a green field three hundred miles east of here, Anthoren Vey. Full of room. The fullest room in the world. With a girl on a crutch who keeps it.*" The long grey face was very still. "*Room can be carried. In a cup. Someone once made cups for carrying silence. Horseshoe iron, with a word inside. I believe you know them.*"

Antho stared at him.

"*Hush-cups*," he said.

"*I do not know what you call them*," said the Ferryman. "*I know what they are.*"

---

His mother had lifted her head.

Her face was wet. Her eyes were red. She looked at the Ferryman for a long time.

"*If I go*," she said. "*Across. Through the crack. To fetch it.*"

"*Mam*—"

"*If I go*," said Seren Harrow. Not looking at Antho. Looking at the Ferryman. "*Will I come back?*"

The Ferryman turned his face toward her.

"*You will not go*," he said.

She stared at him.

"*You will not go*," said the Ferryman again. Very gently. "*Not across. Not this year.*" A pause. "*You will be at a window. Every evening. Looking west.*"

---

Antho felt his mother go still.

All of her. All at once. The way she went still at doors.

"*Why*?" she said.

The Ferryman didn't answer for a long time.

He stood beside his boat with his long grey hand on his pole and his eyeless face turned toward her, and the lantern on the top of the pole swayed, very slightly, in a wind that wasn't there.

"*I told your son*," he said at last. "*Last winter. At the pool. That I would wait.*" A pause. "*I am very patient. I have been here since before the world was made.*"

"*Arion*," said Seren.

"*I will come for your husband*," said the Ferryman, "*before the year turns.*"

---

Nobody moved.

The river ran. Silver. Slow. A long way off, on the hill behind them, Ashka shifted in her sleep, and grumbled, and was still.

"*When*?" said Seren. Her voice was barely there.

"*I do not know*," said the Ferryman. "*I never know. Not exactly. I only know before.*" His long grey hand moved on his pole. "*The little one gave him room. Ordinary room. A year. Perhaps two.*" A pause. "*It is not two.*"

His mother put her hand flat on the shingle.

As if the ground had moved. As if she needed something to hold on to.

"*Before the year turns*," she said.

"*Before the year turns*."

"*Before Lantern Night*?"

The Ferryman didn't answer.

He didn't need to.

---

"*Can I pay*?" said Seren.

The eyeless face turned toward her.

"*For my memories*," she said. Very fast. As if she had to get it all out before she lost her nerve. "*Of him. The ones I paid you. Thirty years. Before he goes.*" Her hand was flat on the stones. White at the knuckles. "*You said a love for a love. You said that was the price. You told Antho. Last spring.*"

"*I did*."

"*Then I'll pay it*," said Seren Harrow. "*Tell me what. Tell me whose. I'll pay it.*"

---

The Ferryman was quiet for a long time.

"*A love*," he said at last. "*Not yours for him. That is what you would be buying.*" A pause. "*Another love. Whole. Given freely. By the one who holds it.*"

"*Mine*," said Seren. "*For—for my sons. For*—"

"*Would you*?" said the Ferryman.

She stopped.

"*Would you give me your love for your sons*," said the Ferryman. Very gently. "*All of it. To have him back. For a few months. Before he goes.*" A pause. "*You would not remember loving them. You would look at them the way you looked at him in the cold room at the Keep. Kindly. Politely. As strangers with nice voices.*"

His mother's face was white.

"*Would you*?" said the Ferryman.

She didn't answer.

She couldn't. He watched her try. Her mouth opened. Closed. She looked at Antho. At her son, sitting beside her on the shingle, with his arm round her. With his father's eyes and his mother's face.

And she couldn't.

---

"*Mine*," said Antho.

They both looked at him.

"*Mine*," he said again. His voice was shaking. He didn't let it stop him. "*For—*" He didn't know. He didn't know what to say. He thought of Seraphine. Of her spectacles. Of a list five hundred and sixty-three reasons long. "*For anything. For anyone. Whatever you want. So she can have him back.*"

"*Antho, no*—"

"*Yours is for a woman with ink on her fingers*," said the Ferryman. "*Is it not. And a list.*" The eyeless face turned toward him. "*Would you give me that, Anthoren Vey? Would you go home to her and look at her across the kitchen table and not know why she was smiling at you?*"

Antho opened his mouth.

And couldn't.

He sat on the shingle and thought of Seraphine, at the top of the passage stair, writing in her book. *Reason five hundred and sixty-two. He counts the lanterns every night.*

And couldn't.

---

"*It cannot be taken*," said the Ferryman. Very gently. To both of them. "*It must be given. And it must be the giver's to give.*" A pause. "*And it must be someone who could bear to give it.*"

He looked at them both. With no eyes.

"*Think carefully*," said the Ferryman. "*Both of you. When I come for him, someone will ask me for a price. Someone always does. At the very end, at the edge of the water, someone always asks.*" His long grey hand was still on his pole. "*And I always collect.*"

---

He was gone when they looked up.

Of course he was. Antho had stopped expecting anything else. There was the boat on the shingle. There was the river. There was the long valley and the green hills and the sky.

No Ferryman. No lantern.

Just a flat grey stone. On the shingle. Where he'd been standing.

His mother picked it up.

It was very smooth. River-smooth. Flat as a coin. Exactly the right size to fit between your finger and your thumb.

The kind of stone you'd skip.

---

They flew home.

Two days. Over the green hills. Over the Teeth. His mother behind him with her arms round his waist and her eyes open, all the way. She didn't say much. Neither did he.

On the second evening, over the Greyspine, with the sun going down red behind them and the Vale opening up below, long and green and gold, she started whistling.

Very softly. Into the wind. So softly Antho barely heard it.

She'd never been able to whistle. She'd told him that. When he was small. *Your da whistles*, she used to say. *Not me. Harrow women can't. We don't have the lips for it.*

She was whistling now.

Badly. Breathily. Missing notes. A tune he didn't know. Slow, and a little sad, and somehow very old. Like a song you'd hear a tinker whistle on a road at the end of summer.

He didn't ask her what it was.

He didn't think she knew.

---

They came down in the clearing at Harrow at dusk.

The stream was running. The birches were green, just, with the first leaves of spring. Across the water, in the east window of the other cottage, a hornlight glowed soft and gold behind its horn.

And in the west window of the blue-door cottage, a shape. Small. Bent. In a chair. With a blanket round its shoulders and a white wisp of hair.

Looking west.

His mother was off the dragon before Ashka had folded her wings.

---

Antho watched her go.

Across the clearing. Up the oak ramp. Barefoot. Fast. Almost running. With a flat grey stone in her fist.

He watched her stop in the doorway. And stand there.

He watched his father turn his head. Slowly. From the window.

He couldn't hear what either of them said.

He didn't need to.

He sat on the dragon in the dusk and watched his mother walk across the room and kneel down by the wheeled chair and put the stone in his father's hand and close his spotted fingers round it, one by one, the way you'd close a child's fingers round a coin.

And put her head down on his knees.

And stay there.

---

*Before the year turns*.

Antho sat on Ashka in the clearing until it was dark.

He thought about a love, whole, given freely, by the one who holds it.

He thought about who in the world could possibly bear to give one.

He didn't know.

He went home to the Keep the next morning and counted the lanterns. One thousand, one hundred and twelve. One of them gold.

And then he went and found Seraphine in the library and sat down beside her and didn't say anything at all, for an hour, while she wrote. Just to be sure he still knew why she was smiling.

He did.

He was very, very glad.
