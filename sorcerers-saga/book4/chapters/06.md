# Glass

Wren Ashdown had never been any good at sitting still.

She'd been a tracker at fourteen. Out on the high moors above Thistledown before dawn with a bow and a bag and a hollow belly, reading the ground for hare-tracks because if she didn't find one there'd be no supper for Tam and Robbie that night. She'd been a Pathfinder at twenty. Running rooftops in Highcrest in the rain. A Mistwalker at twenty-two, stepping off the top of the Spire into a gale and walking down it like a staircase.

She'd stood on the wind above the Blightmarch on Lantern Night, eight months gone, with her belly out in front of her like a drum, and held a hundred miles of air still while a Well came home.

And now she sat in a chair in the dark at three in the morning with a baby on her chest. And didn't move. For an hour. Every night.

She'd never been so tired in her life.

She'd never been so happy.

She hadn't known you could be both at once.

---

The loft was cold. The fire downstairs had burned low hours ago and the heat didn't rise this far when it was banked. She'd pulled the quilt off the bed and wrapped it round both of them, her and Lark, and sat in the old chair under the little window with her feet up on the end of the bed and her head tipped back against the wall.

Lark was feeding. Steadily. Seriously. With her eyes shut and one square fist clamped round a handful of Wren's shirt and the other flung out sideways, opening and closing on nothing. She fed the way she did everything. With total concentration. As if it were a job and she meant to do it properly.

*Smith's hands*, Brannoc had said. *Smith's grip*.

*Smith's appetite*, Wren had said, the first week. Raw. Aching. Sitting up in the tent by the pool at three in the morning, the way she sat now. *She eats like a blacksmith after a double shift. She's going to eat me alive.*

Lex had laughed. Sleepily. From the blankets.

He was asleep now. On the bed. On his back. With one arm flung over his face and his mouth open.

Snoring.

---

He didn't usually snore. He didn't usually sleep. Not properly. Not since the bell rang in the winter. She'd lain beside him for six weeks and felt him lie awake. Staring at the ceiling. His hand on the bell. Listening.

He'd slept tonight.

He'd come back from the pool two days ago with his feet bare and a look on his face she'd never seen before, and told them about Elric and the Key and the iron coming down the thread. And then there'd been the dust at Seren's door. And the next night, Orrin in the village, knocking. Brannoc and Lex had stood in the street outside the Crooked Kettle till dawn with lanterns, and in the morning old Gaffer Wim's daughter had come out of their cottage by the mill white-faced and silent and wouldn't let anyone in. And the morning after that, the widow Tobbin.

Two of them. In Ashbourne. Gold. In their chairs.

He'd come home tonight grey with tired and lain down on the bed in his clothes and been asleep before she got his boots off.

She'd let him.

She'd sat in the chair with Lark and listened to him snore and thought, *good. Good. Sleep. One of us should.*

---

She was looking at the window when she saw it.

She hadn't meant to look. She'd been looking at nothing. At the dark. At the little square of the loft window under the slope of the roof, black, with the night outside and the cold coming off the glass.

You could see yourself in it. At night. With the candle lit. The way you can in any window when it's darker outside than in.

She could see herself. A pale smudge of a face. A wild red tangle of hair. The quilt round her shoulders. The dark shape of the baby at her breast. The candle-flame, small and gold, doubled in the glass.

She looked at herself.

And her reflection looked back.

And smiled.

---

Wren didn't move.

She didn't breathe. She sat in the chair with the baby feeding at her breast and her feet on the end of the bed and looked at the window.

Her reflection was smiling.

She wasn't.

She knew she wasn't. She could feel her own face. Stiff. Cold. Her mouth shut.

The face in the glass had its mouth open. A little. Smiling. Curious. Tilting its head, very slightly, the way you'd tilt your head to look at a bird on a branch.

And it wasn't her face.

---

It had been, for a moment. It had been her outline. Her hair. Her shoulders.

It wasn't now.

It was changing. Slowly. Like frost spreading on a pane. The red tangle of her hair going pale. Paler. White. The freckles fading off the cheeks. The cheeks sharpening. The jaw lengthening. The skin going so pale it was almost clear, with a faint cold glitter in it, like frost. Like ice.

And the eyes.

Grey. Pale grey. The grey of ice over deep water.

Looking at her.

Looking at the baby.

---

"*Oh*," said the face in the window. "*Is that how you do it?*"

Its voice was very small. Very far away. As if it came from the bottom of a well. Or from inside a wine glass, when someone runs a wet finger round the rim and it sings.

"*I'd always wondered*," said the face. "*We had nurses. Wet-nurses. For the household. I never watched.*" It tilted its head the other way. "*Does it hurt?*"

Wren couldn't speak.

"*It looks like it hurts*," said the Glass Queen. Interested. "*Your face does a little thing. Every time she pulls.*" A pause. "*There. Just then. You did it again.*"

---

Wren found her voice.

It came out of her very low. Very flat. The voice she used on rooftops, in the rain, when she'd seen something in the street below and didn't want it to know she'd seen.

"*Get out of my window*," said Wren.

The face smiled.

"*I'm not in your window*," it said. "*I'm in the glass. It's different.*" It looked round. At the dark loft. At the bed. At the snoring shape on it. "*I'm in all the glass. All of it. Everywhere. Do you know how much glass you have, in your world? You have so much glass.*"

"*Get out*."

"*I can't*," said Ysenne. "*Not without a door. I'm only a reflection, down here. A finger's width. That's all that fits through a crack.*" She looked back at Wren. "*But a reflection's all I need. To look.*"

She looked at the baby.

Wren's arms tightened round her.

---

Lark didn't notice. She went on feeding. Steadily. With her eyes shut.

The face in the window watched her.

For a long time. Its pale eyes very still. Its sharp face very still. Like something in a jeweller's window. Like a carving in ice.

"*So that's her*," said Ysenne at last.

Wren didn't answer.

"*Oz can smell her*," said the Glass Queen. "*All the way across the world. Through the crack. He says she smells like bread.*" A pause. "*She doesn't look like much. Does she? She looks like all the other ones. Small. Pink. Cross.*"

"*Don't look at her*."

"*Why not*?"

"*Because I'll kill you*."

The face in the window laughed. The small surprised delighted laugh of a girl in a room full of mirrors.

"*Oh, I like you*," said Ysenne. "*You're like Hes. She said things like that. She said them to Oz once, at dinner, when she was twelve, and he laughed so hard he fell off his chair.*" Her pale eyes glittered. "*He stopped laughing later.*"

---

Wren didn't move.

She sat in the chair with her daughter at her breast and her heart going like a hare's and thought. Fast. The way she thought on rooftops.

*It's glass. It's in the glass. It can see me. It can talk. Can it touch?*

*It says it's only a reflection. It says it can only look.*

*Do I believe it?*

*No*.

"*What do you want*?" she said.

---

"*To look*," said Ysenne.

"*At what*?"

"*At everything*." The face in the window tipped back. As if looking up at a ceiling only it could see. "*Do you know how long it's been? Nine hundred years. Nine hundred years in a country with no sun, where the only glass was the glass I made myself, and the only thing in it was me.*" A sigh. Like breath on a cold pane. "*I made a palace of it. A whole palace. Every wall a mirror. And every mirror had me in it. Nine hundred years of my own face.*"

She looked back down.

"*I used to love my face*," said the Glass Queen. "*I don't anymore. I've seen it too much.*" Her pale eyes went to Wren. "*I'd like to see yours for a while. I'd like to see everyone's.*"

---

"*There's a girl in Fenmoor*," said Ysenne. "*Who holds up a pan every morning to see if her hair's straight. It never is. She cries sometimes.*" A small smile. "*There's an old man in Highcrest who talks to himself in his shaving mirror. He tells himself he's handsome. He isn't. He's very kind, though. He gives his crusts to the pigeons.*"

Wren said nothing.

"*Your Queen*," said Ysenne. "*She has a mirror in her bedchamber. Silver-backed. Very old. She looks into it every night and practices being stern.*" The face glittered with amusement. "*She's not very good at it. She has a soft mouth. She keeps having to bite it.*"

"*Stop*."

"*Your husband's mother*," said the Glass Queen. Softly. "*Across the water. She hasn't got a mirror. She's never had one. Did you know? Harrow women don't keep mirrors. They don't like to be looked at.*" A pause. "*But her husband wears spectacles. To read. He leaves them on the table at night, folded, beside the bed.*"

Wren went cold.

"*She sleeps beside him on top of the blankets*," said Ysenne. "*With her bare feet cold. And watches him breathe. She lies awake for hours. Counting. Every breath.*" Her voice was very gentle. "*She's so frightened, your husband's mother. Of one morning. Of one breath that doesn't start again.*"

---

"*Why are you telling me this*?" said Wren.

"*Because it's true*," said Ysenne.

She said it simply. As if it were the only answer anyone could possibly want.

"*That's what I am*," she said. "*That's what glass is. It shows you what's there. Exactly what's there. No more. No less.*" The pale face leaned closer. To the pane. Until Wren could see the faint cold glitter in the skin, like frost, like snow under a moon. "*Oz keeps things. Cad binds them. Rosie grows them. Mae ate them.*" A smile. "*I just look. I've always just looked. Nobody ever liked it much.*"

She looked at Wren.

Really looked. Wren felt it. Like a hand laid flat on her chest. Cold. Light. Curious.

"*You're afraid*," said the Glass Queen.

---

Wren didn't say anything.

"*Not of me*," said Ysenne. Thoughtfully. "*Well. A little of me. But not mostly.*" Her pale eyes moved. Down Wren's face. Her shoulders. The quilt. The baby. "*You're afraid you've stopped.*"

"*Stopped what*?"

"*Being you*."

Wren's hands went still on the baby's back.

"*You used to run*," said the face in the window. Softly. "*On roofs. In the rain. You used to step off the top of towers into the wind. I can see it. In you. Like a picture in a locket.*" The ice-grey eyes didn't blink. "*And now you sit in a chair. In the dark. Every night. With her pulling at you. Not moving. Not running. Just sitting.*"

"*Shut up*."

"*And you love her*," said Ysenne. "*You love her so much it frightens you. And you're so tired. And you think, in the dark, at three in the morning, when he's asleep and snoring and you're not—*" A pause. "*You think, what if this is all I am now. What if the girl on the roof is gone. What if I never run again.*"

---

Wren didn't move.

She sat in the old chair with the quilt round her shoulders and the baby at her breast and her face burning and stared at the woman in the window.

It was true.

That was the thing. That was the worst thing. Every word of it. She'd never said it. Not to Lex. Not to her mother. Not to anyone. She'd barely said it to herself. In the dark. At three in the morning. When he was asleep and snoring and she wasn't.

*What if I never run again*.

"*You see*?" said the Glass Queen. Gently. Almost kindly. "*I only show you what's there.*"

---

Lark let go.

She did it all at once, the way she always did. Finished. Done. Her mouth coming off with a small wet pop and her square fist letting go of Wren's shirt and her face screwing up, red and furious, the way it screwed up every time, because she didn't like being finished. She liked the doing.

She opened her eyes.

Brown. Round. Not quite focused. Looking up past Wren's face. At the window. At the pale glittering woman in the glass.

And looked at her.

---

Ysenne went very still.

Wren felt it. The cold curious hand on her chest went away. All of it. All at once. Turned elsewhere.

The baby looked at the face in the window.

And the face in the window looked back.

For a long moment, neither of them moved. A three-month-old girl in a quilt, with milk on her chin and her hair standing up like a bird's crest. And a Queen nine hundred years old, made of glass, in a pane at the end of the world.

Then Lark frowned.

A deep, fierce, furious frown. Exactly like her father's. Exactly like the frown Lex gave the bellows when they wouldn't catch, or the porridge when it stuck, or a bad weld.

And put out one square fist. Toward the window. And opened it. And closed it.

As if she were reaching for a nail.

---

"*Oh*," said Ysenne. Very quietly.

Something had changed in her voice. Wren didn't know what. It had gone smaller. Further off. As if she'd stepped back from the glass.

"*She looked at me*," said the Glass Queen.

"*She looks at everything*."

"*No*." The pale face in the window was very still. "*No. She looked at me. Not at the glass. Not at the reflection. At* me.*" A pause. "*Nobody does that. They look at what they see. They look at themselves. They look at me and they see themselves.*"

Lark frowned harder.

"*She saw me*," said Ysenne. Wonderingly. Almost to herself.

---

And Wren breathed on the window.

---

She didn't decide it. Her body did it. The way her body did things on rooftops, before her head had caught up. She leaned forward in the chair, with the baby against her shoulder, and breathed out. Long. Slow. Warm. Onto the cold black glass of the little loft window.

The way you breathe on a window on a winter morning to draw a face in it.

And the glass fogged.

---

The face in it went blurry.

All at once. The sharp cheekbones. The ice-grey eyes. The frost-glitter of the skin. Smearing. Softening. Going grey and milky and indistinct, like a face seen through a steamed-up window in a bathhouse.

"*Oh, don't*—" said Ysenne. Sharply. Far off. "*Oh, don't do that, I can't*—"

Wren breathed again.

And this time, she didn't just breathe.

---

She was a Mistwalker.

That was what the net called her. She'd never much liked the name. It sounded like something from a song. Like a ghost in a story who walks on the moors in the fog and leads travellers astray.

But it was true. It had always been true. Long before the net called it anything. On the high moors above Thistledown at dawn, with the mist lying in the hollows like milk in a bowl. She'd always known where it was. She'd always been able to walk in it without getting lost. She'd always felt it, the way other people feel cold or heat. Wet air. Waiting. Wanting to be breath.

She called it.

Not much. Not the great grey wall of it she could call over a battlefield. Just a little. Just a breath. Just the wet in the air of a cold loft at three in the morning, the damp off the stream outside, the steam off a baby's warm head.

*Here*, she told it. *On the glass. All of it. Please.*

---

The window went white.

Completely. In one breath. The little black pane under the slope of the roof went grey, then milky, then white as a winter sky. Frosted. Fogged. Every inch of it. Thick as fleece.

And there was nothing in it at all.

No face. No ice-grey eyes. No glitter. Just white. Just mist on glass. The way every window in the world looks on a cold morning before the sun gets to it.

Wren sat in the chair and breathed hard.

From very far away, from inside the white, so faint she barely heard it, so faint it might have been the wind—

"*...I can't see...*"

And then nothing.

---

She didn't stop.

She stood up. With the baby on her shoulder, wailing now, because she'd been finished and then jolted and then nobody was paying attention to her, which was the worst thing of all. Wren held her and stood in the middle of the loft and turned. Slowly. Looking.

The candle. On the shelf by the bed. Its flame doubled in the brass dish under it.

She breathed. The brass went dull with mist.

The water jug on the floor. A circle of black water in it, with the candle in it, small and gold.

She breathed. The water went cloudy. Grey.

The little hand-mirror on the windowsill. Her mother's. That Maren had given her on her wedding day, so she could see her hair was straight before she went out to the black door. Wren had laughed at her. *Mam. It's never straight.*

She picked it up. Turned it face down on the sill.

And breathed on the back of it anyway.

---

Lex woke up.

He came awake the way he always did now. All at once. Bolt upright. One hand going to the bell on the cord round his neck. The other going to the little hammer on his belt.

"*What*—"

"*Don't look at the window*," said Wren.

He looked at the window.

It was white. Fogged. Thick as fleece.

"*What*—"

"*There was a woman in it*," said Wren. She was jiggling the baby on her shoulder. Up and down. Up and down. The way you do without thinking. Her voice was very calm. She didn't feel calm. She felt like the inside of a struck bell. "*In the glass. A woman made of glass. With white hair and grey eyes and a face like a knife.*" She swallowed. "*She talked to me. She talked to me for a long time.*"

Lex was on his feet.

"*Ysenne*," he said.

"*She said everything's glass*," said Wren. "*She said she's in all of it. Every window. Every mirror. Every jug of water.*" Lark was screaming now. Properly. Furiously. Wren held her tighter. "*She said she could see the Queen practicing being stern in her mirror.*"

---

They went through the house.

Every room. Every window. Lex with the little hammer in his hand and his fire burning low and gold in the other, the way it burned when he was afraid and didn't want to show it. Wren behind him with the baby, breathing.

The kitchen window. The east one. The big deep one, with the sill broad enough to sit on.

And the lantern in it.

They both stopped.

It was lit. Of course it was. It was always lit. A small iron lantern. Plain. With *HERE* cut in the base. And on the side facing the room, and the side facing the road, and the two sides between, four small square panes.

Of glass.

---

Wren breathed on it.

The panes fogged. The little gold flame inside went soft and blurry. A smudge of light behind milk.

Lex stood looking at it.

"*I made it*," he said. Very quietly. "*I made it with glass. So you could see the flame.*"

"*I know*."

"*I put it in the window. Facing out. So anyone on the road could see.*" His voice was very strange. "*So it'd be the first thing anyone saw.*"

"*Lex*."

"*She could look out of it*," said Lex. "*Couldn't she. She could look out of our lantern. At the road. At the house. At us.*"

Wren didn't answer.

She didn't need to.

---

They didn't sleep again that night.

They hung blankets over the windows. All of them. The loft window and the kitchen window and the little one by the door. Lex nailed them up, swearing. Wren held the lantern so he could see. The lantern with its four fogged panes, the flame inside it soft and smudged, and every few minutes she breathed on it again, because the mist kept clearing and she kept being afraid that when it cleared, there'd be a face.

There wasn't.

They emptied the water jug out of the loft window into the night. And the bucket by the door. And the big copper by the hearth that Wren had filled to wash the baby's things.

They turned the brass dishes upside down. They put the mirror in a drawer. They found a polished pewter cup on the shelf that Brannoc had given them for the wedding and Lex wrapped it in a cloth and put it in the drawer with the mirror, and then stood looking at the drawer for a long time.

"*She could be in there*," he said.

"*She can't see in the dark*," said Wren. "*I don't think. A reflection needs light.*"

"*You don't think*."

"*I don't know*." She sat down at the table. Suddenly. All the tired of three months coming down on her at once like a dropped sack. "*I don't know anything. I don't know what she can do. I don't know what she can't.*"

Lark had gone quiet at last. Asleep. Screamed out. Hot and damp and heavy on Wren's shoulder, with one square fist wound in her hair.

"*She said she can only look*," said Wren. "*She said she's only a reflection. A finger's width. That's all that fits through.*" She looked at the blanket over the kitchen window. "*She said she can't touch.*"

"*Do you believe her*?"

Wren looked at him.

"*She told me the truth about everything else*," she said.

---

She didn't tell him the rest.

Not then. Not that night. Not the thing about the roofs. About running. About sitting in a chair in the dark every night and being afraid she'd never move again.

She sat at the table with the baby on her shoulder and didn't tell him.

But he looked at her. Across the table. In the light of the fogged lantern.

And she thought he knew anyway. She thought he'd always known. He was a smith. He knew what it looked like when something was cooling in the wrong shape.

He didn't say anything.

He reached across the table and put his big scarred hand over hers. On the wood. And kept it there.

---

He made the hornlight at dawn.

She watched him from the doorway of the lean-to. With the baby, asleep, in the sling. With the grey light coming up over the birch wood and the stream running loud and the blankets still nailed over every window of the cottage behind her, so that the house looked blind.

He'd lit the little forge. He'd taken the *HERE* lantern apart on the anvil. Carefully. Pane by pane. The four little squares of glass laid out on a cloth on the bench, fogged, the way she'd left them.

And he'd found horn.

Old horn. From somewhere. From a cow, from a ram, from the back of Brannoc's store-shed where Brannoc kept everything anyone had ever given him, in case. He'd boiled it. He'd split it. He'd scraped it, with the edge of a knife, thinner and thinner, until it was pale and cloudy and you could see light through it but nothing else. Not shapes. Not faces. Just light.

The way lanterns were made in the old days. Before glass. Before anyone had windows. Before there was enough glass in the world for a Queen to live in.

He fitted it. Four panes. Into the iron frame. Where the glass had been.

And then he took the little flame—the flame that had burned in the *HERE* lantern every night since the Blightmarch, the flame he'd lit with his own fire—and lifted it out on the end of a taper, and set it in the hornlight.

And it burned.

Soft. Gold. Blurred. A warm cloudy glow behind four panes of pale horn. You couldn't see the flame anymore. You could only see that it was there.

---

"*It's not as pretty*," said Lex.

He was standing at the anvil looking at it. His face grey with tired. His hands black with horn-dust.

"*No*," said Wren.

"*You can't see the flame*."

"*No*."

"*It'll look like a lantern in a fog*," said Lex. "*From the road. Like someone's left a light on in a mist.*"

Wren looked at it.

At the soft gold blur behind the horn. Warm. Steady. Not showing anything. Not reflecting anything. Just there. Just lit.

"*Good*," she said.

He looked at her.

"*I like fog*," said Wren Ashdown. "*I'm a Mistwalker. It's my favourite weather.*" She came across the lean-to. And stood beside him. And looked at the hornlight on the anvil. "*Put it in the window. I'll be able to find it in the dark.*"

---

He put it in the window.

They took the blanket down off the kitchen window, the east one, and he set the hornlight on the sill where the glass lantern had been. Facing out. Facing the road.

And then they stood back and looked at the window itself.

The big deep east window. Twelve panes of glass in a wooden frame. Grey with the dawn. Showing the birch wood. Showing the stream. Showing, faintly, the two of them standing in the kitchen with a baby between them.

Showing everything.

"*I'll soap it*," said Wren. "*All of them. Every window. Like a shop that's closed. Mam used to do it at the farm when the hay-dust got bad.*" She looked at the window. "*We won't be able to see out.*"

"*She won't be able to see in*," said Lex.

"*No*."

They stood there a moment longer.

Then Wren went to get the soap.

---

She stopped in the doorway.

She didn't know why. Something. A feeling. A cold one. The same feeling she'd had on the roof at three in the morning, looking at a window, seeing her own face smile.

She turned round.

And looked at the east window.

At the twelve grey panes. At the hornlight on the sill, glowing soft and gold behind its horn. At the birch wood beyond, grey. At the stream.

At the pane in the bottom corner. The left one. Low down. Just above the sill. Just beside where the glass lantern had stood.

There was a mark on it.

---

She went closer.

Slowly. With the baby asleep against her chest. With Lex behind her, saying *what*, and then not saying anything, because he'd seen it too.

A handprint.

Small. Narrow. Long-fingered. Pressed flat against the glass. Not dirt. Not soot. *Frost*. A handprint made of frost. Delicate. Feathery. Every line of the palm picked out in tiny white crystals, the way frost picks out the veins in a leaf. Every finger long and fine and perfect. Five of them. Spread.

As if someone had stood in the kitchen in the night and laid their hand flat on the window. To feel how cold it was. Or to look out.

Wren put her own hand up beside it. Not touching. An inch from the glass.

Hers was bigger. Broader. A tracker's hand. A farm girl's.

The frost hand was smaller. Thinner. Finer. A lady's hand. A queen's.

---

"*It's on the outside*," said Lex. Hoarsely. "*It must be. It's frost. It's cold out. Something out there*—"

Wren didn't answer.

She moved her hand. The inch. And touched the glass. Beside the frost. With one finger.

And drew it across. Slowly. Across the palm of the frost hand.

And the frost smeared.

Under her finger. On her side. On the *inside*. The crystals melting. Running. A thin wet line through the middle of the palm, where her warm finger had passed.

The handprint was on the inside of the glass.

---

Neither of them said anything for a long time.

Wren stood at the window with her wet finger and the baby asleep against her chest. Lex stood behind her. She could hear him breathing.

*She said she can only look*, Wren thought. *She said she's only a reflection. She said she can't touch.*

She looked at the small cold hand on the inside of her kitchen window. Spread. Delicate. Perfect.

*She told me the truth about everything else*.

"*Lex*," she said.

"*I know*."

"*She lied*."

"*Or she didn't know*," said Lex. Very quietly. "*Not yet. Not till she tried.*"

---

Lark woke up.

She did it all at once, the way she always did. Eyes open. Fists going. Looking round with the deep fierce frown of someone who has woken up somewhere she didn't fall asleep and would like an explanation.

She saw the window.

She saw the hand on it.

And she reached for it.

One square fist. Out of the sling. Toward the glass. Opening and closing.

Wren caught it. Gently. And held it. And folded it back against her chest.

"*No*," she said. Very softly. Into her daughter's hair. "*No, love. Not that one. That one's not for you.*"

And went to get the soap.
