# Fair Wind

Wren had been running for eighty-six days, and her daughter had learned to stand.

It happened in a shepherd's bothy on the north face of the Teeth. Stone walls. A turf roof. A hearth with no chimney, so the smoke went out through a hole in the turf and most of it stayed in. Kestrel was asleep in the corner under every blanket they owned. The wind was howling outside like something that wanted to come in.

Wren was sitting on the floor by the fire with her back against the wall and her eyes half shut.

And Lark pulled herself up.

---

She did it on Wren's hand.

The left one. The gold one. Four gold fingers, stiff, curled, set from the knuckle to the nail. Lark had been fascinated by them since the summer. She chewed them. She patted them. She held on to them when she was falling asleep, the way she used to hold on to Wren's braid.

She held on to them now.

Both square fists. Round two gold fingers. Gripping. Hard. The way she gripped everything.

And *heaved*.

---

Wren felt it. Felt the weight on her hand. Felt the small body on the floor beside her shift, and brace, and push.

She opened her eyes.

Her daughter was on her knees. On the dirt floor of the bothy. Holding Wren's gold fingers in both fists. Her face screwed up. Red. Fierce. Her bottom in the air. Her bare feet, too big for the rest of her like her hands, pushing at the dirt.

Grunting.

The way a smith grunts lifting an anvil.

And then she was up.

---

On her feet. Swaying. Her knees bent. Her whole weight on Wren's gold fingers. Her grey-green eyes enormous. Her mouth open.

Standing.

She looked at Wren.

Wren looked at her.

Neither of them breathed.

Then Lark let go.

---

She didn't fall. Not at once. She stood. On her own. In the firelight. In a shepherd's bothy on the north face of the Teeth with the wind howling outside and the smoke going round and round under the turf roof.

For one breath.

Two.

And then sat down. Hard. On her bottom. On the dirt.

And laughed.

---

It was the laugh from Ashbourne. From the roofs. From the gold. The laugh of someone who has just discovered the best thing in the world and can't believe nobody told her about it before.

Wren laughed with her.

She couldn't help it. She sat on the floor of the bothy with her daughter laughing in the dirt and laughed till she cried. And then cried till she couldn't stop.

Kestrel woke up.

She sat up in the corner under all the blankets with her red hair wild and her amber eyes blinking and looked at them. At Wren, crying. At the baby, laughing.

"*What*?" she said. "*What happened?*"

"*She stood up*," said Wren.

Kestrel looked at the baby.

"*Oh*," she said. And then, after a moment, very quietly: "*Oh, Wren.*"

"*He wasn't here*," said Wren. Through her hands. "*Lex. He wasn't here. She stood up for the first time and he wasn't here.*"

Kestrel didn't say anything.

She got up. And came across the bothy with a blanket round her shoulders. And sat down on the floor beside Wren. And put her arm round her.

"*I'll tell him*," she said. "*If you can't. I'll tell him exactly. Every bit.*"

"*It's not the same*."

"*No*," said Kestrel Varn. "*But it's what we've got.*"

---

She'd stopped counting the valleys.

She'd counted, at first. In the summer. Leaving the Vale. Going north. *One*. The long valley above Thistledown, where she'd grown up, where she knew every hare-track and every hollow. She'd stopped there one night in a barn she knew and lain awake listening to the owls and thought, *I could stay. Just a night. Just to sleep.*

She hadn't stayed.

*Two*. *Three*. *Four*. Over the passes. Into the Greyspine. Through valleys she'd never seen. Sleeping in barns and bothies and once, in the worst of the summer heat, under a rock overhang beside a waterfall with the baby asleep on her chest and Kestrel keeping watch.

Moving. Every day.

*Keep moving*, Arion had said. *Don't stop. Don't stay anywhere long. Don't let him learn your face.*

---

He'd found the trail on the third day.

She'd known he would. She'd felt it. The way you feel weather coming. A warmth. On the back of her neck. On the wind, when it came from the south. A heat that wasn't the sun.

And then the ravens. Not to her; she'd made sure no raven could find her. To the villages she passed. To the shepherds. *A gold light. Walking. Coming north.*

He was following.

Slowly. Five miles a day. Less. Stopping in every village. Sitting on every well.

She went faster.

---

She went up.

That was the thing she worked out, in the first week. Where he couldn't follow. Where there were no villages to stop in, no wells to sit on, no old women to listen to.

The high country.

Above the valleys. Above the shepherds' summer pastures. Up into the bare rock and the snowfields and the passes nobody used. Where there was nobody at all.

He'd follow her there. She knew he would. He'd follow the smell of bread on the wind anywhere.

But there'd be nobody to keep.

---

So she led him.

Up. Into the high country. Round. In great slow loops. Through the Teeth. Past them. North, into the empty land beyond, where the rivers ran white and the wind never stopped and there wasn't a house for fifty miles.

And back. And round again.

She didn't know if it was working. She couldn't see him. She never saw him. She just felt the warmth on the back of her neck, sometimes, on a south wind. Far off. Following.

And at night, sometimes, very rarely, on a clear night—

A glow. Low. On a distant ridge. Gold.

Sitting. Looking.

---

He talked to her once.

In the sixth week. On a night with no wind. She'd made camp in a hollow below a ridge, a high one, bare rock, with snow already on the tops though it was only the end of summer down in the Vale. Kestrel was asleep. Lark was asleep.

Wren was awake. She was always awake.

And the voice came down off the ridge.

---

"*Mistwalker*," said the Gilded King.

Not loud. He didn't need to be loud. It came down the slope like heat off a forge. Warm. Deep. Kind.

Wren didn't move.

She lay in the hollow with the baby on her chest under the blanket and her hand on her knife and didn't move.

"*I'm not coming down*," said the voice. "*Don't be afraid. I'm only sitting. On the ridge. I've been sitting here all evening.*" A pause. "*It's a very good view. You can see the whole of the Teeth from up here. In the moonlight. Like a row of old women in white shawls.*"

Wren didn't answer.

"*You're very good*," said Oszric of the Crowned. "*You know that? I've followed a great many things in my life. Deer. Boars. A thief who stole my mother's necklace when I was fourteen.*" A soft laugh. "*Nothing as good as you. You go where I can't stop. You go where there's no one to sit with.*"

She didn't answer.

"*It's very lonely up here*," said the Gilded King.

---

She lay in the hollow and listened to him not come down.

"*I could*," he said. After a while. "*Come down. You know I could. I could walk down this slope and you couldn't stop me. Your wind won't move me. Your arrows go gold. Your knife.*" A pause. "*I could walk down and pick her up out of your arms.*"

Wren's hand tightened on the knife.

"*I won't*," said Oszric. "*I want you to know that. I won't. Not like that.*" His voice was very gentle. "*I don't take, Mistwalker. I never have. Not once. I only help the ones who ask.*"

"*She won't ask*," said Wren.

It was the first thing she'd said. Out loud. Into the dark. To the voice on the ridge.

He was quiet for a long time.

"*No*," he said at last. "*She won't. She's Hes's. Isn't she. All the way down. Twenty generations of no.*" A pause. "*But you might.*"

"*Never*."

"*One night*," said the Gilded King. "*When she's sick. When she's burning with a fever and you've been walking for a month and there's no healer for fifty miles. When she's crying and you can't make her stop.*" Very softly. "*When you're so tired you can't see. One night, you might come up the ridge and ask me to make it so she's never sick again.*"

Wren didn't answer.

"*I'll be here*," said Oszric. "*On the ridge. Every night. In case.*"

---

She moved camp before dawn.

She didn't sleep. She lay in the hollow with the baby on her chest until the sky went grey, and then she woke Kestrel without a word and they packed and went. North. Over the next pass. Into the next valley.

He followed.

He was on the ridge above her every night after that. Not every night. Some nights. Enough. A glow on the skyline. Sitting. Watching.

He never spoke to her again.

He didn't need to.

---

Lark got sick in the ninth week.

Of course she did. They were in the high country. It was cold. It was wet. They were sleeping in bothies and under rocks and once, for three nights, in a cave with water running down the walls.

She burned.

Her face red. Her eyes glassy. Her square hands hot as coals. She cried. Not her usual cry, short and furious. A thin high wail that went on and on and wouldn't stop. She wouldn't feed. She wouldn't sleep.

Wren held her.

All night. In the cave. With the water running down the walls and Kestrel feeding the fire with everything she could find and the wind howling outside.

And at midnight, she looked out of the mouth of the cave.

And there was a glow on the ridge.

---

She didn't go.

She sat in the mouth of the cave with her burning daughter in her arms and looked at the glow on the ridge for a long time.

*When she's burning with a fever and you've been walking for a month*.

*One night, you might come up the ridge*.

She thought about it.

She let herself. For one breath. The way Seren had let herself at the blue door. *He'd make it stop. She'd never be sick again. She'd never cry like this again. She'd never*—

*She'd never grow up*.

*She'd never stand again. She stood once. In the bothy. On her own. For two breaths. And sat down and laughed.*

*She'd never do it again*.

---

"*Kes*," said Wren.

Kestrel looked up from the fire.

"*Can you do the thing*?" said Wren. "*The fire thing. The one you do. The one that isn't fire.*"

Kestrel looked at her for a long moment.

Then she came across the cave. And knelt down. And put her hands out. Not touching the baby. Near. Over her. A foot away.

And let the heat go.

Not flame. Not black heat. Something else. Something Kestrel had learned in the Sunreach from old women in red, who'd learned it from older women, who'd learned it before there was a net to call it anything. A heat that went *out*. That drew. The way a poultice draws.

Lark's wail went up. High. Higher.

And then broke.

---

She didn't stop crying. Not at once. But the wail broke. Into sobs. Into hiccups. Into a small exhausted whimper.

And then sleep.

Wren felt it. Felt the heat go out of her daughter's forehead under her hand. Not all of it. Some. Enough. Felt the small body go slack against her chest.

Breathing. Slow. Wet. Ragged. But slow.

Kestrel sat back on her heels.

Her face was grey. Her amber eyes were dull.

"*It's not a cure*," she said. Hoarsely. "*It's just—taking a bit of it. Into me.*" She swallowed. "*Asha taught me. She said it's what fire-women do. Take a bit of the burning so the little ones can sleep.*"

"*Kes*—"

"*It's all right*," said Kestrel Varn. "*I've got plenty. I'm made of it.*"

---

Wren looked out of the mouth of the cave.

At the ridge. At the glow on it.

She looked at it for a long time. With her sleeping daughter in her arms and her friend grey-faced beside her and the water running down the cave walls.

And then she lifted her left hand. The gold one. Four gold fingers. And held it up. In the mouth of the cave. So that it caught the firelight.

So that it shone.

Not waving. Just holding it up. So he'd see.

*No*.

---

The glow on the ridge didn't move for a long time.

And then it went.

Not toward them. Away. Over the ridge. Out of sight.

He didn't come back that night.

---

The fever broke on the third day.

Lark woke up hungry and furious and red-faced and bit Wren's gold finger so hard she'd have drawn blood if it hadn't been gold.

Wren laughed till she cried. Again.

She was doing a lot of that.

---

In the eleventh week, he stopped following.

She felt it. The way she'd felt him come. A change in the wind. A coldness on the back of her neck where the warmth had been.

She didn't believe it at first. She thought it was a trick. She thought he'd gone round. She went faster. Higher. Further. Over two passes in two days, with the baby on her chest and Kestrel stumbling behind.

Nothing.

No warmth on the south wind. No glow on the ridges at night.

Nothing.

---

"*He's gone*," said Kestrel.

They were in a bothy. Another one. On the edge of a high valley with a lake in it, black, with snow on the mountains all round.

"*He's not gone*," said Wren.

"*I can't feel him*. *You can't feel him. There's been nothing for four days.*"

"*He's not gone*," said Wren again. She was sitting in the doorway of the bothy looking out at the lake. At the snow. "*He's turned round.*"

Kestrel looked at her.

"*He's not following me anymore*," said Wren Ashdown. "*He's going somewhere else.*"

"*Where*?"

Wren didn't answer.

She didn't need to. She'd known since the first cold morning without the warmth on her neck. She'd known the way you know the weather's going to turn.

*Harrow women never go back*.

That was what he'd worked out. On a ridge. In the dark. Sitting. Looking at her camp in the hollow below. *She'll never stop. She'll keep moving. Twenty generations. It's in the child. I could follow her for a hundred years.*

*Harrow women never go back*.

*But their men do*.

---

"*Lex*," she said.

Kestrel went very still.

"*He's going to Harrow*," said Wren. "*To wait. On the step. For Lex. For the brothers. When they come back through the door.*" Her voice was very flat. "*He knows they'll come home. He knows they'll come to Harrow first. To find me. To find her.*"

"*And you're not there*."

"*And I'm not there*." Wren looked at the black lake. "*And he is. Sitting on the step. With the old man dying inside. Waiting.*"

---

Kestrel didn't say anything for a long time.

The wind came down off the snow and across the lake and in through the door of the bothy. Cold. Hard. Smelling of winter.

"*Arion said keep moving*," said Kestrel at last. "*He said don't stop. Don't go back.*"

"*I know*."

"*He said Harrow women never go back*."

"*I know what he said*," said Wren. She looked down at the baby asleep in the sling against her chest. At the dark fluff of hair. At the square fist curled against her collarbone. "*She's a Harrow.*"

"*And you*?"

Wren looked out at the lake for a long time.

"*I'm an Ashdown*," she said.

---

She'd never thought about it before.

Ashdown. Her father's name. A shepherd's name. From the high moors above Thistledown, where her father had kept sheep until the rock-wyrm took him, when she was eleven.

Ashdowns didn't run.

That wasn't a rule. Nobody had ever said it. It was just what they did. They stayed. On the moors. In the same cottage. For generations. They went out in the snow after lost ewes and came back. They went to market and came back. They went to war, sometimes, when the old king called, and they came back.

Her father had gone out after a ewe on a spring morning and not come back.

Her mother had sat at the window for a month. Looking at the moor. At the path. Waiting.

He didn't come.

And then her mother had got up. And gone out. And found what was left of him. And brought it home. And buried it. And stayed.

---

"*Ashdowns look back*," said Wren. "*Once. At the top of the hill. So they remember the way home.*" She looked at Kestrel. "*Seren said that. On the day I left. Brannoc told me. In a letter.*"

"*Brannoc can't write*."

"*Hedda wrote it*," said Wren. "*Brannoc told her what to say.*" She smiled. Shakily. "*It was very badly spelled.*"

---

She stood up.

In the doorway of the bothy. With the baby on her chest and the wind on her face and the black lake and the white mountains in front of her.

"*I'm going home*," said Wren Ashdown.

Kestrel looked at her.

"*He'll be there*," she said. "*On the step. With his gold. Waiting for her.*"

"*I know*."

"*He said he'd never take*. *But if you walk up to him with her on your chest*—"

"*He won't take*," said Wren. "*He said so. He doesn't take. He only helps the ones who ask.*" She looked down at her daughter. "*And she won't ask. She's Hes's. All the way down.*"

"*And if he's lying*?"

Wren didn't answer for a moment.

"*Then Lex will be there*," she said. "*And Antho. And Seren. And Brannoc.*" She looked at the mountains. South. Toward the Greyspine. Toward the Vale. "*And I'll have the wind.*"

---

"*It's four days*," said Kestrel. "*Walking. Five. Through the passes. In the snow.*"

"*I'm not walking*," said Wren.

---

She'd been walking for eighty-six days.

Not flying. Not on the wind. Walking. Because walking left a trail that looked like walking, and flying left a trail like a comet, and the King could smell a comet from a hundred miles. Because she was a tracker and trackers walk. Because the baby had been sick and the wind was cold and she'd been so tired, so very tired, and every time she called the wind she felt it in her bones afterward for days.

She called it now.

---

It came down off the mountains.

All at once. The way it came when she really meant it. Not a breeze. Not a gust. The *wind*. The north wind. Off the snowfields. Off the Teeth. Off the whole white roof of the world. Cold. Hard. Enormous. Pouring down the valley and across the black lake, so that the water went white with spray, and slamming into the bothy and the doorway and her.

And lifting.

---

She went up.

Off the doorstep. Into the air. With the baby on her chest and the wind under her feet like a hand, like a step, like the first stair of a staircase made of air. Up. Over the bothy. Over the lake. Kestrel shouting something below her that the wind tore away.

And Lark woke up.

---

Wren felt it. The jerk. The breath. The head lifting off her collarbone.

The wind hit her daughter in the face. Cold. Hard. Full of snow.

And Lark laughed.

Not the laugh from the bothy. Not the laugh from the roofs. *More*. The laugh of someone who has been inside for three months, in caves and bothies and under rocks, sick, cold, carried, and has just this moment been taken out into the sky.

She laughed so hard she couldn't breathe. And breathed. And laughed again. With her square fists flung out on either side and her face turned up into the snow and the wind tearing the sound out of her and throwing it back across the black lake and the white mountains.

Wren laughed with her.

She couldn't help it.

---

"*KES*," she shouted. Down. At the bothy. At the small figure in the doorway with red hair.

Kestrel looked up.

"*FOLLOW*," shouted Wren. "*WALK. TO HARROW. I'LL SEE YOU THERE.*"

Kestrel shouted something back. The wind took it.

But Wren saw her lift her hand. And hold it up. And the hand caught fire. Not burning. Just lit. Like a lamp. Like a torch held up in a doorway on a winter night.

So Wren could see it.

So she'd remember the way.

---

She turned south.

And ran.

---

On the wind. Over the high country. Over the snowfields and the passes and the black lakes and the bare rock. Over the bothies they'd slept in and the caves and the rock overhang by the waterfall. Over eighty-six days of walking, rolled up under her like a carpet, gone in an afternoon.

She didn't stop.

She ran the way she'd run on the roofs at Highcrest in the rain, with Lex behind her laughing. The way she'd run over Ashbourne with the gold coming down. Her feet barely touching the air. The wind lifting her over every gap.

The baby laughed the whole way.

---

The Teeth went by on her left. White. Jagged. Like a row of old women in white shawls.

The Greyspine came up ahead. Grey. Snow on the tops. The passes.

She went over the highest one. Not through. *Over*. Higher than she'd ever been. So high the air went thin and her ears rang and the baby stopped laughing for a moment and stared round with her grey-green eyes enormous, at the whole world spread out below them like a map.

Then she laughed again.

Louder.

---

*She's a Mistwalker's daughter*, Wren had told Lex. On the river path. After Ashbourne. *She likes the wind*.

*She's a smith's daughter*, Lex had said. *She grabbed the nail*.

*She can be both*.

Wren ran south over the Greyspine on the north wind with her daughter laughing on her chest and thought, *she can be anything. She can be everything.*

*And nobody's going to keep her*.

*Not ever*.

---

The light was going when she came over the last ridge.

Red. Low. The sun going down behind her in the west, over the mountains, over the Teeth, over the edge of the world. The sky ahead was purple. Cold. Clear.

And below her—

---

The Vale.

Long. White under the snow. A river, black, winding. A village with a forge in it. One chimney smoking. A long roof. A black dot on the chimney-top.

And at the far end, a birch wood.

Gold.

A gold path going up through it. Up the hill. From a clearing at the bottom to the top of the ridge and over. Toward her. Toward the mountains. The way he'd come, following her. In the summer. Three months ago.

And in the clearing at the bottom—

Light.

---

Gold. Huge. Warm. Like metal in a crucible.

On the step. In front of a blue door.

Waiting.

---

Wren stopped.

On the wind. In the air. High over the Vale. With the red sun behind her and the purple sky in front and her daughter on her chest.

She looked down at the light on the step.

And saw something else.

---

Above the birch wood. In the high pasture. Where the dragons slept, in the summer. Two shapes. Coming down out of the red sky from the west. Fast. One enormous, bronze and copper, old gold, with wings like the sails of a ship. One smaller, blue-grey, beating hard.

Landing.

In the snow. In the high pasture. In the last red light.

And figures sliding off them. Small at this distance. Four. One carrying something. One carrying someone. One big, coughing, with an arm that caught the light and shone.

One with a basket on his back.

Running.

Down through the birches. Toward the stream. Toward the clearing.

Toward the light on the step.

---

Wren couldn't breathe.

She hung in the air over the Vale on the north wind and watched a man with dark curly hair cut badly and a basket on his back run down through the birch wood in the dusk.

She'd know that run anywhere.

She'd watched it over a hundred rooftops.

---

"*Lex*," she said.

Out loud. To the wind. To the dusk. To nobody.

And her daughter, on her chest, stopped laughing.

And looked.

Down. At the birch wood. At the running figures. At the one with the basket.

And reached.

One square fist. Out. Down. Toward him. Opening and closing.

The way she reached for the nail.

---

Wren came down.

Out of the sky. On the wind. Fast. Faster than she'd ever come down off anything. With her daughter reaching and her heart going like a hare's and the snow coming up to meet her and the gold light on the step getting bigger and bigger and the man with the basket coming out of the trees at the top of the clearing and stopping.

And turning.

And looking up.

At the sky. At the wind. At a woman with red hair coming down out of the dusk with a baby on her chest.

---

She landed in the snow at the top of the clearing.

Beside him.

She didn't remember landing. She remembered the wind letting go. She remembered her boots hitting the snow. She remembered stumbling. She remembered him catching her.

Both arms. Round her. Round the baby. Round both of them. Hard. Like iron. Like a vice. Like her daughter's grip.

He didn't say anything.

She didn't say anything.

He held her in the snow at the top of the clearing with the gold light on the step below them and his brother beside them and a boy asleep in his brother's arms and a big man with a gold arm coming out of the trees, coughing.

He held her.

And the baby between them reached up.

And grabbed his nose.

---

He made a sound.

Wren felt it. Through his chest. Not a laugh. Not a sob. Something with both in it. Something that came up out of him from very deep, from a place he'd been keeping shut for months that had been a fortnight.

"*Ow*," said Lex Vey.

"*She's got good taste*," said Wren. Into his shoulder. Crying. Laughing. "*She always goes for the nose.*"

"*She's*—" He pulled back. Just enough. To look. At the baby. At the square fist on his nose. At the face. Round. Fierce. With a fluff of dark hair standing up like a bird's crest. With grey-green eyes, Wren's eyes, looking at him. "*She's so big.*"

"*She's nearly a year old*," said Wren.

"*She was five months*. *When I*—" His voice broke. "*She had one tooth.*"

"*She's got six*," said Wren. "*She bit Kestrel. She bit me. She bit a goat.*" She laughed. Shakily. "*She stood up, Lex. In a bothy. On the Teeth. On my hand. On my gold fingers.*" She swallowed. "*She stood up and you weren't there and I'm telling you now. Kestrel said she'd tell you if I couldn't. I can. She stood up.*"

He looked at the baby for a long time.

The baby looked back. With her fist on his nose. Frowning.

As if she were trying very hard to remember him.

---

"*Lex*," said Antho.

Quietly. Beside them. With Pim in his arms. Looking down the clearing.

At the step.

At the light.

They all looked.

---

He was sitting on the oak ramp in front of the blue door. Enormous. Gold. Glowing. With his great hands on his knees and his robe spread out on the snow, turning it gold. With his crown of gold leaves, crooked.

He'd turned his head.

He was looking up the clearing.

At them.

At the man with the basket. At the woman with red hair. At the baby between them, with her fist on her father's nose.

And his face—

Wren had seen it before. In a village in the western shires. On the edge of a well. Looking up at her daughter through a whirl of wind.

A man looking through a window into a warm room on a winter night.

---

"*Cousins*," said Oszric of the Crowned.

His voice came up the clearing. Deep. Warm. Like a bell.

"*You're just in time*," said the Gilded King. "*I was about to put the kettle on.*"

---

Nobody moved.

Then the blue door opened.

---

Behind him. Behind the King on the step. The door swung inward. Into the dim. Into the faint light of a Lantern by a bed, three panes of glass and one of horn.

And a woman stood in it.

Small. Square. Barefoot. In a grey dress with flour on the front. With her greying hair tied back and her sleeves pushed up and her hands on her hips.

She looked over the King's shoulder. Over his great gold head. Up the clearing. At her sons. At her son's wife. At the baby.

Her face did something Wren had never seen it do.

It didn't break. It *lit*. All at once. From inside. Like a window. Like a lamp in a cottage at the end of a long lane on a winter night.

"*Well*," said Seren Harrow. "*Don't just stand there. Come in. The kettle's on.*"

She looked down at the King on her step.

"*You too*," she said. "*Mind your head on the lintel.*"
