# The Old Man's Year

Arion Vey had been careful for two hundred years, and he fell over reaching for a cup.

It wasn't even a good cup. It was the chipped blue one with the crack down the handle that Seren used for her tea because she said the crack made it taste of the sea. It was on the shelf above the hearth. Not high. A man of ordinary height could have reached it without thinking.

He'd been a man of ordinary height once. Taller than ordinary. He'd had to stoop under the arch of the Spire steps, the day he met her, in the rain.

He'd stood up out of the chair. Slowly. Carefully. With his hands on the arms. On *STEADY* and *HOME*. He'd done it a hundred times. He'd stood, and found his balance, and taken one step toward the hearth, and reached.

And the floor had come up and hit him.

---

He lay on it for a long time.

He wasn't hurt. That was his first thought. Not badly. His hip had struck the hearthstone and his elbow had struck the floor and his head, thank God, had struck nothing at all. He lay on his side on the flags between the chair and the fire with his cheek on the cold stone and took stock, the way he'd taken stock of a thousand things in two hundred years. Methodically. In order. As if he were writing it in a ledger.

*Hip: bruised. Not broken. I think not broken.*

*Elbow: skinned.*

*Head: intact. Relatively.*

*Dignity*—

He'd laughed then. A little. On the floor. It hurt his hip.

*Dignity: not recorded.*

---

He couldn't get up.

He tried. He got his hands under him and pushed, and his arms shook, and nothing happened. He tried again. He got as far as his elbow and the elbow gave and he was back on the floor with his cheek on the stone.

He lay there and thought, *well*.

He'd been Warden-Architect of the Spire. He'd held the Root of the whole net in his hands for two centuries. He'd walked into the gold room at Avaloria and sealed himself in crystal for sixteen years to hold the breaches shut from inside. He'd ridden a dragon to the top of the world.

He couldn't get up off his own kitchen floor.

*Well*, he thought again. *There it is. There's the year.*

---

It was quiet.

Seren was across the stream. At the other cottage. With Wren and the baby. She'd gone over after breakfast with a basket of something, and she'd kissed him on the forehead on the way out, the way she always did now, quick and fierce, like stamping a seal, and said *back by noon, don't do anything foolish*.

He'd said, *I never do anything foolish*.

She'd said, *you married a hedge-witch*.

It was a little after ten, he thought. By the light. By the angle of the sun on the floor. He'd always been good at telling the time by the sun on the floor. He'd sat in a lot of rooms in two hundred years, watching the sun move across the floor.

He'd never sat on one before.

---

The fire ticked.

He could see it from where he lay. Close. A yard from his face. The flames small and gold and steady behind the iron grate. He watched them.

*Good morning*, he thought. Without meaning to.

He didn't know where it came from. Lex said it. Every morning. To the hearth at the other cottage. Seren had told him. *He kneels down and says good morning to the fire. Every day. Like it's a dog.*

He'd laughed.

He wasn't laughing now. He lay on the cold flags with his cheek on the stone and his hip aching and looked at the fire, and thought, *good morning*. And felt, very faintly, the way you feel the warmth of a hand held near your face—

Something glad.

---

He lay there an hour.

Perhaps a little more. He counted it by the sun on the floor. It moved across the flags from the window toward the table leg, slowly, the way the hand of a clock moves.

He thought about a great many things.

He thought about Seren's face when she came back and found him. He thought about it very carefully, from several angles, the way he'd once thought about the structure of a ward. And decided he would very much like her not to see him like this, and that there was nothing at all he could do about it, and that this was, perhaps, the first lesson of the year.

*You can't plan the end*, he thought. *You can only be in it.*

He thought about Elric.

He hadn't meant to. He'd been trying not to, for weeks. Since Lex came back from the pool with his feet bare and said *bring him*. He'd been not-thinking about Elric so hard that it had worn a groove in him, like a cart-track worn in a road.

On the floor, he couldn't stop.

---

Forty years.

He'd met him at the Spire. In the library. Elric had been twenty-one. Tall and sharp and laughing, with a raven on his shoulder and a stolen book under his arm. A Stormcaller. Tier II. Brilliant. Poor. From a fishing village on the Saltreach coast that nobody had ever heard of.

He'd looked at Arion across the library table and said, *you're the Warden-Architect*.

And Arion had said, *yes*.

And Elric had said, *you're younger than I thought*.

And Arion, who had been a hundred and eighty-eight years old and looked thirty-five, had said, *I'm older than you think*. And something in his voice must have given it away, because Elric had looked at him for a long time, very sharply, with his storm-grey eyes. And then laughed. And sat down. And said, *tell me*.

Nobody had ever said that to him before.

Not *tell me what to do*. Not *tell me how*. Just *tell me*.

He'd told him. Not everything. Not then. Some of it. Over forty years, most of it.

Almost all of it.

---

*You aren't ready, old friend*.

He'd said that. In the gold room at Avaloria. Through the crystal. As a ghost. With his body sealed and his sons in front of him and Elric standing behind them in his grey cloak with the brass charms, waiting. Hoping. After forty years.

*You aren't ready*.

He'd been right. He knew he'd been right. Elric hadn't been ready. Elric had never been ready. Elric had wanted it too much to be ready.

He'd been right, and he'd said it gently, and he'd meant it kindly.

And that night Elric had opened the wards of Fort Caldris to the Pale Warden and carried Arion's body out of the crystal in a sack.

---

He lay on the floor and thought, *I was right*.

And then he thought, *I was always right*. *That was the worst thing about me. Elric said so. In the Knot. Lex told me.*

*He was right. He was always right. That was the worst thing about him.*

He lay on the floor with the sun moving across the flags and his hip aching and thought about a young man with a stolen book saying *tell me*.

And about how, in forty years, he had never once said it back.

---

Seren found him at a quarter past eleven.

He heard her on the ramp. Bare feet on oak. Fast. She always came back faster than she went. And then the door. And then nothing.

He didn't turn his head. He couldn't, very well.

"*Good morning*," he said to the fire. "*I seem to have misplaced the floor.*"

---

She didn't scream.

He'd known she wouldn't. Harrow women don't. She dropped the basket. He heard it hit the flags and something in it break. And then she was on her knees beside him, her hands on him, on his shoulder, on his face, on his hip, fast and sure and shaking.

"*Where*," she said. "*Where does it hurt. Tell me. Tell me where.*"

"*My dignity*."

"*Arion*."

"*Hip*," he said. "*Left. Bruised, I think. Not broken. Elbow. Skinned. Head—*" He considered. "*Fine. I think. Though I've been talking to the fire, which may be a symptom.*"

She didn't laugh.

He'd thought she might. He'd hoped she might. He'd been lying on the floor for an hour composing the line.

She didn't laugh. She knelt on the flags with her hands on him and looked at his face. And he saw hers.

---

He'd seen it do a great many things.

He'd seen it laugh at the lanterns in the rain at nineteen. He'd seen it at the bottom of the Spire steps, turning round. He'd seen it across a table at a cottage at the end of the Vale, while her mother threw a frying pan at his head. He'd seen it at a wedding. At two births. At a thousand breakfasts. He'd seen it, in the gold room at Avaloria, looking at him through crystal for the last time before she walked into the dark.

He'd seen it in a cold room at the Lantern Keep, a year ago, looking at an old man she didn't know.

He'd never seen it like this.

Not frightened. She'd been frightened before. *Found*. As if she'd been looking for something for a long time without knowing she was looking, and had just this moment found it, and found it on the floor, and broken.

"*How long*," she said.

"*An hour*."

"*An hour*." Her voice cracked. "*On the floor. On your own. For an hour.*"

"*The sun was very interesting*."

"*Don't*," said Seren Harrow. "*Don't you dare. Don't you make a joke out of it. Not this.*" Her hands were very tight on his shoulder. "*I was across the stream. I was laughing. Lark got hold of Wren's nose and wouldn't let go and I was laughing. And you were here. On the floor. For an hour.*"

He didn't say anything.

There wasn't anything to say.

---

Brannoc carried him to bed.

Seren went for him. Down the track to the Ashbourne road at a run, barefoot, and came back twenty minutes later with the old smith thundering up behind her like a landslide. He came in through the blue door and stopped and looked at Arion on the floor.

Then he bent down. And slid his enormous arms under him. And lifted.

Arion felt himself go up off the flags the way a child goes up. As if he weighed nothing. As if he were a sack of feathers. As if two hundred and thirty-four years and a Warden-Architect's ledgers and a whole net's worth of Root had all burned off him somewhere along the way and left only this. A small old man in a big man's arms.

"*Mind the hip*," he said.

"*I'm minding it*," said Brannoc.

"*You're very careful*."

"*I've carried a lot of things*," said Brannoc Holt. "*Anvils. Horses' legs. Your boy, once, when he was eleven, out of the ashes.*" He laid Arion down on the narrow bed under the window. Very gently. As if he were laying down something that might break. "*I've never dropped one yet.*"

---

The healer came from Ashbourne in the afternoon.

Ilsa Brand. Twenty-two. Plump and freckled and brisk, with a basket of salves and a [Healer] class she'd got at sixteen, in Fenmoor, on the day Lex Unbound a whole town. She'd been levelling ever since. She was Level fifty-eight now, and she ran the Ashbourne infirmary out of the old chapel vestry, and Sister Agathe said she had hands like warm bread.

She put them on his hip. On his elbow. On his head.

She was quiet for a long time.

"*Not broken*," she said at last.

"*I told them*."

"*You did*." She took her hands away. Sat back on her heels by the bed. Looked at him. With her brisk freckled face not brisk at all. "*Master Vey. I can heal bruises. I can heal a skinned elbow.*" She swallowed. "*I can't heal the other thing.*"

"*I know*."

"*It's not an illness*," said Ilsa. "*There's nothing wrong. Nothing broken. Nothing sick. It's just*—" She stopped. "*It's just everything wearing out at once.*"

"*Like a coat*," said Arion.

"*Like a coat*."

"*Worn through at the elbows*," said Arion Vey. "*Thin at the collar. Still a good coat. Just old.*" He smiled at her. "*I know, Ilsa. Thank you. It's very good of you to say it plainly.*"

She looked at him for a long moment.

"*You should stay in bed*," she said. "*A few days. A week. Let the hip mend.*"

"*And then*?"

"*And then the chair*," said Ilsa Brand. "*But not for long. A few hours. Not all day.*" She stood up. Gathered her basket. "*And don't reach for cups.*"

---

Lex came at noon the next day.

Arion heard the dragon. Then the stepping stones. Then the ramp. Then the door, and his son in it, stopping. Barefoot. With his boots in one hand and black coal-dust on his clothes and his face grey.

Seren was sitting by the bed. She didn't get up.

"*He fell*," she said.

"*Brannoc told me*."

"*He was on the floor for an hour*."

Lex looked at his father.

Arion looked back. From the narrow bed under the window. With his white wisp of hair on the pillow and his hip aching under the blankets and his skinned elbow bandaged in clean linen.

"*I'm told I misplaced the floor*," he said.

Lex didn't laugh either.

Arion was beginning to think the line wasn't as good as he'd thought.

---

His son sat down on the end of the bed. Heavily. And looked at him.

"*How was Coldwater*?" said Arion.

"*Don't*."

"*Don't what*?"

"*Don't change the subject*."

"*I'm not changing it*," said Arion mildly. "*I'm choosing it. It's different. I've been in bed for a day and a half with nothing to think about but my hip. I'd very much like to think about something else.*" He looked at his son. "*Please.*"

Lex looked at him for a long moment.

Then he told him.

---

The Crown and Pick. The old men. The stone with a hammer cut in it and crossed out. The Old Gallery going down and down. The note.

The crack. The iron. The great black ring, ten feet across, standing on its edge in the heart of the mountain with the silver thread through it.

Hask's pick finding the flaw. The size of a fingernail.

*Tink*.

The ring breaking. Falling away into the dark in black shards, ringing. The thread going up a half-step.

The voice. *You broke it. Nobody's broken one of my rings.*

The new ring growing back. Whole. Perfect. No flaw.

*It's my road, smith. And I'm on it.*

---

Arion lay on the pillow and listened.

He listened the way he'd listened to reports for two hundred years. With his eyes half shut. Without moving. Fitting each piece into place as it came, like stones into a wall.

When Lex had finished, he didn't say anything for a while.

"*It broke*," he said at last.

"*It grew back*."

"*It broke first*." Arion opened his eyes. "*Nobody's broken one in nine hundred years. He told you so himself. And you broke it with a hammer the size of a spoon.*"

"*And he grew it back in the time it takes to breathe*."

"*Yes*," said his father. "*Because he could. Because he was there. Because you broke it on his road, with him standing on it.*" His spotted fingers moved on the blanket. "*So the thread can't be cut by force. Not while he's on it. We know that now.*"

"*That's not much*."

"*It's one road closed*," said Arion. "*Lysandra gave us three. Now we have two.*" He looked at his son. "*That's not nothing, Lex. That's a third of the problem. Solved. By walking down a hole and finding out.*"

Lex didn't say anything.

"*Finding out is never nothing*," said Arion Vey. "*I spent two hundred years finding out things. Most of them were things that didn't work.*" A faint smile. "*It's how you find the ones that do.*"

---

Lex looked at the floor.

"*Hask got cut*," he said. "*His arm. The iron. It'll scar black.*"

"*Is he all right*?"

"*He's standing in front of the pool with a stick*." Lex's mouth twisted. "*He's always all right. He'd say he was all right with his arm off.*"

"*He's a Coldwater hewer*," said Arion. "*They're like Harrow women. They don't scream.*"

Seren, by the bed, made a small sound.

Arion looked at her.

"*I didn't scream*," said Seren. Very quietly. "*Yesterday. When I came in.*"

"*No*," said Arion. "*You didn't.*" He reached out. Slowly. Across the blanket. And found her hand. "*I'd have liked it if you had. Just a little. So I'd know.*"

She looked at him.

"*So I'd know what*?"

"*How much*," said Arion Vey.

---

Lex went very red and found something interesting to look at out of the window.

---

When Seren had gone to make tea—she'd made a great deal of tea in the last day and a half; Arion suspected she made it so she'd have somewhere to put her hands—his father said, "*There's a box under the bed.*"

Lex looked at him.

"*A box*," said Arion. "*Wooden. Under the bed. On this side. Would you?*"

His son bent down. Felt under the bed. Came up with it.

It was a small box. Plain. Oak. Brannoc had made it for him in the winter, without asking what it was for. With a lid that fitted so well it sighed when you opened it.

"*Open it*," said Arion.

---

There were letters in it.

Three of them. Folded. Sealed with plain grey wax. Each one with a single line written on the outside, in a small neat hand. The hand of a man who'd written ledgers for two hundred years.

Lex took them out. One by one. And read the lines.

*For Lark. On her first birthday.*

*For Lark. On her second birthday.*

*For Lark. On her third birthday.*

He didn't say anything.

He sat on the end of the bed with the three letters in his hands, and his face did something Arion had never seen it do. Not even in the gold room. Not even at the bottom of the world.

---

"*There'll be more*," said Arion. "*I'm doing one a week. More, if my hand holds.*" He looked at the letters in his son's hands. "*Twenty-one. One for every birthday till she's grown.*"

"*Father*—"

"*It's practical*," said Arion. "*It's simply practical. I'm a Warden-Architect. I plan for contingencies.*" His voice was quite steady. "*She won't remember me. She can't. She's four months old. By the time she's old enough to remember anything, I'll be—*"

He stopped.

"*Gone*," said Lex.

"*Gone*," agreed his father.

---

He'd thought about it a great deal. On the floor. Before that. In the long nights, with Seren asleep beside him on top of the blankets and her bare feet cold.

He'd been a grandfather for four months. He'd held Lark three times. She'd grabbed his nose once and wouldn't let go, and he'd laughed so hard his hip had ached for a day.

She'd never know.

She'd grow up with a story. *Your grandfather. He was very old. He was Warden-Architect. He loved your grandmother. He died when you were small.*

She'd never know his voice.

"*So I'm writing it down*," said Arion. "*What I'd tell her. If I could. One for every year.*" He looked at the box. "*What it was like to be one. What it was like to be two. What I thought about when I was ten. What I wish someone had told me at sixteen, at my Kindling, before I knew what I was.*" A pause. "*What her father was like at her age. What her grandmother was like. What the Spire looked like in the rain.*"

"*She'll have that*," said Lex hoarsely. "*She'll have us. Mam and me and Wren. We'll tell her.*"

"*You'll tell her your side*," said his father. "*I want her to have mine.*"

---

Lex put the letters back in the box.

Very carefully. One by one. In order. And closed the lid. And it sighed.

He sat holding it on his knees.

"*Give it to Wren*," said Arion. "*When the time comes. She'll keep it safe. She's very good at keeping things.*"

"*Why not me*?"

"*Because you'll read them*," said his father. Mildly. "*All of them. The first night. You won't be able to help it.*" A faint smile. "*You're my son. I'd have read them.*"

Lex laughed.

It came out of him cracked and wet and sudden. He sat on the end of the bed with the box on his knees and laughed with tears running down his face, and his father lay on the pillow and watched him, and smiled.

*There*, thought Arion. *There. That one landed.*

---

"*I want to talk to it*," said Arion.

Lex wiped his face. Looked at him.

"*Ember*," said his father. "*I want to talk to it. Properly. Not through you. Not with you passing messages like a schoolboy.*" He looked at the hearth. "*It talked to me once. On the rubble. At the Blightmarch. In a coal, in a fire. Hello, old man.*" A pause. "*Can it do that again?*"

Lex looked at the fire for a long moment.

*[Yes*,] said a voice in the back of his head. Very quiet. *[If he wants. If he really wants.]*

"*It says yes*," said Lex.

"*Then let it*," said Arion Vey.

---

Lex knelt at the hearth.

Arion watched him from the bed. Watched his son put his big scarred hand out to the fire, flat, palm down, the way you'd put your hand on the neck of a horse. Watched the flames lean toward it. Rise. Brighten.

And a coal in the middle of them began to glow.

Not red. Not orange. *Gold*. A deep warm gold, brighter than the rest, pulsing very slightly, like a heart. Like something breathing.

And out of it, small and clear and dry, came a voice.

*[Hello, old man*.]

---

He'd heard it once before. On the rubble. In the dark. With his hand going slack in Antho's.

He'd thought he'd dreamed it.

He hadn't.

It was his voice.

Not his voice now. Not this thin dry thread of an old man's voice that came out of him like wind through a cracked door. His voice from *before*. From twenty-two years ago. From the night he'd stood over a cot in a cottage at Harrow with his Root open and his hands on a sleeping boy and pressed a piece of himself into the boy's blood like a maker's mark.

Steady. Low. Precise. With an old-fashioned courtesy he'd forgotten he ever had.

"*Hello*," said Arion. "*Young man.*"

---

The coal pulsed.

*[I'm not young*,] said Ember. *[I'm twenty-two years old and two hundred and thirteen, depending how you count. I'm older than Lex.]*

"*You're younger than me*."

*[Everyone's younger than you*,] said Ember. *[That's not a fair measure.]*

Arion laughed.

The real laugh. Startled out of him. It hurt his hip. He didn't care.

"*Lex says you write*," he said. "*A book.*"

*[The Book of What Was. Volume Two.]* A pause. *[Volume One was mostly Aurelian's notes. Volume Two is mine. It's mostly footnotes.]*

"*Footnotes*."

*[About things I don't like*,] said Ember. *[There are a great many. Lex keeps doing things I don't like. Going down holes. Talking to Crowned. Falling in love with Mistwalkers who break his nose.]*

"*That was a Moth Queen*."

*[It was both. On separate occasions.]*

---

Seren came back in with the tea.

She stopped in the doorway. With a cup in each hand. Looking at Lex, kneeling at the hearth. At the gold coal. At the old man in the bed, laughing.

"*Is that*—"

*[Hello, Seren*,] said Ember.

She put the cups down. Very slowly. On the table.

"*Hello*," she said.

*[Please don't drop the tea*,] said Ember. *[He's been asking for it for ten minutes. He's very particular about his tea. He was particular about it two hundred years ago. It's in Volume One.]*

---

She sat down on the edge of the bed.

She didn't say anything for a while. She listened. Arion watched her listen. She did it the way she'd done it at the table when Lex told them about Elric. Completely. Without moving. Her face turned toward the gold coal in the fire as if it were a window.

Ember told him things.

Small things. Things he'd forgotten. That he'd liked his eggs runny at thirty and hard at sixty. That he'd once spent a whole winter trying to teach a Spire cat to fetch and the cat had taught him to throw things instead. That he'd been afraid of horses until he was ninety. That the first time he saw the Vale, he'd stood at the top of the Ashbourne road and said, out loud, to nobody, *oh*.

*[You said it again*,] said Ember. *[Forty years later. The first time you came to the cottage. You stood at the edge of the birch wood and looked at it and said oh.]*

Arion didn't say anything.

*[You'd brought Seren home*,] said Ember. *[To meet her mother. She was walking ahead. She turned round and saw your face and laughed at you.]*

Seren made a small sound.

*[She said, you look like you've never seen a tree before*,] said Ember. *[And you said, I've never seen this one.]*

---

Nobody spoke.

The fire ticked. The coal pulsed. Gold. Steady. Like a heart.

"*I don't remember that*," said Arion. Very quietly.

*[I know*,] said Ember. *[That's why I told you. It's the kind of thing you'd want to remember. So I kept it.]* A pause. *[I keep everything. That's what I'm for.]*

---

"*Ember*," said Arion.

*[Old man*.]

"*Lex says you're frightened*."

The coal went still.

Not dark. Still. The pulse stopping. The gold holding steady. The way a breath holds.

*[Lex talks too much*,] said Ember.

"*Lex never talks*," said Arion. "*Lex is the least talkative person I've ever met. It took him two years to tell me he loved Wren and he only told me then because she'd broken his nose and he needed me to explain it to the healer.*" He looked at the coal. "*He told me you were frightened. So you must be very frightened indeed.*"

Silence.

Lex, kneeling at the hearth, had his eyes shut.

*[I'm a piece of you*,] said Ember at last. Very quietly. *[From before the seal. Everything you were. Pressed into him. Like a maker's mark.]* A pause. *[When you go—*]

It stopped.

*[When you go*,] said Ember again, *[I don't know what happens to me.]*

---

Arion lay on the pillow and looked at the coal.

He'd thought about this. Of course he'd thought about it. He'd been thinking about it since the Blightmarch. Since a gold coal in a fire on the rubble said *hello, old man* in his own voice from twenty years ago.

He hadn't found an answer.

"*You're not mine*," he said.

The coal pulsed. Once.

"*You haven't been mine*," said Arion, "*since the night I made you. I gave you away. To a little boy asleep in a cot.*" He was quiet. "*At Avaloria I offered to take you back. Do you remember? You said no. You said you'd rather stay.*" A faint smile. "*I said it was always a gift.*"

*[I remember*.]

"*A gift doesn't go back*," said Arion Vey. "*When the giver dies. It doesn't. If I give Lark a nail, and I die, she still has the nail. If I give Seren a stone, she still has the stone.*" He looked at the coal. "*I gave you to Lex. You're his. You've been his for twenty-two years.*"

*[But I'm* made *of you*,] said Ember. *[The nail isn't made of you. The stone isn't. I'm made of you. I'm your voice. I'm your memories. I'm the way you tap two fingers on a table.]* The coal flickered. *[When you go, the thing I'm made of goes. How can I stay?]*

---

"*Lex is made of me too*," said Arion.

The coal went still.

"*He's made of me*," said Arion. "*Half of him. My blood. My eyes. The way he holds a pen. The way he goes still when he's angry.*" He looked at his son. Kneeling. With his eyes shut. "*When I go, he won't stop. He'll still be made of me. He'll go on being made of me. And of his mother. And of Brannoc, and Wren, and everyone who ever taught him anything.*"

*[Lex is made of you and Seren*,] said Ember. Very quietly. *[And Brannoc and Wren and everyone. He's made of a lot of things.]* A pause. *[I'm only made of you.]*

---

Arion didn't answer.

He lay on the pillow and looked at the gold coal pulsing in the fire. His own voice. His own memories. The way he used to tap two fingers on a table, before his fingers got too stiff to tap.

He didn't have an answer.

For the first time in a very long time, he didn't have an answer.

---

"*Twenty-two years*," said Seren.

They both turned to her. The old man and the coal.

She was sitting on the edge of the bed with her hands in her lap. Looking at the fire.

"*Twenty-two years in Lex*," she said. "*Watching him. Talking to him. Writing footnotes about him.*" She looked at the coal. "*You said it yourself. You keep everything. Everything about him. Every hole he went down. Every Crowned he talked to.*" A small crooked smile. "*Every nose that got broken.*"

The coal was very still.

"*You're not only made of Arion*," said Seren Harrow. "*Not anymore. You haven't been for twenty-two years.*" She leaned forward. Toward the fire. "*You're made of every morning Lex said good morning to a fire. You're made of every time he did something stupid and you didn't like it. You're made of his whole life.*"

The coal didn't move.

"*Arion made you*," said Seren. "*Lex grew you.*"

---

There was a long silence.

Then the coal pulsed. Once. Brighter than before.

*[...that's very kind*,] said Ember. Very small. *[I'll put it in a footnote.]*

"*Not a footnote*," said Seren. "*Put it in the main text.*"

*[...all right*,] said Ember.

And then, after a moment, so faint Arion barely heard it—

*[I'm still frightened*.]

"*I know*," said Arion. "*So am I.*"

---

Lex let the fire go.

The gold coal dimmed. Slowly. Went red. Went orange. Went back to being a coal among other coals. The voice went with it.

His son knelt at the hearth for a long time with his hand still out, flat, over the flames. As if he'd forgotten to take it back.

Then he stood up. And came and sat on the end of the bed. And didn't say anything.

"*Lex*," said Arion.

"*Father*."

"*Take me down*."

Lex looked at him.

"*To Elric*," said Arion. "*Not at Lantern Night. Not when it's convenient. Now. Next week. As soon as Ilsa says I can sit in the chair.*" His voice was very steady. "*The floor was a reminder. I'd like to not need another one.*"

His son didn't answer for a long time.

Then he nodded.

"*Next week*," he said.

---

Seren stayed when Lex had gone.

She sat on the edge of the bed in the grey afternoon light with the tea going cold on the table and didn't say anything. She just sat. With her hands in her lap. Looking at him.

He looked back.

"*Seren*," he said.

"*Hush*."

He hushed.

She looked at him for a long time. At his face on the pillow. At the white wisp of his hair. At the walnut of his skin, every fold. At his eyes.

"*I'm going to kiss you*," said Seren Harrow.

He didn't say anything.

"*I've been working up to it*," she said. "*For three months. More. Since the cold room at the Keep. Since you said good evening and I said you have a nice voice.*" She swallowed. "*I bring you pies. I read you letters. I sit beside you at the window with my hand next to yours. I've been courting you like a farm lad at a hiring fair.*" Her mouth twisted. "*And I've kissed you on the forehead. Every morning. Like you're my grandfather.*"

"*I'm old enough to be your grandfather's grandfather's*—"

"*Don't say anything clever*," said Seren.

He didn't.

---

She bent down.

And kissed him.

Not on the forehead. Not quick and fierce, like a seal stamped. Slowly. Carefully. On the mouth. The way you'd drink from a cup you were afraid of spilling.

He'd been kissed by her before. Thousands of times. In thirty years. Ember had told him about some of them. He remembered some himself. On the Spire steps, in the rain, the first time, when she'd laughed and pulled his head down because he was too tall. At their wedding. In a doorway at midnight with a baby asleep on her shoulder. In the gold room at Avaloria, through crystal, the last time, before she walked into the dark.

She didn't remember any of them.

This was the first one she remembered.

---

When she sat back, her face was wet.

So was his.

"*Well*," said Seren. Wiping her eyes with the heel of her hand. "*That's done, then.*"

"*That's done*," agreed Arion.

"*Was it all right*?"

"*It was*—" He stopped. He was a Warden-Architect. He had written ledgers for two hundred years. He had a word for everything. "*It was all right*," he said.

She laughed.

"*All right*," she said. "*Thirty years and I get all right.*"

"*You asked me not to say anything clever*."

"*I didn't mean that*. *I meant*—" She stopped. Looked at him. "*Say something clever now. Go on. You're allowed now.*"

He looked at her. At her square face. At her brown eyes, wet. At the flour on her cheek, because there was always flour on her cheek.

"*I turned back twice*," said Arion Vey. "*On the steps. In the rain. The first time I saw you.*" His voice was a thread. "*I've been trying to work out why. For thirty-two years. Why I turned back. Why I nearly didn't come round the corner at all.*"

"*Why*?"

"*Because I knew*," said Arion. "*Even then. Even before I'd seen your face. I knew if I came round that corner I'd never be able to go back.*" He looked at her. "*I was right. I was always right. It's the worst thing about me.*"

---

She lay down beside him.

On top of the blankets. In her skirt. With her bare feet cold and her head on the edge of his pillow and her hand on his chest. Over his heart. Where she could feel it.

She didn't say anything.

She didn't need to.

---

He wrote the first line that night.

She was asleep. Beside him. Breathing slow. Her hand still on his chest. He didn't want to move it. He moved anyway. Very carefully. An inch at a time. Until he could reach the table by the bed.

The grey book was there. Where Lex had left it. On the table. With its pencil tucked into the binding.

He'd given it to Lex a year ago. With the Vhessan names in it, the eleven thousand. And Kell, after. And the twenty-three from the rim.

And a blank page at the end. For later.

*Write me last*, he'd said. On Lantern Night. *After everyone. When it's time.*

---

He opened it.

Slowly. In the dark. By the light of the fire, banked low. To the last page. The blank one. Clean. Waiting.

He took the pencil out of the binding.

His hand shook. It always shook now. The letters came out crooked. Wavering. An old man's hand. Nothing like the small neat hand that had written ledgers for two hundred years.

He wrote anyway.

*Arion Vey. Of the Spire. And of Harrow.*

He stopped.

He looked at it for a long time. The two lines. Crooked. Shaking.

And then he wrote one more.

*He turned back twice. And then he didn't.*

---

He put the pencil back in the binding. And closed the book. And put it back on the table.

The rest was for Lex.

He'd promised.

---

He reached for his spectacles.

He didn't know why. He couldn't read in this light. He didn't want to read. He just reached for them, the way he'd reached for them every night for a hundred years, the way you reach for a familiar thing in the dark to make sure it's there.

They were folded on the table. Beside the grey book. Where he always left them.

He picked them up.

And looked at the lens.

---

There was a face in it.

Tiny. Pale. No bigger than his thumbnail. White hair. Grey eyes. Cheekbones like the edge of a knife.

Looking at him.

He didn't drop them.

He'd known she might come. Wren had told them. *She said, her husband wears spectacles. He leaves them on the table at night, folded, beside the bed.*

He held the spectacles in his shaking fingers and looked at the tiny pale face in the lens.

"*Good evening*," said Arion Vey.

---

"*You're not afraid*," said the face.

Its voice was so small he could barely hear it. Like a finger round the rim of a very small glass. Like a fly at a window.

"*I'm too old to be afraid of much*," said Arion. Very quietly. So as not to wake his wife. "*And you can't come through. Not in my spectacles. They're too small.*"

"*No*," agreed Ysenne. "*I'm only looking.*"

"*What at*?"

The tiny face was quiet for a moment.

"*At you*," said the Glass Queen. "*Mae talked about you.*"

---

He hadn't expected that.

"*Did she*," he said.

"*In her letters*. *Through the crack. Last year. Before.*" The tiny face tilted. "*She sent them back with her moths. To Orrin. To read to us in our sleep. She knew we couldn't answer. She sent them anyway.*" A pause. "*She said there was a man in that world who was the cleverest she'd ever met. And very old. And very honest. And very sad.*" The pale eyes glittered. "*She said she liked you.*"

"*She tried to eat my wife*."

"*She liked you anyway*," said Ysenne. "*Mae liked a great many things she tried to eat.*"

He didn't answer that.

---

"*What's it like*?" said the Glass Queen.

He looked at her.

"*Dying*," said Ysenne. Simply. Curiously. The way a child asks what the sea is like, having never seen it. "*I've never done it. None of us have. Not properly. Four came apart against the edge. That's not the same.*" The tiny face was very still. "*Mae did it. At the end. She came apart into light. Orrin says she smiled.*" A pause. "*I'd like to know. Before. What it's like.*"

Arion lay in the dark with the spectacles in his shaking fingers and his wife asleep beside him with her hand on his chest.

He thought about it.

He thought about it very carefully. From several angles. The way he'd once thought about the structure of a ward.

"*It's like being very tired*," he said at last. "*At the end of a very good day.*"

---

The tiny face didn't say anything.

For a long time. So long he thought she'd gone.

"*We don't have those*," said Ysenne. Very quietly. "*Days.*"

"*No*," said Arion. "*I don't suppose you do.*"

"*Or tired*. *Not that kind.*" The tiny pale face looked at him. "*Or good.*"

He didn't answer.

He looked at her. At the tiny face in his spectacle-lens, no bigger than his thumbnail, white and cold and glittering.

And felt sorry for her.

He didn't want to. He'd been feeling sorry for things he didn't want to feel sorry for all his life. Morvane, at the end. Elric, sometimes, in the long nights. A Moth Queen on a hill.

He felt sorry for her anyway.

"*Good night, Ysenne*," he said.

---

He folded the spectacles.

Gently. Carefully. The way you'd close a door on a sleeping child.

And put them face down on the table. Beside the grey book. With the lenses against the wood.

And lay back on the pillow. In the dark. With his wife's hand on his chest. Over his heart. Where she could feel it.

It was beating.

Slowly. Steadily. A little tired.

At the end of a very good day.
