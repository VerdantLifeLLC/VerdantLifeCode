# Prologue: The Thorn-Crowned

She had been dreaming of roses for nine hundred years.

Not the same rose. Never the same one. That was the mercy of it. Her dreams had always been gardens, ever since she was a girl, and the gardens in them grew. A red rose by a white wall. A yellow one climbing an apple tree. A dark one, nearly black, that smelled of cloves and rain, in a stone trough by a kitchen door. A bed of small pink ones in a cabbage patch, where a girl in a grey dress sat with her knees up, talking to a puddle.

She dreamed them all. One after another. Down the long years. Down in the warm close silk-dark of the cocoon, where there was no light and no time and no hunger, or not much, or not enough to wake for.

She dreamed them and they bloomed and she was happy.

And then she woke up, and they were thorns.

---

She woke because someone had put a hand on her.

She felt it through the silk. Big. Heavy. Hot. Flat on the cocoon over her breastbone, the way you'd lay a hand on a door to feel if there was a fire behind it.

She knew that hand.

She'd known it all her life. It had lifted her onto a pony when she was four. It had slapped her once, when she was twelve and had said something about their father. It had held a gold cup to her lips on the last evening, in the hall, by the pool, and tipped it, gently, and said *drink, Rosie, it's only water*.

"*Wake*," said her brother. "*Rhosyn. Wake up.*"

His voice was rough. Rough as a road. She'd never heard it rough before. It had always been warm and deep and easy, a voice that filled a room the way a fire fills a grate.

It filled this one too. But it had broken something on the way.

"*Our sister is dead*," said the Gilded King.

---

She came out of the silk slowly.

Not like him. She could tell he'd come out all at once; she could smell it on the air, torn silk, a great sour rip of it. That was Oszric. He'd always got out of bed like a man leaping off a cart.

She came out like a seed comes out of the ground. Slowly. A crack of grey-gold light. A seam opening. Her hands first, pushing. Then her face. Dust everywhere. Her own dust. Nine hundred years of it, pouring off her shoulders onto the glass floor with a sound like a long sigh.

She knelt on the floor of the hall in the dust and breathed.

It hurt.

Everything hurt. Her joints. Her skin. Her eyes, which had been closed so long they had forgotten how to see anything but dark. And under it all, low and slow and steady, the thing that had hurt the whole time and that she had slept through by going down very deep.

The hunger.

It woke when she did. It always had. It stretched in her like a cat on a hearth and opened its eyes and looked about for something to drink.

There was nothing.

There had been nothing for nine hundred years.

---

"*Up*," said Oszric. "*Come on. Up.*"

His hand under her elbow. Hot. Lifting. She let him lift her. She'd always let him. She stood in the dust with her knees shaking and looked at him.

He was the same. That was the terrible thing. Nine hundred years and he was exactly the same. Tall. Huge. A great gold beard to the middle of his chest and gold hair to his shoulders and skin like old ivory. His gold crown of leaves on his head, a little crooked, the way he'd always worn it, as if he'd put it on in a hurry on his way to somewhere more important.

Only his eyes had changed.

She remembered them brown. Warm brown. Their mother's eyes. Laughing at her over the rim of a cup.

They were furnaces now. White and gold and roaring. Like looking into the mouth of a forge. She had to look away.

"*Mae's dead*?" she whispered.

"*Yes*."

"*How*?"

He didn't answer. He held out his other hand.

There was a crown in it.

Silver wire. Fine as frost. Shaped like a pair of folded wings.

Rhosyn looked at it for a long time.

She remembered it on a head of silver hair, bending over a sickbed. Over a little girl with a fever, in the dark, in a room with a toy bear on the windowsill. She remembered Maelis's voice, low and kind, telling a story about a girl who married a bear.

She remembered her standing at the edge of the pool on the last evening with her cupped hands full of light, saying *it isn't wrong, Rosie. It's just a spring.*

"*Oh*," said Rhosyn. "*Oh, Mae.*"

And she began to cry.

---

Oszric let her.

He stood in the dust with the silver crown in his hand and watched her cry, the way he'd watched her cry over dead birds when she was small. Not unkindly. Not kindly either. Patiently. The way a man waits for rain to stop before he goes out.

When she'd finished he put the crown back inside his robe. Against his chest. Over his heart.

"*Better*?"

"*No*."

"*No*," he agreed. "*Nor me.*" His furnace eyes moved down the hall. "*Come. The others are up. Orrin's going to tell you all of it, and then I'm going to tell you what we're going to do.*"

---

The others were up.

She saw them as she came down the hall behind her brother, her bare feet in the dust, her thin legs shaking.

Four cocoons. Torn, all four. Hanging from the high glass rafters like empty sacks. And a fifth at the end, split from top to bottom, long empty. Hers, Oszric's, Ysenne's, Caddoc's. Maelis's. Under each one a dark place on the floor where a crown had lain in the dust for nine centuries and left its shape.

And at the end of the hall, by the tall glass doors, in the grey-gold light that came from nowhere, her brother and her sister. Waiting.

Ysenne first.

She was sitting on the floor. Of course she was. Sitting on the floor of the hall in her long white gown with her knees drawn up and her face bent down, close to the glass, looking at herself.

The floor was dusty. There was nothing to see. She'd wiped a patch clean with her sleeve. A small round patch, like a hand-mirror. And she was looking into it.

She was beautiful. She'd always been the beautiful one. Maelis had been lovely, and Rhosyn had been pretty, once, when she was young and pink and smelled of roses. But Ysenne was *beautiful* the way a cut stone is beautiful. Sharp-cheeked. Long-necked. Her skin so pale it was nearly clear, with a faint cold glitter in it, like frost on a window. Her hair white. Her eyes the pale grey of ice over deep water. Her crown a narrow band of glass, perfectly clear, so you could only see it when the light caught it.

She didn't look up.

"*Rosie*," she said to her reflection. "*You've got thin.*"

"*So have you*."

"*I know*." Ysenne turned her face, a little. Studied the line of her jaw in the cleaned glass. "*It suits me. It doesn't suit you. You look like a broom.*"

And beside her, standing, with his arms crossed over his chest—

Caddoc.

He hadn't changed either. He'd never changed. Not since they were children. He'd been born looking forty, their nurse used to say, and he'd stayed looking forty ever after. Square and dark and heavy. A face like a closed door. Black hair cut short as a soldier's. And his crown, which was not a crown at all, but a plain band of black iron, a hand wide and a finger thick, with no ornament on it of any kind. Like a manacle. Like the iron ring of a gate.

He looked at Rhosyn as she came down the hall.

He nodded. Once.

He'd always done that. He'd nodded at her when she was born, their mother said. Just looked into the cradle and nodded, as if confirming that a delivery had arrived as ordered.

"*Sister*," said Caddoc.

"*Brother*."

That was all. That was Caddoc. He'd said perhaps four thousand words to her in their whole lives before the drinking, and perhaps four hundred since, and every one of them had been exactly what he meant.

---

And kneeling on the glass at their feet, in the dust, with his brow nearly touching the floor—

Orrin.

She hardly knew him. She remembered a steward. A tall thin careful man in grey with a gold chain, who walked half a step behind Oszric everywhere and carried a ledger and never smiled. Who'd held the cup for the servants, on the last night, after the Ten had finished. Who'd drunk the dregs.

This wasn't a man.

It was the shape of one. Grey. Thin as a rake. Bent. Its skin like old paper, its hands like bundles of twigs. The gold chain gone black round its neck. And its eyes—two dim sparks, barely lit, like the ends of pipes glowing in a dark room.

He'd been awake the whole time. She realized it, looking at him. Nine hundred years. Not in a cocoon. Not asleep. Awake. Kneeling. Waiting on the King.

"*Majesty*," whispered Orrin. To her. "*Majesty. You wake.*"

She didn't know what to say to that.

So she said, "*Thank you, Orrin*," the way she'd said it to the gardeners when they brought her the first buds of spring.

The dim sparks of his eyes flickered.

She thought, for a moment, he might cry.

He didn't. He had nothing left to cry with. He bowed his head lower, and the dust slid off his shoulders, and he began to tell her how her sister had died.

---

It took a long time.

Orrin told it the way stewards tell things. In order. Plainly. With dates.

The Veil loosening. A light lit before a door, far away. Maelis waking, and going through, alone, and some of her household coming apart against the edge as they followed. The young Well. Full again. Rising. The smith. The warden. The Harrow woman, coming up out of the dark.

Maelis drinking, once, in the deep. Growing bright. *Sovereign*. Their sister, Sovereign, in a world with no Ceiling.

And then the room. A hundred miles of it. Cut into the ground by people with spades. The Well coming up into it. The hewer's pick. The smith's little hammer. The flaw.

Everything she'd ever drunk, nine hundred years of it, pouring out of her down the point of a pick.

And then—

Orrin stopped.

"*Say it*," said Oszric. Very quietly. He'd heard it already. He wanted to hear it again. Rhosyn could see it in his face, in the white roar of his eyes. He wanted to hear it every time someone told it, the way you press on a bruise.

"*And then a Harrow woman knelt down in front of her*," whispered Orrin. "*And gave her a lamp.*"

Nobody spoke.

"*And she took it*," said Orrin. "*And it stayed. In the middle of her. Where she was always cold.*" He was shaking. "*And she was warm. And she came apart into light.*"

---

Rhosyn stood in the dust with her hands pressed together under her chin.

*A Harrow woman*.

She didn't hear the rest. She heard Ysenne say something sharp about lamps. She heard Caddoc say nothing at all. She heard Oszric's breath go in and out, slow, like a bellows.

She heard only the name.

*Harrow*.

She didn't know it. It wasn't a name from home. But Orrin had said *Hesper's line*, and *the tenth*, and *the sister who wouldn't drink*, and Rhosyn had felt something in her chest she had not felt for nine hundred years.

Not hunger.

Something older.

*Oh, Hes*, she thought. *You had children. You had a line. You had daughters, and they had daughters.*

*You lived*.

---

"*So*," said Oszric.

He turned round. Away from the doors. Toward them. The four of them. The last four of the Ten. In the dust, in the hall, under the empty cocoons, in the grey-gold light.

He looked enormous. He always had. When he stood in a room the room arranged itself round him, like iron filings round a lodestone. He'd been like that at nine years old.

"*Our sister went first*," said the Gilded King. "*As she always did. She went toward the light and she drank it and they killed her for it.*" His voice was very even. "*A smith. A warden. A hewer. A Harrow woman with a lamp.*"

"*And three hundred and fourteen miles of people with spades*," murmured Ysenne. To her reflection.

"*And them*."

He looked at each of them in turn.

"*There's a crack in the Veil*," said Oszric. "*Low down. A finger wide. Our sister's last moth brought Harrow blood to it. Hesper's blood. The blood the Veil never learned.*" His eyes burned. "*It opened. A little. Not enough. Not for us. Not yet.*"

"*Then what's the use of it*?" said Ysenne.

"*It's a beginning*."

"*It's a crack*." She looked up from the floor at last. Her ice-grey eyes on her brother. "*I can't get through a crack, Oz. None of us can. Mae could squeeze through when the knot was loose. Mae was always thin.*" She smiled. Faintly. "*You're not thin.*"

"*No*," said Oszric. "*But you don't need to be thin, sister. You only need to be seen.*"

Ysenne went very still.

---

"*Go and look*," said the Gilded King.

Ysenne looked at him for a long moment.

Then she bent her head back down to the small round patch of clean glass on the floor. To her own face in it.

And went in.

That was the only way Rhosyn could ever describe it, after. Ysenne didn't move. She didn't lean forward. She didn't change. She simply looked into her own reflection, close, closer, and then her reflection was looking back out at her, and then it was the reflection that was real and the woman on the floor that was the picture. And then neither of them was there at all, and the patch of glass on the floor was empty.

And then, far away, as if from the bottom of a well, Ysenne laughed.

It was a small, surprised, delighted laugh. Rhosyn hadn't heard it in nine hundred years. She'd forgotten it. A girl's laugh. A girl in a room full of mirrors trying on her mother's earrings.

"*Oh*," said Ysenne's voice. From the glass. From nowhere. "*Oh, Oz. Oh, look.*"

"*Tell us*."

"*Glass*," breathed the Glass Queen. "*So much glass. They have glass now. All of them. Not just palaces. Not just kings. Every house. Every cottage. Every inn. Windows. Little ones and big ones. Lanterns with glass in them. Bottles on shelves. Spectacles on old men's noses.*" Her voice was rapt. "*There's a girl in a kitchen in a town called Fenmoor holding up a pan to see if her hair's straight. I can see her. I can see right up her nose.*"

Caddoc grunted.

"*I can go anywhere*," said Ysenne. "*Through the crack. A finger's width. That's all a reflection needs. Oh, Oz. I can go everywhere.*"

"*Then go*," said Oszric. "*Look. Find them. The Harrow woman. Her sons. The child.*"

A pause. Far away, in the glass.

"*And then*?" said Ysenne.

"*And then come back and tell me what you saw*," said the Gilded King. "*And we'll decide what to take.*"

---

"*Caddoc*."

Their brother uncrossed his arms.

"*The Key*," said Oszric.

Caddoc nodded.

He'd known. He'd been waiting. Rhosyn saw it in the set of his heavy shoulders. He'd been awake perhaps an hour longer than she had, and in that hour he'd worked it all out, the way he'd always worked things out, slowly and completely, like a man laying a stone wall.

"*The Veil's woven into their net*," said Caddoc. His voice was low and flat and rough with disuse, like a cart going over gravel. "*I felt it when I woke. Under the floor. Under the Court.*" He looked down. At the glass. At the dust. "*A thread.*"

"*One thread*?"

"*The last one*." Caddoc looked up. "*The little man with the hammer tied it last. When he was done. To hold the edge. It runs from here—*" he stamped one heavy foot on the glass "*—to the middle of their net. All the way down. To the knot at the bottom.*"

"*And the Key*?"

"*In the knot*." Caddoc's dark face didn't change. "*Barred. Someone's holding it. A man. Mostly thread now. Tired.*" He was quiet. "*I can feel him. On the other end. Like feeling a fish on a line.*"

Oszric's furnace eyes burned very bright.

"*Can you climb it*?"

"*Yes*."

"*How long*?"

Caddoc thought about it. He always thought before he answered. Even when they were children and their father asked him a sum. He'd stand there with his heavy face screwed up while the others shouted out guesses, and then he'd say the right number, and their father would ruffle his hair, and he'd nod.

"*The world's wide*," said Caddoc of the Iron Crown. "*And the thread goes down as well as across. And I'll have to ring it as I go, or it'll throw me off.*" He looked at his hands. Square. Heavy. "*Their autumn. The end of it. They light lanterns then. Orrin says.*"

"*Lantern Night*," murmured Orrin from the floor.

"*Then*," said Caddoc.

"*And when you get there*?"

"*I take the bar out*," said Caddoc. "*I take the Key. I bring it to you.*" He shrugged. A small heavy movement, like a boulder settling. "*And you turn it.*"

---

Rhosyn found she was holding her breath.

She knew about the Key. They all did. The little man with the hammer had made it, the night he wove the world. Bow, bit and blade. A key that could undo anything it was turned against. Their father's scholars had whispered about it in the last days. *He's made a key. He can unlock the pool. He can unlock anything.*

"*Oz*," she said.

Her voice was very small. She hadn't meant it to come out at all.

He looked at her.

"*If you turn it*," said Rhosyn. "*The Key. In their net.*" She swallowed. "*What happens?*"

Her brother smiled.

It was the old smile. The warm one. The one from the pony and the gold cup and the hall. Their mother's smile, in a gold beard.

"*Ysenne*," he said. Not looking away from Rhosyn. "*Tell her what you told me. About their net.*"

From the glass, from very far away, the Glass Queen laughed.

"*It hasn't got a king*," she said.

Rhosyn stared.

"*It had one*," said Ysenne's voice. "*The little man with the hammer. And then his line, for a while. Vey. The smith is one. He held it.*" A pause. Delicious. "*And he gave it away, Rosie. Two years ago. He wove the whole net new and then he just took his hands off it and walked away.*"

"*Walked away*—"

"*It's lying there*," said Oszric softly. "*The whole thing. Every thread. Every knot. Every Ceiling and every level and every Stone. With nobody's hand on it.*" His furnace eyes were very bright. "*A crown, Rosie. Lying in the road. And whoever turns that Key in the knot at the bottom—*"

He opened his great golden hand. Slowly. Palm up. Empty.

And closed it.

"*Picks it up*," said the Gilded King.

---

Rhosyn didn't say anything.

She looked at her brother's closed hand. At the gold in it. At the gold in everything he'd ever touched.

She thought about the household. Theirs. Hers. His. The cooks and the grooms and the gardeners and the nurses and the stewards and the little scullery girls with red hands. Nine thousand people. Who'd come with them, on the boat, across the grey water, because they were the Ten's people, and where else would they go.

Who'd started to come apart, in the first years. When there was nothing left to drink.

And Oszric had gone out among them. Down the white road. With his hands.

And kept them.

She could see them from here. Through the tall glass doors. Along the road. Standing in the dust in their thousands, in rows, all the way to the edge of the sky. Gold.

She didn't look.

She never looked.

"*And Orrin*," said Oszric.

The steward lifted his head.

"*You'll go through*," said the Gilded King. "*You're dust, mostly. Dust goes through cracks. Take this.*" He reached into a fold of his robe and brought out something small. A pouch. Silk. Tied with a gold cord. It chimed faintly as it moved, like sand made of bells. "*A pinch of mine. For anyone who wants it.*"

Orrin took it in his twig-hands as if it were a sleeping bird.

"*Who'll want it, Majesty*?"

"*Everyone*," said Oszric. "*In the end. Everyone who's ever sat by a bed.*" He smiled. "*They die there, Orrin. In their world. Did you know? Our sister's moth saw it. They grow old and their hands shake and they die. All of them. Even the strong ones. Even the kings.*" His voice was very gentle. "*Offer them what I offered ours. They'll come. They always come.*"

---

"*And me*?" said Rhosyn.

They all looked at her.

Even Orrin. Even Caddoc. Even, far away, in the glass, she felt Ysenne look.

She hadn't meant to ask. It had come out of her the way the crying had. Small and stupid and unasked for.

Oszric looked down at her for a long moment.

"*You grow, sister*," he said. "*As you always did.*"

"*Grow*—"

"*Into the crack*." He nodded west. Toward the doors. Toward the white road and the plain and the edge of the world. "*Send your thorns into it. Into the stone. Push. Thorns are good at that. They get into a wall and they break it, slowly, from inside. Every gardener knows.*" His furnace eyes didn't blink. "*Widen it. For me. Make it wide enough for a king.*"

She looked at him.

"*And the child*?" she said. "*The smith's child. What will you do with her?*"

---

He didn't answer at once.

He turned. Away from her. Toward the doors. And stood looking out at the white road, at the plain of grey-gold dust, at the far rows of his golden people. His back very broad. His hands at his sides.

"*Nine hundred years*," said the Gilded King.

Nobody spoke.

"*Nine hundred years, Rosie. And not one child.*" He didn't turn round. "*Not one. Not among us. Not among the household. Not among anyone who drank.*" His voice was quiet. "*We drank the thing that makes room. And there's been no room in us since. Not for anything new. Not for a child. Not for a thought we haven't had before. Not for a rose.*"

He looked back over his shoulder. At her. At her crown.

At the black thorns. Twisted. Dry. That had been roses once. Red ones. On a white wall.

She flinched.

"*She was born the night our sister died*," said Oszric. "*The smith's child. On the rubble. While the Well came home. I could smell her, when the crack opened. Through the crack. Like bread.*" He turned all the way round. "*Something new. Something that's never been anyone's. Full of room.*"

"*Oz*—"

"*I'll put a crown on her*," said the Gilded King. "*And I'll keep her. And she'll be ours. Forever. Just as she is. And the Court will have something in it, at last, that we didn't have before.*"

He smiled at his sister.

"*An heir, Rosie*," he said. "*Wouldn't that be nice?*"

---

She left them in the hall.

She didn't ask permission. She'd never needed to. Nobody had ever cared very much where Rhosyn went, or what she did, so long as she came when she was called.

She went out through the tall glass doors. Onto the white road. Into the grey-gold light.

The road ran west. Straight as a ruler. Across the plain. Toward the edge of everything. White stone, the white of old bone, laid down nine hundred years ago by people who were still alive and still had hands.

They lined it now. On both sides. In rows.

She didn't look. She walked down the middle of the road with her eyes on her bare feet and her hands pressed together under her chin and she did not look.

But she heard them.

You couldn't not hear them. Not if you'd been a gardener. Not if you'd spent your whole girlhood listening for the small sounds things make when they're growing.

They didn't make those sounds.

That was what she heard. Nine thousand people standing in the dust in rows, gold from crown to heel, still as statues, still as stones. And not one of them breathing. Not one heart. Not one small shifting foot. The silence of nine thousand people who had been *kept*.

And under it, faint, so faint it was barely there at all—

Something like a hum. A held note. Like a finger run round the rim of a glass. Nine thousand voices that couldn't speak, all saying the same thing.

She'd heard it for nine hundred years. In her sleep. In her dreams of gardens.

She didn't know what it was saying.

She'd never let herself listen hard enough to find out.

---

The thorns began a mile off the road.

She'd grown them herself. Of course she had. Nobody else in the Court could grow anything. Thirty-two years ago, by the steward's reckoning. In the one hour she had been awake, between one long sleep and the next.

She didn't let herself think about that hour. Not yet.

She came off the white road onto the dust, and walked north, and the thorns rose up in front of her.

Black. Dry. Taller than the palace doors. Thick as a forest, thick as a wall, a great twisted tangle of hooked black stems running east and west as far as she could see and curling round in a ring a mile across. Every stem as thick as her wrist. Every thorn as long as her finger. Not a leaf on any of them. Not a bud.

They knew her.

They leaned toward her as she came. Very slightly. The way plants lean toward a window. And then, at her touch, they drew back. Creaking. Unhooking from one another. Opening a way.

She walked into the dark of them. And they closed again behind her.

---

In the middle of the ring, there was a tower.

A small one. Slender. No higher than a tall tree. Made of wood. Not cut wood. *Grown*. The living trunk of something that had been a great white-barked tree once, coaxed up and up and round on itself, twisted into the shape of a tower, with a door at the bottom and a window at the top and a little peaked roof of woven branches.

It was dead now. Grey. Cracked. The bark peeling. It had been dead for nine hundred years.

She'd grown it in the first year.

The first year of the exile. When they still had power to spare, and the drinking was still warm in them, and the household still had faces. Ysenne had built her palace of glass that year. Caddoc his of iron. Oszric his of gold. Maelis hadn't built anything at all; she'd sat at the window of the hall and looked west, toward the grey, toward the world, toward a light that wasn't there.

And Rhosyn had come out here. To the edge of the plain. And knelt in the dust. And put her hands flat on the ground.

And grown a tower for her little sister.

*In case*, she'd said. When the others found out and laughed. *In case she changes her mind. In case she comes. She'll want somewhere to sleep.*

Ysenne had laughed hardest.

Hesper never came.

---

Rhosyn sat down in the dust in front of the door.

She didn't try to open it. She never had. Not in nine hundred years. And not in that one hour, thirty-two years ago, when she'd stood here with her heart going like a bird's and her hands full of new thorns and watched someone else come out of it.

She just sat. With her thin knees drawn up and her arms round them. Like a girl on the edge of a cabbage bed.

And talked to the door.

"*Hes*," she said.

Nothing. The tower stood grey and cracked and silent. The thorns creaked faintly overhead.

"*Hes. They're awake.*" Her voice was very small. "*All of them. Oz and Ysy and Cad. Mae's dead. Did you know? Somebody gave her a lamp.*" She pressed her chin on her knees. "*Somebody from your line. A Harrow. I don't know that name. Is it yours? Did you take it?*"

The door didn't answer.

"*I didn't tell them*," whispered Rhosyn. "*About this. About what's in here.*" She looked up. At the little window at the top of the tower. Dark. "*I never will. I promise. I know I promised things before. I know I said I heard you and then I went down to the hall and drank anyway.*" Her eyes were stinging. She didn't know she still had tears. She'd thought the hunger had drunk them all. "*But I won't tell them about this. Not ever. Not if Oz asks. Not if he holds my hand in the fire.*"

The thorns creaked.

"*He wants the child*," said Rhosyn. "*The smith's child. A little girl. One of yours, Hes. Your line. He wants to put a crown on her and keep her.*" Her voice cracked. "*He's going to make me help.*"

---

She sat there for a long time.

There was no time, in the Court. But she sat there for what would have been a long time, if there had been.

Up in the tower, behind the dark little window, something sat on a chair.

She'd never seen it. Not properly. Only once, for a moment, thirty-two years ago, carried past her in a long grey hand.

Small. Silver. A circle a little bigger than a man's hand could span. With points on it. Seven. Rising from the rim at even spaces. Like the tines of a fork.

She couldn't go near it. None of them could. She'd tried, in that hour. She'd stood at the foot of the stair and put her foot on the first step and felt it.

*No*.

Not a word. Not a voice. A *refusal*. Coming down the stair like cold air down a chimney. Pushing. Gently. Firmly. The way the Veil pushed. *Not you. Not ever. You drank.*

So she'd grown the thorns instead. A mile of them. Round and round. So nobody else would ever find the stair. So Oz would never send anyone. So Ysenne would never look. So it could sit up there, on the little chair Rhosyn had grown for a girl who never came, and say *no* to all of them, in the dark, forever.

And they'd slept.

That was the thing she'd never told anyone. For thirty-two years, ever since it came, they had all slept more soundly than they had in nine hundred. Even Oszric. Even Mae. She'd felt it in her cocoon. A quiet. A stillness. Like a hand laid on a fevered forehead.

Someone in the Court, at last, saying no.

---

"*I'm sorry*," said Rhosyn. To the door. To the dark window. To the thing on the chair. "*I'm sorry, Hes. I heard you. I should have listened.*"

She'd said it on the shingle. Nine hundred years ago. Over and over. Holding her little sister's hands while the Ferryman waited.

It hadn't been enough then.

It wasn't now.

She looked down at her own hands. Thin. Grey-green. The fingers long and knotted, like twigs. The nails black. She'd had beautiful hands once. Gardener's hands. Pink and strong and always a little dirty. Their mother used to scold her for it at dinner.

She hadn't grown a living thing since the night she drank.

Only thorns. Only dry black thorns, with no leaves and no buds and no flowers. Nine hundred years of them. As if whatever she put her hands to remembered the pool and grew hungry and hooked.

She turned her hands over in her lap.

And thought, for no reason at all, of a cabbage bed. A girl in a grey dress sitting on the edge of it with her knees up. Saying, very seriously, to a boy with bare feet, *I always could. Since I was four. I thought I was mad.*

*You're not mad*, the boy had said. *You're a Listener.*

---

Something moved in her hands.

She looked down.

There was a stem there. A new one. Thin and black and hooked, like all the others, coming up out of the dust between her bare feet. Curling up round her wrist. Seeking.

She'd grown it without meaning to. She did that sometimes. Thorns came up wherever she sat.

She looked at it.

And then, very slowly, the way you'd reach out to a bird on a windowsill, she put the tip of one finger on the very end of it.

And thought of her sister.

Not Mae. Not Ysy. *Hes*. Hes on the edge of the cabbage bed. Hes in the doorway of the hall. Hes on the shingle in the grey light, saying *not me, not ever*, with her voice shaking and not letting it stop her.

Hes, who'd lived. Who'd walked off east into the world with no light and a crown in a bundle and somehow, somewhere, had daughters.

Who'd had a *line*.

---

The tip of the stem swelled.

Rhosyn stopped breathing.

It was very small. A knot. A bump. Hard and green. Green. Not black. *Green*, the colour of a pea, the colour of a new leaf, a colour she had not seen in nine hundred years and had stopped dreaming of somewhere around the five-hundredth.

It split.

A thread of red.

She made a sound. She didn't know what sound. Something small and cracked and wordless, like a hinge.

The red unfolded. Slowly. Petal by petal. Five of them. Then ten. Curling outward. Soft. Damp. Dark as wine, dark as blood, dark as the rose by the white wall in her mother's garden when she was four years old.

A rose.

In the Outer Court. In the dust. In the grey-gold light that came from nowhere.

A rose.

---

She held it for a long time.

She didn't dare move. She was afraid if she breathed on it, it would go. It would curl up and blacken and drop, the way her roses had dropped on the shingle, one by one, onto the grey stones while the Ferryman waited.

It didn't.

It stayed. Red. Open. Alive. On the end of a black thorny stem curled round her thin grey wrist.

And it smelled.

Faintly. Very faintly. Of summer. Of rain on a white wall. Of a garden full of bees. Of the world.

Rhosyn of the Crowned, the Thorn-Crowned, whose name the others didn't say, sat in the dust at the foot of an empty tower and cried over a single rose until she had no more tears left in her at all.

---

Then she wiped her face.

And reached into the sleeve of her robe. Grey-gold silk. Rotten with age. She tore a strip from the hem. Narrow. No longer than her finger.

She had no ink. She had no pen. She had a thorn.

She broke one off the stem by her foot and scratched, very slowly, very carefully, on the grey silk, three letters. The way she'd learned to write them as a girl, at her mother's knee, in a language with eleven words for *crown* and none for *enough*.

It wasn't the old language she wrote in. She didn't know why. It came out of her in the shapes Orrin had used, when he spoke of the world on the other side. The world's letters. Plain ones. Square.

*HES*

And after it, a little hook. A curl. A question.

*?*

---

She tied the scrap to the stem, under the rose. With a thread pulled from her sleeve.

Then she knelt. And put her hands flat on the dust. The way she had in the first year, when she grew a tower.

And sent the stem west.

Not up. *Down*. Into the ground. Under the dust. Under the stone. A root, travelling. Through the dark under the Outer Court, under the plain, under the white road and the nine thousand gold people and the glass palaces and the hall. West. Toward the edge of the world. Toward the place where a finger's depth of Harrow blood had run across frozen ground, and the Veil had not known it, and had opened.

Oz had told her to grow into the crack.

She would.

She'd send her thorns. As many as he wanted. Into the stone. To push. To widen. To make it wide enough for a king.

But the first thing she sent through it would be this.

---

It took the rest of the night that wasn't night.

She felt it go. All the way. Like feeling a thread run out through her fingers. Under the plain. Under the edge. Into the place where the crack was, a dark seam low down at the foot of the grey, smelling of dust on this side and, on the other, of something else.

Something green.

She felt it push.

She felt it come up.

---

On the other side of the world, at the foot of the Veil, in the last cold dark before a winter dawn, the ground was gold.

Not frost. Not sunrise. *Gold*. Flat and hard and shining. For a mile round the crack. Grass, gold. Stones, gold. A beetle, gold, halfway across a gold stone, one leg raised. A hawk hanging in the air above a field, gold, its wings half folded, in the very act of dropping onto something that was no longer there.

And in the middle of the field, a flock of sheep.

Forty of them. Standing in the gold grass. Gold. Every one. Heads down, grazing, the way they'd been grazing when the dust came on the wind. Still as a carving.

And among them, a boy.

Twelve, perhaps. Thin. In a shepherd's smock and a felt cap. Gold. From the cap to the soles of his gold boots. Caught halfway through a step, one foot lifted, one arm out, his crook in his hand. His mouth open, as if he'd been about to call his dog.

His eyes were moving.

Slowly. In his gold face. Left. Right. Down.

Down at the gold ground by his lifted foot.

Where something was pushing up.

Black. Thin. Hooked. Splitting the gold like a crack in ice. Curling up out of the ground into the cold dawn air. Unfolding.

And at the end of it, red. Soft. Damp. Dark as wine. Open.

A rose.

The only living, moving thing for a mile.

And tied to the stem beneath it, fluttering, very faintly, in a wind that had come all the way from the east and smelled of rain—

A scrap of grey silk.

*HES?*
