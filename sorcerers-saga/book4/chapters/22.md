# The King Walks

The raven came on the fourth morning, and it was the wrong raven.

Wren knew before it landed. She was in the clearing at Harrow with Lark on her hip and a bucket in her other hand, the good one, at the stream, and she heard it come over the birch wood. Not the harsh croak of Quill. Not the heavy beat of one of Roole's big ravens.

Something ragged. Something limping in the air. Something that had flown a very long way very fast and had nothing left.

It came down on the stepping stone in front of her and missed its footing and fell in the stream.

---

She got it out.

She dropped the bucket and knelt on the bank with the baby on her hip and fished it out of the water with her good hand. It was old. One-eyed. The milky eye half shut. Its feathers soaked and ragged and its breast heaving. A thin silver ring on its leg.

And a scrap of paper. Rolled tight. Tied with grey thread.

Lark reached for the raven.

"*No*," said Wren. "*Gently. He's tired.*"

The raven looked at the baby with its one good eye.

"*Smith*," it croaked. Faintly.

"*He's not here*," said Wren. "*He's gone. Across.*" Her hands were shaking. "*Give it to me. Give me the letter.*"

---

It was Roole's hand. Small. Square. Plain.

It wasn't finished.

*Vey. Wren. Whoever reads this.*

*He's out. Came through the door at dawn the day after they went in. Walking. Gold. Taller than anything. Everything he walks past goes gold.*

*I've got them out. The Keepers. Nan Ashby. She didn't want to go. I carried her. She bit me.*

*He's coming east. Slowly. He's not hurrying.*

*I'm going to stand*

That was all.

The last word trailed off. The pen had gone on a little way after it, a thin line across the paper, as if the hand holding it had been pulled away.

---

Wren read it three times.

Then she stood up. In the clearing. With the baby on her hip and a half-drowned raven at her feet and the stream running loud and the birch tree at the edge of the clearing standing shining in the morning sun. Gold. Kept.

"*Seren*," she said.

Not loud.

The blue door opened anyway.

---

His mother-in-law came down the oak ramp barefoot and took the paper out of her hand and read it.

She didn't say anything.

She read it twice. Then she folded it, very carefully, along the creases Roole had made. And held it.

"*He went to stand*," said Seren. Quietly.

"*He promised*," said Wren. "*Antho told him. Get out. Don't stand in front of him. You can't.*" Her voice cracked. "*He promised he'd get them out first.*"

"*He did get them out*," said Seren. "*He carried Nan Ashby. She bit him.*" She looked at the folded paper. "*And then he went back and stood.*"

"*Why*?"

Seren looked at her for a long moment.

"*Because somebody has to stand somewhere*," she said. "*That's what Fenn said. At the south edge. Antho told me.*" She held the paper against her chest. "*Daxian Roole's been standing somewhere his whole life, love. He didn't know how to stop.*"

---

They heard the rest from a Keeper.

On the ninth day. A woman. Grey cloak. Iron-grey hair. A Keeper's staff, and her face burned red by the wind, and her boots worn through. She came up the track through the birch wood on foot, alone, and stood at the edge of the clearing and looked at the two cottages and the gold birch and the stream.

Ada Fell.

"*Is he here*?" she said. "*The smith?*"

"*No*," said Wren. "*He's across. He went through the door.*"

Ada Fell sat down on the grass.

Just sat. As if her legs had gone. With her staff across her knees.

"*Then I'll tell you*," she said. "*Somebody should know. Somebody should write it down.*"

---

She told them in the blue-door cottage.

With Arion in the bed under the boarded window, propped on his pillows, with the Lantern burning beside him. Three panes of glass and one of horn. With Seren on the edge of the bed holding his hand. With Wren on the floor by the hearth with the baby asleep in her lap and the raven, dried now, perched on the back of a chair, eating crusts.

Ada Fell sat at the table with a cup of tea she didn't drink.

"*He got us out*," she said. "*All of us. Two hundred Keepers. And the crofters from Westerling. And Nan Ashby. He made us go east. He made us walk. He said, don't stop, don't look back, get to the Blightmarch and keep going.*"

"*And you went*."

"*We went*," said Ada Fell. "*Most of us.*" She turned the cup on the table. "*I didn't. I hid on the wall. I wanted to see.*"

---

"*He came at noon*," she said. "*The King. Walking. Down out of the door at the edge of the world, into the gold. He didn't hurry. He walked the way an old man walks in his garden on a summer evening. Looking at things.*"

Nobody spoke.

"*He was so big*," said Ada Fell. "*You don't understand. You can't. Nobody can, till they see him. Taller than the gate. Broader than two men. Gold all over. Gold beard. Gold hair. A crown of gold leaves, crooked. And his eyes*—" She stopped. "*I couldn't look at his eyes. Nobody can. They're like the sun. Like the inside of a forge.*"

"*And Roole*?"

The Keeper looked at the cup.

"*Commander Roole was at the gate*," she said. "*The east gate of Greyfall. Standing in it. In the middle of it. In his grey. With Fenn's staff.*"

Seren's hand tightened on Arion's.

"*Fenn's staff*," said Ada Fell again. "*He'd taken it out of the grave. In the night. After we left. I saw him do it, from the wall. He went out to the rim with a spade and dug it up and cleaned it off.*" Her voice was very flat. "*And then he stood in the east gate. With it held crosswise. The way Fenn held it at the south edge, all night, between the families and the Mothwing.*"

---

"*And the King came up the road*," said Ada Fell. "*And stopped.*"

"*Stopped*?"

"*In front of him*. *In front of the gate.*" She looked up. "*He could have walked through him. He could have walked round him. He could have walked through the wall. Nothing stopped him. Nothing touched him. The gold went out from his feet in every direction, everything he passed.*" She swallowed. "*He stopped. In front of Commander Roole. And looked down at him.*"

"*Did he say anything*?" said Arion. From the bed.

"*He said*, *what's your name*," said Ada Fell. "*Very kindly. Like you'd ask a child.*"

"*And Roole*?"

"*Roole said*, *Daxian Roole, Commander of the Keepers of the Stones. You're not coming through this gate.*"

Wren closed her eyes.

"*And the King laughed*," said Ada Fell. "*Not unkind. Delighted. Like Roole had said something wonderful.*" She turned the cup. "*And he said, why not? And Roole said, because somebody has to stand somewhere.*"

---

The fire ticked.

Nobody said anything for a long time.

"*And then*?" said Seren. Very quietly.

"*And then the King said*, *that's beautiful*," said Ada Fell. "*He said, that's the most beautiful thing anyone's said to me since I woke.*" Her voice was shaking now. She didn't let it stop her. "*He said, you should never stop. You should stand there forever. Exactly like that. With your staff and your grey and your face.*"

"*Oh no*," said Wren.

"*And he put out his hand*," said Ada Fell. "*And touched him. On the forehead. Very gently. With one finger. The way you'd touch a sleeping child.*"

She stopped.

"*And he's there*," she said. "*Now. In the east gate of Greyfall. Gold. Standing. With Fenn's staff held crosswise.*" She put her face in her hands. "*His eyes move. I went down after. After the King walked on. I went and looked at him.*" Her voice came through her fingers. "*His eyes move. They followed me. When I touched his arm, they looked at me. And I couldn't tell what they were saying.*"

---

Wren didn't cry.

She'd thought she would. She sat on the floor by the hearth with the baby asleep in her lap and her gold-fingered hand on the baby's back and thought about a hard plain man with a hatchet face and one stiff arm. Who'd stood at the edge of the gold with his arm out in front of Lex. *Don't step on it*. Who'd dug Fenn's grave with one hand. Who'd written to Lex because he didn't know who else to tell.

*Come back*, he'd said. At the crack. At dawn. To the four of them. *Don't thank me. Come back.*

She didn't cry.

She sat with it. In her chest. Like a stone.

---

"*He's not dead*," said Arion.

They all looked at him.

He was lying very still on the pillows. Looking at the boards over the west window. His grey eyes very bright in the Lantern-light.

"*He's not dead*," he said again. "*None of them are. Not Roole. Not Bet Dale. Not the boy with the sheep. Not the nine thousand on the road.*" He breathed. In. Catch. Out. "*They're kept. It's not the same thing. It's worse. But it's not the same.*"

"*Can they come back*?" said Ada Fell. Through her hands.

"*Lex found the room in front of it*," said Arion. "*In Tilda. In Wren. Before it set.*" He turned his head on the pillow. To look at Wren. At her hand. "*Pim made one of them cry. Bet Dale. Through solid gold. For one breath.*"

"*That's not coming back*."

"*No*," agreed Arion. "*But it's a crack.*" He looked back at the boards. "*My son says everything's a crack first. He gets that from Brannoc. Brannoc gets it from fifty years of mending things.*"

---

The King walked east.

Slowly.

They heard about it every day. Ravens. Riders. Keepers on foot. Farmers fleeing ahead of him with carts piled high and their children on top and their faces white.

He walked perhaps five miles a day. Perhaps less. He walked the way Ada Fell had said, like an old man in his garden. Looking at things. Stopping.

He stopped at every village.

---

That was the thing nobody understood. Not at first.

He didn't attack them. He didn't burn them. He didn't march through them with an army, the way the Pale Warden had marched, the way armies did in the old songs.

He *visited*.

He walked into a village in the western shires at noon, with the gold spreading out from his feet across the green, and the people who hadn't fled watching from their windows. And he sat down. On the edge of the well. Or the steps of the chapel. Or a bench outside the inn.

And waited.

And they came.

---

Not all of them. Not most.

But some.

The old. The sick. The ones who'd been sitting by a bed for a month, or a year, listening to someone breathe. A woman with a son wasting in the back room. A man whose wife didn't know him anymore. A girl with a grandmother who screamed in the night with pain no healer could touch.

They came out of their houses. Slowly. One by one. And crossed the gold green. And stood in front of the Gilded King on the edge of the well.

And he listened.

Every one. For as long as they wanted to talk. Riders who'd seen it said so. He sat on the edge of the well with his great gold hands on his knees and his furnace eyes half shut, so you could almost look at them, and listened to every word.

And then he said, *I can help*.

And they said yes.

---

"*Eleven at Marrowby*," said Elowen.

She'd come on the fifteenth day. On her grey warhorse. With Oathkeeper on her hip and dust to her knees and her face hard as a door.

"*Eleven*," she said again. In the clearing. Not getting down. "*Old ones. Sick ones. The baby with the croup. Her mother came out with her in her arms.*" Her jaw worked. "*Sixteen at Hollin's Rest. Nine at Thistledown.*"

"*Thistledown*," said Wren.

Elowen looked at her.

"*That's mine*," said Wren. "*That's where I'm from. That's my village.*"

"*I know*."

"*Who*?"

Elowen didn't answer for a moment.

"*Old Mother Tansy*," she said. "*From the mill. And the Cartwright twins, both of them, they had the wasting.*" A pause. "*And Gaffer Holm.*"

Wren put her hand over her mouth.

Gaffer Holm. Who'd taught her to set snares when she was eight. Who'd given her a hare, once, a whole one, when there was no food in the house and Tam was crying with hunger, and said *don't tell your mam where you got it*. Who'd been ninety-one. Who'd been dying of something in his belly for a year.

"*He went himself*," said Elowen. "*Nobody took him. He walked out of his house on his own two sticks and across the green and sat down on the edge of the well next to the King and said, I'm tired, lad. Can you help?*"

"*And he*—"

"*He said yes*," said Elowen. "*And put his hand on the old man's shoulder.*"

---

Wren went to see him.

She didn't decide to. She stood in the clearing at Harrow on the eighteenth day with her daughter asleep in the sling against her chest and her bow on her back and her hand, her left hand, gold from the knuckles to the nails, and found she was already calling the wind.

"*Wren*," said Seren. From the ramp.

"*I know*."

"*He's a hundred miles off*."

"*I know*."

"*You've got the baby*."

Wren looked down at the dark fluff of hair against her chest. At the small square fist curled against her collarbone.

"*I know*," she said.

She didn't stop calling the wind.

"*I need to see him*," said Wren Ashdown. "*Once. Before he gets here. I need to see what he is.*" She looked at Seren. "*I'm a tracker. I've tracked everything. Wolves. Rock-wyrms. Mothwing across the Sundered Lands.*" Her voice was very steady. "*I need to see his tracks.*"

Seren looked at her for a long moment.

Then she came down the ramp. And put her hand flat on the baby's back. Next to Wren's gold fingers.

"*Don't let him see her*," she said.

"*He'll see her*," said Wren. "*He sees everything.*" She swallowed. "*But I won't let him near.*"

---

She went on the wind.

A hundred miles. In a morning. West over the Vale and the downs and the shires, high, higher than she'd been since Lantern Night. With her daughter on her chest, laughing. Lark loved it. She loved the cold and the speed and the great wide rushing of the air. She laughed the whole way. Her fists out. Her face turned up.

Wren didn't laugh.

She looked down.

---

She saw the road first.

From a long way off. From the air. A line. Gold. Running across the green of the western shires from the far edge of the world. Not straight. Not like the white road in the Court. *Wandering*. Bending. Going from village to village. A gold thread drawn across a green cloth by a hand that stopped to look at things.

Wide. A furlong wide. More, in places. Where he'd stopped.

Everything in it gold.

Fields. Hedges. Copses. Barns. A mill. A bridge over a river, and the river under it, a gold ribbon, still, flat, not flowing. A flock of sheep, gold, scattered across a gold hillside. A farmhouse with its door open and gold washing on the line.

And at the end of it, where the gold stopped and the green began again—

Light.

---

She came down in a copse a mile off.

Green. Not gold. Yet. On the edge of a village she didn't know. Brick houses. A chapel with a square tower. A green with a well in the middle.

She climbed a tree. An elm. Tall. With the baby on her chest. She went up through the branches the way she'd gone up the apple tree at Old Mag's, with petals falling. There were no petals. It was high summer. The elm was dark and full and still.

From the top she could see the green.

And him.

---

He was sitting on the edge of the well.

Ada Fell had said he was big. Elowen had said it. Everyone had said it.

They hadn't said it right.

He sat on the stone rim of the well with his knees apart and his great gold hands resting on them, and he was the biggest thing Wren had ever seen that was shaped like a man. Bigger than Hask. Bigger than Brannoc. His shoulders were as wide as the well. His head, bent, was higher than the wellhead's roof. A beard of gold to the middle of his chest. Gold hair to his shoulders. A crown of gold leaves, crooked.

A robe of white and gold, spread out on the grass round him.

The grass was gold. In a circle round the well. Spreading. Slowly. A foot an hour. Ten feet. Twenty.

And he glowed.

Not like a lamp. Not like a fire. Like *metal*. Like gold in a crucible, just before it pours. A deep warm heavy light coming up out of him, out of his skin, out of the gold of his beard and his hands and his robe. Lighting the green. Lighting the faces of the people round him.

There were people round him.

---

A dozen. Perhaps more. Sitting on the gold grass at his feet. Old. Most of them. A man in a wheeled chair, pushed by a woman with grey hair. A girl, holding a baby. A boy of ten with his arm round his grandmother, who was shaking, who couldn't stop shaking, whose hands flew and jerked in her lap like birds.

They weren't afraid.

That was the thing Wren would remember. Later. That they weren't afraid. They sat on the gold grass at the feet of the Gilded King in the summer sun and looked up at him the way you'd look up at a kind uncle telling a story by the fire.

And he was talking to them.

---

She couldn't hear. Not from the elm. Too far.

She called the wind.

Not much. Just a little. Just enough. A thread of air, coming off the green, up the slope, into the top of the elm. Carrying the sound.

His voice came with it.

Deep. Warm. Like a bell. Like a fire roaring up a chimney. Not loud. Gentle. Talking to the shaking grandmother. Very slowly. Very kindly.

"*—and it won't hurt*," said the Gilded King. "*I promise. Not at all. It'll feel like nothing. Like falling asleep in a warm room.*" A pause. "*And then you'll never shake again. Never. Your hands will be still. Your heart will be still. You'll sit in your chair by your window and your grandson will come and see you every day and you'll never be frightened again.*"

"*Will I know him*?" said the old woman. Her voice was a thread. Shaking with her hands. "*Will I know he's there?*"

"*You'll see him*," said the Gilded King. "*Every time. Your eyes will follow him across the room.*"

The boy's arm tightened round his grandmother.

"*Gran*," he said. "*Gran, you don't have to*—"

"*I'm so tired, Pip*," said the old woman. "*I'm so tired of shaking.*"

---

Wren watched him put out his hand.

Slowly. Gently. One great gold finger. Toward the old woman's forehead. The way Ada Fell had said. The way you'd touch a sleeping child.

The boy didn't stop him.

The old woman closed her eyes.

And the finger touched her.

---

It didn't take a breath.

From the forehead. Outward. Down. The old woman's grey hair. Her face. Her shaking hands, flying in her lap, slowing, stopping, *stopped*. Her shawl. Her skirt. Her feet in their worn shoes on the gold grass.

Gold.

Still.

She sat on the green in front of the well with her eyes closed and her hands folded in her lap. Perfectly still. For the first time in years. Gold.

And then her eyes opened.

Gold.

And moved. Slowly. So slowly. To her grandson.

---

The boy made a sound.

Wren heard it. On her thread of wind. Small. High. Not a word.

He was holding her. His arm still round her shoulders. Round gold shoulders. Hard. Cold. Still. He was holding her and she wasn't holding him back and she couldn't.

He looked up. At the King.

"*She's not shaking*," he said.

"*No*," said the Gilded King. Gently. "*She'll never shake again.*"

"*She's not*—" The boy's voice broke up. "*She's not anything.*"

The King looked down at him for a long moment. With his furnace eyes half shut.

"*She's kept*," he said. "*That's better than anything, child. You'll see. When you're older. When it's your turn to sit by a bed.*"

---

Wren didn't know she'd moved until she was on the wind.

Off the top of the elm. Into the air. Over the copse. Over the brick houses. Over the green.

She didn't plan it. She didn't think. Her body did it, the way it did on rooftops. She was in the air over the green with her daughter on her chest and her bow in her hand and an arrow on the string before her head caught up.

She loosed.

---

It was a good shot. She'd been shooting since she was eight. She'd shot hares on the high moors above Thistledown with Gaffer Holm watching. The arrow went down off the wind in a long clean arc, fast, true, straight at the great gold head bent over the boy.

And a yard from it, it went gold.

Not slowed. Not stopped. *Gold*. All at once. Shaft and fletching and head. In the air. And fell. Heavily. Straight down. Onto the gold grass at his feet with a sound like a dropped coin.

The King didn't look up.

He looked at the arrow.

At the gold arrow lying on the gold grass at his feet. For a long moment.

Then he bent down. Slowly. With a creak like a great oak in a wind. And picked it up. Between his great gold finger and thumb.

And turned it over. Looking at it. The fletching. The head. Very carefully. The way a jeweller looks at a ring.

"*Goose feathers*," said the Gilded King. "*Grey ones. Trimmed very neatly. With a little nick in each so they'll spin.*" He looked at the head. "*Hand-forged. Leaf-shaped. With a word cut in it. KNIT.*"

He looked up.

---

Wren couldn't look at his eyes.

She tried. She hung in the air over the green on the wind with her bow in her hand and tried, and her eyes watered and burned and slid away, the way your eyes slide away from the sun. She looked at his beard instead. His mouth. Which was smiling.

"*Mistwalker*," said Oszric of the Crowned.

---

She didn't answer.

"*It's a beautiful arrow*," he said. Warmly. "*Your husband made it, didn't he. The smith. I can feel him in it. The way you can feel a baker in his bread.*" He turned it over again. "*He's very good. He's better than any smith we had. We had very good smiths.*"

"*Let them go*," said Wren.

The King looked round. At the people sitting on the gold grass round him. The man in the wheeled chair. The girl with the baby. The boy with his arm round a gold grandmother.

"*Let who go*?" he said. Puzzled. "*I'm not holding them. They came to me. They can leave whenever they like.*" He looked back up at her. "*Nobody's made to stay, Mistwalker. Not ever. I only help the ones who ask.*"

"*She asked because she was tired*," said Wren. "*Because she was frightened. Because she couldn't bear it.*"

"*Yes*," said the Gilded King. "*Isn't that the best reason?*"

---

Lark woke up.

Wren felt her. Against her chest. A jerk. A breath. Her head lifting. Wobbling. Looking round.

Looking down.

At the light.

At the great gold shape on the edge of the well, glowing like metal in a crucible. At the gold grass. At the gold grandmother.

At him.

---

The King went still.

Wren saw it. All of him. His great gold hands, stopping. His smile, stopping. His furnace eyes, opening. All the way. So that the light coming out of them was like the sun coming out from behind a cloud.

He was looking at the baby.

"*Oh*," said Oszric. Very softly. "*Oh, there she is.*"

---

Wren pulled the wind round them.

Tight. Fast. A whirl. The way she'd done in the cellar. So nothing could land. So nothing could settle. So there was a wall of moving air between her daughter and anything that wanted to reach her.

The King didn't move.

He sat on the edge of the well with the gold arrow in his fingers and looked up at the baby through the whirling air. And his face—

His face did something Wren had never seen a face do.

It *hungered*.

Not like the Moth Queen. Not lit and patient and courteous. Not like a thing that wanted to drink.

Like a man looking through a window into a warm room on a winter night. Into a room with a fire and a table and children round it. A room he'd never been allowed into. A room he'd stood outside for nine hundred years.

"*She's new*," said the Gilded King. "*Isn't she.*" His voice was very quiet. "*I can feel it. From here. She's full of room. She's full of things that haven't happened yet.*" He leaned forward. Just a little. "*Do you know how long it's been since I saw anything new?*"

"*You can't have her*," said Wren.

"*I don't want to have her*," said Oszric. Gently. Patiently. As if explaining something to a child. "*I want to keep her. That's different.*" He smiled. "*I want her to be safe. Forever. Exactly as she is. Exactly this new. I want her never to be hurt, or sick, or old, or frightened, or sad.*"

"*Or anything*," said Wren.

The King looked at her for a long moment.

"*Yes*," he said. "*Or anything. Isn't that the point?*"

---

She ran.

On the wind. Up. Away. East. As fast as she'd ever gone. Faster. Over the green and the brick houses and the copse and the gold road wandering away across the shires behind her.

She heard him call after her.

Not loud. He didn't need to be loud. His voice came after her on the air like the heat off a forge. Warm. Deep. Kind.

"*Tell the old man I'm coming*," called the Gilded King. "*Tell him I'm walking. I'll visit everyone on the way.*"

A pause.

"*Tell him to wait for me*," said Oszric. "*I'd like to have tea.*"

---

She cried on the way home.

A hundred miles. On the wind. With her daughter on her chest, asleep again, worn out from laughing. She cried the whole way and the wind took the tears off her face and carried them away behind her.

She didn't know what she was crying for.

For Gaffer Holm. For Roole. For a boy called Pip with his arm round a gold grandmother.

For a great gold man on the edge of a well looking at her daughter like a starving man looks through a window.

She didn't know.

She cried anyway.

---

Seraphine was at Harrow when she got back.

On Ashka. Antho had left the dragon at Harrow. Seraphine had flown down from the Keep that morning, with a satchel of books and ink on her fingers and her spectacles on her nose.

She was sitting at the table in the blue-door cottage. With Arion propped on his pillows beside her. With a book open. With a page of sums.

She looked up as Wren came in.

She looked at her face.

"*You saw him*," she said.

"*I saw him*."

Seraphine looked at her for a long moment. Then down at the page of sums. Then back up.

"*Sit down*," she said. "*There's something I need to tell you. And I don't think you're going to like it.*"

---

"*Eighteen days*," said Seraphine.

They were all round the table. Wren and Seren and Seraphine. Arion in the bed beside it. The baby asleep on the sheepskin by the hearth. The raven on the back of a chair.

"*That's how long they've been gone*," said Seraphine. "*Since they walked through the door. Since Roole's raven said they went in.*"

"*They said fourteen*," said Wren. "*Fourteen days. Fourteen cups.*"

"*I know*."

"*So they're late*," said Wren. "*They're four days late. They're coming. They'll be*—"

"*They're not late*," said Seraphine.

She pushed the page of sums across the table.

---

Wren couldn't read it. It was Seraphine's hand, small and neat, and it was numbers, and it was sums, and it went down the whole page and onto the back.

"*There's no time in the Court*," said Seraphine. "*That's what the prologue to every story says. That's what Maelis said. In the Hall of Lanterns. That's what Orrin told Old Mag.*" She tapped the page. "*Nine hundred years. And they slept. And they woke.*"

"*So*?"

"*So Maelis woke up*," said Seraphine, "*when a third lamp was lit at the Hollowmaw. A year and a half ago. The night before the wedding.*" She tapped the page again. "*And she came through. And she was in our world for nine months.*" Another tap. "*And Orrin was awake in the Court the whole time. Waiting. Kneeling. Nine hundred years.*" She looked up. "*And when Ysenne spoke to Wren in the window, the first night, she said she'd been looking through the crack for weeks. Weeks, Wren. The crack had been open six days.*"

Wren stared at her.

"*It's not the same*," said Seraphine. "*Time. On the other side. It doesn't go the same speed.*" She took her spectacles off. Rubbed her eyes. "*I've been working it out for a week. From Orrin. From Ysenne. From the frost on my spectacles. From the Ferryman, who said the grey country has no days.*" She put the spectacles back on. "*It's slower there. Much slower.*"

"*How much slower*?"

Seraphine looked at the page.

"*Ten*," she said. "*Near enough. A day there is ten days here.*"

---

Nobody said anything.

The fire ticked. The raven shifted on the back of the chair. On the sheepskin by the hearth, the baby sighed in her sleep.

"*Fourteen days*," said Wren slowly. "*In the Court.*"

"*A hundred and forty here*."

"*That's*—"

"*Five months*," said Seraphine. Very quietly. "*Near enough. Twenty weeks.*" She looked at the page. "*They walked in three days after midsummer.*"

"*Twenty weeks after midsummer*," said Arion. From the bed. Very quietly.

They all looked at him.

He was lying on his pillows looking at the boards over the west window. His grey eyes very steady.

"*The end of autumn*," he said. "*The night everyone lights a lantern for the lost.*"

---

Wren put her hands flat on the table.

Her right hand. Pink. Freckled. A tracker's hand. And her left. Pink to the knuckles. Gold from the knuckles to the nails.

"*They don't know*," she said.

"*No*."

"*They think it's fourteen days*. *They think they'll come back through the door and it'll be the end of summer and the King will still be walking and*—" Her voice cracked. "*They don't know.*"

"*No*," said Seraphine. "*They don't.*"

"*Five months*," said Wren. "*He walks five miles a day. Less. He stops at every village.*" She looked at the window. The boarded window. West. "*It's two hundred and forty miles from Greyfall to Harrow. Roole said.*"

Nobody answered.

They didn't need to. They could all do the sums.

---

"*Lantern Night*," said Arion.

He said it to the boards. To the ceiling. To nobody.

"*He'll be here by Lantern Night*," he said. "*The King. For tea.*" He breathed. In. Catch. Out. "*And Caddoc will be at the Knot. And the boys will come out of the door at the edge of the world thinking it's the end of summer.*"

"*Arion*—" said Seren.

"*And I'll be here*," said her husband. "*In this bed. Waiting.*" He turned his head on the pillow. And looked at her. "*I told Lex I'd try. I told Antho I'd try.*" A faint smile. "*I didn't know I'd have to try for five months.*"

Seren didn't say anything.

She took his hand. And held it. And didn't let go.

"*Then you'll try for five months*," she said. Very steady. "*You've been trying for two hundred and thirty-four years. Five months is nothing.*"

"*It's quite a lot*," said Arion. "*At my age.*"

"*I'll bring pies*," said Seren Harrow.

---

That night Wren stood at the edge of the clearing and looked west.

The sun had gone down. The birch wood was dark. The stream was running. Behind her, in the east window of her own cottage, the hornlight sat on the sill. Gold. Kept. The little iron lantern and its horn panes and its wick, all gold, all still. It didn't glow. It would never glow again.

She'd lit a candle beside it. An ordinary one. In a holder Brannoc had made, with no glass in it at all.

It was very small.

She stood at the edge of the birch wood with her daughter asleep in the sling and her gold fingers on the baby's back and looked west over the trees. Over the Vale. Over the downs. Toward the shires. Toward a gold road wandering across the green from village to village, a furlong wide.

Toward the end of it.

She couldn't see it. It was a hundred miles off.

But she thought, if she looked very hard, if she looked the way she'd looked at the ground on the high moors when she was eight and there was no food in the house—

She thought she could see a glow.

Low. On the horizon. In the dark. Gold. Warm. Not a fire. Not a town. Not the sunset; the sunset was long gone.

Like metal in a crucible. Just before it pours.

---

"*Five months*," said Wren Ashdown. Out loud. To the dark. To the baby. To nobody.

Lark didn't wake.

"*He's going to come here*," said Wren. "*And sit at the table. And have tea. And look at you through the window like a man who's been standing in the snow for nine hundred years.*" Her voice was very quiet. "*And your da won't be here. And your uncle won't be here. And your grandda will be in that bed, trying.*"

She put her good hand on the back of the baby's head.

"*And I'll be here*," she said. "*With the wind.*"

She stood there a long time.

Then she went in. And put the baby in her cradle. And sat down in the chair by the window, the one in the loft, under the slope of the roof.

And didn't sleep.

At three in the morning, Lark woke. And looked at the window. Soaped. White.

And laughed.

As if she were dreaming of the wind.

As if she'd seen something in the glass that nobody else could see, and it was the best thing in the world.

Wren sat very still and didn't look.
