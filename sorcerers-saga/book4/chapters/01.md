@@PART Part One: The Last Thread
# The Bell That Rang Flat

The bell had rung once, flat, in the middle of winter. It hadn't rung since.

Lex knew. He'd been listening for it every day.

It hung on a cord round his neck, under his shirt, against his breastbone, where it had hung for two years. Small. Silver. The kind of bell you'd tie round a goat's neck so you'd know which hedge she was in. With a clapper inside that was bent a little, crooked, so that when it rang it rang very slightly off.

Malachi's bell. Hesper's before that. It rang when someone was sad.

It had rung in tune, once, on the rubble of Duskhold, when an old man and a little brother sat down together in the middle of a Well that had come home.

And then it had rung flat. In the night. In a chair by the east window, with his daughter asleep on his chest and his wife asleep against his shoulder and a voice in the dark, out of nowhere, out of the bottom of the world, saying his name.

*Lex. Lad. Wake up.*

*They're coming for the Key.*

He'd opened his eyes. He'd sat very still in the chair with Lark's warm weight on his chest and listened to the house. The fire ticking. The stream outside. Wren breathing. Nothing else.

He'd told himself it was a dream.

He'd told himself that every night since.

---

"*Good morning*," said Lex to the fire.

It was banked down to a heap of grey ash with a red eye in the middle. He knelt and blew on it, gently, the way you'd wake someone who'd been up all night.

It woke. Flickered. Caught the kindling. Climbed.

*Good morning*, said the fire. Not in words. Just a warmth. A lift. A small bright gladness. The way a dog is glad when you come down the stairs.

*[Good morning*,] said Ember.

Lex sat back on his heels.

*You're up early*.

*[I'm always up. I don't sleep. I'm a piece of your father pressed into your blood like a maker's mark on a horseshoe. Horseshoes don't sleep.]* A pause. *[You're up early. You were up at two. And at four. And at a quarter past five.]*

*She was hungry*.

*[She was hungry at two*,] said Ember. *[At four she was wet. At a quarter past five she was neither. At a quarter past five she was asleep, and you were lying in the dark looking at the ceiling with your hand on the bell.]*

Lex didn't answer.

He fed the fire another stick. It took it gladly.

*[You dreamed it again*,] said Ember. Quietly.

*Yes*.

*[The same*?]

*The same*, thought Lex. *Dark. Something wound round something. A voice.* He watched the flames climb. *He said my name. He said wake up. That's all. That's all it ever is.*

*[Elric*.]

*Yes*.

Ember was quiet for a while.

*[I don't like it*,] it said at last. *[I want to put that on the record. In the second volume. In a footnote. "Ember did not like it."]*

*Noted*.

*[Underlined*,] said Ember.

---

The cottage at Harrow was four rooms and a loft. Grey Vale stone and new oak beams. A door that stuck in wet weather. A window on the east side, deep and wide, with a sill broad enough to sit on.

And in the window, a small iron lantern. Plain. With one word cut into the base, where only someone who picked it up would see it.

*HERE*.

It was still lit. It was always lit. Lex had lit it with his own fire on the first night home from the Blightmarch, and it had never gone out. It wasn't waiting for anyone. It was just there. For whoever was in the room.

This morning, that was Lex. And the porridge pot. And, upstairs in the loft, his wife. And, beside her, in a cradle he'd made out of ash and iron with *KNIT* on the rockers so it would never come apart, his daughter.

She was three months old.

He still didn't believe it.

---

He heard her before he saw her.

Not crying. Lark didn't cry much. She'd cried on the rubble on the night she was born, one long furious howl, and then stopped, as if she'd made her point. Since then she mostly *talked*. In a language of her own that seemed to be made entirely of vowels and indignation.

"*Aaah*," said the loft.

Lex looked up.

"*Aaah. Ah. Eh*."

"*She's awake*," said Wren's voice. Muffled. Into a pillow. "*She's your daughter. Go and get her.*"

"*She's your daughter too*."

"*Not before porridge she isn't*."

He went up the ladder.

---

The loft was dim. Grey light coming in through the little window under the slope of the roof. Wren was a heap under the quilt, with a fox-red tangle of hair on the pillow and one bare arm flung out across the place where he'd been.

The cradle was on the other side of the bed. Under the window.

He looked in.

His daughter looked back.

She was lying on her back, kicking. Small. Pink. Round in the face, like Wren. Dark-haired, like him. With a fluff of it standing up on top of her head like the crest of a bird. Her eyes had been grey when she was born. They were going brown now. Wren's brown. Slowly. From the edges in. Like a field going from frost to earth.

And her hands.

They were too big. Everyone said so. Brannoc had said so on the first day, holding her by the pool. Square hands. Broad in the palm. Short strong fingers. A smith's hands. Too big for the rest of her, the way a puppy's paws are too big. As if she'd been made in the wrong order, hands first, and the rest of her was still catching up.

They were reaching for him now. Both of them. Opening and closing. Grabbing at the air.

"*Morning*," said Lex.

"*Aah*," said Lark. Severely.

He picked her up.

She grabbed his nose.

---

She had a grip like a vice. Everyone said that too. Hedda had said it. Old Mag had said it, cackling, when Lark got hold of her one bright black eye's worth of spectacles and wouldn't let go. Tilda said she'd seen dogs with weaker jaws.

She had his nose now, in one square fist, and his collar in the other, and she was hanging on with an expression of fierce satisfaction, like someone who has caught a fish and has no intention of letting it back in the river.

"*Ow*," said Lex.

"*Eh*," said Lark.

"*That's my nose*."

She pulled it.

"*She knows it's your nose*," said Wren. From the pillow. Not moving. "*That's why she's pulling it. She's got good taste.*"

"*She's got good grip*."

"*She gets that from you*." Wren rolled over. Looked at them. Her brown eyes half shut. Her face soft and creased with sleep. "*Everything awkward she gets from you. Her hands. Her hair. Her temper. The way she glares at porridge.*"

"*She doesn't eat porridge*."

"*She glares at yours*," said Wren. "*Every morning. Like you glare at the bellows when they won't catch.*" She yawned. Enormously. "*It's very funny. You have exactly the same face.*"

Lark let go of his nose.

And reached up. Past his face. Over his shoulder. Toward the window. Toward the beam above it.

Where a thing hung on a red ribbon, turning slowly in the grey light.

Old. Black. Square-headed. Small.

A horseshoe nail.

---

They'd hung it there on the first night home. Wren's idea.

*She is not chewing on a nail*, Wren had said. Holding it up between finger and thumb. Looking at it the way she'd look at a dead mouse the cat had brought in. *I don't care if it's from Brannoc. I don't care if it's a family tradition. I don't care if every smith since the beginning of the world has given his apprentice's baby a nail. She is three weeks old. She is not having a nail.*

*She held it*, Lex had said. *By the pool. She wouldn't let go.*

*She'd hold a live coal if you gave it to her*, said Wren. *That's not an argument. That's a warning.*

So they'd tied it to a red ribbon. And hung it from the beam over the cradle. Where Lark could see it.

She'd looked at it every morning since. First thing. Before she looked at either of them. Reaching. Opening and closing her square hands.

As if she was saying good morning to it.

*[She does it every morning*,] said Ember. Very softly. *[Without fail.]*

*I know*.

*[You do it to the fire*.]

Lex looked at his daughter. Reaching for a horseshoe nail on a ribbon in the grey morning light.

*I know*, he thought.

---

He made the porridge one-handed, with Lark on his hip.

It was a skill. He'd had to learn it. In the first weeks he'd tried putting her down, in the cradle, in a basket by the fire, on a sheepskin on the floor, and every time she'd looked at him with her round brown eyes and said, quite clearly, in her language of vowels, that this was unacceptable. So now he made the porridge with one hand and held her with the other and she watched the spoon go round and round in the pot with the grave interest of a master inspecting an apprentice's weld.

"*It's porridge*," Lex told her.

"*Ah*."

"*You'll like it. When you've got teeth.*"

"*Eh*."

"*Your mam likes it with honey*."

"*Your mam likes it with honey and that's not a joke*," said Wren, coming down the ladder. In one of his shirts. In thick wool socks. With her hair everywhere. "*Your mam likes it with honey because your da can't cook anything else. Isn't that right?*"

"*I can cook eggs*."

"*He can burn eggs*," Wren told Lark. Taking her. Settling her on her own hip, the way she did, easily, like a girl who'd carried two little brothers about on a farm. "*He can burn eggs very well. He's a Worldforger. He can burn anything.*"

Lark grabbed her braid.

"*Ow*," said Wren.

---

They ate at the table by the east window, with the lantern between them.

Outside, the birch wood was grey and wet and dripping. The snow had gone a week ago, all at once, in a warm wind from the south. The stream was loud. The track down to the Ashbourne road was mud. On the far side of the stream, a hundred paces off, through the bare white trunks of the birches, Lex could see the other cottage.

Small. Stone. One storey. With a blue door.

And a ramp up to it. Of oak planks. Gentle. Wide enough for a wheeled chair.

The chimney was smoking.

"*They're up*," said Wren.

"*She's always up*." Lex looked at the smoke. "*She says she's been getting up at dawn for thirteen years. She says she doesn't know how to stop.*"

"*And him*?"

Lex didn't answer straight away.

"*Depends on the day*," he said.

---

Brannoc had built it.

In a fortnight. In the first two weeks of winter, after they all came home from the Blightmarch. He'd brought twelve of the older students from the Forge and Hedda and Tilda and a cart full of stone, and they'd come up the track through the birch wood in the snow, and he'd stood in the clearing on the far side of the stream with his hands on his hips and his breath smoking and said, *Here*.

*Here what*? Lex had said.

*Here's where it goes*, said Brannoc. *A house. For your mam and your da. Across the water. Near enough to shout. Far enough you don't have to listen to each other snore.* He'd looked at Lex. *They can't live with you, lad. You've a wife and a baby and four rooms. And they can't live in Ashbourne, not with him the way he is. People would come by. People would want to look.*

He'd turned back to the clearing.

*And he can't do ladders*, said Brannoc Holt. *So it'll be one floor. And it'll have a ramp. And the door will be wide.*

They'd built it in fourteen days. In the snow. With Brannoc swearing at the mortar and Hedda swearing at Brannoc and Tilda making the hinges and Seren, on the last day, coming across the stream barefoot in the snow with a pot of blue paint she'd found God knew where and painting the door.

*Blue*? Brannoc had said.

*Blue*, said Seren. *Every house I ever lived in, somebody else chose the door. My mam. My mam's landlords. A Warden-Architect with no taste. I want a blue one.*

*It's very blue*.

*Good*, said Seren Vey.

---

He went across after breakfast.

Over the stream on the stepping stones. Through the birches. Up the oak ramp. He knocked on the blue door, and didn't wait, and went in.

It was one room, mostly. A big one. Low-beamed. With a hearth at one end and a bed at the other behind a curtain, and in between, a long table under a window that faced west, so they'd have the sunset.

His father was at the window.

---

He was in the chair.

The wheeled one. Ash and iron. Brannoc had made it in a night, on Lantern Night, on the rubble of Duskhold, out of a broken cart and a good deal of swearing. It had big wheels at the sides and a little one at the back and a footboard, and on the arms, where his father's hands rested, Brannoc had cut two words. One on each side. In his big careful hand.

*STEADY*, on the left.

*HOME*, on the right.

His father sat in it with a blanket over his knees and another round his shoulders, looking out of the window at the birch wood and the grey sky.

He was very old.

Lex had stopped being shocked by it. Mostly. He'd had a winter to get used to it. But every morning, coming in through the blue door, there was still a moment, a half-breath, before he got used to it again.

His father had been forty-five when Lex met him. To look at. Silver at the temples. Straight-backed. A quiet voice. A face like a closed book.

He'd aged a year a week, all last spring and summer, as two hundred years came due. And then ten in one night. And then, on Lantern Night, pouring the stolen light back out of himself in a dark room at the top of the Spire, he'd gone past old. Past counting. Past anything.

Mal had made room for him, after. A little. A year. Perhaps two. *Ordinary time*, Seraphine said. *The kind everyone gets. He'll age now the way anyone does. Day by day. Not a year a week. Just a day a day.*

But he'd started it from here.

His face was a walnut. Brown and creased and folded in on itself. His hair a white wisp, thin as smoke, that stood up off his skull when the door opened and let in a draught. His hands on the arms of the chair were spotted and thin and twisted at the knuckles, the skin over them as fine as old paper. His shoulders were narrow under the blanket. He was small. He'd been tall once. He was small now. A little bent old man in a chair at a window, like a heron in winter.

His eyes were the same.

Grey. Clear. Sharp. Turning from the window as the door opened.

Seeing him.

"*Good morning*," said Arion Vey.

---

"*Good morning*," said Lex.

"*You're late*."

"*I'm not late. It's barely light.*"

"*Your mother's been up since dawn*," said his father. "*She's been reading to me for an hour. I'm on volume two. I've reached the part where I'm sixty-one and having trouble with my knees.*" His mouth twitched. "*It's riveting.*"

"*It's not*," said Seren.

She was at the hearth. Kneeling. Small and square and barefoot, with her sleeves pushed up and her greying hair tied back in a knot, poking at the fire with a stick. She looked round at Lex and smiled. The smile went all the way up into her eyes. It always did. It was the most Harrow thing about her.

"*It's not riveting*," said his mother. "*It's a list. He wrote down his blood pressure every morning for forty weeks. In a column.*"

"*It was a careful record*."

"*It was a column*," said Seren. "*Your father is the only man in the history of the world who could make his own death boring.*" She stood up. Dusted her hands off on her skirt. Came over and kissed Lex on the cheek. The left one. The one with the mark on it. She always kissed that one. "*Morning, love.*"

"*Morning, Mam*."

"*Did she sleep*?"

"*Twice*."

"*Twice is good*." Seren went back to the fire. "*You slept twice. Antho slept never. Not once. For a year. I used to walk him up and down the cottage singing every verse I knew until I'd run out and then I'd start making them up.*" She poked the fire. "*Your father used to come home from the Spire and find me asleep standing up in the doorway with a baby on my shoulder.*"

She stopped.

Lex saw her stop. A small hitch. A half-breath. Her hand with the stick, held still above the fire.

"*I don't remember that*," said Seren. Quietly. "*I just said it. But I don't remember it. I remember Antho. I remember the doorway. I remember the singing.*" She was looking into the fire. "*There's nobody there. Coming home. There's just—a space. The shape of somebody.*"

Nobody said anything.

His father didn't move. He sat in the chair with his spotted hands on the arms, on *STEADY* and *HOME*, and looked at his wife's back. His face very still.

"*Page two hundred and four*," said Arion Vey. Gently. "*Of the letter. The forty-page one.*" His voice was a thread. "*I came home at midnight in the rain. You were asleep in the doorway. I took him off your shoulder and you didn't wake up. I carried you both to bed.*"

Seren didn't turn round.

"*I'll read it again*," she said. "*Tonight.*"

"*You've read it eleven times*."

"*Then it'll be twelve*," said his mother. And went on poking the fire.

---

The Ferryman had given her one memory back.

Just one. As a gift. Not a refund. On the grass beside the pool at the Blightmarch, at dawn, in the first week of winter. The first one. The steps of the Spire, in the rain. A young man in a grey coat coming round the corner, very casually, as if he just happened to be passing. Turning back. Twice.

She had that now. She'd had it all winter.

The rest of him was still in the river.

Thirty years. Every day. The courtship and the wedding and the cottage and the boys. The doorways and the midnights and the rain. All of it paid to a tall grey eyeless man on a shingle shore thirteen years ago, as the price of crossing. *The memory you would least wish to lose.*

She didn't remember marrying him.

She'd married him again anyway. Sort of. In her way. In the only way she could. She'd come home to an old man she didn't know and looked at him in a cold room at the Lantern Keep and said *you have a nice voice*, and then she'd stayed.

She read him his own letters at night. And his grey book in the mornings. And she laughed at his jokes, which were terrible, and he laughed at hers, which were worse. And when people came up the track and saw them sitting together at the west window in the evening, a little old man in a chair and a square barefoot woman twenty years younger than his face and two hundred younger than his bones, with their heads bent together over a book, they stopped. And went quietly away again.

*[She's courting him*,] Ember had said once. In the first week. Amazed. *[Lex. She's courting him. She doesn't remember being married to him so she's courting him. She brought him a pie.]*

*It was a good pie*.

*[That's not the point*,] said Ember. *[The point is it's the most extraordinary thing I've ever seen. And I've seen a dragon hatch.]*

---

Lex sat down at the long table, across from his father, under the window.

His father looked at him.

He'd always been able to do that. Even before. Even when his face was forty-five and closed like a book. He'd look at you with his grey eyes, steady, patient, and wait, and you'd find you were telling him things you hadn't meant to tell anyone.

"*You're not sleeping*," said Arion.

"*There's a baby*."

"*There's a baby*," agreed his father. "*And there's something else.*"

Lex didn't say anything.

"*You've had your hand on that bell*," said his father, "*every time you've come in this door for six weeks. You put it there when you think I'm not looking.*" He tilted his head. A small movement. Like a bird. "*Has it rung?*"

---

Lex hadn't meant to tell him.

He'd decided that. In the night. Lying in the loft with his hand on the bell. *Don't tell him. Don't put this on him. He's got a year. Maybe two. He's sitting in a chair by a window with a woman reading him his own letters and he's happy. For the first time in two hundred years he's happy.*

*Don't put Elric on him.*

"*Once*," said Lex. "*In the winter. In the night.*"

"*Flat*?"

"*Flat*."

His father's hands didn't move on the arms of the chair.

"*And*?" he said.

Lex looked at the table. At the grain of the wood. At the pale ring where his mother's teacup stood every evening.

"*And I heard a voice*," he said.

---

He told them.

All of it. It didn't take long. There wasn't much. The dark. The thing wound round the other thing. The voice. The same three sentences, over and over, every few nights, for six weeks.

*Lex. Lad. Wake up.*

*They're coming for the Key.*

When he said the name, his mother turned round from the fire.

His father didn't move at all.

That was the thing Lex would remember, after. Not that his father flinched. Not that his face changed. That it *didn't*. That it went perfectly, utterly still, the way water goes still in a bucket when you stop carrying it. The way his face had gone still, two years ago, in the gold room at Avaloria, when Lex had said, *Elric opened the wards.*

His grey eyes didn't blink.

"*Elric*," said Arion Vey.

"*Yes*."

"*Elric Thorne*."

"*He's the only one who calls me lad*."

His father didn't say anything for a long time.

Then he looked out of the window. At the birch wood. At the grey sky. At nothing.

"*He's in the Knot*," said Arion. Very quietly. "*He's been in the Knot for three years. Holding the Lockward. He gave back his threads at the lid and went in and wrapped himself round the Key and he hasn't said a word since.*" His voice didn't change. "*Not one. Not to anyone. I asked. Seraphine asked. We tried to reach him. The first winter.*" A pause. "*He doesn't talk.*"

"*He talked*," said Lex.

---

His mother came and sat down.

At the end of the table. Between them. Her hands flat on the wood. Her square freckled hands. She looked from one of them to the other.

"*The Key*," she said. "*The Key of Shadows. The one that opened the lid.*"

"*Yes*."

"*And someone's coming for it*." She looked at Lex. "*Did he say who?*"

"*No*."

"*He didn't have to*." Seren's voice was very level. She'd grown up in a dozen cottages before she was nine, with a mother who kept her voice level when the moths came, and she'd learned. "*Oszric. The Gilded King. He's awake. We know he's awake.*" She turned her hands over on the table. Palms up. "*Mam used to say it in the songs. The old verses. The ones she only sang at the end of summer.*" She half-closed her eyes. "*Gold for the eldest. Glass for the vain. Iron for the one who never laughed. Thorns for the one who listened.*"

"*And silver for the moth*," said Arion. Not looking away from the window.

"*And silver for the moth*," said Seren. "*Five. Five went through. The moth's dead.*" She looked at Lex. "*Four left.*"

---

"*What does the Key do*?" said Lex.

He knew. He'd held it. He'd watched Elric turn it, on the black glass at the lid of the world, and felt the lid lift. But he wanted to hear his father say it. His father, who'd been Warden-Architect of the Spire for two hundred years. Who'd known the net better than anyone alive.

His father turned from the window.

"*It unwrites*," said Arion. "*Whatever it's turned against. That's what Aurelian made it for. He wove the net, and then he made a key to unweave it. In case.*" His mouth twisted a little. "*He always made a key. He made a lock with a slot for a ward in it, because he was afraid someone would need to bar it. He made a key to undo his own net, because he was afraid he'd got it wrong.*"

"*He did get it wrong*."

"*He got it wrong in a great many ways*," said his father. "*And then you wove a new one.*" He looked at Lex. Steadily. "*And then you gave it away.*"

Lex didn't say anything.

He remembered it. In the gold. At the Heart. The whole net in his hands, every thread, every knot, every Ceiling and every Stone, new-woven, shining. And the Root. The place where a hand went. The place where Aurelian's hand had been for nine hundred years, and then his father's, and then his.

And he'd taken his hand off it.

*It isn't mine*, he'd said. *It isn't anyone's. That's the point.*

"*There's nobody holding it*," said his father. Very quietly. "*The net. There's been nobody holding it for two years. It runs on its own now. The way a river does. It doesn't need a hand on it.*" His spotted fingers moved on the arm of the chair. On *STEADY*. "*But it could take one.*"

Lex went cold.

"*If someone turned the Key*," said Arion Vey. "*In the Knot. With the bar out. With nobody's hand on the net.*" He looked at his son. "*I don't know what would happen, Lex. I don't think anyone does. Aurelian might have. He's not here to ask.*"

"*Guess*."

His father was quiet for a long moment.

"*I think the net would ask them what they wanted*," he said. "*And I think it would give it to them.*"

---

*[I don't like this either*,] said Ember. In the back of Lex's head. Very small. *[Another footnote.]*

Nobody else heard it. Nobody else ever did, except, sometimes, his mother, when she was close. She'd heard it in the deep. *Hello, Seren*, it had said. And she'd known it. Known its voice. Known whose voice it was.

She looked at Lex now. At his face. And he saw her hear it.

She didn't say anything. She just reached across the table and put her hand on his.

---

"*Why now*?" said Lex. "*Why's he talking now? After three years?*"

"*Because they're awake*," said his mother.

"*Because he can feel them*," said his father.

They'd said it together. They looked at each other. A little startled. The way people look when they've finished each other's sentences for the first time.

Seren looked away first. A little pink.

"*He's at the bottom of the net*," said Arion. "*In the middle. Everything runs through the Knot. Every thread. If something's pulling on the edge, he'd feel it there first.*" He looked down at his hands. "*Like a spider. In the middle of a web. Feeling a fly land at the edge.*"

"*A big fly*," said Seren.

"*A gold one*," said Arion. "*With a crown.*"

---

Lex stayed until the light was full.

They talked about other things. They made themselves. His mother made tea. His father told a story about a Warden in the year eleven-forty who'd tried to arrest a goat for unlatticed sorcery, which might have been true and might not; his father's stories were like that. Seren laughed at it. Arion laughed at her laughing. Lex sat at the long table under the west window and watched them and drank his tea.

And all the while his father's hands didn't leave the arms of the chair.

*STEADY*, on the left. *HOME*, on the right.

His knuckles were white.

---

When Lex got up to go, his father caught his sleeve.

His grip was weak now. Lex could have pulled away without noticing. He didn't. He stood by the chair and looked down.

"*If he talks again*," said Arion. "*If he says anything. Anything at all.*"

"*I'll tell you*."

"*Tell me exactly*." His father's grey eyes were very bright. "*The words. How he said them. Whether he sounded—*" He stopped.

"*Whether he sounded what*?"

His father let go of his sleeve.

"*Nothing*," said Arion Vey. And turned back to the window. To the birch wood. To the grey. "*It doesn't matter. Go home. Kiss your daughter. Tell Wren the pie was excellent.*"

"*What pie*?"

"*Your mother's taking you a pie*," said his father. "*This afternoon. It's a surprise. Act surprised.*"

"*Arion*," said Seren.

"*He'd have found out*."

"*That's not the point of a surprise*."

"*I've been alive two hundred and thirty-four years*," said Arion Vey. "*I've had a great many surprises. I've found I prefer them with a little warning.*"

---

His mother walked him to the door.

She stood on the oak ramp in her bare feet, in the wet, with her arms folded. Looking out at the birch wood. At the stream. At the other cottage, across the water, with the lantern in its east window.

"*They were friends*," she said. "*Him and Elric. Forty years.*"

"*I know*."

"*He told me*." She didn't look at him. "*In the letter. The forty-page one. Page thirty. About Elric. About the night at Caldris.*" She was quiet. "*It's the only page in the whole letter where his hand shakes.*"

Lex didn't know what to say.

"*He doesn't talk about him*," said his mother. "*He talks about everything. About Aurelian. About the Spire. About the price of bread in twelve-ninety. He doesn't talk about Elric.*" She turned and looked at him. With her Harrow eyes. Brown and warm and very steady. "*I think he's the only person in the world your father hasn't forgiven.*"

"*He betrayed him*."

"*So did you*," said Seren. "*Once. You told me. At Avaloria. You said you hated him. You said you'd never forgive him for sealing you.*" Her mouth twitched. "*He's forgiven you.*"

"*That's different*."

"*Is it*?"

She didn't wait for an answer. She went back in. And closed the blue door behind her.

---

He was halfway across the stepping stones when the raven came.

He heard it before he saw it. Everyone always did. A great harsh croak out of the grey sky, and then the beat of wings, heavy and close, and then it was coming down through the birches in a tumble of black feathers and landing on the stone in front of him so hard it nearly went in the stream.

It wasn't Quill.

Quill was at the Forge. Quill lived on the chimney in a nest of stolen spoons and had opinions about everyone. This was bigger. Older. Its feathers ragged. One eye milky. A thin silver ring round its leg, and a scrap of paper rolled tight and tied with grey thread.

One of Roole's.

Commander Daxian Roole of the Keepers, who had taken Fenn's staff to the grave and dug it in with one arm. Who lived at Greyfall now. At the western edge. At the last fort before the Veil. With his Keepers and his ravens and the wind that never stopped.

The raven looked at Lex with its one good eye.

"*Smith*," it croaked.

It always said that. Roole had taught it. Lex suspected Roole thought it was funny.

He knelt on the stepping stone. Untied the paper. Unrolled it.

It was Roole's hand. Small. Square. Plain. The hand of a man who wrote reports.

It was very short.

---

*Vey,*

*The ground at the foot of the Veil has gone gold. For about a mile round. Grass, stones, everything. We found it four days ago. It's spreading. Slowly. A few feet a day.*

*There was a flock on it. Forty head. Gold. Standing.*

*There was a boy with them. Col Ashby, twelve, from the croft at Westerling. Gold. Standing. Mid-step.*

*He's not dead.*

*His eyes are moving.*

*Come.*

*— R.*

---

Lex read it twice.

Then he stood up. On the stepping stone. In the middle of the stream. With the water running loud and brown and cold round his boots, and the paper in his hand.

Behind him, through the birches, a blue door. An old man at a west window, with his knuckles white on the arms of his chair.

In front of him, through the birches, a small stone cottage. A lantern in the east window, lit. Not waiting for anyone.

And upstairs, in a cradle under a slanting roof, his daughter. Reaching up, with her too-big square hands, for a horseshoe nail on a red ribbon.

*He wants the smith's child*.

He didn't know where that thought came from. Nobody had said it. Not his father. Not his mother. Not the voice in the dark.

But he knew it. The way you know iron's hot before you touch it.

*[Lex*,] said Ember.

The bell on the cord round his neck didn't ring.

It didn't have to.
