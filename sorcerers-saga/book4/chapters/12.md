# Gold Dust

Old Mag said the baby had the look of a woman who'd been cheated at market.

"*There*," she said. Cackling. "*There. See it? Just there. Round the mouth.*" She leaned forward in her chair by the fire, with her shawl round her shoulders and her cat in her lap and her one bright black eye fixed on Lark. "*That's a face that's been sold a lame horse and knows it.*"

Lark, on Wren's knee, glared at her.

"*She's not cheated*," said Wren. "*She's just like that.*"

"*She's like her father*," said Old Mag. "*He had that face at four. Stood in my doorway with his thumb in his mouth glaring at my cat like it owed him money.*" She stroked the cat. "*Now look at him. Worldforger. Weaves the whole world new.*" She grinned. Toothless. "*Still glares at my cat.*"

---

It was a good morning.

Wren would think that, after. Over and over. That it had been a good morning. That she'd come down the river path from Harrow with Lark in the sling in the spring sunshine, three miles, walking, because Lex was at the pool with his father and Seren and the cottage was too quiet. That she'd stopped at the Forge first, and Brannoc had come out of the long hall with soot to his elbows and seen the baby and stopped dead in the doorway, the way he always did, as if he'd forgotten for a moment that she existed and then remembered all at once.

*Give her here*, he'd said.

*You're covered in soot*.

*She's a smith's daughter*, said Brannoc Holt. *She'd better get used to it*.

And Lark had grabbed his beard. And hung on. And Brannoc had stood in the doorway of the Forge with forty students watching and a baby hanging off his beard by both fists and laughed till he cried.

And then she'd come on to Old Mag's. The cottage by the chapel. With the apple tree in the garden behind it in full white bloom, the way it had been every spring since the little one began to rise, every branch so heavy with blossom it bent down to brush the grass.

A good morning.

---

"*Two of them*," said Old Mag.

She said it quietly. Out of nowhere. With her black eye on the fire.

Wren didn't need to ask what she meant.

"*Wim*," said Old Mag. "*And Edda Tobbin. Edda and I were girls together. We used to steal plums off the old Reeve's wall and he'd set his dog on us.*" She stroked the cat. "*She had a bad heart. She'd had it for ten years. She was ninety.*"

"*I'm sorry*."

"*Don't be sorry for her*," said Old Mag. "*She's not sorry. She's not anything. She's sitting in her chair by her fire, gold, looking at her son.*" The black eye glittered. "*Be sorry for him. Rafe. Sitting outside her door every day. Twenty-six years old. Level forty. Big as a barn.*" She shook her head. "*Weeping like a child. Because he couldn't bear to hear her heart stop. And now he has to look at her eyes.*"

Wren held Lark tighter.

"*It came to me*," said Old Mag. "*Did you know?*"

---

Wren looked up.

"*Two nights after*," said Old Mag. "*At the window. That one.*" She nodded at it. The little window by the fire, with its soaped panes, and the apple tree white beyond it. "*Tapped on the glass. Very polite. Asked to come in.*"

"*What did you say*?"

"*I said, you can't, dear, the window doesn't open, it's been painted shut since the old king died*." Old Mag cackled. "*So it talked through the crack. Lovely voice. Like an old man reading a book in a library. Said it had a gift. Said I'd never have to be afraid.*"

"*And*?"

Old Mag stroked her cat.

"*I said, I'm not afraid now, dear*," she said. "*I'm ninety-six. I've been afraid of a great many things. Wolves. The Gloam. My husband's mother. I haven't got room to be afraid of dying. There's too much else in here.*" She tapped her chest. "*And then I said, you sound very tired. Have you eaten?*"

Wren stared at her.

"*It didn't answer that*," said Old Mag. "*It went away. Quite quickly.*" She looked at the window. "*I think I upset it.*"

---

Lark had gone quiet.

Wren noticed it. The way you notice a sound stopping. The baby on her knee had stopped glaring at Old Mag and was looking at the window instead. At the soaped panes. At the apple tree beyond.

Frowning.

"*What is it, love*?"

Lark didn't answer. Of course she didn't. She was five months old. She only had vowels.

But she was frowning. Deeply. Fiercely. At the window. And her square fists were opening and closing.

The way they did when she reached for the nail.

---

Wren stood up.

She didn't know why. Something. A feeling. The rooftop feeling. The one that ran down the back of her neck like cold water when she'd seen something move in the street below before she knew what it was.

She went to the window. And breathed on the soap, so it went clear for a moment, the way soap does when it's damp.

And looked out.

At the apple tree. White. Heavy with blossom.

At the garden wall. At the chapel beyond it. At the green, and the well, and the long low roofs of the village, and the river, and the fields going up toward the downs in the spring sun.

At the sky.

In the west, low down, over the downs, the sky was gold.

---

Not sunset. It was ten in the morning. Not cloud. Not dust from a road. A haze. A shimmer. Low. Wide. Spreading across the whole of the western sky like a stain spreading through a cloth. Glittering.

Coming.

She could see it move. That was the thing. She could see it come over the brow of the downs and pour down the slope toward the Vale. Slowly. Like smoke from a burning field. Like a great soft gold blanket being drawn across the land.

And everything it passed over, it left behind it shining.

---

"*Mag*," said Wren. Very quietly. "*Do you have any wet cloths?*"

The old woman looked at her.

"*Wet cloths*," said Wren. "*As many as you can find. Sheets. Towels. Anything. Wet them in the bucket. Stuff them under the door. Round the window.*" She was already moving. Settling Lark in the sling against her chest. Pulling the straps tight. "*Don't open the door. Don't open it for anyone. Not for me. Not for the King of Caldor.*"

"*Wren*—"

"*It's dust*," said Wren Ashdown. "*Gold dust. On the wind.*" She looked at the old woman. "*It's him.*"

---

She went out the back.

Through the kitchen. Through the little door into the garden. Into the spring sunshine and the white blossom and the smell of apples that weren't there yet.

The sky over the downs was gold.

She could hear it now. Not a roar. Not a wind. A *hiss*. Faint. Dry. Like sand poured slowly out of a sack. Coming from everywhere at once. From the whole western sky.

She climbed the apple tree.

She didn't think about it. Her body did it. The way her body had always done things, before she was a mother, before she sat in chairs at three in the morning. Her hands found the branches and her feet found the crook of the trunk and she went up, through the blossom, with the baby strapped to her chest, petals falling round her like snow.

From the top she could see the whole Vale.

---

The gold was on the downs.

It had come over the brow and was pouring down the long slope toward the village. Wren could see it settle. On the fields. On the hedges. On a flock of sheep in a high pasture that didn't run, that didn't even look up, that went on grazing in the spring grass until the gold came down on them and they were gold sheep in a gold field, heads down, still.

On a cart on the Thistledown road. A man on the seat. A horse in the shafts. Gold.

On the mill at the top of the village. On its wheel, turning. Turning slower. Slower.

Stopped.

Gold.

---

And in the middle of it, in the middle of the great soft glittering cloud as it came down the hill toward the village—

A shape.

Thin. Tall. Bent. Made of the same gold as the cloud, so you could only see it because it was thicker there. Darker. A gallows of dust with two dim sparks for eyes. Walking. Not hurrying. Walking down the slope in the middle of his storm with his twig-arms held out to either side.

Like a man scattering seed.

---

"*Forgive me*," said a voice.

It came out of the cloud. Out of the hiss. Out of every grain of gold in the air. Soft. Dry. Kind. Like an old man reading a book in a library.

It came across the whole Vale.

"*Forgive me*," said Orrin. "*Good people of Ashbourne. I'm sorry for this. Truly.*" The cloud came on. Down the hill. Over the top fields. "*His Majesty asked me to come. He said they don't understand, Orrin. They keep saying no. They say no to the gift because they can't see it.*" A sigh. Like dust settling. "*He said, show them.*"

The cloud reached the first house.

"*So I'm showing you*," said the voice. "*I'm so sorry. It won't hurt.*"

---

Wren heard the screaming start.

At the top of the village. By the mill. Somebody had been outside. Somebody had looked up and seen the gold coming and not understood, and not run, and not gone in. A woman's scream. High. Short.

Cut off.

Not stopped. *Cut off*. In the middle. Like a candle snuffed.

Wren stood at the top of the apple tree with petals falling round her and her daughter strapped to her chest and her heart going like a hare's.

And then she did the thing she hadn't done in five months.

She stepped off.

---

Into the wind.

There wasn't one. Not a real one. It was a still spring morning and the gold was coming on its own, the way smoke comes, the way fog comes, drifting.

She didn't need there to be one.

She was a Mistwalker. She'd stood on the wind above the Blightmarch on Lantern Night and held a hundred miles of air still. She'd stepped off the top of the Spire into a gale and walked down it like a staircase. She'd carried the First Breath in her lungs for three days.

She called the wind.

And it came.

---

It came up under her feet like a hand. Like a step. Like the first stair of a staircase made of air. And she stood on it. Above the apple tree. Above the garden. Above the white blossom, with the petals swirling round her in the sudden gust.

And Lark, strapped to her chest, laughed.

Wren looked down.

Her daughter's face was turned up to the sky. Her eyes wide. Her mouth open. Her square fists flung out to either side, opening and closing on the moving air.

Laughing.

Not a giggle. Not the small surprised hiccup of a baby who's been tickled. A *laugh*. Loud and delighted and astonished, the laugh of someone who has just discovered the best thing in the world and can't believe nobody told her about it before.

The wind took the laugh and carried it away over the roofs.

Wren felt something crack open in her chest.

"*You like it*," she said. Out loud. In the air. Over Ashbourne. With the gold coming down the hill. "*Oh, love. You like it.*"

Lark laughed harder.

---

She took the wind and spun it.

Round. Round her. Round the baby. A tight bright fast whirl of moving air, a yard thick, spinning like a top. Like the eye of a storm. Like the inside of a shell.

*Dust can't land on what's moving*, she thought. *It can't settle. It can't stick. It needs still air. Still ground. Still skin.*

*So I won't be still*.

She ran.

---

Across the roofs.

She hadn't done it in a year. More. Not since before her belly got too big to balance. Not since Highcrest, in the rain, with Lex behind her, laughing, the two of them leaping from tile to tile across the city while the Wardens shouted in the street below.

She did it now.

With her daughter on her chest and a whirlwind round her and the gold coming down into the top of the village like snow. From Old Mag's roof to the chapel's. From the chapel to the long low thatch of the Crooked Kettle. Her feet barely touching. The wind lifting her across the gaps.

Every window she passed, she shouted.

"*In! Get in! Shut the doors! Wet cloths! Under the doors! Round the windows! Don't go out! Don't go out!*"

---

The gold reached the green.

She saw it come. Down the main street from the mill. A soft glittering wave, rolling, no higher than a man, settling on everything it touched. On the cobbles. On the cart standing outside the chandler's. On a dog, asleep in a doorway, that lifted its head as the gold came and was a gold dog lifting its head, forever.

On the well.

On the Ashbourne Stone.

---

Wren stopped.

On the roof of the Crooked Kettle. In the middle of her whirlwind. With the baby laughing on her chest.

The Stone stood on the green where it had always stood. Grey. Ancient. Taller than a man. The Lattice Stone of Ashbourne, where every child in the Vale had put their hand for their Kindling for nine hundred years. Where she'd watched Lex Unbind the whole village, two summers ago, with his hand on it and gold in his eyes. Where Ivy Moss had been Kindled with an unrecognized class, beside a hole in the ground that breathed.

The gold came down on it.

And it was gold.

---

She felt it.

Not in her head. Not in her ears. Somewhere else. Somewhere deep. The way you feel a bell stop ringing in another room.

The Stone had been humming. She'd never noticed. Nobody ever noticed. A faint deep note, under everything, under the whole village, all her life. The net, running through it. The threads, passing.

It stopped.

Not broken. Not cut. *Stopped*. Kept. A Stone that would never Kindle another child. A knot in the net, gold, still, finished, with the threads running into it and not coming out the other side.

---

*Oh no*, she thought. *Oh no. Oh no.*

And then, *Lex. Lex. Where are you*.

---

There were people on the green.

Three of them. Caught. They'd been at the well when the gold came down the street. Two women with buckets and a girl. They'd seen it coming. They'd run. Not fast enough.

One of the women was gold. Mid-stride. Her bucket swinging out from her hand, a gold bucket, a gold arc of water frozen in the air as it slopped over the rim.

The other woman was on her knees. Screaming. Her arm out in front of her. Her hand gold to the wrist. The gold creeping. Up her forearm. Slowly. Like frost up a window.

And the girl.

Twenty. Broad-shouldered. Square-jawed. With a blacksmith's apron on and burn-scars on her forearms.

Tilda.

---

Wren came down off the roof.

On the wind. Fast. Hard. Hitting the cobbles of the green in a burst of moving air that blew the gold dust back off them in a ring. Spinning her whirlwind out. Wider. A yard. Two. Five. Round her and round the girl.

Tilda looked up at her.

Her face was white. Her eyes were huge. She was standing very still in the middle of the green with her left hand held out in front of her.

It was gold.

The fingers. The palm. Gold. Shining. And the gold was moving. Up. Past the wrist. Slowly. A line of it. Creeping up the girl's arm under the skin like a tide coming in over sand.

"*Mistress Wren*," said Tilda Crane. Very quietly. "*I can't feel it.*"

---

Wren didn't know what to do.

She stood in the middle of her whirlwind on the green with her baby on her chest and Tilda in front of her, and the gold creeping up the girl's arm, and she didn't know what to do.

She couldn't blow it off. It wasn't on her. It was *in* her. Under the skin. Spreading.

She couldn't fog it. It wasn't glass.

She couldn't do anything.

"*Hold still*," she said. Uselessly. "*Hold still, Tilda, hold still, don't move, Lex is coming, he'll know what to do*—"

"*He's not here*," said Tilda.

"*He's coming*."

"*Mistress Wren*." The girl's voice was shaking now. "*It's past my elbow.*"

---

And then the sky roared.

Not the hiss. Not the soft dry whisper of the cloud. A *roar*. Deep. Vast. Like a mountainside coming down. Like the forge at Ashbourne when Brannoc opened the great bellows. From the east. From the sky over the river.

Wren looked up.

Bronze. Copper. Old gold. Wings like the sails of a ship. Coming down out of the clear spring sky over the river at a speed that shouldn't have been possible for something that size, with its great jaws open and fire pouring out of them.

Not at the cloud.

*Above* it. Over it. A sheet of flame laid across the sky over Ashbourne like a blanket, so hot the air went white.

And the gold dust, caught in the rising heat, went *up*.

---

It didn't burn. Lex had said it wouldn't. Nothing burned it.

But it *rose*. Lifted. Swirling up off the roofs in a great glittering column, carried on the hot air like sparks up a chimney. Up. Away. Out of the street. Into the sky.

Vharuun came down on the green like a landslide.

And Lex came off his back before he'd landed. Barefoot. With coal-dust on his clothes. With his face white.

He saw Wren.

He saw the baby.

He saw Tilda.

---

"*It's in her*," said Wren. "*It's in her arm. It's spreading. I can't*—"

Lex was already on his knees.

On the green. In front of the girl. In the middle of Wren's whirlwind, with the gold dust swirling up past them into the column of Vharuun's heat. He took Tilda's arm in both his hands. Her gold hand. Her wrist. The skin above the wrist, where the gold was creeping.

"*Tilda*," he said.

"*Master Lex*." She was crying. "*I can't feel my hand. I can't feel it. I'll never—I can't work, I can't hold the tongs, I'll never*—"

"*Hold still*."

"*It's past my elbow*—"

"*Hold still*," said Lex Vey. "*It hasn't set yet.*"

---

Wren didn't understand.

Not then. Later, he told her. Lying in the dark at Harrow with his hand on the bell and his voice hoarse.

*It's like a quench*, he said. *Like tempering. When you heat steel and plunge it, there's a moment. A breath. Before it sets. Before the grain locks. You can still change it then. If you're quick. If you know where to touch.*

*The boy at the Veil was set. Bet Dale was set. Wim and Edda were set. They were finished. Full. There was no room.*

*But Tilda wasn't. Not yet. The gold was still moving in her. Still spreading. It hadn't filled her up.*

*And where it hadn't filled her up, there was still room.*

---

She watched him do it.

She watched him kneel on the green with Tilda's arm in his hands and shut his eyes. She watched his face change. Not the gold-eyed look he got when he used the Root, before he gave it away. Not the fierce bright look he got when he called fire.

Something quieter.

The look he'd had in the autumn, kneeling by her, with his hand on her belly, feeling their daughter turn over inside her for the first time.

*Listening*.

---

He listened to the Hollow.

Not in the gold. There was no room in the gold. He'd found that out at the Veil. He listened to the place *just ahead* of it. In the girl's arm. Above the elbow. Where the gold hadn't reached yet. Where there was still flesh and blood and bone and room.

He asked the Hollow to make more.

Not to take the gold out. It couldn't. Nothing could. He asked it to make room *ahead* of the gold. In front of it. Between the edge of the gold and the rest of her.

A gap. A space. An emptiness, just a hair wide. A room with nothing in it.

And the gold, creeping up the girl's arm like a tide over sand—

Came to the room.

And didn't know what to do.

---

Wren saw it stop.

Just above the elbow. The creeping line of gold under Tilda's skin. Moving. Moving.

Stopping.

Hesitating. The way water hesitates at the edge of a step. As if it had come to the edge of something and didn't know how to go on. Because there was nothing to go on *into*. Because the gold could only fill things, and the room in front of it was the one thing in the world that couldn't be filled.

Because it was already empty on purpose.

---

And then it went back.

Slowly. So slowly. Down. From above the elbow. To the elbow. Past it. The gold under the skin draining back down the forearm like a tide going out. The skin behind it pale and pink and freckled. Flesh again.

Down to the wrist.

And stopped.

The hand stayed gold. The fingers. The palm. Set. Finished. Full. Too far gone for room.

But the arm was hers.

---

Tilda stared at it.

At her arm. Pink. Freckled. Scarred with old burns. Ending in a hand of gold.

She moved her elbow. It bent. She moved her shoulder. It turned. She tried to move her fingers.

They didn't.

"*I'm sorry*," said Lex. He was still on his knees. Still holding her arm. His face grey. "*I'm sorry, Tilda. The hand was set. I couldn't*—"

"*I can feel my arm*," said Tilda Crane.

She was crying. She didn't stop. She looked at her arm. At the line where pink met gold, at the wrist, sharp as a seam.

"*I can feel it*," she said again. "*I can feel all of it. Up to the wrist.*" She looked at him. At her master. At the man who'd taught her to call fire into a forge. "*I've got another hand, Master Lex. I can learn to hold the tongs in the other hand.*"

Lex looked at her for a long moment.

Then he put his head down. On her shoulder. On the leather of her blacksmith's apron.

And Wren, standing over them in the middle of her whirlwind with their daughter laughing at the wind, saw his shoulders shake.

Then they went to work.

There was no time for anything else. The gold was still coming down. Vharuun had lifted most of it, but not all. It was still sifting down out of the sky over the village, still drifting down the side streets, still settling on anything left still.

And there were people in it.

Wren went over the roofs. With her whirlwind. With Lark. She found them. In doorways. In yards. On the riverbank. People who'd been outside when the cloud came and hadn't got in. Some of them gold. Finished. Set. Standing in their gardens with their hoes raised, their washing half pegged, their children's hands in theirs.

Some of them not.

Some of them on their knees, screaming, with the gold creeping up from their hands, their feet, their faces.

Those she shouted for.

"*LEX*!"

And he came. On the wind she made for him. Across the roofs. Badly. Landing in a hedge twice. Getting up. Coming. Kneeling. Listening. Finding the room in front of the gold.

---

They saved eleven.

Wren counted. She couldn't help it. She'd been a tracker. You counted.

Eleven people with gold hands, gold feet, a gold ear, a gold patch across the cheek like a birthmark. Eleven people who'd keep a little of it forever. Who'd live.

Seven they didn't.

Seven people who'd been caught too far from a door, too long in the open, too slow. Who were standing in the streets and yards and doorways of Ashbourne, gold, finished, with their eyes moving.

And the dog in the doorway of the chandler's.

And the sheep in the high pasture.

And the man on the cart on the Thistledown road, and his horse.

And the mill-wheel.

And the Stone.

---

Brannoc's hands were gold at the tips.

Wren found him at the Forge. In the yard. Standing by the pump with forty students huddled in the long hall behind him and the great doors shut and wet sacking stuffed in every crack.

He was holding his hands out in front of him. Looking at them.

The ends of his fingers were gold. All ten. To the first knuckle. Shining. Like he'd dipped them in a pot of gilt.

"*Brannoc*—"

"*There was a lad*," said Brannoc. Very quietly. "*Little Dunny Tobbin's brother. Six. Outside. By the woodpile. When it came.*" He didn't look up from his hands. "*I put my hands over his head. Over his face.*" He turned them over. Slowly. The gold tips catching the light. "*It came down on the backs of my hands. And ran down. To the fingers.*"

"*And the boy*?"

"*Inside*. *With Hedda. Not a speck on him.*" Brannoc looked up. His face under the soot was grey. "*I can't feel the ends of them, lass. I can't feel the hammer.*"

---

Lex came.

He knelt in the forge-yard in front of the old smith and took his enormous hands in his and shut his eyes and listened.

It took a long time.

Wren stood by the pump with Lark asleep at last on her chest, worn out from laughing, and watched. Watched Lex's face. Watched Brannoc's. Watched the old smith look down at the boy he'd pulled out of the ashes fourteen years ago, kneeling in front of him in the yard, holding his hands.

"*It's set*," said Lex at last. Hoarsely. Not opening his eyes. "*The tips. To the first knuckle. It's set.*" He swallowed. "*I can stop it. It's not going further. But I can't take back what's*—"

"*Lad*."

Lex opened his eyes.

"*I've been a smith fifty years*," said Brannoc Holt. "*I've got calluses on these hands thicker than boot-leather. I couldn't feel the ends of my fingers anyway. Not properly. Not since I was thirty.*" He turned his hands over in Lex's. Looked at the gold. "*Now I've got an excuse.*"

"*Brannoc*—"

"*And they'll never get burned*," said the old smith. "*Will they. Gold doesn't burn. I'll be able to pick up a bar straight out of the fire.*" He looked at Lex. His eyes were wet. His mouth was twitching. "*Every smith in Caldor's going to be jealous.*"

---

Old Mag was sitting in her chair by the fire with her cat in her lap.

Wren found her last. When the gold had stopped falling. When Vharuun had lifted the last of it and Wren's wind had carried it east, out of the Vale, over the downs, scattering it so thin across a hundred miles of empty moor that it settled on nothing but heather. When the village had gone quiet. When people were coming out of their doors, slowly, with wet cloths over their mouths, to look.

She went in the back way. Through the garden.

The apple tree was half gold.

One side of it. The west side. The side the cloud came from. Every branch, every leaf, every blossom. Gold. Still. Shining. A gold half-tree, with gold petals that would never fall.

And the other side white. Moving. The petals still dropping, one by one, onto the grass, in the little breeze that was coming up now, off the river.

---

There was someone in the kitchen.

Wren stopped in the back door.

A shape. By the fire. Thin. Tall. Bent. Grey-gold. With two dim sparks for eyes.

Sitting on the other chair. Opposite Old Mag. With its twig-hands folded on its knees.

And Old Mag was pouring it tea.

---

"*Come in, dear*," said Old Mag. Not looking round. "*Shut the door. There's a draught.*"

Wren didn't move.

"*It's all right*," said the old woman. "*He came in under the door after the storm. I told him he might as well sit down.*" She set the teapot down. "*He looked so tired.*"

The dust-man turned its long head toward Wren. The dim sparks of its eyes flickered.

It didn't move toward her. It didn't move at all. It sat in the chair by Old Mag's fire with its twig-hands folded, and there was a cup of tea on the hob beside it, steaming, that it hadn't touched.

That it couldn't touch.

"*Mistress*," whispered Orrin.

---

"*Get out*," said Wren.

"*Hush*," said Old Mag. Mildly. "*He's a guest.*"

"*He's turned seven people to gold*. *He's turned the Stone. Brannoc's hands. Tilda*—"

"*I know what he's done*," said Old Mag. "*I watched from the window. Before the cloths went up.*" She picked up her own cup. Sipped. "*And now he's sitting in my kitchen, and I've asked him why, and he was just about to tell me.*" Her one black eye went to the dust-man. "*Weren't you, dear?*"

The sparks flickered.

"*I'm sorry*," whispered Orrin.

"*You keep saying that*," said Old Mag. "*I want to know why.*"

---

The dust-man was quiet for a long time.

"*Because he asked me*," it said at last.

"*The King*."

"*His Majesty*. *Yes.*" The long head bowed. "*He said they don't understand. He said show them.*"

"*Show them what*?"

"*How easy it is*," whispered Orrin. "*How quick. How it doesn't hurt.*" The sparks dimmed. "*How you never have to be afraid again.*"

Old Mag looked at him over the rim of her cup.

"*Are you afraid*?" she said.

---

The dust-man didn't answer.

"*You came in under my door*," said Old Mag. "*Two nights ago. And talked to me through a crack in the window. And offered me forever.*" She put her cup down. "*And I said, you sound very tired, dear. Have you eaten.*" She tilted her head. Like a bird. "*And you went away very fast.*"

"*Mistress*—"

"*You're not kept*," said Old Mag. "*Are you. You're not gold. You're grey. You're dust. You're falling apart.*" Her one black eye was very bright. "*He keeps everyone. Every old woman in every chair. But he didn't keep you.*"

"*I asked him not to*," whispered Orrin.

"*Why*?"

The dust-man was silent for a long time.

"*Because someone had to be awake*," it said. "*To serve him. To bring the cup. To tell him the hour.*" The sparks flickered. "*Nine hundred years. Kneeling. In the dust. Waiting on the King.*" A pause. "*Everyone else is kept. Everyone. Nine thousand of them. Along the road. I walk past them every day.*"

"*And you're tired*," said Old Mag.

"*I'm so tired*," said Orrin.

---

Wren stood in the back door with her baby asleep on her chest and didn't move.

She didn't want to feel sorry for it. She'd watched it walk down a hill scattering gold like seed. She'd watched a woman's scream cut off. She'd watched the Ashbourne Stone go still.

She felt sorry for it anyway.

"*I'm ninety-six*," said Old Mag.

The dust-man looked at her.

"*I've had ninety-six years*," said the old woman. "*I've had a husband and two daughters and a son who went to sea and never came back. I've had this cottage and that tree and eleven cats, one after another, all called Biscuit.*" She stroked the cat in her lap. "*This one's Biscuit too. He doesn't mind.*"

"*Mistress*—"

"*I'm not greedy*," said Old Mag. "*I've had my share. More than my share. When it's done, it's done, and I'll go and see my boy at the bottom of the sea and tell him off for not writing.*" She looked at the dust-man with her one bright eye. "*I don't want to be kept, dear. I want to be finished. There's a difference.*"

---

The dust-man looked at her for a long, long time.

Then it stood up.

Slowly. Its twig-joints creaking. Dust sifting off it onto the hearth. It stood in Old Mag's kitchen, thin and tall and bent, with its head nearly touching the low beams.

And bowed to her.

The old bow. Low. Courteous. One twig-arm folded across its middle.

"*Thank you for the tea*," whispered Orrin.

"*You didn't drink it*."

"*No*," said the dust-man. "*But it was very kind.*"

---

It turned toward Wren.

She didn't step back. She stood in the doorway with her hand on her knife and her baby on her chest and her whirlwind ready in her lungs.

The dim sparks looked at the baby.

For a long time. Very still.

"*She laughed*," said Orrin. "*In the wind. I heard her. Over the whole village.*" A pause. "*I haven't heard a child laugh in nine hundred years.*"

Wren didn't say anything.

"*His Majesty will want to hear it*," whispered Orrin.

---

"*Tell your husband*," said the dust-man. "*Tell the smith.*"

"*Tell him what*?"

"*At midsummer*," said Orrin. "*On the shortest night. When the windows are open for the heat.*" The sparks flickered. "*His Majesty's sister will come. In every window. In every glass. For the child.*"

Wren's hand closed on her knife.

"*I'm sorry*," said Orrin. "*I'm only the steward. I carry the messages.*"

And let go.

---

It went out under the door.

All of it. All at once. A thin grey stream across the flags of the kitchen floor and out through the gap under the back door, past Wren's feet, into the garden.

She turned. Watched it go. Up. Into the air. Past the apple tree. Past the gold half and the white half. Up into the clearing spring sky.

West.

Not east this time. Not toward the downs. West. Back toward the edge of the world. Toward the Veil. Toward the crack.

Going home.

---

"*Well*," said Old Mag. Behind her. "*That's done.*"

Wren turned round.

The old woman was sitting in her chair by the fire. Stroking her cat. Looking at the untouched cup of tea on the hob.

"*Poor thing*," she said.

"*Mag*—"

"*I know*," said Old Mag. "*I know what it did. I'm not daft.*" She stroked the cat. "*But somebody should feel sorry for it, dear. It's been nine hundred years and nobody has. Not even itself.*" She picked up the untouched cup. Looked at it. "*It'll go cold now. What a waste.*"

And drank it.

---

Wren went home at dusk.

Along the river path. Three miles. Walking. With Lark asleep in the sling and Lex beside her and Vharuun overhead, circling slowly, low, in the red light.

They didn't talk much.

At the place where the path came out of the alders and you could see the Vale behind you, she stopped. And turned. And looked back.

Ashbourne. Small and grey in the dusk. Smoke going up from the chimneys. Lights coming on in the windows, soaped and blurred.

And here and there, catching the last of the sun—shining.

The mill-wheel. Stopped. Gold.

The top field. Gold.

A cart on the Thistledown road. Gold.

The Stone on the green. Gold.

And behind a cottage by the chapel, half an apple tree. Gold on one side. White on the other.

---

"*I ran*," said Wren.

Lex looked at her.

"*Over the roofs*," she said. "*With her. On the wind.*" She looked down at the baby asleep on her chest. "*I haven't done that since before. I thought*—" She stopped. "*That woman in the glass. She said I'd stopped. She said I was afraid I'd never run again.*"

Lex didn't say anything.

"*I ran*," said Wren Ashdown. "*And she laughed. All the way across the village. She laughed at the wind like it was the best thing she'd ever seen.*" Her voice cracked. "*She's a Mistwalker's daughter, Lex. She likes the wind.*"

He put his arm round her.

"*She's a smith's daughter*," he said. "*She grabbed the nail.*"

"*She can be both*," said Wren. Fiercely. Into his shoulder. "*She can be anything. She can be everything. That's the whole point. That's the whole—*"

She stopped.

*She'll never grow up*. That was what she'd said, in the Hall of Lanterns. To a hundred faint faces behind soaped glass. *She'll be five months old forever.*

"*Midsummer*," she said.

"*I know*."

"*In every window*."

"*I know*," said Lex. "*I heard.*"

---

They walked home.

Over the stepping stones. Up through the birches. Past the blue-door cottage, dark and empty. His father and mother were still at the pool, in the little black house on the slope, where Brigid had put the old man to bed under three blankets with a hot brick at his feet. Vharuun would fetch them in the morning.

To their own.

The hornlight was lit in the east window. Soft. Gold. Blurred behind its four panes of scraped horn. Like a lantern in a fog.

Wren stood in front of it for a long time.

Then she went in. And put the baby in her cradle. And climbed up to the loft. And lay down on the bed in her clothes.

And slept.

For the first time in five months, she slept the whole night through.

Lex sat up with the baby.

He said, in the morning, that Lark had woken at three. And looked at the window. And frowned.

And then laughed. In her sleep. Out loud.

As if she were dreaming of the wind.
