# The Grey One

There were four million, one hundred and six thousand, three hundred and twelve threads in the net, and Elric Thorne had counted every one of them nine times.

He'd lost count the first eight. Somewhere round the eight-hundred-thousandth, every time, his attention would slide. He'd think about something else. Quill, and his spoons. His mother's hands on a herring-net. A library table and a young man across it saying *I'm older than you think*.

And he'd lose his place. And have to start again.

The ninth time, he got all the way through.

He didn't tell anyone. There was nobody to tell. He was a thread wound round a Key at the bottom of the world.

But he'd done it.

Four million, one hundred and six thousand, three hundred and twelve.

He thought his mother would have been proud. She'd been very good at counting. Fish. Coins. Days till the boats came back.

---

It was quiet in the Knot.

That was the thing nobody would ever understand, if he could have told them. They'd think it was loud. The centre of the net. Every thread in the world running in, in, in, to one point. Humming. Singing. Four million voices.

It was loud, at first.

After a year, it was quiet. The way a mill is quiet to the miller. The way the sea is quiet to a fisherman's son who grew up with it under his window. You stopped hearing it. It became the floor you stood on.

He'd stood on it for three years.

---

He'd had a body once.

He tried not to think about it. It didn't help. But sometimes, in the long dark, he'd find himself remembering. What it was like to have hands. To hold a cup. To feel the wind off the Saltreach on his face, the morning he left the village for the Spire at nineteen, with a stolen book in his pack and his mother crying on the quay.

To have a raven on his shoulder.

Quill had been a fledgling when he found him. Fallen out of a nest in the Spire's bell tower. One wing bent. Elric had splinted it with a quill pen and two strips of linen and fed him on cheese for a month, and named him after the pen. The bird had never forgiven him for the name.

*Spoons*, the lad had said. In the spring. Kneeling on the black glass in his bare feet. *He's got a nest. Made of spoons.*

Elric had thought about that for weeks.

*He always liked spoons*.

---

He'd had a friend once, too.

He'd had a friend for forty years. And then for one night he hadn't. And then for three years, in the dark, he'd been not-thinking about it so hard it had worn a groove in him, the way a rope wears a groove in a well-head.

And then the friend had come down.

In a bubble. In a chair. To the bottom of the world. Old. So old. A walnut in a drawer. A heron in winter.

And said, *tell me*.

---

He'd told him.

He'd told him everything. The village. The herring. His mother. The book. What he'd wanted. For forty years. For his whole life.

*To be first*.

And Arion had listened. All of it. With his grey eyes. Without saying anything until the end.

And then he'd said, *you were*.

*You were the first friend I had in two hundred years*.

---

Elric had held on to that for a long time.

It was the warmest thing in the Knot. Warmer than the threads. Warmer than the gold that came up through the glass sometimes, faint, from the place where the Well had been before it went home.

He held it the way he held the Key. Wound round it. Tight. So it wouldn't slip.

*You were first*.

---

*Clink*.

---

Loud, now.

Not like the spring. Not a stitch in his side every few hours, far off, down the long silver line. Not a smith striking one blow a very long way away.

*Here*.

At the edge of the black glass. Where the last thread came in out of the dark. He could feel it. Not just in the thread. In the *glass*. A shiver. A jolt. Like a hammer on an anvil when you're standing on the anvil.

*Clink*.

A ring. Black. Closing round the silver thread. Just past the edge of the lid. So close he could have reached out and touched it, if he'd had hands.

He didn't have hands.

He had a thread. Grey. Thin. Frayed. Wound round a Key and a Lockward so many times it was more like a cocoon. Holding.

He held.

---

*Clink*.

And breathing.

He could hear it now. For the first time. Up the thread. Out of the dark. Heavy. Slow. Even. Not tired. Not hurrying. The breathing of a man who has been climbing for eight months and knows exactly how much further he has to go, and is going to go exactly that far, and no further, and no less.

"*Thorne*," said the voice.

Low. Flat. Rough as gravel.

"*Tonight*," said Caddoc of the Iron Crown. "*I'll be there tonight. When they light their lanterns.*" A pause. "*I wanted you to know. So you'd have time.*"

"*Time for what*?" said Elric.

"*Whatever a man does*," said Caddoc. "*Before.*"

---

Elric didn't answer.

He sat—he didn't sit; he was a thread; but he'd never stopped thinking of it as sitting—wound round the Key in the middle of the black glass and thought about that.

*Whatever a man does*.

He'd had three years. He'd counted the threads nine times. He'd read every knot Aurelian left. He'd said what he needed to say to the one person he needed to say it to.

He didn't know what else a man did.

"*Thank you*," he said. Up the thread. To the dark. To the iron. "*That's very kind.*"

"*I'm not kind*," said Caddoc. "*I'm only certain.*" A pause. "*I'm certain you'd want to know.*"

"*You keep saying that*," said Elric. "*Nobody who isn't kind keeps saying they aren't.*"

The thread was quiet for a moment.

"*Huh*," said Caddoc.

---

The boat came at what would have been midnight.

He didn't hear it come. He felt it.

Not on the thread. Not in the glass. *Under* it. Below the black lid. Below the Knot. In the deep, where the Well had been, where Aurelian and Malachi had sat in the dark singing.

Water.

A river. Running. Very slow. Very deep. Under everything. The way rivers run under deserts, under cities, under the floors of old houses. Grey. Patient. Older than the net.

And on it, a boat.

---

He saw the light first.

At the edge of the black glass. Where the lid ended and the dark began. A light. Small. Grey. Swaying. The colour of a lantern seen through river-mist.

And in the heart of it, gold.

A small warm steady flame. Gold. Burning in the middle of the grey. Like a coal in a banked fire. Like a lamp in a window at the end of a long lane on a winter night.

He'd never seen that before. Not in three years. Not in a lifetime of stories about the Ferryman.

*Grey*, the stories said. *Grey lantern. Grey pole. Grey boat. Grey man.*

Nobody had ever said anything about gold.

---

The boat came up out of the dark.

Onto the glass. Not *on* it. *Through* it. As if the black lid of the world were only the surface of the river, and the boat had risen to it the way a fish rises to the top of a pond. Long. Flat. Grey. Low in the water that wasn't water.

A tall thin figure in the stern. Leaning on a pole. With the lantern on top of it.

And in the bow, facing forward—

---

Elric would have stopped breathing, if he'd been breathing.

---

He was young.

That was the first thing. Not forty-five. Not the face Elric had known for forty years, that never changed, that sat across a library table looking like a portrait of a man who was older than you thought.

*Younger*. Twenty-five. Thirty. Tall. Thin. Stooping a little, even sitting, the way tall men stoop when they've spent their lives ducking under arches. In a grey coat. With ink on his cuff. With his hair too long.

With his face *open*.

All of it. Not closed like a book. Not still like water in a bucket. Open like a door with the wind blowing through it.

Looking at him.

---

"*Well*," said Arion Vey. "*Took you long enough.*"

---

Elric couldn't speak.

He hung over the Key at the centre of the black glass, wound round it, a grey smudge on the air, and looked at the young man in the bow of the Ferryman's boat.

"*I'm*—" he said. And stopped.

"*You're a thread*," said Arion. "*I know. You told me. In the spring.*" He smiled. And it was a smile Elric had never seen on that face. Not once in forty years. Wide. Startled. Like a boy. "*I said threads can be frightened. You were very rude about it.*"

"*You're*—"

"*Dead*," said Arion. "*Yes. Last night. In a bed at Harrow. With my wife holding my hand.*" He tilted his head. "*She remembered me. At the end. All of it. Thirty years. She remembered everything.*"

Elric didn't say anything.

"*Someone paid*," said Arion Vey. "*A love for a love. I'll tell you about it.*" He looked at the tall grey figure in the stern. "*If there's time.*"

"*There is time*," said the Ferryman. "*A little.*"

---

The boat came across the glass.

Slowly. Gliding. The pole going down into the black and coming up again, the way a pole goes into water. The lantern swaying. Grey, with a gold heart.

It stopped. Beside the Key. Beside the Lockward. Beside the grey thread wound round them both.

The young man in the bow looked at it.

At the cocoon. At the frays. At how thin the thread was worn in places, like an old rope on a winch.

"*You've been holding it*," he said. Quietly. "*The whole time. Since the spring. Since before.*"

"*It's what I'm for*."

"*Three years*," said Arion. "*Alone. In the dark. Counting threads.*"

"*Four million, one hundred and six thousand, three hundred and twelve*," said Elric. "*I got all the way through. The ninth time. I wanted to tell someone.*"

The young man looked at him.

"*Now you have*," he said.

---

"*I promised*," said Arion.

Elric waited.

"*In the spring*," said his friend. "*When I went up. You said, tell me. When you get there. Whatever it's like on the other side. I'd like to know, before I go.*" He leaned forward. In the bow. Toward the grey smudge on the Key. "*So I asked the Ferryman to stop. On the way. He said it was on his way.*"

"*It is*," said the Ferryman.

"*So*," said Arion Vey. "*I'm here. I'm telling you.*"

---

The threads hummed.

Four million, one hundred and six thousand, three hundred and twelve. Singing. All round. In the dark.

Elric didn't hear them.

"*Tell me*," he said.

---

"*It's like being very tired*," said Arion. "*At the end of a very good day.*"

Elric didn't move.

"*I told Ysenne that*," said his friend. "*In my spectacles. In the dark. She asked me what it was like. Dying. I said that.*" He smiled. "*I was right. I'm always right. It's very annoying.*"

"*And then*?"

"*And then*—" Arion stopped. Thought about it. The way he'd always thought about things. Carefully. From several angles. Like the structure of a ward. "*And then someone says, you can sit down now.*"

---

"*That's all*," said Arion. "*That's all it is. You've been standing for a very long time. Holding something. And somebody puts a hand on your shoulder and says, you can put it down. You can sit down now.*" He looked at the grey thread on the Key. "*And you do. And it's the best thing you've ever felt.*"

"*Who says it*?"

His friend didn't answer for a moment.

"*Everyone*," he said.

---

"*My wife*," said Arion. "*Holding my hand. My sons. My granddaughter, with her fist on all of ours.*" He looked down at his own hands. Young. Long-fingered. With ink on the cuff. "*And then the door. And I stood up. And it was so easy. Elric. It was so easy. I'd forgotten what it was like, to stand up without thinking.*"

Elric didn't say anything.

"*And then I looked back*," said Arion. "*Once. From the doorway. At her.*" His voice was very quiet. "*I didn't turn back. I've done that twice. On the steps. In the rain. I didn't do it again.*"

"*Why not*?"

"*Because she'd left a light on*," said Arion Vey.

He looked up. At the lantern. At the top of the Ferryman's pole. At the grey light, swaying. At the gold flame in the heart of it.

"*Someone always does*," he said. "*That's the thing nobody tells you. On the other side of the door. There's always a light on.*"

---

Elric looked at the lantern.

At the gold flame. Small. Warm. Steady. Burning in the middle of the grey river-light.

"*That's new*," he said.

"*That's Ember*," said Arion.

Elric went very still.

"*Lex's*," said his friend. "*The voice in his head. A piece of me. Pressed into his blood twenty-two years ago.*" He looked at the flame. "*It paid. For Seren. A love for a love. Its love for Lex. The only thing in it that wasn't me.*" His voice cracked. Just a little. "*And then it came with me. Because it was mine. Because pieces should go home.*"

"*And it's*—"

"*Lighting the way*," said Arion. "*Across the river. It asked to.*" A small strange smile. "*It said, someone should. It said, he'll want to see where he's going. It said, it's very dark on the river and he's always been bad at finding his way in the dark. He walked into a pillar once.*"

---

Elric laughed.

He didn't mean to. It came out of him. Out of the thread. The whole grey cocoon on the Key shaking, trembling, the way it had in the spring when a hedge-witch held up a frying pan.

"*A pillar*," he said.

"*The big one by the fountain*," said Arion. "*Below the Spire. With the Founder's face on it.*" He was smiling. "*I'd had mulled cider. I'd never had it before. I drank it in one go.*"

"*You never told me that*."

"*I never told you a great many things*," said Arion Vey. "*I'm telling you now.*"

---

They talked.

Elric didn't know how long. There was no time in the Knot. There was only the hum and the dark and the slow grey river under the glass.

And his friend, in the bow of the Ferryman's boat, telling him things.

Small things. Things he'd never said, in forty years. The cat in the Spire library that Arion had built his first mirror-ward for. The time he'd tried to teach a Spire cat to fetch and the cat had taught him to throw things instead. That he'd been afraid of horses till he was ninety. That he'd once fallen asleep in a meeting of the Spire Council and woken up having apparently voted to abolish the Archivist's lunch.

*Tell me*, Elric had said. Forty-three years ago. Across a library table.

And now, finally, Arion was.

---

"*Your turn*," said Arion.

Elric looked at him.

"*Your mother*," said his friend. "*You said, in the spring. She mended nets. She sang to them, so the fish would come.*" He leaned forward. "*What did she sing?*"

Elric didn't answer for a long time.

He hadn't thought about it. Not in years. Not in the Spire, not at Caldris, not in the Knot. He'd thought about the village. The herring. The salt-cracked hands. He'd thought about leaving.

He'd never thought about the song.

And then he did.

---

He didn't have a voice. He had a thread.

But a thread can hum. That's what threads do. That's all the net was, four million of them, humming.

He hummed.

Very softly. Thin and cracked and strange, the voice of a grey thread wound round a Key at the bottom of the world. A tune. Simple. Slow. Going up and coming down, the way a wave goes up and comes down. The way a net goes out over the side of a boat and comes back in full.

A herring-wife's song. From a village on the Saltreach coast that nobody had ever heard of.

*Come in, come in, my silver ones*—

---

Arion listened.

All of it. Every verse. Sitting in the bow of the Ferryman's boat with his young face open and his long hands in his lap.

When it was done, he didn't say anything for a long time.

"*She'd have liked you*," said Elric. "*My mother. She'd have fed you till you couldn't move. She'd have told you you were too thin.*"

"*I was too thin*."

"*You were always too thin*," said Elric Thorne. "*For forty years. I used to bring you bread from the kitchens because you forgot to eat.*"

Arion looked at him.

"*I didn't know that was you*," he said.

"*Who else would it have been*?"

---

"*It is time*," said the Ferryman.

---

Arion looked up.

At the tall grey figure in the stern. At the lantern. At the gold flame.

Then back at the grey smudge on the Key.

"*He's coming tonight*," he said. "*Isn't he. Caddoc. I heard him. Up the thread. Clink.*"

"*Tonight*."

"*And Lex*," said Arion. "*And Antho. They're coming too. Through the little one's pool. With a crown.*" He looked at the Key. At the Lockward. At the grey thread round them. "*Hers. Hesper's. The one that says no.*"

"*I know*," said Elric. "*I felt it come out of the Court. Like a cold wind down the last thread. Three days ago.*"

"*You're going to wear it*."

It wasn't a question.

Elric didn't answer.

---

"*You're going to wear it*," said Arion again. Very quietly. "*And turn the Key. All the way. Past closed. Past open. Into the place where it was made.*" He looked at the cocoon of grey. "*And go with the thread. With the Veil. With whatever it unwrites.*"

"*Someone has to*."

"*Someone does*," agreed his friend. "*It doesn't have to be you.*"

"*Yes it does*," said Elric Thorne.

Arion looked at him.

"*Because I'm already here*," said Elric. "*Because I'm already holding it. Because I've had three years to work out how.*" A dry sound. A rasp. "*Because the lad's got a baby who said no to a king. And a wife who breaks noses. And a dragon who wants sheep.*"

"*And because you want to go first*," said Arion.

Silence.

"*Yes*," said Elric. Very quietly. "*That too.*"

---

His friend didn't say anything for a long time.

He sat in the bow of the boat and looked at the grey thread on the Key. With his young face. With his open face. With his grey eyes, the same grey eyes, the only thing about him that hadn't changed.

"*I'll wait*," he said.

Elric didn't understand.

"*On the other side*," said Arion Vey. "*On the far shore. Where the light's on.*" He smiled. "*I'll wait for you. I'll save you a seat.*"

"*In front*?" said Elric. Before he could stop himself. The old bitterness. Forty years of it. Coming up out of him like water out of a well. "*Ahead? So you can go first again?*"

"*No*," said Arion.

He leaned forward. In the bow. As far as he could. Toward the grey thread on the Key.

And put out his hand.

---

It didn't reach. The boat was too far. The Key was too far. He held his hand out over the black glass, young, long-fingered, with ink on the cuff. Open.

And the thread moved.

A strand of it. Just one. Unwinding from the cocoon on the Key. Slowly. Carefully. Grey. Thin. Frayed at the end. Reaching across the gap between the Key and the boat.

And touching his fingers.

The way it had in the spring. In a wheeled chair. An old man's spotted hand.

Young now.

"*Beside*," said Arion.

---

Elric would have wept, if he'd had eyes.

He wept anyway. The way threads weep. Shaking. The whole grey cocoon on the Key trembling, the way it had trembled in the spring, the way it had trembled when a hedge-witch held up a pan.

"*Beside*," he said.

"*Beside*," said Arion Vey. "*Not in front. Not behind. Not first. Not second.*" His fingers closed. Round the strand of grey thread. Gently. The way you'd close your hand round someone else's. "*Beside. Like we should have been. For forty years. Across the table.*"

---

The strand let go.

Slowly. Reluctantly. Winding back across the gap. Back onto the Key. Back into the cocoon. Holding.

Arion sat back in the bow.

"*Tell the lad*," he said. "*When he comes. Tell him*—" He stopped. Thought about it. "*No. Don't tell him anything. He knows. I told him everything I needed to. And Ember told him the rest.*" He smiled. "*Tell him to say good morning to the fire.*"

"*He will*," said Elric. "*He always does. Every morning. It's in his file. I read it. In the Spire. Before I came to Ashbourne.*" A dry rasp. "*Says good morning to forge-fire. Possible sign of instability.*"

Arion laughed.

The wide startled laugh. Cracking out of him like ice off a roof. Echoing off the black glass and the dark and the four million threads.

"*Possible sign of instability*," he said.

"*I underlined it*," said Elric Thorne. "*Twice.*"

---

The boat moved.

The Ferryman leaned on his pole. And the long grey hull slid back. Away from the Key. Across the black glass. Toward the edge. Toward the dark. Toward the slow grey river running under everything.

Arion didn't look back.

Elric watched him not look back. All the way. To the edge of the glass. A young man in a grey coat sitting in the bow of a boat, facing forward. Toward the far shore. Toward the dark.

And at the very edge, just before the boat sank down through the glass into the river beneath—

He lifted one hand.

And tapped two fingers. Twice. On the side of the boat.

*Tap. Tap*.

The way he used to tap two fingers on a library table, forty-three years ago, waiting for Elric to finish a sentence.

And then the boat went down. Into the glass. Into the river. Into the dark.

And the lantern went with it.

Grey. With a gold heart.

---

Elric held the Key.

In the dark. At the centre of everything. With four million, one hundred and six thousand, three hundred and twelve threads singing round him.

He'd never felt less alone in his life.

---

*Clink*.

---

The edge of the glass.

Where the last thread came in out of the dark. Silver. Bright. Taut as a harp-string.

And on it, at the very edge, where it met the lid—

A ring.

Black. Iron. Ten feet across. Smooth. Perfect. Closing round the silver thread.

The last one.

---

And then a hand.

Square. Heavy. Dark. Gripping the iron ring. Pulling.

And a man came up out of the dark onto the black glass.

---

He wasn't big. That was the first thing Elric noticed. He'd expected big. The King was big, everyone said. Taller than anything. Broader than two men.

This man was ordinary. Middling height. Square. Heavy-shouldered. Thick-necked. With black hair cut short as a soldier's and a face like a closed door. Heavy brows. A jaw like a block of stone. A face that had been born looking forty and stayed that way.

He wore a coat. Black. Plain. Old. Rotten at the hem, nine hundred years old. And over his shoulders, looped round and round, chains. Iron. Black. Hundreds of links. Coiled over both shoulders and down his back like a sailor's rope. Where he'd unwound them, ring by ring, link by link, for eight months, climbing the last thread from the Court to the bottom of the world.

There were only a few left.

And on his head, a crown.

Not a crown. A band. Black iron. A hand wide. A finger thick. No ornament on it at all. Like a manacle. Like the iron ring of a gate.

He stood at the edge of the black glass and looked across it.

At the Key. At the Lockward. At the grey thread wound round them both.

He nodded.

Once.

---

"*Thorne*," said Caddoc of the Iron Crown.

"*Iron*," said Elric.

---

The Crowned king didn't move.

He stood at the edge of the glass with his chains on his shoulders and his square hands at his sides and looked at the grey smudge on the Key. For a long time. The way a mason looks at a wall before he starts to take it down. Measuring. Seeing where the stones sit.

"*You're thinner than I thought*," he said.

"*Everyone says that*."

"*You've been holding it a long time*." Caddoc's dark face didn't change. "*Three years. I felt it. All the way up the thread. Every time you let a strand go slack and pulled it back.*" A pause. "*You're very stubborn.*"

"*So are you*."

"*Yes*," said Caddoc. "*That's why it's taken eight months.*"

---

He came across the glass.

Slowly. Not hurrying. His boots on the black. *Thud*. *Thud*. The iron chains clinking on his shoulders with every step. *Clink*. *Clink*. Like a bell with a cracked clapper. Like a cell door.

He stopped a few paces from the Key.

He looked at the Lockward. At the grey stone notched like a mountain range. At the thin line of gold runes down its face. At the last rune, near the hilt.

*STAY*.

"*Good work*," said Caddoc. "*Whoever made that.*"

"*A smith*," said Elric. "*And a dying Warden. And a girl who carried a breath for three days. And a dead man's blood in the quench.*"

"*Huh*," said Caddoc. He looked at it for a long moment. "*It's good. It'd hold most things.*"

"*But not you*."

"*No*," said Caddoc of the Iron Crown. "*Not me.*" He looked at the grey thread. "*Not with you this tired.*"

---

"*I'll be quick*," he said.

"*You keep saying that*."

"*I mean it*," said Caddoc. "*I'll take the bar out. I'll take the Key. I'll go back up the thread. To my brother.*" His dark face was very still. "*You'll feel the bar go. It'll pull your thread. It'll hurt.*" A pause. "*And then you won't be holding anything. And you can rest.*"

"*And then your brother turns it*."

"*And then my brother turns it*."

"*And picks up the net*," said Elric. "*And keeps it. Forever. Every thread. Every child. Every Ceiling.*"

Caddoc didn't answer.

"*You sat with your father*," said Elric. "*Arion told me. You held his hand. At the end. You didn't say anything.*"

The dark face didn't move.

But Elric saw something. In the eyes. Black. Heavy-browed. A flicker. Like a candle in a draught.

"*Yes*," said Caddoc.

"*And you watched him go*. *Through the door. And you let him.*"

"*Yes*."

"*And now you're going to bring your brother a Key*," said Elric Thorne, "*so that nobody ever goes through that door again. Not ever. Not one person. In the whole world.*"

---

Caddoc didn't answer for a long time.

He stood on the black glass with his chains on his shoulders and his square hands at his sides and looked at the grey thread on the Key.

"*I gave my word*," he said at last.

"*I know*."

"*On the shingle*. *In chains. To my brother. Who couldn't go in the room.*" His voice didn't change. Low. Flat. Gravel. "*I said I'd go where he went. I said I'd do what he asked.*" A pause. "*I don't break my word, Thorne. I'm not like the rest of them. Not like Mae, who said she'd bind the Well and drank it. Not like Rosie, who said she'd listen and drank anyway. Not like Ysy, who never says anything she means.*"

"*Not like me*," said Elric. "*Who said he was Arion's friend for forty years and opened the wards at Caldris.*"

Caddoc looked at him.

"*No*," he said. "*Not like you.*"

---

"*I'm not like me either*," said Elric. "*Anymore.*"

Caddoc waited.

"*I was*," said Elric. "*For a long time. I was the man who opened the wards. The man who carried his friend out in a sack. The man who always wanted to go first.*" A dry rasp. "*And then I gave back the threads I'd stolen. At the lid. And I came in here. And I held on.*" A pause. "*And then my friend came down to the bottom of the world in a chair and said, tell me.*"

"*And you told him*."

"*And I told him*," said Elric. "*And he said, beside.*"

Caddoc didn't understand. Elric could see that. A heavy frown. A mason looking at a stone that doesn't fit.

"*It doesn't matter*," said Elric Thorne. "*It's mine. Not yours.*"

---

And then he felt it.

At the edge of the glass. Not where the last thread came in. The other side. Opposite.

A softness.

Warm. Dark. Opening. Like a mouth. Like a cupped hand. Like a room being made in the air where there had been no room before.

Frightened. So frightened. Shaking. The way it had shaken in the spring, carrying an old man in a chair down to the hungry place.

Coming anyway.

---

Caddoc felt it too.

Elric saw him feel it. The dark head turning. Slowly. Away from the Key. Toward the far edge of the glass. Toward the soft dark mouth opening in the air.

"*What*—" he said.

"*The little one*," said Elric. "*The Well. The one you drank.*"

Caddoc went very still.

"*It's home now*," said Elric. "*In a pool. In a green field. With a girl on a crutch who tells it about sheep.*" He looked at the soft dark room opening at the edge of the glass. "*And it's bringing someone down.*"

---

They came out of the dark.

Two of them. Stepping out of the soft dark mouth onto the black glass. Barefoot. Both of them. Like their mother.

The first one big. Broad-shouldered. With dark curly hair cut badly and a grey-gold handprint on his left cheek and a basket on his back. A smith's hands. A smith's shoulders. A little hammer on his belt.

The second one thin. Tall. With long black hair and a wild beard and burn-scars on his hands and a gold patch on the back of the left one, the size of a coin. A Threshold Warden. A door.

They stopped at the edge of the glass.

And looked across it.

At the Key. At the Lockward. At the grey thread wound round them both.

At the square dark man with chains on his shoulders, standing a few paces off.

---

Elric lifted his head.

What he had instead of a head. Slowly. The way an old man lifts his head from a book. Coming back from a long way off.

And looked at the smith.

"*Well*," said Elric Thorne. "*Took you long enough, lad.*"
