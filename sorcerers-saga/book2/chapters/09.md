# The Glass Desert

There was a hawk on the queen's shoulder.

Lex hadn't seen it from the pass. It was the color of the desert, red-gold and ochre, and it sat so still on the bronze pauldron of her armor that it might have been part of it. But as he came down the long red slope toward the line of riders, with Kestrel at his side and Dorran at his back and the twins muttering and Oriel's mule picking its way behind, the hawk turned its head. And looked at him. With eyes like drops of honey.

And he understood.

"*You've been watching us*," he said. "*With hawks.*"

Queen Ishara of the Sunreach smiled. It was a hawk's smile. All edges.

"*Nine days*," she said. "*Since you came down out of the Bitter Hills. Every morning, a hawk over your camp. Every evening, another.*" She tilted her head. "*Your thread was dark. My Stone-readers could not find you. They told me the Root-heir of Caldor had vanished from the world.*" She stroked the hawk's breast with one finger. "*But Zahir does not read threads. Zahir reads* people. *Big ones, with red hair beside them. Walking south.*"

*[The Lampshade hides you from sight,]* said Ember, sounding chagrined. *[Not from* sight. *From Lattice-sight. A bird's just a bird.]*

*Ilse didn't mention that.*

*[I don't think Ilse's ever been hunted by a queen with hawks.]*

Behind him, he heard Ivo say, very quietly, "*Hawks. Of course it's hawks. We never thought of hawks.*"

"*We never thought of hawks*," agreed Ilse, in a tone of real professional grief.

---

She took them to Saffaran.

It took two days on the tall golden horses, riding south across the red plains with fifty white-cloaked riders around them like a moving wall. Lex had never ridden a horse for more than an afternoon. By the end of the first day, he couldn't feel his legs. By the end of the second, he could feel them far too much.

Dorran, who was too large for any horse in the Sunreach, walked. He walked the whole way. He kept pace with the riders without seeming to try, his great hammer on his shoulder, his shaved head turning slowly as he looked around at the red country. The riders looked at him sideways and muttered to each other. Lex heard the word *ghul* more than once. He didn't ask what it meant.

Saffaran was built on a mesa.

A great flat-topped tower of red rock, rising sheer out of the plain a thousand feet high, with a green river curving around its foot. And the whole top of it was city. Domes and towers and terraces of red stone, white-washed walls, gardens hanging over the edge of the cliff in great green cascades. Bridges of rope and timber swinging between the mesa and smaller pillars of rock around it. Hawks, everywhere, wheeling over the towers in the hot blue sky.

And in the center of the city, on the highest point of the mesa, a Lattice Stone.

Lex felt it from the plain. A great silver weight. Older than the Highcrest Stone. Bigger. And *heavy*. Heavier than any Stone he'd ever felt in Caldor. Its threads ran out from it into the city and the plains, thousands upon thousands of them.

And every one of them was capped.

Not the old Ceiling. Something worse. He'd felt it once, at the Spire, in the High Warden's private records: a Ceiling set not just on a person but on a whole people. Every thread pressed down flat. Every one marked, in the deep silver code of the net, with the same flag.

*SUNREACH — TREATY OF SCALD — CAPPED (permanent)*

*[Wren told us,]* said Ember quietly. *[At the festival. Thousands of flagged Stones. I didn't—I didn't understand what she meant.]*

He didn't reach for it. He wanted to. Every instinct in him wanted to reach into that Stone with the Root and *pull*, the way he'd pulled at Harrowgate and Kessick and two hundred and fifty-eight other places. Unbind. Unfold. Let the light out.

He kept his thread shaded, and kept his hands at his sides, and rode up the long switchback road into the city of hawks.

---

"*Two hundred years*," said Queen Ishara.

She'd taken them to her hall. A great round chamber at the top of the highest tower in Saffaran, open on all sides to the wind, with a floor of red and gold tiles and a ring of carved pillars holding up a domed roof painted with stars. Hawks perched on every pillar. They watched the Unlit with honey-colored eyes.

The queen stood at the edge of the hall, where the floor ended in a low wall and then nothing, a thousand feet of red air down to the green river. Zahir sat on her shoulder.

"*Two hundred years ago*," she said, "*the Sunreach fought a war with Caldor. Over the Scald. Over water. The usual things.*" Her voice was very calm. "*We were winning. We had the old fire, the Ashari fire, and your Caldorean knights could not stand against it. And then the Order came. Your Wardens. With their silver net.*" She turned. "*They did not fight us. They did not need to. They simply went to our Stones, one by one, in the night, and capped every thread in the Sunreach. Every man. Every woman. Every child.*" Her hawk's eyes were very steady. "*And then they wrote a treaty. And made my great-great-grandmother sign it. And the Ceiling has been on us ever since.*"

Lex didn't say anything.

"*When I heard*," said Ishara, "*three years ago, that a smith in Caldor had broken the Ceiling. That he was walking from Stone to Stone, freeing every thread he touched.*" Her voice didn't change. "*I sent riders. To the border. To wait for him.*" She tilted her head. "*He did not come.*"

"I didn't know," said Lex quietly.

"*No. Your friend with the red braid told me so. When she came last spring.*" Ishara's mouth twisted. "*She was very sorry. She was very angry, on our behalf. She said she would tell you, and you would come.*" A pause. "*You did not come.*"

"I didn't—" He stopped. *I didn't have time. There were four thousand Stones in Caldor. I was going to come.* Every word true, and every one an excuse.

"I'm sorry," he said instead.

Ishara looked at him for a long moment.

"*And now you have come*," she said. "*Not to free us. To find something in the glass.*" Her voice was very cold. "*And behind you, something has come too.*"

---

She showed them.

A village at the edge of the plain, a day's ride south of Saffaran, at the very rim of the glass. Lex saw it from the back of a golden horse at dawn. Mud-brick houses. A well. A grove of date palms.

And in the air above the well, a keyhole.

Black. Healed over. Hanging in the bright morning like a hole cut in the sky.

"*Six*," said Ishara. "*Six of my people. In four villages along the glass. In the last month.*" She didn't look at him. "*Their families do not remember them. Their beds are empty. Their names are gone.*" She looked at the keyhole. "*My Stone-readers found the holes in the records. And my hawks found these. Hanging in the air.*"

Lex took out the Ledger.

He hadn't told Ishara about it. He opened it now, on the saddle in front of him, and turned to the last page.

*Bram Sowerby, of the White Shore. Sixty. Shepherd.*

And below that, six more names. In a narrow precise elegant hand, very faint, as if written in water.

*Amira bint Hassa, of Qarn. Nineteen. She was going to be a healer.*

*Tarek al-Nur, of Qarn. Twenty-two. He played the oud at every wedding in the village.*

He read them out. All six.

Ishara went very still on her horse.

He looked up. Her face had changed. All the hawk had gone out of it. She was staring at the book in his hands.

"*Read them again*," she said. Her voice was not steady.

He read them again.

When he finished, the Queen of the Sunreach turned her horse away from him and rode a little way off, to the edge of the date grove. And sat there for a long time, with her back to them, and her hawk on her shoulder, and her head bowed.

*[She knew them,]* said Ember quietly. *[One of them, at least. Lex. She knew them.]*

When she came back, her face was the hawk's face again. But her eyes were red.

"*Tarek al-Nur*," she said. "*He played at my daughter's naming. Four years ago.*" A pause. "*I did not remember him. Until you read his name.*"

"The Ledger keeps them," said Lex. "As long as it's kept, they're not gone. Not all the way. They can be brought back."

"*How?*"

He looked at her.

"With the key," he said. "The thing that took them. It's in three pieces. He has one. One is under my brother's house in Caldor. And one—" He looked south. At the line of blinding light on the horizon. "One is in your glass."

Ishara followed his gaze.

"*No one comes back from the glass*," she said.

"I have to."

"*If you go into the glass*," said the Queen of the Sunreach, "*and find this thing, and bring it out, the man who is taking my people will take it from you.*" She looked at him. "*That is what your red-haired witch tells me. That he wants you to find it for him.*"

"Yes."

"*Then why should I let you go?*"

Lex was quiet for a while.

"Because he'll find it anyway," he said. "Sooner or later. He's got a voice in his head that knows where everything is. And every month it takes him, he takes more of your people." He closed the Ledger. "And because I can't promise I'll keep it from him. But I can promise I'll try. And I'll promise you something else."

"*What?*"

"When this is over," said Lex, "if I'm still alive, I'll come back. To Saffaran. To your Stone. And I'll take the Ceiling off every thread in the Sunreach." He met her eyes. "Every one. I'll start with yours."

Queen Ishara looked at him for a very long time.

"*Your friend with the red braid*," she said at last, "*said you would say something like that.*" Her mouth twisted. "*She said you are very stupid, and very stubborn, and that you always keep your promises, even the ones you should not have made.*"

"That sounds like her."

"*She also said*," said Ishara, "*that you are the best man she has ever known. And that if I hurt you, she would come back and put an arrow through my hawk.*"

Lex laughed. He couldn't help it.

Something moved at the corner of the queen's mouth. Not quite a smile.

"*I liked her*," said Ishara. "*Very much.*" She turned her horse toward the glass. "*Come, then, smith. I will take you to the edge. No further. My people do not go into the glass.*"

---

The glass began without warning.

One moment they were riding across the red stony plain, through scrub and thorn and dry yellow grass. The next, the ground under the horses' hooves changed. From stone to something smooth and hard and ringing. The horses shied. The riders reined them in.

And ahead of them, stretching away south to the horizon, under a sky so bright it was nearly white, the Glass Desert.

It was beautiful. That was the first thing. Lex hadn't expected it to be beautiful. A vast flat plain of glass, a hundred miles of it, smooth and clear and golden-green, like the glass of an old bottle held up to the sun. In places it was flat as a millpond. In others it rose in frozen waves and ripples, great curling crests of glass taller than a house, caught in the moment of breaking. Here and there, columns of it rose up out of the plain like trees, branching, twisting, their glass limbs catching the light.

And everywhere, the light.

It bounced. It danced. It came up off the glass in great sheets and flares and blinding white streaks. Every surface caught it and threw it back, so that the whole desert seemed to be on fire with reflected sun. Lex had to squint to look at it at all. Even then, it made his eyes water.

"*The Sunfall*," said Ishara. "*That is what we call it. In the old songs, a sun fell out of the sky. Here. Before the Founders. Before the Lattice. Before anything.*" She was looking out at the glass with an expression Lex couldn't read. "*It burned for a year. When it was done, the desert was glass.*"

*[Not a sun,]* said Ember, very quietly. *[A Flame-Well. A Well of Flame, erupting. Lex, the power it would take to do this—]*

*Malachi said the Wells were wells. Places where the elements come up from below.*

*[Malachi said the Wells were* fed. *By the river under the world. And if something went wrong with one. If something poisoned it, or broke it open—]* A pause. *[I think this is what's left of a Well that broke.]*

"*There are reflections in the glass*," said Ishara. "*That is why no one comes back. You walk in, and the glass shows you things. People. Places. Things you have lost.*" Her voice was very flat. "*And you follow them. Deeper and deeper. And the sun cooks you. And the glass cuts your feet. And when my riders find you, months later, you are lying on the glass with your eyes open and a smile on your face.*"

Kestrel shifted beside him.

Lex looked at Oriel Sant.

She was sitting on her mule at the very edge of the glass, with her white eyes turned south. Her face was calm. She was the only one of them, he realized, who wasn't squinting.

"*Well*," she said. "*It's a good thing I can't see any of it, then.*"

---

They went in at noon, on foot. The horses wouldn't go.

Ishara gave them boots. Thick-soled desert boots, with layers of hardened leather and a sole of something black and rubbery that the Sunreach people harvested from a tree in the southern canyons. They gave them white robes and head-cloths, and water-skins, and little round mirrors of polished bronze to signal with if they got lost.

"*If you signal*," said Ishara, "*no one will come. But at least we will know where you died.*"

"*Comforting*," said Ivo.

"*I am not here to comfort you, little thief.*" The queen looked at Lex. "*Seven days. If you are not out in seven days, I will tell the red-braided one that you were stupid and stubborn to the end.*"

"Tell her I'm sorry," said Lex. "If it comes to that."

Ishara looked at him. Then, unexpectedly, she reached out and gripped his forearm. Hard. A warrior's grip.

"*Find it*," she said. "*Bring my people's names home.*"

And then she turned her horse and rode back toward the red plain, with Zahir lifting off her shoulder and wheeling up into the white sky over the glass.

Lex watched the hawk go up. Higher and higher. Until it was just a speck.

*She's still watching*, he thought.

*[Good,]* said Ember. *[Somebody should be.]*

---

Oriel led.

She sat on her mule. The mule had been given boots too, four of them, and walked with an expression of profound and personal offense. Oriel held the reins loosely in one hand and her black thorn stick in the other, and tapped the stick on the glass every few paces. Listening. Her white eyes turned south. She didn't hesitate. She didn't look around.

Everyone else did.

It started within the first hour.

Lex saw it out of the corner of his eye. A flicker in the glass. A shape, moving under the surface, the way a fish moves under ice. He turned his head.

There was a woman in the glass.

Not on it. *In* it. Under the smooth golden-green surface, as if the glass were water and she were swimming just beneath. Dark-haired. Laughing. In a blue dress, with flour on her hands.

His mother.

He stopped walking.

He knew it wasn't her. He *knew*. Ishara had told them. Oriel had told them. The glass showed you what you'd lost. But he'd been ten when the fire took her, and he'd forgotten so much: the exact color of her eyes, the shape of her hands, the way her hair fell when she bent over the hearth. And there it all was. In the glass. Under his feet. Looking up at him.

She smiled. She lifted one floury hand, and beckoned.

*This way, love.*

"*Smith*."

Oriel's voice. Thin and dry and very precise.

"*Whatever you're looking at*," said the old woman, without turning around, "*stop.*"

Lex dragged his eyes away from the glass.

His mother was still there. He could feel her, at the edge of his vision. Beckoning. But he made himself look at the back of Oriel's mule. At the old woman's red shawl. At the black thorn stick tapping on the glass.

And walked.

---

Everyone saw something.

Lex could tell from their faces. Kestrel walked with her jaw clenched and her fire burning in her hair, staring straight ahead, and every so often her eyes would slide sideways and go wide and wet before she wrenched them back. He didn't have to ask what she saw. Tamsin walked beside her, faint as ever, but Lex had seen her look down once and stop dead, and he'd known. In the glass, her brother would be solid. Whole. Laughing. Tam the Ram.

The twins held hands. Lex didn't think they knew they were doing it. They walked shoulder to shoulder, eyes front, fingers laced, and every so often Ivo's grip would tighten until his knuckles went white.

Dorran walked at the back. He was the only one who looked down at the glass on purpose. Steadily. His great slab of a face unchanging. Lex didn't know what he saw. Children, maybe. Four thousand one hundred and nine of them. Looking up at him through the glass, trusting.

Nobody talked.

And Oriel Sant rode at the front on her offended mule, tapping her stick on the glass, her white eyes turned south. Calm. Untroubled. The only one of them who couldn't see a thing.

---

They camped the first night in the lee of a glass wave twenty feet high, frozen in the act of breaking. It held the day's heat like an oven. The sun went down and the glass went from gold to red to violet, and the reflections went with it, until the whole desert seemed to be full of ghosts in a sunset.

Then it went dark, and the reflections went out, and there were stars.

So many stars. The glass caught them too. Every star in the sky reflected in the glass below, so that sitting in the middle of the desert was like sitting in the middle of the sky. Stars above. Stars below. Nothing else.

"*It's lovely*," said Ilse quietly. "*I hate it.*"

"*Me too*," said Ivo.

Kestrel had lit a fire. A little one, from nothing, on the glass. It burned without fuel, the way her fires did when she wanted them to. Gold and red. They sat around it. Nobody wanted to look away from it into the dark.

"*What did you see?*" said Kestrel. To Lex. Not looking at him.

"My mother."

She nodded.

"*Tam*," she said. "*Solid. Laughing. He was holding out his hand.*" Her voice was hard. "*I wanted to take it so much I could feel my fingers aching.*"

Beside her, Tamsin put his faint hand over hers on the glass. She couldn't feel it. But she went quiet. And after a moment, her fire burned a little brighter.

"*Children*," said Dorran.

Everyone looked at him. He was sitting at the edge of the firelight, his great hands on his knees. He didn't look up.

"*My list*," he said. "*All of them. Looking up through the glass.*" He paused. "*Not angry. Just looking.*" He was quiet. "*That's worse.*"

Nobody said anything.

"*Oriel*," said Ivo after a while. "*What do you see?*"

The old woman was sitting on a folded blanket with her black stick across her knees and her white eyes turned toward the fire. She smiled faintly.

"*Nothing, boy*," she said. "*I've never seen anything in my life. That's my gift.*" She tilted her head. "*But I can hear it. The glass. Under all the light.*"

"*What does it sound like?*"

Oriel was quiet for a long moment.

"*Like something weeping*," she said. "*Very far down. Very old. For a very long time.*"

---

Lex couldn't sleep.

He lay on the warm glass in the dark with the stars above and the stars below, and the Ledger against his chest, and thought about not using the gold.

It was harder than he'd expected. Harder than the Lampshade. Every hour of the day, he wanted to reach for it. To look at the glass with True Sight and *see* it. To read it. To feel for the Bit with the Root and find it the way he'd found eleven holes in the net from his forge at Ashbourne. It would be so easy. One breath. One reach.

And something far down in the gold would open its eye.

*[I've been looking*,] said Ember, very quietly, in the dark. *[Every night. Since the Wickhollow. Since Oriel said it.]*

*And?*

*[Nothing. I can't find anything. I go down as far as I can go, into the Root, into the deep gold where your father's thread runs and your mother's used to and Aurelian's ran first of all.]* A pause. *[And there's nothing there. Nobody. Just the gold. Going down and down.]*

*Oriel said you weren't made to see that far.*

*[I know.]* Ember was quiet. *[I keep thinking about that. That I was made. That someone made me. Your father, out of a piece of himself. And he made me to see some things and not others.]* Another pause. *[Lex, what if he made me not to see it on purpose?]*

Lex lay very still.

*Father wouldn't—*

*[Wouldn't he? He hid you from the Order. He hid Antho from the Order. He hid your mother's fate for eleven years. He hid the Key for three.]* Ember's voice was very small. *[What if he's been hiding this too? From both of us?]*

Lex didn't answer.

He lay on the glass and looked up at the stars, and down at the stars, and thought about the letter he'd sent from the Wickhollow. *Something is watching through the Root, Father. Through my eyes. Did you know? Tell me if you knew.*

He didn't know if he wanted the answer.

---

It took four days to reach the center.

Four days of white light and terrible reflections and glass that rang under their boots like a struck bell. Ilse cut her hand on a glass branch on the second day, a long clean slice across the palm, and the blood ran down onto the glass and the glass drank it, and for an hour afterward she kept looking at her hand as if it belonged to someone else. Ivo stopped talking on the third day, which frightened Ilse more than anything. Dorran carried the water. All of it. On his back, in great sloshing skins, without complaint.

And Oriel led. Tapping her stick. Listening. Never once turning aside.

On the fourth day, in the afternoon, the glass began to slope downward.

Gently at first. Then more steeply. They were walking down the inside of a great bowl, a crater in the glass, miles across. The glass here was darker. Not gold-green but deep amber, and then brown, and then, near the bottom, black. Black glass, smooth and dark and gleaming, like obsidian.

And at the very bottom of the bowl, at the center of the crater, a tower.

It wasn't built. It had grown. A great spire of black glass, rising out of the crater floor, twisted and branching like a lightning strike frozen in place. Three hundred feet high. At its foot, a doorway. Narrow and dark.

And carved into the black glass above the doorway, an open hand.

And above the palm, a keyhole.

*[Ysmir's sigil,]* said Ember. *[Lex. That's it. That's where she put it.]*

Lex stood at the bottom of the crater and looked up at the black spire.

He didn't feel anything from it. Not with his thread shaded. Not without the gold. He couldn't feel its Wellscript, or its wards, or whatever was waiting inside. He could only see it with ordinary eyes. A tower of black glass in the middle of a desert, with a door.

It was the strangest feeling. Like being deaf in a room where everyone else was singing.

"*There's a fire in there*," said Kestrel.

He looked at her. She was standing very still beside him with her hands at her sides and her head tilted, the way Oriel tilted hers when she listened.

"*Under it*," said Kestrel. "*Under the whole crater. I can feel it in my feet.*" Her amber eyes were wide. "*It's not burning. It's—it's like a coal that's gone grey on top. Still hot underneath. Very hot. Very old.*" She swallowed. "*My grandmother's fire. The Ashari fire. It comes from there.*"

*[The Well,]* said Ember. *[The Flame-Well. It's not dead. It's banked.]*

And in the depths of himself, in the place below the Lattice where the river ran, Lex felt Flame turn its head.

He hadn't been reaching for it. He hadn't done anything. Flame wasn't the gold; it was below the gold, older than the Root, the deep wild river that Malachi had taught him to hear. And it had heard *him*. The way it always did, when he came near a fire. It turned toward him, from somewhere very far down under the black glass, and *leaned*.

*Hello*, he thought at it.

Something answered. Very faint. Very old.

Very, very sad.

---

The doorway opened to blood.

Of course it did. Lex cut his palm, the same way he'd cut it at Ysmir and Avaloria and the Frostvault, and laid it on the open hand carved in the glass, and the glass drank, and the doorway, which had been black and solid, went clear.

"*Just you*," said Oriel Sant, behind him. "*I can hear it. It'll only let the blood through.*"

"*Like the vault*," said Kestrel.

"*Like every Vey door in the world*," said Ivo. It was the first thing he'd said in two days. His voice was hoarse. "*Family's got a lot of doors.*"

Lex looked back at them. The Unlit. Burned and cut and half-blind from four days of glass. Kestrel, with her fire low and her brother at her shoulder. Dorran, with the water-skins on his back. The twins, holding hands. Oriel on her mule.

"If I'm not out by morning," he said.

"*We'll come in after you*," said Dorran.

"You can't. It won't let you."

"*Then we'll break it*," said Dorran simply. He hefted his hammer. "*I'm very good at hitting things too hard.*"

Lex almost laughed.

He went in.

---

There were stairs. Going down. Black glass, spiraling, lit from below by a faint red glow.

He went down a long way. Further than the tower was tall. Down into the crater floor, into the rock beneath the glass. The air got hotter. The red glow got brighter. The walls of the stair changed from black glass to red-black stone, veined with seams of something that glowed like the inside of a forge.

And then the stair ended, and he was standing at the edge of the Well.

It was a pit. Round. A hundred feet across. Its walls went straight down into a red darkness he couldn't see the bottom of. And from the darkness, very faint, came heat. And light. A dull, sullen, banked red glow, like a forge-fire that's been left overnight under a heap of ash.

In the middle of the pit, rising out of the darkness on a column of black glass as thin as a candle, was a cradle. A little cup of glass, the size of two hands held together.

And in the cradle, something black.

He couldn't see it properly. He looked at it, and his eyes slid off. A hole in the world, small and jagged, like a row of teeth.

The Bit.

There was no bridge. No path. Just the edge of the pit, and a hundred feet of red-glowing nothing, and the thin black column rising out of it with the black thing at its top.

*[Of course*,] said Ember. *[Of course there's no bridge. There's never a bridge.]*

Lex stood at the edge and looked down into the Well.

And the Well looked back.

---

He felt it. Through his feet. Through the stone. Through the deep place in him below the Lattice where the river ran. Flame. Not the wild bright Flame of Malachi's hearth, or the eternal fire under Qel'Varran's waters, or Kestrel's furious red. Something older. Banked. Grey on top. Hot underneath.

And grieving.

He didn't know how he knew. But he did. The way you know, walking into a house, that someone in it has died. The Flame-Well under the Glass Desert was grieving. It had been grieving for a very long time.

*[What happened to it?]* breathed Ember.

Lex didn't answer. He knelt at the edge of the pit. He put his scarred hands flat on the hot red-black stone. And he didn't reach for the gold.

He *listened*.

The way Malachi had taught him. The way he listened to iron in the coals, waiting for the color. The way he'd listened to the bedrock under Ysmir's temple. Not *do this*. Not *give me that*. Just: *I'm here. I'm listening. Tell me.*

And the Well told him.

Not in words. In heat. In a long slow pulse of red warmth that came up through the stone into his palms and his arms and his chest, carrying with it a picture, the way Thraxis had thrown a picture at him across the black hall at Duskhold.

*A sky. Blue. A plain of red earth and green grass and rivers. People, living on it. Herders. Farmers. Singing.*

*And something coming up out of the ground. Out of the deep dark under the world. Something hungry. Something with no shape, that ate light.*

*And the Well, rising to meet it. All of its fire. Everything. Pouring up out of the earth in a great burning column, a sun, a sun rising from below, to burn the hungry thing back down into the dark.*

*And it worked. It worked.*

*And when it was over, the plain was glass. And the people were gone. Every herder. Every farmer. Every singer. Burned in the light of the sun that had saved them.*

Lex knelt at the edge of the pit with tears running down his face and didn't know when he'd started crying.

*It saved them*, he thought. *It saved the whole world. And it killed everyone it was saving. And it's been grieving ever since.*

*[Nine hundred years,]* whispered Ember. *[No. Longer. Before the Lattice. Before the Founders. It's been down there alone in the dark all this time, banked under the glass, and nobody's ever come down to listen.]*

---

He spoke to it.

Not with Command. Not with the Root. With Flame. Speaking, the third stage of the Wells, the one Malachi had said was the hardest to reach and the easiest to misuse. *To Speak is not to give orders, smith. It is to answer back. To have a conversation. And most people, when they finally get to talk to something greater than themselves, only want to talk about what they want.*

He didn't talk about what he wanted.

*I'm sorry*, he told the Well. *I'm so sorry. Nobody should have to carry that alone.*

The heat under his palms flickered.

*You did the right thing. You saved the world. You couldn't have known. You couldn't have done it any other way.* He pressed his palms flatter. *I'm a smith. I know what fire does. Fire doesn't choose who it burns. It just burns. The only thing you can choose is what you burn it* for.
