# Epilogue: The Everlasting Legacy

Arion Vey was two hundred and thirty-three years old, and he had eleven grey hairs.

He'd counted them that morning. In the little mirror Seraphine had given Antho, which Antho had put in the cold-room, which Teo had used to look at fish, and which now hung on a nail in the passage outside the library, because Odile said someone in the Lantern Keep ought to be able to see what they looked like.

Eleven. At the temples, mostly. And one, very bold, right at the front, curling over his forehead like a question mark.

And lines. At the corners of his eyes. Around his mouth. Faint. But there.

He'd stood in front of the mirror for a long time. Looking. A lean man with dark hair going grey, and grey eyes, and a face that had stopped being forty-five forever and had started, at last, to be whatever it was going to be.

Then he'd gone down to breakfast and told Pim, who had said, very seriously, "*You look like a badger*," and then laughed so hard he fell off the bench.

Pim laughed a lot now. Arion had never heard a sound he liked better.

---

The Hollowmaw was full.

He'd never seen it so full. Not in the three years he'd kept the door. Not even on the night of the Blade, when three hundred Keepers stood on the slopes and two dragons held the sky.

It was the eve of the wedding. *Weddings*. Two of them. Tomorrow, at noon, in the bell-shaped cavern at the bottom of the mountain, in front of the sealed black door, among one thousand one hundred and eleven lanterns.

Both his sons. On the same day.

Antho had said it was the only sensible thing. *One wedding's bad enough. Two would be unbearable. Get it all over with at once.* Seraphine had said he was the least romantic man in Caldor, and then kissed him, and then gone off to argue with the Queen's steward about seating.

Lex had just said, *yes*. And looked at Wren. And gone red to the ears.

So everyone had come.

---

The Queen of Caldor was sleeping in the library, on a camp bed, because it was the only room left and she'd refused to let anyone give up theirs. Elowen Dunmore was sleeping across the doorway, with Oathkeeper across her knees. Roole and Fenn and half the Keepers of the Stones were in tents on the slope.

Queen Ishara of the Sunreach had arrived that afternoon on a golden horse with fifty riders and Zahir on her shoulder, and had taken one look at the Hollowmaw and said, *you live in a* cave? to Antho, who had said *yes, Majesty*, and Ishara had laughed until she cried.

Kestrel Varn had come with her. Brown from the southern sun. With a new scar on her chin and her hair burning gold, quietly, all the time now, like a lamp left on in a window. She'd brought Tamsin, who'd brought a cow. Nobody knew why. The cow was called Prudence. It was in the pen with Correspondence, and they'd taken against each other immediately.

Teo Brask had come from the Tidewater coast on Ashka's back, dripping, with a fish. A very large fish. Silver. Still alive, somehow, in a barrel of seawater. *The sea sent it*, he said. *As a wedding present. For Master Antho. It says he'll know why.* Antho had looked at the fish for a long time. And then laughed. And put it in the cold-room.

Brannoc and Hedda had come on a cart full of iron and opinions. Tilda Crane had come with them, with Brannoc's hammer on her back and forty students behind her. Mother Orla had come, with bacon. Quill had come, for the bacon.

The Marrow Players had set up their cart on the slope below the cave mouth. Ilse and Ivo were rehearsing something. Every so often an illusion flickered up over the cart: a dragon, a knight, a lantern the size of a house. Ivo kept falling off the cart. Ilse kept laughing.

Corin Bell had come from Harrowgate with his sister. Mella sang to the goats. They didn't like it.

Hollis Penn had come from the Lower Town with a wagon of bread, because, he said, nobody was getting married at the Lantern Keep without a proper loaf, and he'd heard the cooking in caves was terrible.

A seventeen-year-old standard-bearer named Benno had come with the Ashen Banners, all two hundred of them, carrying a black and grey banner and a greatsword with a hilt worn smooth by a two-fingered hand.

And an old woman from Fen Hollow named Bess Rook, Spring Kindling, Ceiling twelve, had walked three hundred miles with a stick, because, she said, she'd never been to a wedding in a cave, and she wanted to see the big smith who'd made the lights come on.

---

Arion stood at the mouth of the Hollowmaw at dusk and looked at all of them.

On the slope below. Around the cookfires. In the tents. On the cart. Laughing and arguing and eating Hollis Penn's bread. Two dragons sprawled across the far ridge like a pair of sleeping storm-clouds. A storm-mother, vast and whole and blue, with her daughter curled against her side.

And in the middle of it, his sons.

Lex was by the biggest fire. With Wren beside him. Telling a story, badly, with his hands. Brannoc was laughing at him. Tilda Crane was laughing at Brannoc.

Antho was sitting a little apart. On a rock. With Seraphine leaning against his shoulder and Pim on his other side. Not talking. Just watching. The way he always did. Counting, probably. Arion would have bet money he was counting.

*Two hundred and thirty-three years*, he thought. *And I've never once seen anything like this.*

He thought about Seren.

He thought about her every evening, at this hour. When the light was going. When the lanterns in the Hollowmaw began to glow gold behind him. He thought about a woman he'd met on the steps of the Spire in the rain, two hundred years into his long life, who'd looked at him as if he were the first light she'd ever seen.

Who didn't remember that now.

Who'd fallen in love with him again anyway. In the dark. Through the Root. Not knowing who he was.

*She'd have loved this*, he thought. *All of it. The bread and the dragons and the cow.*

*She'd have sung. Flat on the high note.*

---

He went down to the door at midnight.

He always did. Every night. Down the long passage, past one thousand one hundred and eleven lanterns, into the bell-shaped cavern at the bottom of the mountain. To the sealed black door. And the niche beside it. And the two lamps.

He'd been doing it for a year and a half. Since the spring after the Heart. Since his sons came home and told him she'd stayed. In the dark. To walk the lost ones home.

*Leave a light on, love*.

So he did.

He took the little copper oil-flask from his coat. He filled the clay lamp, which didn't need it. He filled the iron one, which didn't need it either. He stood back and looked at them. Two small gold flames, side by side, in front of a door of black glass.

"*Good evening*," said Arion Vey.

And the clay lamp leaned.

---

He went very still.

It leaned. Toward the door. The way it had leaned every night for three years, when she came to the other side of the glass. The way it had leaned, very rarely, very faintly, this past year and a half, late at night, when she passed somewhere near on her long walk.

But not faintly.

*Hard*. Far over. Almost flat. The little gold flame bent so far toward the black glass that it nearly touched it.

And then the iron one leaned too.

Arion had never seen the iron one lean. Not once. It was Lex's. Twenty Hush-runes. *STAY* on the inside of the ring. It burned straight up, always, steady as a forge.

It was leaning.

Both of them. Side by side. Toward the door.

And on the black glass, in the gold light of the leaning lamps, words.

---

Not carved. Not written. *Shining*. Like a panel. Like the old Lattice panels, silver and gold, that had hung over people's heads for nine hundred years before his son reforged the net and gave it away.

But not over anyone's head.

On the door.

---

Arion read it.

He read it twice.

And then he sat down. On the black floor of the cavern. Very suddenly. With his back against the wall beside the niche, and the two lamps leaning over his head.

*I'm coming home*.

He couldn't breathe.

*I'm coming home*.

After eleven years on the far side of a door. After paying him away to the Ferryman. After a year and a half walking in the dark, walking the lost ones home. She was coming home.

He put his face in his hands. And laughed. And wept. Both. At once. The way his sons did. The way he'd never, in two hundred and thirty-three years, let himself do.

And then he took his hands away.

And read the last line again.

*But not alone*.

---

The lamps were still leaning.

Arion looked at them. At the two small flames, bent toward the black glass. At the words shining on its surface.

And beneath the words, in the glass, faint, like a reflection in deep water, something moving.

He leaned closer.

Shapes. On the other side of the door. Very far down. Very faint. Walking. Coming up out of the deep dark, the way you'd see someone coming up a long road at night with a lantern.

One small. In a faded blue dress. Holding nothing in her hands. Singing, he thought. Though he couldn't hear it.

One tall and gaunt, in robes that had been white. Walking a little behind. Straight-backed. Like a Warden.

And behind them—

He couldn't see. It was too far. Too faint. A shape. Or shapes. Following. Many, maybe. Or one, very large. He couldn't tell. Something that was coming up out of the deep dark behind his wife, along the long road home, the way the drowned dead had come toward Antho's lantern.

He couldn't tell if it was following her.

Or if she was leading it.

---

The words on the glass faded.

The lamps straightened. Slowly. The clay one, then the iron one. Until they burned straight up again, side by side, small and gold and steady, in front of the sealed black door.

And the shapes in the glass were gone.

Arion sat on the floor of the cavern for a long time.

Then he got up.

Slowly. One hand on the wall. The way an old man gets up. He was an old man now. Or starting to be. He had eleven grey hairs and lines at the corners of his eyes, and he'd spent two hundred years keeping secrets because he was afraid of what would happen if he didn't.

He looked at the door. At the two lamps.

*Antho*, he thought. *Lex*.

*They're asleep. It's midnight. They're getting married tomorrow. In front of this door. With the Queen and the cow and the fish.*

*I could wait. I could tell them after. Let them have tomorrow. Let them*—

He stopped.

He stood in front of the door with his hand on the cold black glass and thought about the portraits in the secret room in the Spire. Two hundred years of his own face. Arion Vey the scholar. Arion Vey the Warden. Arion Vey's son. Arion Vey's grandson. Every one of them keeping something. Every one of them waiting for the right moment to tell.

*No*, he thought.

*No more*.

---

He went up the long passage.

Past one thousand one hundred and eleven lanterns. Not counting them. He'd never been the one who counted.

He found Lex asleep by the dying fire on the slope, with Wren curled against his back and the little silver bell on a cord round his neck, silent. He found Antho in the library doorway, asleep sitting up against the frame, with Seraphine's head in his lap and the Lantern beside him.

He woke them both.

He didn't wait. He didn't make it gentle. He knelt down in front of his sons, at midnight, on the eve of their wedding, and said:

"*She sent a message. Through the door. Just now.*"

And told them. Every word.

---

They went down together.

All three of them. Father and sons. With the Lantern. And Wren and Seraphine behind them, who had woken too, and come, and weren't going to be left behind, obviously.

They stood in front of the door.

The lamps burned straight up. The glass was smooth and black and silent. No words. No shapes.

But Antho put his hand on it.

And stood there for a long time. With his clear grey eyes closed. Listening. The way he listened to the dark. The way he'd listened to a door for eight years from one side and three from the other.

"*She's there*," he said at last. Very quietly. "*Far down. Very far. Walking.*" He opened his eyes. "*Coming up.*"

"*And the rest*?" said Lex. "*Not alone. What did she mean?*"

Antho was quiet.

"*I don't know*," he said. "*It's too far. It's too deep.*" He took his hand off the glass. "*But there's something with her. More than one. I can feel the shape of the quiet around them.*" He looked at his father. "*And it's a big shape.*"

Nobody said anything.

---

Lex took something out of his coat.

A lantern. Small. Iron. Squat and round, like a clay lamp, with a ring to carry it by. Not Hush-runes. Not twenty runes. One. On the glass.

*ARDENT*.

"*I made it at the forge*," he said. "*Last week. For the wedding.*" He looked at it. "*For the window. At the cottage. At Harrow.*" His voice was rough. "*I thought, when we go home after, there should be a light in the window.*"

He set it down.

In the niche. Beside the clay lamp and the iron one. Three lamps now. Side by side. In front of the sealed black door.

He lit it. With his own fire. The fire he said good morning to every day.

It burned. Small. Gold. Steady.

And Antho, standing beside him, with his father on his other side and the two women they loved behind them and a door in front of them with their mother somewhere on the other side of it, coming home, counted.

"*One thousand one hundred and thirteen*," said Antho Vey.

He looked at his brother.

"*I always count*," he said.

---

Upstairs, on the slope, under the stars, a vast storm-mother lifted her head off the ridge and looked toward the cave mouth. And made a sound. Low and deep and rumbling. Like thunder over mountains a hundred miles off.

Like recognition.

And in the cold-room of the Lantern Keep, in a barrel of seawater, a silver fish turned once in the dark, and was still.

And on a cord around Lex Vey's neck, against his chest, under his shirt, a little silver bell with a crooked clapper, which had not made a sound since the Heart, since an old man's hands went still around it, since a nine-hundred-year sadness ended—

Rang.

Once.

Very softly. Flat.

*Ting*.

---

*Here ends* The Sorcerer's Legacy, *Book Two of the Lattice Chronicles.*

*The story continues.*
