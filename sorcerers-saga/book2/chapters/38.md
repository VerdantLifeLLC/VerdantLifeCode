# A New Era of Balance

They buried Malachi at the Stillwater, by his fire.

It was burning when they got there. Lex hadn't expected that. Nobody had tended it in months. Malachi had left it to go out every night at midnight, drawn down the road to the Heart, and walked away up the Greyspine with his goats.

But when they came down into the hidden green valley in the white salt of the Wastes, the eternal fire was burning in its ring of stones in the hearth of the little hut by the still pool. Small. Gold. Steady. As if it had relit itself the moment the Tithe ended. As if it had been waiting for him.

They dug the grave beside the pool. Lex and Antho. With borrowed spades. While Wren and Seraphine and the others stood in a ring, and the goats wandered among them eating everyone's hems, and Correspondence stood at the head of the grave chewing very slowly and watching with its yellow eye.

They laid him in it in his old robe, with his straw hat on his chest and his driftwood staff beside him. Small and brown and barefoot.

Lex took out the bell.

He'd carried it all the way from the Heart. In his coat. Beside the Ledger and Dorran's book. It hadn't rung once.

He knelt at the edge of the grave and held it out over the old man's folded hands.

And didn't let go.

*[Lex*?] said Ember.

*He'd want me to keep it*, thought Lex. *Wouldn't he.*

*[Why*?]

*So I'd know*. He closed his hand around the little silver bell. *If anyone was sad. So I'd know to come and find them.*

The coal in his hearth was quiet for a long time.

*[Yes*,] said Ember. *[Yes. He'd want that.]*

---

Antho said the words. There weren't many. Antho had never been good at words.

"*He was the youngest of seven*," he said. Standing at the head of the grave, with the Lantern in his hand. "*He was sad for nine hundred years. He taught me the dark wasn't my enemy. He taught my brother to say good morning to fire.*" He was quiet. "*And when it mattered, he got up off the bucket.*"

He looked down at the old man's face.

"*Thank you, Mal*," said Antho Vey. "*Go and sit with your brother.*"

And Correspondence, standing at the head of the grave, bleated. Once. Very loudly.

And then walked away and ate a bush.

Wren started laughing. She couldn't help it. And then Seraphine. And then Lex. Standing by an open grave in the Wastes, laughing until they cried, while a goat ate a gorse bush and the eternal fire burned gold in the hut behind them.

Malachi would have liked that, Lex thought. Malachi would have liked that very much.

---

The world was different.

Lex noticed it the moment they came out of the Wastes. Small things, at first. The way the light looked. The way people walked.

There were no Ceilings.

Not anywhere. Not on anyone. He couldn't see threads anymore. He'd given that away at the Heart, with the rest of the Root. But he didn't need to see them. He could see it in people's faces. In the way they stood. Like people who'd been holding their breath their whole lives and had only just noticed they could stop.

The first village they came to was a farming place on the edge of the salt, called Brine. Forty houses. A Stone on the green.

There was a girl at the Stone. Fourteen. Kindling-day. With her hand on it.

And there was no Warden standing beside her with a hand on her shoulder. Just her mother. And her grandmother. And half the village, standing round, waiting.

The Stone lit up under her hand. Silver and gold. A panel rose over her head. Lex couldn't read it anymore. But he heard her read it. Out loud. Her voice shaking.

"*Healer*," said the girl. "*Rare. Level one.*" She stared at it. "*No Ceiling. Mam. Mam, it says no Ceiling. It says* growth: unbounded.*"

Her mother put her hand over her mouth.

And her grandmother, who was seventy if she was a day, who had been capped at fourteen at this same Stone sixty years ago, sat down very suddenly on the grass and started to cry.

---

"*Unbounded*," said Wren. That night. In the inn at Brine. Sitting on the end of the bed with her boots off. "*That's the word. Unbounded.*"

"Yes."

"*Everyone*." She looked at her hands. "*Everyone in the world. Unbounded. Forever.*" She shook her head slowly. "*Hammer, do you know what that means? Every child who Kindles from now on. They can be anything. As high as they want. As far as they can go.*"

"Yes."

"*You did that*."

"*We did that*," said Lex. "*All of us. Kestrel and Teo and you and Antho and Mam. And Malachi. And Elric, at the Rift. And Dorran. And Kell, at the end.*" He was quiet. "*And Aurelian. At the very end. When he let go.*"

Wren looked at him for a long time.

"*You gave it away*," she said. "*The Root. All of it. You could've been the most powerful man in the world.*"

"*I know*."

"*Why didn't you*?"

He thought about it. The way he always did now.

"*Because I'd have been at the center*," he said. "*Holding everything. Like him. Like Father.*" He looked at his hands. Big. Scarred. Ordinary. "*And I don't want to hold everything. I want to hold you.*"

Wren didn't say anything for a long moment.

Then she reached out and took his hand. And held it. Not tight. Not afraid. The way you hold something you've chosen to hold.

"*Idiot*," she said softly.

---

Highcrest was full of lanterns again.

They came in on the first night of summer, on Vharuun's back, all of them, and the whole golden city was lit up like the festival. Every window. Every eave. Every balcony and lamppost and rooftop. Gold.

The Queen met them on the castle wall. In a plain blue dress, with no jewels, and a thin silver circlet on her brow. She didn't say anything. She came down the steps and put her arms around Lex. And then around Antho. And then, after a moment's hesitation, around Wren, who went very red.

"*They're for you*," said Lysandra. Looking out at the lanterns. "*All of them. Nobody ordered it. They just started lighting them. The night the World Notice came.*" She was quiet. "*They've lit them every night since.*"

There was a lantern in the window of the Lower Town bakery. A big one. Shaped like a loaf of bread.

---

Lord Chancellor Varric Sorn had been found in the spring.

In a fishing village on the northern coast, in a borrowed coat, trying to buy passage across the White Shore. He'd shaved his silver beard. A ten-year-old girl had recognized him anyway, from the coins.

He'd been brought to Highcrest in chains. Ordinary chains. Not Severance. There was no Severance anymore. Nobody could cut anyone off from the net.

The Queen had stripped him of his titles. His lands. His seat on the Council.

And then, instead of the dungeon, she'd sentenced him to ten years as schoolmaster in the village of Fen Hollow. Past the Blightmarch. Teaching letters to the children of the people Dorran Vask had capped.

"*He'll hate it*," said Elowen Dunmore, with deep satisfaction.

"*He'll hate it*," the Queen agreed. "*And every one of those children is unbounded now. Every one. Bright as stars.*" Her mouth twitched. "*He'll spend ten years teaching the very people he wanted to put on a list.*"

"*That's cruel*, *Majesty*," said Roole.

"*That's justice*, *Commander*." Lysandra looked at him. "*There's a difference. Seraphine taught me. She found it in a book.*"

---

The Order was gone.

Not destroyed. *Changed*. Roole had done it. Over the summer. Standing in front of the High Council in the Spire with his hatchet face set like stone, and telling them that there was nothing left to ward. No Ceilings to set. No Sanctions to grant. No Inspections. No Severance.

"*So what are we for*?" an old Warden had asked. From the tiers.

"*The Stones*," said Roole. "*Someone has to look after them. Keep them clean. Keep them straight. Stand beside them on Kindling-day, so children aren't frightened.*" He looked around the chamber. "*Not to put the lid on. To be there when it doesn't.*"

They called themselves the Keepers of the Stones now. Roole was their Commander. Fenn was his lieutenant.

The first thing they did was straighten every leaning Stone in the west.

---

Arion was getting old.

Lex saw it the first time he went back to the Hollowmaw. In late summer. On Ashka, alone, with Wren on the wind beside him.

His father was at the mouth of the cave. Sitting on the rock where Pim always sat. With Pim beside him. Talking.

*Talking*. Pim. The boy who'd said six words in his first year at the Keep. Sitting on a rock in the sunshine, with his knees pulled up, telling Arion Vey something long and complicated about the bats in the cold-room. Waving his hands.

Arion was listening. Gravely. With his lean face turned toward the boy.

And his hair was going grey.

Not much. At the temples. A few threads. Silver in the dark. Lex saw them as Ashka came down on the slope, and felt something in his chest go tight and warm at the same time.

His father looked up. Saw him. And stood up.

Slowly. The way an old man stands. One hand on the rock.

"*Lex*," said Arion Vey.

"*Father*." Lex slid down off Ashka's neck. He walked up the slope. He stopped in front of his father.

And looked at the grey at his temples.

"*I found another one this morning*," said Arion. He touched it, with two fingers. Almost shyly. "*And a line here.*" He touched the corner of his eye. "*Odile says I look distinguished. Teo says I look like a badger.*"

"*You look old*," said Lex.

His father's lean face did the thing it almost never did.

"*Yes*," said Arion Vey. And smiled. A real smile. Wide. Startled. Like a man who has been given something he stopped hoping for a long time ago. "*Yes. I do. Isn't it wonderful?*"

---

The door at the bottom of the Hollowmaw was sealed.

Lex went down to see it. With his father. Down the long passage, past one thousand one hundred and eleven lanterns, into the bell-shaped cavern.

The black glass was smooth. Unbroken. The crack that had been there since the night of the Blade was gone. Healed. As if it had never been. When Lex put his hand on it, he felt nothing on the other side. No hunger. No pushing. No whisper.

Just a deep, soft, peaceful quiet. Like the dark between the stars.

*Shade*, he thought. *Not the Hollow. Not anymore. Just the dark.*

And in the niche beside it, two lamps. A little clay one, brown and round and plain, with a chip in its spout and a thumbprint in its base. And an iron one, squat and heavy, with twenty runes on its glass.

Both burning.

"*Every night*," said Arion. Beside him. Quietly. "*I fill them. They don't need it. I fill them anyway.*" He looked at the clay lamp. "*And I say good evening.*"

"*Does she answer*?"

His father was quiet for a long time.

"*No*," he said at last. "*She's too far. She's walking.*" He looked at the black glass. "*But sometimes, very late, the clay one leans. Just a hair. Toward the door.*" He swallowed. "*I think that's her. Somewhere. Passing near. On her way to somewhere else.*"

Lex looked at his father. At the grey at his temples. At his hands, with the brown spot between the knuckles.

"*She doesn't remember you*," he said. Very gently. "*Father. She told us. At the Heart. She paid you to the Ferryman. Eleven years ago. To cross back.*"

"*I know*," said Arion.

Lex stared at him.

"*Antho told me*," said his father. "*In the spring. When you came back.*" He looked at the clay lamp. "*He said she'd paid away every memory of me. Our meeting. Our wedding. Everything.*" His voice was very steady. "*And then she spent three years in the dark talking to me through the Root every night, not knowing who I was. And fell in love with me again anyway.*"

He was quiet.

"*Do you know how many people get to be fallen in love with twice*?" said Arion Vey. "*By the same person? Without her even remembering the first time?*"

Lex didn't say anything.

"*I'll wait*," said his father. "*For her to come home. I'll get old waiting. Older.*" He touched the grey at his temples. "*And when she comes, I'll tell her. Everything. The Spire. The rain. The steps. Every word.*" He smiled. "*And she'll say, tell me about us. And I'll tell her.*"

"*Like Antho and the stone*."

"*Like Antho and the stone*." His father looked at him. "*It won't be the same. I know that. It'll be something else.*" He was quiet. "*But it'll be something. That's not nothing.*"

---

Ilse and Ivo Marrow put on a play in the square at Highcrest on the last night of summer.

They called themselves the Marrow Players. Two people and a cart. And a great deal of illusion. They'd been touring all summer. The Bitter Hills. The Vale. The twelve villages past the Blightmarch, where they stood beside every Stone on Kindling-day, in Dorran Vask's place, with their hands on children's shoulders.

The play was *The Knights of the Ford*.

Lex watched it from the steps of the Spire, with Wren beside him. Two knights, holding a river crossing, back to back. The river rising. Made of light, of illusion, so real that the people in the front rows flinched from the water.

And at the end, the last verse. The real one. Sung by Ilse, alone, in the middle of the square, in a clear thin voice.

*But they did not drown because they held.*
*They drowned because each tried to let the other go,*
*and the other one came back.*

The square was very quiet.

And then Ivo, standing at the side of the stage, made his own wrist look like nothing. Held it up. A trick of the light. A hand, ending in empty air.

And Ilse reached out and took hold of it anyway.

The square erupted.

---

Kestrel Varn went to the Sunreach.

She came to say goodbye in the autumn, at the Ashbourne Forge, with Tamsin beside her. She had a pack on her back and a new red coat, not scorched at all, and her hair was tied back with a strip of Sunreach cloth.

"*Ishara wrote*," she said. "*She says there are fire-women in the south. Old ones. Ashari. Who remember things about the fire that nobody in Caldor ever knew.*" She looked at her hands. "*She says I should come and learn.*"

"*You should*," said Lex.

"*I know*." Kestrel looked at her brother. "*Tam's not coming.*"

"*I'm going home*," said Tamsin Varn. "*To Coldbrook. To the farm.*" He shrugged. "*Someone has to bring the cows in.*"

"*He names them*," said Kestrel. "*All of them. Talks to them by name. Every evening.*" Her mouth twisted. "*It's mortifying.*"

"*It's in the Ledger*," said Lex.

Kestrel looked at him.

"*Ysmir wrote it*," said Lex. "*In the Ledger. The first time. Tamsin Varn, of Coldbrook. Twenty-one. Farmer. He brought the cows in every evening and talked to each one by name.*" He smiled. "*She thought it was worth remembering.*"

Kestrel stared at him for a long moment.

Then she laughed. And hugged her brother. Hard. And hugged Lex, harder. And walked out of the Ashbourne Forge into the autumn sunshine, heading south.

She didn't look back.

But Lex saw her lift one hand, at the bend in the road, without turning around. And a little flame came up off her fingers, gold, and danced there for a moment.

And then she was gone.

---

Teo Brask went home to the Tidewater.

He was sixteen. He said he'd been homesick for a year and a half and he'd had enough. And besides, the sea had told him it wanted him back.

"*It says I should be the Tide Speaker*," he said. Dripping, on the steps of the Lantern Keep. "*For the coast. For the fishing villages. It says there hasn't been one in nine hundred years and it's been very lonely.*" He went red. "*It says I'm the only one who ever asks it nicely.*"

Antho put both hands on the boy's shoulders.

"*Write*," he said. "*Every week. Or send a fish. Or whatever the sea does.*"

"*I will, Master Antho*." Teo was crying. Or the sea was. It was hard to tell. "*Thank you. For the cold-room. For the fish in the bucket. For not laughing.*"

"*I laughed*," said Antho. "*Every day. When you weren't looking.*"

Teo laughed too. And hugged him. And climbed up onto Ashka's back, dripping, for the flight east. And waved all the way out of sight.

---

Tilda Crane got Brannoc's hammer.

Lex didn't see it happen. He heard about it from Hedda, who told it with her cudgel across her knees and a face like thunder that didn't fool anyone.

Brannoc had called the girl over to the great hearth at the end of the day. In front of everyone. All forty students. And held out his hammer. The big one. The one he'd used for fifty years. The one he'd swung in the Ashbourne forge when Lex was eleven, and on Hedda's barricade at the Hollowmaw, and every day in between.

*It's yours now, lass*, he'd said.

Tilda had stared at it.

*I'm too old for the big work*, Brannoc had said. *Knees. Arm. Hedda says I'll fall over.* He'd shrugged his enormous shoulders. *And you hear the fire. Properly. I never did. Not once in sixty years. You talk to it, and it talks back.* He'd put the hammer in her hands. *You should have a hammer that's been talked to.*

"*She cried*," said Hedda. "*All over it. Rusted the face. He had to grind it.*" She scowled. "*Sentimental old fool.*"

"*You cried too*," said Lex.

"*I did not*."

"*Hedda*."

"*Something in my eye*," said Hedda Vorne. "*Soot. It's a forge.*"

---

He made the ring in winter.

In the Ashbourne Forge. At night. When the hall was empty and the side-fires were banked and the great hearth glowed red.

He'd been thinking about it since the Wellworld of Gale. Since Wren had opened her hand in the sky and let him fall and trusted the wind to catch him. Since she'd said *never again* in a wrecked ballroom with her shoes off.

He'd known what he wanted to make it from for longer than that.

The nail was in a box in Brannoc's loft. He'd asked for it. Brannoc had gone very quiet, and then climbed up the ladder, slow, with his bad knees, and come back down with it in his palm.

Old. Black. Square-headed. Hand-forged. A hundred years old at least.

It had come out of the door of the old Ashbourne forge. The little one, with a single anvil, where Lex had learned his trade. Brannoc's forge, and his father's before him. When they knocked the wall through to build the long hall with twenty anvils, Brannoc had pulled the nail out of the old door frame with his own hands and put it in a box.

*First nail I ever drew*, Brannoc had said. *My da showed me. When I was eleven.* He'd put it in Lex's hand. *Same age as you. When I showed you.*

Lex held the nail over the fire for a long time.

Then he worked it. Slowly. Carefully. Asking. The way the Hearth had taught him. The way Brannoc had taught him, before that. *You're not making it do anything, lad. You're asking it nicely to be something else for a while.*

He drew it out. Thin. Round. Bent it into a circle. Closed it. Small. Plain. Black iron, with the faint grain of a hundred years of a forge door still in it.

And on the inside, where only the person wearing it would see, he cut one rune.

*STAY*.

*[It's beautiful*,] said Ember. In the hearth. Very softly.

*It's an old nail*.

*[It's the most beautiful thing you've ever made*,] said Ember. *[And you made a Lockward out of the bones of the world.]*

---

He asked her on the roof of the Spire.

At dawn. On the first day of spring. A year after Fen Hollow. Two years after the festival. Five years after he first met a girl with a fox-red braid at Thistledown, on the road to the Greyspine, and she told him he walked like a bear in a china shop.

She'd waited for him on that roof. Every dawn, for a month. When he was in the north, and she was guarding his father, and she didn't know if he was alive.

He climbed up the long spiral stair in the dark. She was already there. Sitting on the parapet, with her back to the eight-hundred-foot drop and her boots dangling over the roof, watching the sky go grey over the golden city.

She looked round when he came up through the trapdoor.

"*Hammer*," she said. "*What are you doing up here? You hate heights.*"

"*I know*."

"*You're shaking*."

"*I know*."

She stared at him. Then her green eyes narrowed.

"*What*," said Wren Ashdown, "*have you done.*"

He didn't answer. He came across the roof. He stopped in front of her, where she sat on the parapet. He knelt down. On the cold white stone of the roof of the Spire, eight hundred feet above Highcrest, at dawn.

And held out his hand.

The ring sat in his palm. Small. Plain. Black iron.

Wren looked at it for a very long time.

"*It's a nail*," she said at last.

"*It's a nail*," Lex agreed. "*From the door of Brannoc's first forge. His father showed him how to draw it, when he was eleven.*" His voice was shaking. "*And there's a rune on the inside. You can't see it unless you're wearing it.*"

"*What rune*?"

"*Put it on and see*."

---

She put it on.

He watched her do it. Slowly. Her hands not quite steady. She slid it onto her finger and turned it, and he saw her feel the rune. Cut into the inside of the band. Against her skin.

He saw her face change.

"*Stay*," she whispered.

"*The bearer cannot be severed*," said Lex, "*from those they hold dear.*" He swallowed. "*By any working of the Lattice or the Hollow. Or anything else.*"

Wren Ashdown sat on the parapet of the Spire with the dawn coming up behind her and the ring on her finger and stared at him.

"*You absolute*," she said. "*You stupid, idiot, stubborn, enormous—*"

"*I know*."

"*On the* roof. *At* dawn. *When you hate heights.*"

"*You waited for me up here*," said Lex. "*Every morning. For a month. When you didn't know if I was coming back.*" His voice cracked. "*I wanted to ask you somewhere you'd waited for me. So you'd know I came.*"

She didn't say anything.

Then she slid down off the parapet. Onto her knees. On the roof. In front of him. And put her hands on either side of his face. And looked at him. Fierce and windburned and so beautiful he couldn't breathe.

"*You haven't actually asked*," she said.

"*Will you marry me*?" said Lex Vey.

"*Yes*," said Wren. "*Obviously. You idiot.*"

And she kissed him. On the roof of the Spire. At dawn. With the sun coming up gold over the city of lanterns, and somewhere below, very faintly, a church bell starting to ring for the morning.

---

Antho skipped a stone on the lake beyond the Teeth in the first week of summer.

Lex had promised. Years ago. On the plinth at Duskhold, and in the forge at the Hollowmaw, and in a hundred letters. *When this is done. When it's all quiet. We'll go to your lake. And skip stones.*

It was a lake in a high valley on the far side of the Teeth. Where the world had been thin and grey. It wasn't grey now. The Tithe was gone. The world had come back. Green and blue and full of birds. The lake was very still. Very flat. Perfect for skipping stones.

They stood on the shore. The two of them. With Wren and Seraphine sitting on a rock a little way back, pretending not to watch.

Lex had told him the story that morning. The way he did every morning. The millpond. The willows. The heron. Five.

"*Your turn*," said Lex. And held out a stone.

Flat. Grey. Smooth. He'd found it on the shore. He'd looked for an hour.

Antho took it.

He weighed it in his hand. Looked at the lake. Looked at his brother.

"*Low*," said Lex. "*Flatter. Like it's trying to fly under a door.*"

"*I know how to skip a stone*."

"*You don't remember*."

"*I've seen it*," said Antho. "*From the water. And from the inside. At the Ferryman's shore.*" He turned the stone over. "*I know how it goes.*"

He threw.

---

Low. Flat. Spinning. Leaving his hand like a bird.

It touched the water.

Lifted.

*One*, said Antho. Out loud.

Touched. Lifted.

*Two*.

Lex had stopped breathing.

*Three. Four*.

And Antho's whole face was lighting up. From inside. Like a window when someone lights a lamp. Like a small freckled boy on a riverbank turning round with the sun coming out on his face.

*Five*.

The stone sank. A little ring spreading out on the still water where it went down.

And on the far shore, a heron lifted off the reeds. Great grey wings flapping. Startled.

The two brothers stood on the shore of the lake beyond the Teeth and watched it go.

"*Five*," said Antho.

"*Five*," said Lex.

"*I counted*." His brother's voice was shaking. "*I always count.*"

And then he turned round. And picked Lex up. All of him. The size of a door. And swung him round. Staggering. Nearly falling into the lake. While Lex shouted and Wren shrieked with laughter on her rock and Seraphine put her face in her hands.

"*You're brilliant*," said Antho Vey. Into his brother's shoulder. Laughing. Crying. "*You're brilliant. You're going to be better at this than me.*"

---

He asked Seraphine that evening.

By the lake. At sunset. With a ring he'd had Lex make. Silver. Plain. With a rune on the inside: *ARDENT*. The first rune. The one that lit every lantern in the Hollowmaw.

Lex didn't hear what he said. He and Wren had gone up the hill, on purpose, and sat on the slope with their backs to the lake. Not looking.

But they heard Seraphine.

Everyone in the valley heard Seraphine.

"*YES*," shouted the Royal Archivist of Caldor. "*YES, you ridiculous, wonderful, lantern-counting, absolutely* impossible *man, YES, of course yes, did you think I'd say anything else, did you think I'd read every letter you ever wrote me eleven times and kept them in a box under my bed and NOT say yes*—"

Wren was laughing so hard she fell over.

Lex lay on his back on the hillside beside her, with the stars coming out over the Teeth, and the lake going dark below, and the sound of his brother laughing drifting up the slope.

*[Lex*,] said Ember. In his hearth. Very softly.

*Mm*.

*[I'm writing it down. All of it. The stone. The heron. The ring.]* A pause. *[I've been writing everything down. Since the Heart. In the hearth. A book.]*

*A book*?

*[Somebody should*,] said Ember. *[So it's kept. The way Ysmir kept the names.]* Another pause. *[The Book of What Was. Volume Two.]*

Lex laughed. Out loud. On the hillside.

"*What*?" said Wren.

"*Ember's writing a book*."

Wren rolled over. Propped her chin on his chest. Looked at him in the starlight with her ring on her finger and her green eyes bright.

"*About what*?"

Lex thought about it.

"*About us*," he said. "*All of us. Everything that happened.*" He looked up at the stars. "*So nobody forgets.*"

Wren was quiet for a long moment.

"*Tell it to put in the bit about the roof*," she said. "*And the nail. And how you were shaking.*"

*[Already done*,] said Ember. *[Page four hundred and twelve.]*
