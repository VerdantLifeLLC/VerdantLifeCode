@@PART Part One: The Key of Shadows

# Three Years of Peace

The iron was wrong, and Lex knew it before the hammer came down.

He knew it the way he'd always known it: a tiny lurch of the stomach, a sense of the world being a finger's width off true. A dull note under the iron's ring. A seam, deep in the heart of the bar, where some careless smelter had let a bubble of slag cool inside.

He didn't turn his wrist this time. He didn't need to.

He laid his palm flat on the glowing bar instead, the way he'd never once been able to before the night of the gloamhounds, and *listened*. Not with Forge Sense. With Stone. The slow heavy hum of the earth that the iron had come from, that it still remembered, deep in its grain. And under the hum, the flaw. Small and dark and hard, like a seed in a plum.

*Come out*, he told it. Not in words.

And the slag came out. It rose up through the glowing iron like a bubble rising through honey, and broke the surface, a bead of grey-black scum, and he flicked it off the anvil with the edge of his hand. The bar beneath it was clean. Perfect. Ringing.

"*How* did you *do* that?"

The girl at the next anvil was staring at him. Seventeen, round-faced and freckled, with a smith's burn scars already on her forearms and a leather apron two sizes too big for her. Tilda Crane, from Millbridge. The one who'd sat in front of a cold hearth for nine days until the fire leaned toward her.

"Stone," said Lex. "It's in the iron. It remembers where it came from. If you listen, it'll tell you where the flaws are."

"I can't hear Stone. I can only hear Flame."

"Then you'll learn Stone. Or you won't, and you'll find the flaws with Flame instead. There's more than one way to find a slag pocket." He picked up the clean bar in his tongs and turned it to the light. "Brannoc used to do it by ear. Forty years. Never once heard a slag pocket in his life, he says. But he could tell by the ring of the hammer when a bar was wrong."

From the far end of the forge, a voice like distant thunder: "*I heard that.*"

"You were meant to."

"*I could always tell*," said Brannoc, without looking up from his own anvil. "*Didn't need any fancy rock-listening to do it. I just knew.*"

"He just knew," Lex told Tilda gravely.

"*And I could still put you through that wall, lad.*"

Tilda giggled. All down the long hall, at all twenty anvils, other students were grinning.

---

The Ashbourne Forge had twenty anvils now.

It had swallowed two more houses and another barn and the whole of Farmer Tobbin's old east pasture, which his widow had sold to them for a copper penny and a promise that her grandson could learn to strike runes. There was a long hall with twenty hearths, open to the air in summer, with slate roofs and timber posts and a great brick chimney at each end. There was Hedda's reading room, which had become two reading rooms and a library and a copying-house where three Scribes from Highcrest sat all day transcribing the *Book of Making* and the Listeners' Codex into fresh copies to send to the other forges.

Because there were other forges now. Seven of them. In Millbridge and Thistledown and Saltreach and Highcrest itself, and three more in the healing Blightmarch, in villages that had been grey three years ago. Small ones, mostly. Two or three anvils. Run by smiths who'd come to Ashbourne and learned and gone home.

They called themselves runesmiths. The Lattice called them that too, sometimes. Not always. The Lattice was still learning, Seraphine said. Still catching up with what three years of Unbinding had done to it.

Lex walked the length of the long hall at midmorning, the way he did every day, stopping at each anvil. Looking at the work. Saying a word here, a word there. *Your upstroke's crooked. Your heat's too low; it'll crack. That's good. That's very good. Do it again.*

*[You're a good teacher,]* said Ember.

*I'm a terrible teacher. I just say the same five things over and over.*

*[That's what good teachers do.]* A pause. *[Your father was the same. With the Circles. He'd say "shape the channel" until people wanted to throw things at him.]*

*Did they?*

*[Elric did. Once. A teapot.]* Another pause. Shorter. *[He missed.]*

Lex didn't say anything to that. Ember didn't either.

They didn't talk about Elric much. Not in three years.

---

*Sixty-one.*

Three years ago he'd been fifty-one, kneeling on the black floor of Duskhold with his father's crystal clearing behind him and a dead knight in front of him. He'd thought, then, that he'd climb fast. That he'd be seventy by now, Tier IV, the way his father was.

He'd climbed ten levels in three years.

*[That's not slow,]* said Ember, reading the thought the way it always did. *[That's actually rather fast, for Tier Three. Most people take ten years to climb ten levels at Master.]* A pause. *[You've just been busy. Teaching doesn't give much experience. Unbinding Stones gives a little. Forging gives a little. Nothing's tried to kill you in three years.]*

*That's a good thing.*

*[It's a very good thing. I'm only saying.]*

Three years. Two hundred and sixty Stones. He'd counted. Seraphine had a ledger in Highcrest with every one of them written down, with dates and places and the number of threads unbound. Two hundred and sixty Stones, out of something over four thousand in Caldor alone. Lex and Antho, on Ashka's back, flying from one to the next, laying their hands on cold grey stone and breaking three hundred years of Ceilings.

Four thousand Stones. At this rate, it would take them forty years.

*[Longer, if you count Saltreach and the Sunreach and the Fells,]* said Ember helpfully.

*Thank you, Ember.*

*[You're welcome.]*

---

The cottage stood on the hill above the village, at the end of a lane lined with chestnut trees.

It was new. Lex and Brannoc had built it, the summer after Duskhold, on the bones of the old one. The Harrow cottage. The one that had burned eight years before, with his mother inside it, or not inside it, or somewhere else entirely. They'd cleared away the last of the old charred timbers and the blackened hearthstones and the iron pots melted into lumps. Brannoc had kept one of the pots. He'd put it on a shelf in the forge without saying anything.

The new cottage was stone, with a slate roof and a woodpile against one wall and a door painted blue. Like the old one. Lex hadn't planned that. Brannoc had brought the paint.

And in the kitchen window, facing out over the village, there was a little clay lamp.

Not Seren's lamp. That was still on its rock above the Hollowmaw, a hundred miles north, burning. This was a new one. Lex had made it the first night they slept in the new cottage, with clay from the riverbank and *Ardent* struck into its base. It had burned every night since. It would burn as long as he lived.

*A light left on for the lost.*

Wren wasn't home.

He'd known she wouldn't be. She'd been gone three weeks. The Queen's business. Wren was a Mistwalker now, Epic, level fifty-two, and Mistwalkers were rare enough that the Queen had asked her, very politely, if she'd consider serving as a royal scout. Not a soldier. Not a guard. A *pathfinder*. Someone who could go where no road went and come back and say what was there.

Wren had said yes before the Queen finished the sentence.

So she was gone a great deal. North, to the Ironbacks. East, to the edge of the Blightmarch where it bordered the Sundered Lands. South, to the Sunreach deserts. She'd come back windburned and grinning and full of stories, and sleep for two days, and then be restless by the third, standing in the cottage doorway at dusk looking at the sky.

He didn't mind. Not really. She'd always been like that. He'd met her in a tavern in Thistledown, bargaining to take a stranger up a mountain for four times what anyone else would ask.

He just missed her.

*[She'll be back soon,]* said Ember. *[She said three weeks. It's been three weeks.]*

*She said three weeks the last time too. It was five.*

*[That was the Sunreach. Things are slower in the Sunreach. Everyone says so.]*

Lex stood in the cottage doorway and looked at the little lamp burning in the window in the middle of the day. Then he turned and looked north. At the Greyspine, blue and white against the summer sky. At the highest peak. At the faint glow, even in daylight, from the great cleft in its face, where a hundred lanterns burned.

---

His father's letter came that afternoon.

Quill brought it. The raven had stayed with Lex after Duskhold. Nobody had ever been entirely sure whose raven he was now. He spent most of his time on the forge chimney, eating Mother Orla's bacon and insulting students. But when there was a letter from Highcrest, he always brought it.

He dropped it on the anvil in front of Lex and sat down beside it and looked at him expectantly.

"I don't have any bacon," said Lex.

"*Liar*," said Quill.

Lex gave him the rind from his lunch. Then he opened the letter.

It was in his father's hand. Small and neat and slanting slightly backward, as if the writer had been left-handed and fighting it. The same hand as the letter to Elric, which Lex still kept in his coat.

*Lexander—*

*I won't be able to come on the first of the month. I'm sorry. Brannoc will be furious. Tell him I owe him a drink and an argument, with interest.*

*Things are difficult in Highcrest.*

*Lord Chancellor Sorn spoke in the High Council again last week. Two hours. The usual complaints, but louder. The Unbound are taking places in the guilds. The noble houses' children can't get apprenticeships, because commoners with Rare classes are winning them. The Royal Observatory has more farmers' daughters than lords' sons. The Ceiling, he says, was a kindness. It kept order. It kept everyone in their place. And now no one knows their place, and the realm is falling into chaos.*

*It isn't. But that doesn't matter. What matters is that half the noble houses agreed with him, loudly, and the other half agreed with him quietly. And three of them have withdrawn their knights from the Royal Guard.*

*The Queen is holding. She's very good. But she's very young, and very alone, and there are a great many people in Highcrest who would be happier with a different Queen.*

*I'm staying here. She needs me.*

*And Lexander. There's something else.*

*The Royal Archivist has been tracking disappearances. Or what she thinks are disappearances. She's found gaps in the Lattice records. Places where a thread should be and isn't. Not dead. Not ended. Just—gone. As if it were never written. Nine villages in the north this summer. Perhaps more. Nobody in those villages remembers anyone missing. That's the strangest part. They don't remember. There's no one to report it.*

*Seraphine only found them because she reads the records every day, and she noticed the holes.*

*I don't know what it means. I have a guess. I'm afraid of my guess.*

*Be careful. Watch the Stones. If anything feels wrong, write to me at once.*

*—A.*

*P.S. Mind the quench.*

Lex read it twice. Then he folded it very carefully and put it in his coat, beside the others.

*[He has a guess,]* said Ember quietly. *[And he's afraid of it.]*

*He's always afraid of his guesses. And he never tells anyone what they are.*

*[Lex—]*

*Three years*, Lex thought. *Three years since Duskhold. Three years since he said "I need to be sure." About Mam. Three years since "A Key has been turned." Three years he's been searching for something and he won't say what.* He picked up the hammer. *He asked Antho to forgive him for keeping secrets. Antho did. And he's still keeping them.*

Ember was quiet for a long moment.

*[I know,]* it said at last. *[I'm a piece of him. I know exactly how he thinks.]* A pause. *[He thinks if he tells you, and he's wrong, it'll hurt you worse than not knowing.]*

*And if he's right?*

*[Then he thinks it'll hurt you worse than that.]*

Lex brought the hammer down on the anvil. *Chang.* The iron rang clean.

---

He felt it that evening.

He was walking back up the lane to the cottage at dusk, under the chestnut trees, with the lamp glowing in the kitchen window ahead of him. The sky was pale gold over the Greyspine. The swallows were out. Somewhere down in the village, someone was playing a fiddle at the Crooked Kettle.

And he stopped.

He didn't know why. He just stopped, in the middle of the lane, with his hand on the gate.

There was something wrong.

Not here. Not in the lane, or the village, or the Vale. Somewhere else. Far away. North. He could feel it the way he felt a slag pocket in iron. A dull note under the ring. A flaw in the grain of the world.

*[Lex?]*

*Do you feel that?*

*[Feel what?]*

He closed his eyes. He reached down, the way he reached for Stone. Down past the silver, past the gold. And then *out*. Not to Stone. To the net. To the Lattice. To the great silver web spread over all of Aldermere, every thread, every knot. He'd learned to feel it, these three years. Faintly. Like the hum of a forge in another room. Unbinding Stone after Stone, he'd started to feel the whole net, the way you feel the shape of a building you've walked through many times, even in the dark.

There was a hole in it.

Not a breach. Not a tear, like Vhessa. Smaller. Neater. *Precise*. As if someone had taken a very fine knife and cut a single thread out of the weave. And then another. And another. Little holes. A line of them. Far to the north.

And at the end of the line, so faint he almost thought he'd imagined it, something *turning*. Like a key in a lock.

*[Oh,]* said Ember very softly. *[Oh. I feel it now.]*

*What is it?*

*[I don't know.]* Ember's voice was strange. *[But it feels like the panel. Three years ago. At the festival. "A Key has been turned."]* A long pause. *[It feels like that, only smaller. Like an echo.]*

Lex stood in the lane with his hand on the gate and his eyes closed, feeling the holes in the net.

Nine of them, his father had said. Nine villages.

He counted.

Eleven.

---

He didn't sleep that night.

He sat at the kitchen table in the new cottage with the little clay lamp burning in the window and a cup of tea going cold in front of him and the *Book of Making* open on the table, turning the pages without reading them.

Something was cutting threads out of the Lattice. People. Unbound people. He was almost sure of that. The holes he'd felt were in the new places, the bright places, the places where Ceilings had been broken. Like someone walking through an orchard picking only the ripest fruit.

And nobody remembered them.

He thought about the Inkwraiths in the Drowned Archive. *They eat meaning.* He thought about the Thornwood, eating memories. He thought about the Barrow-King's soldiers, nine hundred years in the black water, who had forgotten their own names because the Lattice had written them out of the world.

He thought about the bronze foil from Qel'Varran. *It wants to unmake them. To take them back to before they were. Before they had names.*

*[You think it's the Unmaker,]* said Ember quietly.

*I don't know what I think.*

*[You think it's the Unmaker. Or something to do with it.]* A pause. *[And you think your father thinks so too. And that's why he's afraid of his guess.]*

Lex didn't answer.

He sat at the table until the sky outside began to grey. Then he got up and went to the door and stood in it, looking north.

And that was when he heard the horses.

---

They came up the south road from Millbridge in the dawn. Two of them, tired, lathered, ridden hard. Lex saw them from the cottage doorway as they came over the last rise and down into the village. He saw them stop on the green beside the white Stone. He saw the riders get down.

He was already running.

He came down the lane and across the green at a run, and the taller of the two riders turned at the sound of his boots, and Lex saw a hatchet face and close-cropped iron-grey hair and pale blue eyes, and a little silver lantern pinned to the left breast of a plain grey coat.

"Commander," he said, out of breath.

"Harrow." Roole had never stopped calling him that. Three years, and a Queen's advisor for a father, and it was still *Harrow*. "I'm sorry for the hour."

"What's happened?"

Roole didn't answer. He turned and looked at the second horse.

There was a boy on it. Sitting behind Corporal Fenn, who'd ridden with Roole since the Hollowmaw and was a Keeper-Lieutenant now with a squint to match. The boy was small and thin and pale, eleven or twelve, with a shock of fair hair and enormous eyes and a blue wool shawl wrapped around his shoulders, though it was summer. He was clutching the shawl with both hands.

He was looking at Lex.

At his eyes. At the gold in them, which never quite went away anymore.

"Are you him?" said the boy. His voice was very small. "The smith? The one with the gold eyes?"

"I'm a smith," said Lex. "My eyes are gold."

The boy looked at him for a long moment.

"My sister faded," he said. "And everyone forgot her. Everyone in the whole village. My mam and my da and her friends." His voice cracked. "Everyone except me."

Lex went very still.

He looked at Roole. The Keeper-Commander was looking back at him, and his hatchet face was grey with exhaustion and something else. Something Lex had seen on it once before, on a snowy slope below the Hollowmaw, when he'd lain in the slush with a burning hammer over his head and hadn't flinched.

Fear.

"His name's Corin Bell," said Roole quietly. "He's from Harrowgate. In the far north. Under the Ironbacks." He paused. "His sister was Unbound in the spring. By you. At the Harrowgate Stone. She was a Weatherwise. Rare."

Lex remembered. A grey Stone in a grey square, at the end of a long cold flight north on Ashka's back. A crowd of villagers watching. A girl of sixteen in a blue shawl, laughing, laughing, at the front of the crowd, when her panel changed.

"*Mella*," he said.

The boy's head came up so fast his fair hair flew.

"*You remember her*," he whispered. "*You remember. Nobody remembers. Nobody, not anyone, not even Mam—*"

"I remember," said Lex. "She laughed. When her class changed. She laughed so hard she had to sit down on the Stone."

Corin Bell stared at him.

Then he slid off the back of Fenn's horse, and walked across the green, and stopped in front of Lex, and put his arms around his waist, and wept.

---

*[Lex,]* said Ember, very quietly, while the boy cried against him in the dawn. *[You remembered her. You. Not anyone else. The boy and you.]*

*I know.*

*[The boy because she was his sister. Blood. And you—]* Ember stopped. *[You because you Unbound her. Because your hands were on her thread. Your Root touched it.]*

Lex looked over the boy's fair head at Roole.

"How many?" he said quietly. "Villages."

"Nine that we've found," said Roole. "Seraphine found the holes in the records. We went looking. Nine villages in the north, since spring." His jaw tightened. "We only found the boy in the ninth. He's the only one anywhere who remembers."

"It's eleven," said Lex.

Roole's pale eyes narrowed.

"I felt them," said Lex. "Last night. In the net. Holes. A line of them. Going north." He swallowed. "Eleven."

Roole looked at him for a long moment.

"There's something else," he said. He reached into his coat and drew out a folded paper. Old. Creased. A map. He unfolded it and held it out. It was drawn in the Spire's precise style, faded brown ink on yellowed paper, and in the margin, beside a cluster of notes in a neat cramped hand Lex didn't recognize, there was a drawing.

A keyhole.

"I found this in Isaura Kell's private office," said Roole. "After she fled. I took it with every other paper she left behind. I've had it three years. I never knew what it meant." He tapped the drawing. "There was a scar in the air above the place where the boy's sister faded. A thin black line. Healed over." His voice was very quiet. "This shape."

Lex looked at the keyhole drawn in the margin of the High Warden's map.

And beside it, in Kell's neat cramped hand, two words.

*The Key.*

*[Lex,]* said Ember. And its voice had gone very cold. Very quiet. Very sure. *[Lex, I know whose hand that is. Under Kell's notes. The fainter writing, in the corner. Do you see it?]*

He saw it. Very small. Very faint. Almost rubbed away. A second hand, under the first. Spiky. Rapid.

He'd seen that hand before. On a slip of paper tied to a raven's leg, on a cold morning outside the Hollowmaw.

*If you are reading this, you are alive, and so presumably is your brother, and I am an old man who gambles well.*

He looked up at Roole.

"Elric," he said.
