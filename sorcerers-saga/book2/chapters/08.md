# The Unlit

"*You can't go through Fallowmere*," said Kestrel.

They were standing on a ridge in the southern foothills of the Ironbacks, eleven days out of the White Shore, looking down at a market town in a green valley. Smoke from a hundred chimneys. A church spire. A Lattice Stone in the square, glowing faint silver in the afternoon light. Lex could feel it from here. Its threads. Its people. Six thousand souls, give or take, and nine hundred and forty of them Unbound, their threads running bright and loose and free.

He'd Unbound that Stone himself. Two summers ago. He remembered the miller's daughter who'd cried, and the old soldier who'd sat down in the square and laughed until he couldn't breathe.

"Why not?"

"*Because you're you*." Kestrel looked at him. "*You're Lex Vey. The Forgeheart. The man who broke the Ceiling. There's a song about you, did you know that? Every alehouse in the north. 'The Smith of Ashbourne.' It's terrible. It rhymes 'anvil' with 'grand will.'*" Her lip curled. "*You walk into Fallowmere, they'll ring the church bell. There'll be a feast. Children'll want you to touch their heads.*"

"I'll keep my hood up."

"*You've got gold eyes and you're the size of a door.*" She folded her arms. "*And that's not even the real reason.*"

He waited.

"*He's following you*," said Kestrel quietly. "*The old man. You know he is. He said it himself. You'll bring me the bit.*" She nodded down at the town. "*Every place you stop, he can find. And every place he finds, he takes someone. You walk into Fallowmere and have your feast, and next week some girl in that town is going to wake up and nobody will remember her name.*"

Lex looked down at the town.

*[She's right,]* said Ember quietly. *[I hate it. But she's right.]*

He'd known it. He'd known it since the White Shore. Since he'd opened the Ledger and found a forty-eighth name at the bottom of the page. *Bram Sowerby, of the White Shore. He named every one of his lambs after a star.* A shepherd. Last night. A mile from the temple. While Lex was walking north toward it.

Elric had been *close*. Following. Taking, as he went.

"Then where?"

"*Round*," said Kestrel. "*West, through the Bitter Hills. It's longer. It's wild. Nobody lives there.*" She hesitated. "*Nearly nobody.*"

Lex looked at her. She didn't look back. She was staring down at Fallowmere with her jaw set, and her fire was very low in her hair, barely a glow.

"Who's nearly nobody?"

Kestrel was quiet for a long moment.

"*Friends*," she said at last. "*Of a sort. My grandmother's sort.*" She glanced at him sideways. "*They won't like you.*"

"Why not?"

"*Because you're the net*," said Kestrel simply. "*And they're the ones who fell through it.*"

---

The Bitter Hills were well named.

They rose up south and west of the Ironbacks in a long, broken, ugly sprawl of brown scrub and grey stone and old, old mine-workings. Nothing grew there but gorse and thorn and a kind of tough wiry grass that cut your ankles. The streams ran orange with rust. The wind smelled of slag.

*[Iron country,]* said Ember. *[Once. A long time ago. Before the Lattice. They mined it out and left.]*

There were no Stones here. Lex felt it the moment they crossed into the hills: the net going thin and quiet around him, the silver threads of the world spreading out and out until they were so far apart he could barely feel them. Like walking out of a crowded hall into an empty field at night.

It should have made him uneasy. It didn't. It was restful, in a way he hadn't expected. For the first time in years, there weren't six thousand people humming at the edge of his mind.

Tamsin walked beside them. Fainter, now that Kestrel's fire was banked. Lex could see the gorse through his chest. But his face was clearer than it had been on the White Shore. He had a name. He held onto it the way a drowning man holds a rope, and every hour or so Lex would hear him whisper it to himself, very softly. *Tamsin. Tamsin Varn. Tam*.

---

They walked three days into the hills before anyone found them.

Lex felt it first. On the third evening, as they were coming down a long scree slope into a narrow valley full of thorn. A prickle at the back of his neck. A sense of being *looked at*.

He stopped.

"*What?*" said Kestrel.

"Someone's watching."

She looked around. At the empty slope. The thorn. The brown hills going purple in the dusk. "*I don't see anyone.*"

He didn't either. That was the thing. He looked at the valley with his ordinary eyes and saw nothing. Thorn and rock and an orange stream.

He started to reach for True Sight.

And stopped.

He didn't know why. It was a feeling. Like a hand on his wrist. *Don't*. The same way he sometimes knew a weld was going to fail before he struck it.

He looked with his ordinary eyes again. Slowly. The way Wren had taught him to look in the forest, years ago, at the very start, on the road to the Greyspine. *Don't look for the deer. Look for where the forest is wrong.*

There. Twenty paces down the slope. A thornbush. Exactly like every other thornbush in the valley. Except that the evening wind was blowing hard from the west, bending every thornbush in the valley eastward.

And this one wasn't moving.

"That one," said Lex, and pointed.

There was a pause.

Then the thornbush said, in a young woman's voice, deeply annoyed: "*Oh, for—how?*"

And a second thornbush, three paces to its left, which Lex hadn't noticed at all, said in an almost identical voice, also annoyed: "*I* told *you the wind was wrong.*"

"*You didn't tell me anything.*"

"*I* thought *it very loudly.*"

The two thornbushes rippled. Like a reflection in a pond when you drop a stone. And were gone. And in their place stood two people.

Twins. Lex could see that at once. Small and slight and sharp-faced, perhaps twenty-four, with identical untidy mops of mouse-brown hair and identical narrow grey eyes and identical expressions of profound irritation. One was a young woman in a patched green coat. One was a young man in a patched brown one. They each had a long knife in their belt and a sling in their hand, and they were looking at Lex the way a cat looks at a dog that has unexpectedly learned to open doors.

"*Kestrel*," said the young woman, without looking away from Lex. "*What have you brought us.*"

"*A smith*," said Kestrel.

"*Is that—*" The young man was staring at Lex's face. At his eyes. "*Is that* the *smith? The song one? Anvil and grand will?*"

"*Yes.*"

"*Oh, Kes*," said the young woman. "*Oh, Kes, no. Dorran's going to have a fit.*"

---

Their names were Ilse and Ivo Marrow. They were from Kessick, a mill town on the Silverrun, and they were thieves. They said so cheerfully, within the first five minutes, as they led Lex and Kestrel down the thorn-valley in the dusk.

"*Illusionists*," said Ivo. "*Technically. But mostly thieves.*"

"*Mostly thieves*," agreed Ilse. "*Illusion's only really good for two things. Stealing and not getting caught stealing.*"

"*And parties.*"

"*And parties*," Ilse allowed. "*But there aren't many parties in the Bitter Hills.*"

They were Unbound. Lex could feel it, faintly, even without True Sight. Two bright loose threads, running out from them into the thin net. But there was something strange about the threads. Something blurred.

"How did you hide?" he said. "From me. I couldn't see you at all."

Ilse glanced at him. A quick, sharp, assessing look.

"*We didn't hide from you*," she said. "*We hid from the light.*"

"*Lampshade*," said Ivo. "*That's what we call it. It's an illusion. But not on your eyes. On the—*" He waved a hand vaguely at the air. "*The silver. The net. We make our threads look like nothing. Like the net's just empty where we are.*"

Lex stopped walking.

*[Lex,]* said Ember sharply.

"You can hide your threads," he said. "From the Lattice."

"*Well, not from the Lattice exactly. The Lattice knows we're there. We can't fool the Stones.*" Ivo shrugged. "*But from anyone* looking *at it. Anyone reading it. You, for instance. Wardens. Anyone with sight.*" He paused. "*That old man with the key.*"

"*That's why we're alive*," said Ilse simply.

---

The Unlit lived under a hill.

Not in caves. In a mine. An old iron mine, abandoned centuries ago, its shafts and galleries running deep into the heart of the hill. The entrance was a black hole in a cliff-face, half hidden by thorn and gorse, and from inside it came a smell Lex hadn't expected.

Beeswax.

They went in, and down, and the passage widened into a gallery, and Lex stopped.

Candles.

Hundreds of them. Thousands. On every ledge, in every niche, on every jut and outcrop of the old rust-red rock. Fat beeswax candles and thin tallow ones, candles in jars, candles in tin cups, candles stuck in their own wax on the bare stone. The whole gallery glowed with soft, warm, flickering yellow light. Not silver. Not gold. Not Lattice-light, or Root-light, or the steady glow of the lanterns in the Hollowmaw.

Just candles. Ordinary fire.

"*The Wickhollow*," said Kestrel quietly. "*Welcome to the Unlit.*"

There were people. Not many. Forty, maybe fifty. Sitting on benches carved into the rock, or working at tables, or cooking at a long stone hearth at the far end of the gallery. Men and women and a few children. Old and young. Some with the bright loose threads of the Unbound. Some with the dull capped threads of people who'd never been freed.

And some with no threads at all.

Like Kestrel. Like Malachi. Nothing there. The Lattice looking at them and seeing empty air.

They all stopped what they were doing and looked at him.

*[Unlatticed,]* said Ember. *[A dozen of them, at least. Lex, I didn't think there were this many in the whole of Caldor.]*

Lex stood in the doorway of the gallery with forty people staring at him and felt, for the first time in a long time, entirely out of place.

---

"*No*," said Dorran Vask.

He was the biggest man Lex had ever seen. Bigger even than Brannoc.

Lex was big. He'd been big since he was fourteen. Brannoc used to say he'd been built for the forge the way some men are built for the sea. But Dorran Vask was half a head taller and half again as broad. He was perhaps fifty, with a great slab of a face, a nose that had been broken at least three times, a shaved head and a short grey beard. He wore an old leather coat and a smith's apron, of all things, and his hands were the size of shovels.

And on his right forearm, burned deep into the skin, faded and scarred and very old, there was a mark Lex knew.

The Order's lattice-sigil. The brand they put on Wardens of the Kindling. The ones who stood beside the Stones at every village Kindling-day and pressed the Ceiling down on every child who came of age.

"*No*," said Dorran Vask again. He was standing at the long stone hearth with a ladle in his hand, staring at Lex across the gallery. His voice was a deep slow rumble, like rocks shifting in a stream. "*Kestrel. What have you done?*"

"*He's not with the Order, Dorran.*"

"*He's worse than the Order. He's the* Root. *He's the man with the net in his hands.*" Dorran set the ladle down. Very carefully. As if he were afraid he'd break it. "*You've brought the Lattice into the Wickhollow. Into the one place in Caldor where it can't see us.*"

"*He's hunting the key*," said Kestrel. "*The old man's key. The one that's taking people.*"

"*And the old man is hunting* him." Dorran looked at Lex. Steady, heavy, unblinking. "*Isn't he.*"

"Yes," said Lex.

"*So you've brought him here too.*"

"I don't know," said Lex honestly. "I hope not. I don't know how he follows me. I don't know if he does." He met the big man's eyes. "If he does, I'll go. Tonight. I won't put your people in danger."

Dorran Vask looked at him for a long moment.

"*You already have*," he said quietly. "*By being born.*"

---

They gave him food anyway. A bowl of thick barley stew from the pot on the hearth, and a heel of dark bread, and a seat at the end of a bench as far from everyone else as the gallery allowed. Kestrel sat beside him. Tamsin stood behind her. Nobody else came near.

Lex ate and watched them. The Unlit. Watching him back.

*[They're frightened of you,]* said Ember quietly.

*I know.*

*[Not of you. Of what you are. Of the gold in your eyes.]* A pause. *[Most of these people have spent their lives running from the Lattice. Hiding from Wardens. Hiding from Kindling-day. And you're the thing at the very center of it.]*

*I broke the Ceiling. I Unbound forty thousand people.*

*[And the people who weren't* in *it? The ones who were never Kindled at all? The ones who ran?]* Ember was silent for a moment. *[You didn't do anything for them, Lex. You couldn't. They weren't in the net to free.]*

He looked at a little girl, perhaps eight, sitting on her mother's lap at the far end of the gallery. No thread. Nothing. She was staring at him with enormous solemn eyes over the edge of her bowl.

*I didn't even know they existed*, he thought.

*[No,]* said Ember. *[Nobody did. That was the point.]*

---

Oriel Sant found him after supper.

He didn't hear her come. She was simply there, sitting on the bench beside him, where Kestrel had been a moment before. Kestrel had gone to argue with Dorran at the hearth. Their voices rose and fell in the candlelight.

Oriel Sant was very old. Older than Mag the herbwoman had been, older than anyone Lex had ever met except Malachi. She was tiny and bent and brown, wrapped in a shawl of faded red wool, and her eyes were white. Not clouded. White. Pure white, like the inside of a shell, without iris or pupil.

She had no thread.

"*Smith*," she said. Her voice was thin and dry and very precise.

"Mother," said Lex. It was what you called old women in Ashbourne. It seemed right.

She smiled. It made her whole face crease like crumpled paper. "*Mannerly. Good. Dorran thinks you'll have us all killed. Kestrel thinks you'll save her brother. The twins think you're the most interesting thing to happen in the Bitter Hills since the goat fell down the north shaft.*" She tilted her head. "*I think you have someone looking out of your eyes.*"

Lex went still.

"What?"

"*I'm blind*," said Oriel Sant. "*Born blind. Never been Kindled. My mother hid me from the Wardens, because a blind girl at Kindling-day was a girl who'd be capped at nothing. Useless. Better off dead, the Wardens used to say.*" Her white eyes were turned toward him. "*So I never had the Lattice. And I never had sight. But I've always been able to* see. *Not with eyes. Not with silver. I see what's there.*"

"What do you see?"

"*A young man*," she said. "*Big. Tired. Very sad, and pretending not to be. With a little gold fire in him that talks.*" She smiled again. "*Hello, little fire.*"

*[She can see me,]* said Ember, sounding stunned. *[Lex, she can see* me.*]*

"*And behind the fire*," said Oriel Sant, "*far back, in the deep part of the gold, where you can't see it yourself—*" Her smile went away. "*An eye.*"

Lex didn't move.

"*It isn't always open*," said Oriel. "*It isn't always looking. Most of the time, it's asleep. But when you use the gold—when you reach into that deep place and pull the power up—it opens. Just a crack.*" Her thin dry hand found his wrist. Her fingers were cold. "*And it sees what you see.*"

---

*[No,]* said Ember.

It said it very fast. Very sharp. The way it had said *no* in the black hall at Duskhold when Elric had raised his staff.

*[No. No, that's not—Lex, I'm* in *the gold. I live there. I'd know. I'd* know *if there was something else in there. I've been in the Root with you every day for four years and there's nothing, there's nobody, it's just us—]*

"*The little fire doesn't believe me*," said Oriel Sant gently. "*That's all right. The little fire was made by someone very clever, out of a piece of someone very good. It sees what it was made to see.*" She patted Lex's wrist. "*It wasn't made to see that far down.*"

"Whose eye is it?"

Oriel was quiet.

"*Old*," she said at last. "*Very old. Older than anything I've ever seen. And very kind.*" She tilted her head. "*Kind the way a cat's kind to a mouse it hasn't decided to eat yet.*"

Lex sat very still on the bench, in the warm candlelight, with the old woman's cold fingers on his wrist.

*My blood. The smith. Will find it for you.*

*The voice says so, and the voice has never once been wrong.*

He thought about the moor, and the Umbral Wardens, and the way he'd used True Sight to find their flaws. The vault at Ysmir, and the blood on the Ledger. The night he'd felt eleven holes in the net, from his forge at Ashbourne, reaching out with the Root as far as it would go.

Every time he used the gold.

Every time.

*He's been watching through me*, Lex thought. *Aurelian. He's been watching through my eyes. And telling Elric what he sees.*

*[Lex—]*

*The bit's in the glass. He knew that already. But he doesn't know where in the glass. A hundred miles of desert, Kestrel said.* His hands had gone cold. *He doesn't need to know. He just needs me to go and find it. And look at it. With the gold.*

*[Then don't. Don't use it. Not until we know.]*

*How am I supposed to find a Founders' artifact in a hundred miles of glass without Root sight?*

Ember didn't answer.

---

"*There are ways*," said Oriel Sant.

Lex looked at her. He hadn't said anything out loud. But she was smiling faintly, her white eyes turned toward him, as if she'd heard every word.

"*There's always ways*," she said. "*That's what the Unlit are for, smith. We've spent our whole lives finding ways to live without the gold and the silver.*" She patted his wrist again. "*You've spent your whole life inside it. You've never had to learn.*"

"Will you teach me?"

The old woman laughed. A thin dry crackle, like dead leaves.

"*Me? No. I'm too old, and I'm blind, and I've seen how this ends.*" She turned her white face toward the hearth, where Kestrel and Dorran were still arguing. "*But they might. If you ask them properly.*" A pause. "*Dorran most of all. Though he won't want to. Ask him about his list.*"

"What list?"

But Oriel Sant had already got up, slowly, leaning on a stick of black thorn, and was shuffling away down the gallery into the candlelight.

---

He found Dorran Vask the next morning at the far end of the mine, in a side gallery Lex hadn't seen before.

It was a forge.

Not much of one. A stone hearth built against the rock, with a chimney cut up through the hill to the surface. A bellows of old patched leather. An anvil, battered and black and very old, with a horn worn smooth by a hundred years of use. A rack of tools on the wall.

Dorran was at the anvil, working a bar of iron. A plowshare. Lex could tell from the shape. He was doing it badly. Not badly enough that anyone else would notice. But Lex noticed. The angle of the blows was wrong. Too heavy. Too much shoulder, not enough wrist.

He stood in the doorway and watched for a while. Then he said, before he could stop himself: "You're hitting it too hard."

Dorran's hammer stopped mid-swing.

He turned his great slab of a head and looked at Lex in the doorway. His face didn't change.

"*I've been hitting iron for thirty years.*"

"You've been hitting it too hard for thirty years," said Lex. "It's a plowshare, not a Warden's mace. You're trying to make it do what you want. You've got to let it do what it wants, and help."

There was a very long silence.

Then Dorran Vask held out the hammer.

Lex came and took it. He looked at the bar of iron on the anvil, cooling. He pumped the bellows three times, slow. Laid the bar back in the coals. Waited. Took it out when it was the color of a summer sunset. Laid it on the anvil.

And struck.

Light. Fast. All wrist. Letting the hammer bounce. *Tink-tink-tink*, the way Brannoc had taught him at twelve, in the Ashbourne forge, before he'd ever heard of the Lattice or the Root or a dead man's hammer. Ordinary smithing. No gold. No Flame. Just iron and fire and the ring of a hammer on an anvil.

The bar bent. Easily. Gratefully, almost. Curving into the shape it had wanted all along.

Dorran watched him. He didn't say anything.

When Lex had finished, and quenched it, and laid it on the bench, the big man picked it up. He turned it over in his huge hands. He ran his thumb along the edge.

"*Huh*," he said.

That was all.

But he didn't ask for the hammer back. And when Lex picked up the next bar from the pile and laid it in the coals, Dorran went and stood at the bellows without being asked, and pumped them, slow and steady, the way a striker does for a smith.

They worked all morning like that. Not talking. The bellows and the hammer and the ring of the iron. And by noon, there were six plowshares on the bench, and Lex's arms ached the good way, and the side gallery smelled of hot iron and coal-smoke, and for a few hours he'd forgotten about keys, and eyes, and everything.

---

"*Oriel said to ask you about your list*," said Lex, when they stopped to eat.

They were sitting on the floor of the forge with their backs against the warm stone of the hearth. Dorran had brought bread and cheese and a jug of small beer. He'd been quiet all morning. He was quieter now.

For a long time, he didn't say anything at all.

Then he reached into his old leather coat. And took out a book.

Small. Thick. Bound in cheap brown leather gone black with handling. He held it in his great hands for a moment. Then he gave it to Lex.

Lex opened it.

Names.

Pages of them. Hundreds of pages. Written in a big clumsy careful hand, in faded brown ink. Names, and villages, and dates, and a number beside each one.

*Aldo Cray. Fen Hollow. Spring Kindling. Ceiling 18.*

*Bess Rook. Fen Hollow. Spring Kindling. Ceiling 12.*

*Wat Lister. Fen Hollow. Spring Kindling. Ceiling 15.*

On and on. Page after page. Village after village.

"*Kindling-day*," said Dorran Vask. Very quietly. "*Twenty-two years. I was a Warden of the Kindling for twenty-two years. Every spring and every autumn, I went round my circuit. Twelve villages. And every child who'd turned fourteen came up to the Stone, one by one, and put their hand on it.*" He was looking at the cold hearth. "*And I stood beside the Stone, and put my hand on their shoulder, and set their Ceiling.*"

Lex didn't say anything.

"*That's what the brand's for*." Dorran held up his forearm. The faded sigil. "*Gives you the Sanction to set them. You put your hand on the child, and you feel their thread, and you push down. Like closing a lid.*" His great hand closed, slowly, into a fist. "*Some of them cried. Some of them didn't know what was happening. Most of them just looked up at me. Trusting. Because I was the Warden, and that's what Wardens were for.*"

He was silent for a long time.

"*Four thousand, one hundred and nine*," he said. "*I wrote down every one. I don't know why. I started the first year. I just—wanted to remember them.*" He shook his head slowly. "*I didn't know, then. What it was. What I was doing. They told us it was for the children's protection. That an uncapped thread was dangerous. That the Ceiling kept them safe.*"

"When did you find out?"

"*The year the Marrow twins came up.*" Dorran almost smiled. Almost. "*Kessick. Ten years ago this spring. Two little mouse-haired things, holding hands. They put their hands on the Stone together, and I put my hand on their shoulders, and I felt their threads.*" He stopped. "*They were so bright. Both of them. Brighter than anything I'd ever felt. Like two stars.*" His voice dropped very low. "*And I pushed down. And I felt them go dim. And the girl looked up at me. Ilse. And she said, very quietly, so no one else could hear: Why did you put the light out?*"

The forge was very quiet.

"*I put my brand in the fire that night*," said Dorran Vask. "*Tried to burn it off. Didn't work. Sanction's in the thread, not the skin.*" He looked at his scarred forearm. "*Then I walked into the Bitter Hills, and I didn't come out.*"

"Ilse and Ivo—"

"*They found me. Five years later. They'd been running since they were sixteen. Thieving. Hiding.*" Dorran shrugged his enormous shoulders. "*They recognized me. I thought they were going to kill me.*" He paused. "*Ivo wanted to. Ilse wouldn't let him.*"

"Why not?"

"*She said I'd only been doing my job.*" Dorran's great face twisted. "*And then she said the Unlit needed someone big to lift things. And I've been lifting things for them ever since.*"

Lex looked down at the book in his hands. Four thousand one hundred and nine names.

"They're Unbound," he said. "The Marrows. They're Unbound now."

"*Yes. Your Unbinding. Two summers past. They went down to the Kessick Stone in disguise, the day you came through, and stood in the crowd.*" Dorran looked at him. "*Ilse came back and found me in this forge and didn't say anything. Just stood there, glowing. And then she hit me.*" He rubbed his jaw. "*Very hard. And then she cried. And then she went and stole a pie.*"

Lex laughed. He couldn't help it.

And after a moment, Dorran Vask laughed too. A deep slow rumble, like rocks shifting in a stream.

---

"*How many?*" said Dorran, when they'd stopped. "*Of my list. How many have you Unbound?*"

Lex looked at the book.

"I don't know," he said. "I'd have to check. Against the Stones." He turned a page. "Fen Hollow. I haven't been to Fen Hollow. Or—" He turned another. "Brackwater. Or Thornby."

"*Most of my circuit's in the west. Out past the Blightmarch. Small places. The Order never cared much about them.*"

"Neither did I," said Lex quietly. "I went where the big Stones were. Where the most people were. Highcrest. Kessick. The cities." He closed the book. "There are four thousand Stones in Caldor. I've done two hundred and sixty."

"*That's a lot.*"

"It's not enough."

He held out the book to Dorran. The big man looked at it. Didn't take it.

"When this is over," said Lex. "When the key's dealt with. I'll go west. To your circuit. All twelve villages." He met Dorran's eyes. "And you'll come with me. And stand beside the Stone. And put your hand on their shoulders."

Dorran Vask stared at him.

"*Why?*"

"Because you closed the lids," said Lex. "You should be there when they open."

Dorran didn't say anything for a very long time.

Then he took the book. And held it in both his great hands. And bent his shaved head over it, and his shoulders shook, once.

Lex looked away, at the cold forge, and gave him that.

---

"*Teach him*," said Dorran that night.

He was standing at the long stone hearth in the main gallery, with the whole of the Unlit gathered around on the benches in the candlelight. Kestrel. Tamsin, faint behind her. The Marrow twins, perched together on a ledge with their legs dangling. Oriel Sant in her red shawl. Forty-odd others. All looking at him. Then at Lex.

"*Teach him the Lampshade*," said Dorran. "*And whatever else he needs. To find the old man's key without the gold.*"

There was a murmur. Uneasy.

"*Dorran*," said Ivo. "*Last night you said he'd get us all killed.*"

"*He might.*" Dorran didn't look away from Lex. "*He's the Root. He's dangerous just by being. Everyone near him is in danger.*" He paused. "*But I spent twenty-two years putting out lights. He's spent four years lighting them.*" He folded his enormous arms. "*I've decided I know which of us I'd rather follow.*"

Ilse Marrow looked at her brother. Her brother looked at her.

Something passed between them, the way it did between twins. A whole conversation in a glance.

"*Fine*," said Ilse. "*We'll teach him.*" She pointed at Lex. "*But he's terrible at it. He walked straight past Ivo yesterday.*"

"*He did not*," said Ivo. "*He saw me. He saw* both *of us.*"

"*He saw* me. *He didn't see you until you* moved. *You twitched. You always twitch.*"

"*I sneezed.*"

"*Same thing*."

Oriel Sant laughed, a thin dry crackle, at the end of the bench. And somewhere in the candlelight, the little girl with no thread, the one who'd stared at Lex over her bowl the night before, came and stood in front of him. And looked up at him with her enormous solemn eyes.

"*Are you the anvil man?*" she said.

"I'm a smith," said Lex.

"*Can you make me a candle-holder? A proper one? Mine's broken.*"

Lex looked at her. Small and solemn and threadless. The Lattice had never once looked at her and seen anything at all.

"Yes," he said. "Tomorrow. I'll make you the best candle-holder in the Bitter Hills."

She nodded, gravely, as if this were a serious bargain. Then she climbed up onto the bench beside him and sat there, swinging her legs, and leaned her head against his arm.

And that was how the Unlit decided.

---

The Lampshade took him eleven days.

It wasn't magic. Not the way he knew magic. There was no Well in it. No Flame, no Stone, no Root. It was a trick of the mind. A way of thinking about yourself. Ilse explained it on the first morning, sitting cross-legged on the floor of the forge gallery with Ivo beside her.

"*Your thread's a light*," she said. "*Right? Everyone's is. Silver, gold, whatever. It shines. And anyone with sight can look at the net and see it shining.*"

"Yes."

"*So you don't put it out*." Ilse held up her hand. "*You can't put it out. You'd die. You put a shade on it.*" She cupped her other hand over the first. "*Like a lampshade. The light's still there. Still burning. You're just—making it look like the room's dark.*"

"How?"

"*You believe it*," said Ivo. "*That's all. That's the whole trick. You sit very still, and you think about being nothing. Being empty. Being the dark bit of the room where nobody's sitting.*" He shrugged. "*And if you believe it hard enough, the net believes it too.*"

"*Illusion's just lying very convincingly*," said Ilse. "*To other people. Or to the world.*"

"*Or to yourself*," said Ivo.

Lex was terrible at it.

He'd never been good at lying. Brannoc used to say he had a face like an open door. And this was harder than lying. This was lying to the deepest part of himself: the part that was gold, and burning, and *there*, the Root-heir of the Lattice, with Ember humming in him like a hearth.

*Be nothing*, he told himself. *Be empty. Be the dark bit of the room*.

*[You're thinking about being nothing so loudly,]* said Ember, *[that I can hear it from in here.]*

The twins laughed at him. Kestrel laughed at him. Dorran didn't laugh, but his great slab of a face did something at the corners that might have been the beginnings of it.

On the fourth day, he managed it for a heartbeat. On the sixth, for a count of ten. On the ninth, Ilse sat across from him with her eyes narrowed, reading the net, and said, "*Oh. Oh, there you go. There's nobody there.*"

And on the eleventh day, he walked from one end of the Wickhollow to the other with his thread shaded, and Oriel Sant, sitting on her bench in her red shawl, turned her white eyes toward him as he passed and said, "*Smith? Is that you? I can't see your eye anymore.*"

He wrote letters on the last night. He'd promised. *I'll write*. And he hadn't. Not for six weeks.

The Unlit had a way of sending them. They didn't say what it was. A tinker, maybe, or a carter, or a girl with a basket who walked out of the Bitter Hills to Fallowmere market every tenth day. It didn't matter. The letters would go.

He wrote to his father. Short. *I'm alive. I'm going south. Elric has the bow. The bit is in the Sunreach and the blade is under the Hollowmaw. Guard Antho. Guard the door. Something is watching through the Root, Father. Through my eyes. Did you know? Tell me if you knew.*

He wrote to Antho. Longer. Then shorter again. Then he tore it up, and wrote: *I'm sorry I left without you. You were right to be angry. The blade is under your floor. Don't let him have it. I love you. Go and skip stones on your lake and wait for me. I'll come and beat you. —L.*

He wrote to Wren last.

He sat with the pen in his hand for a long time. The candles burned down around him in the forge gallery.

*Wren—*

*I'm an idiot. You said so in your note and you were right. I left because I was afraid for you, and that was a stupid reason, because you're the bravest person I know and you can look after yourself better than I can.*

*I've found people. They're good. You'd like them. There's a fire-witch who's even angrier than you are, and two thieves who could steal the boots off your feet, and a very large man who can't hit a plowshare properly.*

*I miss you. I miss you every day. I think about the festival lanterns, and your hair in the window seat, and I think about how stupid I was not to say it back properly when you said it.*

*I love you. I'm saying it now. Properly.*

*Stay safe. Stay with Father, if that's where you are. Something's wrong with him, I think, and he'd never say so.*

*—Hammer*

He folded it. He sealed it. He sat looking at it for a long time.

---

They left the Wickhollow on the twelfth morning.

Not just Lex and Kestrel. Dorran Vask came, with his great hammer on his shoulder and his book of names in his coat. Ilse and Ivo came, bickering, with their slings and their long knives and a sack of stolen provisions they refused to account for. And Oriel Sant came, which nobody had expected, riding on a mule with her stick across her knees and her white eyes turned south.

"*You'll need me*," she said, when Dorran tried to argue. "*Somebody has to see what's there. And the smith can't look.*"

The little threadless girl stood at the mouth of the mine with her mother and waved. She was holding a candle-holder. Iron, with a twist in the stem like a vine, and a little drip-dish shaped like a leaf, and a ring to carry it by. Lex had made it the morning after he'd promised. It was the best candle-holder in the Bitter Hills.

He waved back.

They went south. Down out of the Bitter Hills, across the broad brown plains at the southern edge of Caldor, toward a long line of red mountains on the horizon that the maps called the Scald. Beyond them, the Sunreach. Beyond that, the glass.

Lex walked with his thread shaded and his eyes ordinary brown-gold, and didn't reach for the Root once. It was like walking with one hand tied behind his back. Like being half-blind. He didn't know how far away anything was. He didn't know who was near. He couldn't feel the net humming at the edge of his mind.

But behind him, somewhere, he knew Elric was following. And now Elric couldn't see him.

*Let's see how he likes walking blind*, Lex thought.

---

On the ninth day, they came up out of the foothills of the Scald onto a high red pass, and looked down into the Sunreach.

Lex stopped.

It was gold. All of it. A vast plain of gold and red and ochre, stretching away south under a sky so blue and wide and hot that it hurt to look at. Mesas. Canyons. Rivers that ran in deep green ribbons through red rock. And far, far away to the south, on the very edge of sight, a line of light. Shimmering. Blinding. Like the sun on the sea.

"*The glass*," said Kestrel softly. "*There it is.*"

And between them and it, coming up the long red slope toward the pass in a great cloud of red dust, riders.

Fifty of them. More. On tall lean golden horses, with long spears and bronze shields and white cloaks streaming out behind them. Coming fast. Straight for the pass.

At their head rode a woman. Tall in the saddle. Dark-skinned, with a great crown of black braids bound up in gold wire, and a curved sword at her hip, and a face like a hawk's.

The riders drew up in a long line across the slope below them. Fifty spears lowered. The dust drifted.

And the woman at the head of them looked up at the pass, at the small ragged band of the Unlit standing on it, and at the big young man in the middle with ordinary brown-gold eyes. And raised her voice.

"*Lex Vey*," she called. In good Caldorean, with an accent like warm honey. "*Smith of Ashbourne. Root-heir of the Lattice. I am Ishara, Queen of the Sunreach.*"

She smiled. It wasn't a friendly smile.

"*You have been hiding from my eyes for nine days. Nobody hides from my eyes.*" She lifted one hand. Fifty spears lifted with it. "*Come down. We need to talk about what you have brought into my country.*"
