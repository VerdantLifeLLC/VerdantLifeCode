# Mother Sallow

The Vale smelled of autumn in the middle of spring.

Lex noticed it first on the road south from the Hollowmaw, two days out, when they crested the last ridge of the Greyspine foothills and the green bowl of the Ashbourne Vale opened below them. It was his home. He'd been born at the far end of it, in a cottage at Harrow. He'd grown up at the near end, in a forge in Ashbourne. He knew every smell it had. Woodsmoke and hay and the river and Mother Orla's bacon.

This was wrong.

Sweet. Heavy. Like apples left too long in a barrel. Like leaves going soft on a forest floor. Like a cellar where something has been forgotten.

"*Rot*," said Kestrel. Beside him on her borrowed horse. Her nose wrinkling. "*That's rot.*"

Lex looked down the Vale. At the river, winding silver through the green. At the villages strung along it like beads. At Ashbourne, tiny, at the near end, with the smoke of the forge going up straight in the still air.

And at the far end, twenty miles off, where Millbridge should be—

Grey-green.

A stain. Spreading out from the river like a bruise. Soft and dull and colorless, eating into the green of the fields around it. He could see the edge of it from here, creeping. Slowly. Very slowly. Like ink soaking into wet paper.

Toward Ashbourne.

---

Teodra Hale was holding the ford.

They found her at dusk, at the Millbridge crossing, where the road from the north came down to the river and crossed on a long stone causeway half a mile above the village. Her company was strung out across the causeway in a line. Two hundred of them. Men and women in battered leather and mismatched mail, with spears and shields and a great banner of grey and black. The Ashen Banners. Standing in a wall across the causeway, facing south.

Facing the grey.

It was close. Two hundred paces off. The edge of the stain. Lex could see it properly now: the fields on the far side of the river, slumped and soft and grey-green, the hedges sagging, the trees drooping as if their trunks had turned to wet bread. A farmhouse at the edge of it, leaning. Its roof caving in, slowly, like a pie crust left in the rain.

And between the grey and the causeway, on the near bank, things moving.

People. Or what had been people. Walking slowly toward the ford. Grey-green. Soft. Their faces slack and peaceful. Their clothes old-fashioned, two hundred years out of date. Shuffling. Not hurrying. Coming toward the wall of shields.

"*The plague-dead*," said Malachi quietly. "*From Millbridge. Two hundred years ago. She tended them.*" His shifting eyes were very dark. "*She's still tending them.*"

---

"*Forgeheart*."

Teodra Hale came down the causeway toward them. She was exactly as Lex remembered her from Fort Caldris, four years ago: a broad weathered woman in battered leather armor, with a greatsword on her back and two fingers missing from her left hand. Greyer. More lined. But the same frank, unimpressed eyes.

She looked at Lex. At Wren. At Kestrel and Tamsin. At the small brown barefoot old man on the mule.

"*That's it*?" she said. "*That's what the Queen sends me? A smith, an archer, a fire-witch, a farmer, and somebody's grandad?*"

"*Somebody's great-great-great-grandad*," said Malachi mildly. "*Give or take thirty generations.*"

Teodra looked at him for a long moment. Then she spat on the causeway.

"*Fine*," she said. "*I've had worse.*" She jerked her head at the grey. "*They come every night. More every time. They don't fight. They don't hit back. They just walk into the shield-wall and try to* hold *you.*" Her jaw tightened. "*And if they get hold of you, you go soft. Like them. I lost eleven last night. Eleven good swords. Went soft and walked off into the grey, smiling.*"

"*Where's she*?" said Lex. "*The midwife.*"

"*In the village*." Teodra pointed. South. Across the river. Into the heart of the stain. "*At the Stone. On the green. Fenn's scouts saw her. Sitting on a stool beside it. Knitting.*"

"*Knitting*?" said Wren.

"*Knitting*." Teodra's face was grim. "*Fenn said she looked up and smiled at him. Like a nice old granny. And then his horse went soft under him.*"

---

They camped on the causeway that night. Behind the shield-wall. With fires burning all along the line, because Teodra said the plague-dead didn't like fire.

"*They don't run from it*," she said. "*They're past running. But they go slower. And they don't reach for you quite so hard.*"

Lex sat by one of the fires with Teodra Hale. She'd brought a flask. She offered it. He shook his head. She drank.

"*I heard about Thorne*," she said.

Lex looked at her.

"*Roole sent word*," said Teodra. "*In the winter. After the longest night.*" She looked into the fire. "*Said he went down to the bottom of the world with a key and turned it. And then turned it back. And died doing it.*"

"*Yes*."

"*Was it true*?"

"*Yes*."

Teodra was quiet for a long time. She turned the flask over in her hands. The two missing fingers on her left. She'd lost them in the border wars, Lex remembered. Twenty years ago. Fighting beside Elric.

"*He saved my life*," she said. "*Twice. In the east. Once from a Gloam-thing in a ditch, and once from my own stupidity, which was worse.*" She drank. "*He wrote me, four years ago. Said there was work. Dead man in a crystal. Stupidest letter I ever got.*" She almost smiled. "*And then I heard what he did at Caldris. To your father. To you lot. And I said, that's it, Thorne, you've used up both lives you saved, we're square.*"

She was quiet.

"*Tell me*," she said. "*How he went. Exactly.*"

So Lex told her. The bottom of the Rift. The kind voice turning its back. *You'll be nothing at all*. The old man's face. The Key, turning back. The fifty-five threads, given up one by one. Getting older with every one. The Lockward. The blood on his glove. *First. At last. At the very bottom of the world, where nobody will ever see it.*

Teodra listened. She didn't interrupt.

When he finished, she sat for a long time looking into the fire.

"*Stupid old goat*," she said at last. Very softly.

She took a long drink. And then poured a little out of the flask, onto the stones of the causeway, into the firelight.

"*For Thorne*," said Teodra Hale. "*Who never once turned down a stupid fight.*"

---

They went in at dawn.

Not the Ashen Banners. Teodra wanted to. She stood at the head of her shield-wall with her greatsword drawn and said, *we'll cut you a road*, and Lex said no.

"*They're people*," he said. "*The plague-dead. They were people. Two hundred years ago. She's been tending them ever since.*" He looked at the grey shapes on the far bank, shuffling toward the ford. "*If you cut them, she'll just tend them again. And again. And they'll never rest.*"

"*Then what*?"

"*Hold the ford*," said Lex. "*Don't let them past. Don't let them get to Ashbourne.*" He looked at Teodra. "*We'll go to the Stone.*"

"*Five of you*." Teodra's face was very flat. "*Into that.*"

"*Five of us*."

She looked at him for a long time.

"*Thorne said that about you*," she said. "*In his letter. Four years ago. He said, the boy's stubborn as an anvil and twice as dense, and he'll walk into anything if someone he loves is on the other side.*" She hefted her greatsword. "*Go on, then. Anvil. We'll hold your ford.*"

---

The grey was soft under their feet.

That was the first thing. The moment they stepped off the causeway onto the far bank, past the shield-wall, into the edge of the stain, the ground *gave*. Not like mud. Like moss. Like a deep soft carpet, springy and damp, that sank a little under every step and slowly rose again behind.

It smelled of apples and leaf-mould and sleep.

Lex felt it go into him. Through his boots. Up his legs. A heaviness. A warmth. A *tiredness*. The kind you feel at the end of a long day at the forge, when you sit down by the fire and your eyes start to close and you think, *just for a moment, just a little rest*.

*[Lex*,] said Ember sharply. *[Lex, that's her. That's the rot. It's in the ground. It wants you to lie down.]*

*I know*.

He lit his hands.

Flame. Commanding. Gold-white fire pouring off his fingers. And where it touched the grey ground, the moss *hissed*. Shrivelled. Went black. And under the black, for a moment, he saw green. Real green. Grass.

The tiredness lifted. A little.

Beside him, Kestrel's hair caught. All at once. Red and gold, the Ashari fire, roaring up off her head and down her arms. She walked through the grey with fire pouring off her like a torch in a cellar, and the moss shrivelled black around her boots.

"*My grandmother*," she said, through her teeth, "*burned foot-rot out of sheep. Every spring. Stank for a week.*" She looked at the grey stretching away ahead of them. "*This is going to stink for a year.*"

---

The plague-dead didn't stop them.

They came. Shuffling across the soft grey fields. Out of the slumped houses. Out of the drooping orchards. Men and women and children in clothes two hundred years old, with slack peaceful faces and grey-green skin and arms held out.

Not attacking. *Reaching*. The way you reach for someone to hug them.

And where Lex's fire touched them, they stopped.

Not burned. Not destroyed. They stopped and stood still, swaying. And their faces changed. The slack peace going out of them. Something else coming in. *Confusion*. As if they'd woken up in the middle of a long dream and didn't know where they were.

A girl of perhaps ten, in a long brown dress, stopped in front of Lex with her grey arms held out and the gold light of his fire on her face. And looked up at him. And said, in a small cracked voice, two hundred years old:

"*Where's Mam*?"

Lex couldn't answer.

The girl looked around. At the grey fields. At the slumped houses. At the other plague-dead, standing still in the fire-light, swaying.

"*It's cold*," she said. "*I want to go to sleep. Mother Sallow won't let me go to sleep.*"

And she turned. And shuffled away. Back into the grey. Toward the village. Toward the Stone.

*[She's keeping them*,] whispered Ember. *[She won't let them go. Two hundred years. She won't let them* sleep.]

---

Millbridge was very quiet.

They came into it at noon. Down the soft grey lane from the north, between houses slumped like melting candles. Roofs caved in. Doors hanging. Windows filmed with grey-green, like the skin on old milk. And every house was full.

Lex looked through a window as he passed. A family. Mother, father, three children. In their beds. Under their blankets. Asleep. Grey-green and soft and peaceful. Not dead. He could see their chests rising and falling. Very slowly.

Tended.

Every house. Every bed. The whole of Millbridge, asleep. Tucked in. Kept.

"*Gods*," said Wren softly. "*Hammer. These aren't the plague-dead. These are* now. *These are the people who lived here three days ago.*"

"*She's tending them*," said Malachi. "*Like she tended the others.*" His voice was very old. "*She can't help it. It's all she knows.*"

Tamsin had stopped walking.

He was standing in the middle of the lane, with his head tilted. Listening. His face was very pale.

"*She's humming*," he said. "*Can you hear it*? *It's not his song. It's different. It's—*" He frowned. "*It's a lullaby.*"

Lex listened.

And heard it. Faint. Coming from ahead. From the green. From the Stone. A woman's voice. Old and warm and a little cracked. Humming. A lullaby. The kind every mother in the Vale sang. The kind Seren had sung.

*Hush now, hush now, the day is done*.

---

She was sitting on a stool beside the Stone.

Exactly as Teodra had said. A short round motherly woman, in a grey midwife's apron, with her sleeves rolled up and her grey hair in a neat bun. Knitting. Her needles clicking, steady and quick. A long grey-green scarf trailing from them across the soft ground, yards and yards of it, winding around the Stone.

The Stone was black. A hole. A shape cut out of the noon light.

And all around it, on the soft grey green of the village, in rows, the plague-dead. Lying down. Laid out. Washed and dressed and tucked in under blankets of grey moss. Hundreds of them. Two hundred years of them.

And among them, newer. The sleepers of three days ago. Carried here. Laid out in their nightclothes. In rows. Tucked in.

Mother Sallow looked up from her knitting.

She smiled.

"*Oh*," she said. "*Oh, more of you. Come in, come in. You look so tired.*"

---

Her voice went into him like warm milk.

Lex felt it. Not like the Cantor's song. Not like the kind voice at the bottom of the Root. *Softer*. A grandmother's voice. A nurse's voice. The voice that comes into the room at night when you're sick and puts a cool hand on your forehead and says *there, there, sleep now*.

He felt his knees start to go.

"*There, there*," said Mother Sallow. Kindly. Her needles clicking. "*You've been carrying so much. I can see it. All of you.*" Her warm brown eyes moved from face to face. "*The big one. Look at him. He's been carrying the whole world on his back. And the little archer, she's been carrying him. And the red-haired one*—" Her eyes found Kestrel. And softened. "*Oh, love. Oh, you've been carrying something terrible.*"

Kestrel's fire flickered.

"*Lie down*," said Mother Sallow. "*All of you. Just for a little. I'll take care of everything. I'll tuck you in and keep you safe and you won't have to carry anything ever again.*" She smiled. "*That's what I'm for, you know. That's what I've always been for. Taking care.*"

---

Lex was on his knees.

He didn't remember kneeling. The ground was so soft. So warm. The moss sinking under his hands like a feather bed. His eyes were closing.

*[LEX*,] Ember was shouting. Far away. *[LEX, GET UP, GET UP, SHE'S*—]

*Just a moment*, he thought. *Just a little rest. I'm so tired, Ember. I've been tired for so long.*

Beside him, Wren had sat down. Her bow across her knees. Her head nodding.

Tamsin was lying on his side. On the moss. His eyes closed. A small peaceful smile.

Malachi was sitting on his mule with his straw hat tipped over his eyes, swaying.

And Kestrel—

Kestrel was standing.

---

She was the only one. Standing in the middle of the soft grey green, with her fire guttering, low, almost out. Swaying. Her amber eyes heavy. But on her feet.

Staring at Mother Sallow.

"*You kept them*," said Kestrel. Her voice was thick. Slurred. Like someone fighting sleep. "*All of them. Two hundred years.*"

"*Of course I did*," said Mother Sallow kindly. "*Somebody had to. Everybody else ran away.*"

"*You wouldn't let them go*."

"*Let them go*?" The midwife looked shocked. Her needles stopped. "*Where would they go? Into the cold? Into the dark? Into the ground?*" She shook her head. "*No. No, I kept them. I keep them warm. I keep them clean. I keep them safe. I'll keep them forever.*"

"*They want to sleep*," said Kestrel. "*Properly. I heard one. A girl. In the field. She said, Mother Sallow won't let me go to sleep.*"

The midwife's face went very still.

"*She's cold*," said Kestrel. "*She wants her mam. She wants to* sleep. *And you won't let her.*"

"*If I let her sleep*," said Mother Sallow, and her voice cracked, just a little, "*she'll be gone. They'll all be gone. And I'll be alone. In the church. With nobody to tend.*"

---

Kestrel's fire went out.

All of it. Her hair went red. Just red. Her hands went cold. She stood in the middle of the green, swaying, with nothing burning.

And she knelt down. In front of the midwife. On the soft grey moss. So their eyes were level.

"*I know*," said Kestrel Varn.

Mother Sallow stared at her.

"*I know*," said Kestrel again. Very quietly. "*I had a brother. Tam. He was taken. Cut out of the world. Nobody remembered him. Nobody but me.*" Her voice was shaking. "*And someone offered me a way to keep him. To bring him back. Whole. If I just gave them one thing.*"

"*Did you*?" whispered the midwife.

"*I did*." Kestrel didn't look away. "*I gave a madman the key to the end of the world. So I could keep my brother.*" Her amber eyes were wet. "*Forty-one people died. A door nearly broke. A man I'd never met gave up the best memory he had to save a dragon because of what I did.*"

Mother Sallow's needles had fallen into her lap.

"*And I got him back*," said Kestrel. "*Tam. He's here. He's right there, asleep on your moss.*" She didn't look at her brother. "*And I'd do it again. I would. That's the worst of it.*"

"*Then you understand*," said Mother Sallow. Almost pleading. "*You understand. You kept him. Like I keep mine.*"

"*No*," said Kestrel.

---

She stood up.

Slowly. Swaying. Fighting the soft warm weight of the moss with everything she had.

"*I kept him*," she said. "*But he's not* mine. *He's his. He gets to walk around and make stupid jokes and tell people I pick the worst horses at the fair. He gets to* live.*" She swallowed. "*You don't keep people by holding on to them forever. You keep them by letting them go and live and be stupid and leave you.*"

She turned. Looked down at her brother, lying on the moss with his eyes closed and a peaceful smile.

"*Tam*," she said. "*Get up.*"

Tamsin didn't move.

"*Tam the Ram*," said Kestrel Varn. Louder. "*Get up. You named a prize ram after yourself and you're lying in a field like a sheep. Mam would be ashamed.*" Her voice cracked. "*Get up, you idiot. I gave away the world for you. Get up.*"

And Tamsin Varn opened his eyes.

---

Lex felt it. Through the moss. Through the soft warm weight pressing him down. A *crack*. Like ice breaking on a pond.

Tamsin was sitting up. Blinking. Staring at his sister.

"*Kes*?" he said. Muzzily. "*What*—"

"*You were asleep*," said Kestrel. "*On a rot-witch's moss. Get up.*"

He got up. Swaying. And as he got up, Lex felt the crack spread. Felt the soft warm weight on his own chest *lift*, just a little. Just enough.

*[NOW*,] shouted Ember. *[Lex, NOW, while it's cracked—]*

He got one knee under him. Then a foot. Then he was standing. His head clearing. Like coming up out of deep water.

And Mother Sallow was staring at Kestrel with tears running down her round kind face.

"*You let him go*," she whispered. "*And he got up.*"

"*He always gets up*," said Kestrel. "*He's terrible like that.*"

---

"*Burn it*," said Malachi.

He'd got off his mule. He was standing on the moss, small and brown and barefoot, with his straw hat in his hand and his shifting eyes gone gold. Steady gold. Like fire.

"*Smith*," he said. "*Burn it. All of it. Not to destroy. To* release.*" He looked at the rows of the tended dead, laid out on the green. "*They've been waiting two hundred years for someone to light the pyre.*"

Lex looked at the rows. At the plague-dead. Two hundred years in their grey moss blankets. The girl in the brown dress, lying near the end of a row, her eyes open, staring at the sky.

*Where's Mam? I want to go to sleep*.

He looked at the newer sleepers. The people of Millbridge, three days asleep.

"*Not them*," he said. "*The living. Not them.*"

"*Then ask it*," said Malachi. "*Ask the fire. You know how. You're Commanding now.*" His gold eyes were very bright. "*The fourth stage is when you can tell it no. Tell it no for the living. And yes for the rest.*"

---

Lex knelt on the soft grey green of Millbridge.

He put his hands on the moss.

And *Commanded*.

Not shouting. Not forcing. The way the Hearth had taught him, in the Wellworld of Flame. *Keep asking. Every time you light the forge. What's this fire for?*

*This fire is for them*, he told it. *For the ones who've been waiting. Two hundred years. Let them sleep. Not the living. Never the living. Only the ones who are already gone.*

*Burn for them. Gently. The way you'd burn a candle in a window. To show them the way home.*

And far away, at the other end of the Vale, in a long hall with twenty anvils, a girl with freckles and a too-big apron felt the great hearth under her hands *leap*.

---

Tilda Crane told him about it later. Weeks later. Sitting on an anvil in the Ashbourne Forge, with her feet swinging.

She'd been at the great hearth. Brannoc had kept it roaring for three days, day and night, every side-fire lit, every lantern blazing, because the fire kept the rot back from the edge of the village. She'd been feeding it. She'd been so tired.

And it had *leapt*. Under her hands. Up. Out of the hearth. A column of gold. Through the roof. Into the sky.

*And it asked me*, said Tilda. *Not out loud. It just—asked. Can I go? Can I go to him?*

*And I said yes*.

---

Lex felt it come.

Down the Vale. Twenty miles. Not along the ground. Along the *river*. The silver river running through the green, from Ashbourne to Millbridge. Fire. Gold. Running down the water like a lit fuse. Like a wick. Every forge it passed, every kitchen hearth, every candle in every window in every village in the Vale, *leaping*, joining it. Feeding it.

A river of fire, running down a river of water, toward Millbridge.

And Kestrel lifted her hands.

Her fire came back. Not red now. *Gold*. Clean and bright and fierce. The Ashari fire, the oldest fire in the world, that the Hearth had looked at and said *it knows me*. Pouring up off her. Joining his.

And the fire came into Millbridge.

---

It didn't burn the houses. It didn't burn the sleepers. It went *around* them. Lex felt it go. Felt it find every living thread in every slumped house and curl around it, warm and gentle, the way Kestrel's fire had curled around his fingers in Ysmir's vault. Not burning. *Warming*.

And the moss on them shrivelled. And the grey on their skin went pink. And in every bed in Millbridge, people began to stir.

And the fire came to the green.

To the rows.

To the plague-dead, two hundred years in their grey moss blankets.

And it burned.

Gently. Gold. Like candles. Like a hundred candles in a hundred windows. Rising up off every one of them. And where it rose, the grey-green faces didn't twist, or scream, or burn.

They *relaxed*.

Every one. The slack peace going out of them. Something else coming in. Something Lex had seen once before, on a dead man's face in a crystal at Duskhold, when Antho said *I forgive you*.

Rest.

The girl in the brown dress was near the end of the row. He watched the gold fire rise off her. Watched her open eyes close. Watched her small grey face soften.

"*Mam*," she said. Very faintly. "*Oh. There you are.*"

And she was ash. Gold ash. Rising up off the green on the warm air, like sparks from a forge. Going up. Into the noon sky.

All of them. Row after row. Two hundred years of them. Going up in gold.

---

Mother Sallow sat on her stool beside the black Stone and watched them go.

She didn't stop it. She didn't move. She sat with her knitting in her lap and her round kind face lifted to the sky, watching the gold sparks rise. Every one of them someone she'd tended. Washed. Dressed. Tucked in. Kept.

Going.

"*Oh*," she said. Very softly. "*Oh. They can sleep now.*"

Kestrel knelt down beside her stool.

"*Yes*," she said.

"*I kept them so long*." The midwife's voice was barely a whisper. "*I was so afraid. That if I let them sleep, I'd be alone. In the church. With no one.*"

"*I know*."

"*And now I'm alone*." She looked at the empty rows. At the gold ash, drifting up. "*And it's not so bad. It's quiet.*" She smiled. A small, tired, grandmotherly smile. "*It's like the end of a long night. When the last one's finally sleeping. And you can sit down.*"

She looked at Kestrel.

"*Will you sit with me*?" said Mother Sallow. "*Just for a little. Until I sleep too.*"

Kestrel looked at her for a long moment.

Then she reached out. And took the midwife's grey hand in her own burning one.

"*Yes*," said Kestrel Varn. "*I'll sit with you.*"

And Agathe Sallow, midwife of Millbridge, who had stayed when everyone else ran, closed her eyes.

And went up in gold.

---

The Stone went silver.

The moss went black, then brown, then green. Real grass. Underneath, all this time. The houses straightened. Slowly. Like old men getting up from chairs. The windows cleared. And all over Millbridge, in their beds, people woke up and sat up and looked around and said *what happened? how long was I asleep?*

Kestrel sat on the green beside the empty stool with her hands in her lap. She wasn't crying. She was looking at the sky, where the last of the gold ash was drifting up.

Tamsin came and sat beside her. He put his arm around her. She leaned into him.

"*Tam the Ram*," she said. Very quietly.

"*I hate that*."

"*I know*." She closed her eyes. "*You can leave, you know. Whenever you want. Go and be a farmer. Go and be stupid. You don't have to stay with me.*"

Tamsin was quiet for a long time.

"*I know*," he said. "*That's why I'm staying.*"

---

The ford was very quiet when they came back to it.

The plague-dead had come in the night. Teodra had said they would. More than ever. Every one of them, pouring out of the grey toward the causeway, toward the shield-wall, toward Ashbourne. And then, at noon, they'd stopped. All at once. And stood still on the riverbank, swaying. And then gone up in gold.

The Ashen Banners were standing on the causeway in the afternoon light. Staring at the sky. At the last gold sparks drifting away.

And Teodra Hale was sitting on the stones at the head of the line.

With her back against the parapet. With her greatsword across her knees. With her two-fingered left hand resting on the hilt.

Grey-green.

From the waist down. Soft. Mossy. Like the plague-dead. Like the sleepers in the houses. And spreading. Slowly. Up her chest. Toward her heart.

"*Anvil*," she said, when Lex came running up the causeway. Her voice was very calm. "*You did it, then.*"

"*Teodra*—"

"*One of them got me*." She looked down at herself. "*At dawn. A big one. Went for Benno, my standard-bearer. Seventeen. First contract.*" She shrugged, very slightly. "*So I got in the way.*"

Lex knelt beside her. He put his hands on her. Fire. Gold. The way he'd burned the rot off the sleepers in Millbridge.

The grey didn't shrivel.

"*Too late*," said Teodra. "*It's been in me since dawn. Your fire'd have to burn me to burn it.*" She looked at him. "*Don't. I'd rather go soft than go up. It doesn't hurt. Ask anyone.*"

"*No*—"

"*Anvil*." Very firmly. "*Listen to me. I've had a good run. Fifty-three years. Never turned down a stupid fight.*" She almost smiled. "*Thorne'd be furious. I was supposed to outlive him by twenty years and spit on his grave.*"

The grey was at her collarbone.

"*Tell Roole*," said Teodra Hale, "*he owes me two hundred crowns. For the ford. He'll say it was a volunteer action. It wasn't.*" Her eyes were getting heavy. "*And tell the Queen I held her road.*"

"*I'll tell them*."

"*And Anvil*."

He leaned close.

"*When you see him*," said Teodra. "*Thorne. In your knot, or wherever he went. If you ever see him.*" She was smiling. A real smile. Frank and unimpressed. "*Tell him I said it was a stupid fight. And I'm glad I came.*"

And her eyes closed.

And she was asleep. Soft and grey and peaceful, sitting on the stones at the head of the causeway she'd held, with her greatsword across her knees and her standard-bearer, seventeen, standing over her with the black and grey banner in his hands and tears running down his face.

---

Lex sat beside her until the grey reached her heart.

It didn't hurt. She'd been right. It never did. She just went still. And softer. And then, very slowly, like the plague-dead on the green at Millbridge, she went *gold*. The rot burning off her in a quiet golden glow, from the edges in, the way the last of a fire glows in the grate.

And then she was ash. Gold ash. Rising up off the causeway into the evening air.

And her greatsword lay on the stones where she'd been. With its hilt worn smooth by a two-fingered hand.

The storm-mother came down out of the sky at dusk.

Lex heard her before he saw her. Thunder, rolling up the Vale from the west. And then the vast storm-blue shape, coming in low over the river, with her wings spread wide and a small figure on her neck holding a lantern.

Antho slid off her shoulder onto the causeway before she'd finished landing. He looked at the Ashen Banners. At the empty place at the head of the line. At the greatsword lying on the stones. At his brother, sitting beside it, with his face in his hands.

He didn't say anything.

He came and sat down beside Lex on the causeway. And put his scarred hand on the back of Lex's neck. And left it there.

They sat like that for a long time. While the sun went down over the Vale, and the last of the gold ash drifted away on the wind, and far off at the other end of the valley, in a long hall with twenty anvils, a girl with freckles banked the great hearth for the night and said, very quietly, *thank you*.

"*Two*," said Antho at last.

"*Two*."

"*The Cantor's gone. Pim did it.*" His brother's voice was very steady. "*And my Clarity's at a hundred. The Murmur's gone. All the way.*"

Lex lifted his head.

Antho was looking out at the dark river. His grey eyes were very clear. Clearer than Lex had ever seen them.

"*Two more*," said Antho. "*The Knight. And Kell.*" He looked at Lex. "*And then the Heart.*"

"*And Ilse's been waiting ten days*."

"*Eleven*," said Antho. "*I counted.*" He stood up. Held out his hand. "*Come on. The river's a long way. And I promised a little girl in a drowned city I'd come back with a light.*"

Lex looked at the hand.

Then he took it, and stood up. And picked up Teodra Hale's greatsword from the stones of the causeway. And gave it, very carefully, to a seventeen-year-old standard-bearer named Benno, who held it in both hands and couldn't speak.

And went to find Ilse.
