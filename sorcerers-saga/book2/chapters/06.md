# The Brother Who Stayed

There were one thousand one hundred and eleven lanterns in the Hollowmaw.

Antho knew, because he counted them. Every evening. He'd counted them every evening for three years, since the night he and Lex had carried their mother's clay lamp down the long black passage and set it in the niche before the sealed door, and every lantern in the mountain had caught fire at once.

He didn't need to count them anymore. They didn't go out. They hadn't gone out once in three years. They burned on their own, without oil, without wicks, with a steady small gold light that came from nowhere and smelled faintly of a summer evening. He could have left them alone. Everyone told him to leave them alone.

He counted them anyway.

He started at the mouth of the cave, where the great cleft opened onto the mountainside and the wind came in off the Greyspine smelling of snow. An old tin lantern, dented and rusted, hanging from a spike. *One*. A brass one beside it. *Two*. Three paper lanterns gone brown with age, strung on a single cord. *Three, four, five*. On down the long passage, past the place where the floor dipped and the walls narrowed, past the great carved hall where the students slept, past the kitchen and the library and the cold-room where Teo kept his fish. *Four hundred. Five hundred.* Down and down, into the deep part of the mountain, where the passage widened into the round high cavern like the inside of a bell.

*One thousand one hundred and ten.*

And at the bottom of the bell, the door.

It wasn't a door, exactly. It had been a breach, once. A wound in the world. A jagged black tear in the rock at the bottom of the cavern, ten feet high, breathing cold and whispers and the smell of wet ash, while a mad warlock held it shut with green fire and his own sanity for six years.

Now it was a wall of black glass. Smooth. Cold. Very faintly warm at the center, the way a hearthstone is warm in the morning when the fire's been banked all night. When Antho put his hand on it, he could feel nothing on the other side. No hunger. No whispers. Just a deep, patient, waiting quiet.

And in a niche carved in the rock beside it, a little clay lamp. Brown and round and plain, with a chip in its spout and a thumbprint pressed into its base. A cheap thing. The kind they sold for a copper at the Ashbourne market. Burning with a small, steady, golden flame.

*One thousand one hundred and eleven.*

Antho stood in front of it, the way he did every evening, and looked at the flame.

"Evening, Mam," he said.

The flame didn't answer. It never did.

He stood there a little longer anyway.

---

*[Still counting,]* said the Murmur.

It was quiet. It was always quiet now. A thread of a whisper, at the very back of his head, in the four percent of him that wasn't clear. It didn't say much. It didn't need to.

*[One thousand one hundred and eleven. Every night. As if one might have run away while you were teaching.]* A faint dry rustle, like dead leaves. *[As if counting kept them lit.]*

Antho didn't answer. He'd learned not to, years ago. You didn't answer the Murmur. You let it talk, the way you let the wind talk, and you kept walking.

But it wasn't wrong. That was the trouble. It was never entirely wrong.

He went back up the long passage to the hall, where nine young people were waiting for him to teach them how to listen to the dark.

---

"*Again*," he said.

Odile Marsh rolled her eyes so hard Antho half expected them to stay up there.

She was seventeen, from a fishing village on the Tidewater coast, with a sharp face and black hair cut short as a boy's and a tongue like a filleting knife. She heard Shade. Better than anyone he'd ever met, except himself and possibly Malachi. She could sit in a dark room and tell you how many people were in it, and which of them were lying, and which of them were afraid. She also found it very, very boring.

"*Master Antho*," she said, "*with respect. I've been sitting here listening to this rock for an hour. It's a rock. It's very quiet. It's not going to say anything.*"

"It's said three things since you sat down," said Antho.

She stared at him. "*It has not.*"

"It said there's water running in a crack forty feet under your left knee. It said there's a family of bats in the roof of the cold-room that Teo hasn't found yet. And it said that you skipped breakfast, because your stomach is making a sound like an upset badger."

The hall erupted. Teo Brask fell sideways off his bench. The twins from Millbridge clutched each other. Even little Pim, who never laughed, who sat at the very end of the front row with his knees pulled up and his thin face turned to the floor, made a tiny sound into his sleeve.

Odile went red to the ears.

"*That's not the rock*," she said. "*That's just you.*"

"It's the same thing." Antho sat down on the edge of the old stone table at the front of the hall. "Shade doesn't talk in words. It talks in absence. In the space where something *isn't*. You listen to the quiet, and you notice where the quiet has a shape." He looked at her. "There's a quiet the exact shape of a bowl of porridge in your middle, Odile. Go and eat something."

She went. Scarlet. Muttering. But she went, and when she came back, she sat down on her bench and closed her eyes and listened to the rock again without being told.

Antho watched her. And thought, not for the first time, that teaching was the hardest thing he'd ever done. Harder than the breach. Harder than the Hollow. Harder than Duskhold.

At least the Hollow had never rolled its eyes at him.

---

There were nine of them now. There'd been three at the festival, three years ago. Then word got round, the way word does, and they'd started arriving. On foot, mostly. Climbing the long mountain path to the Hollowmaw with their bundles on their backs and their faces white with fear, because everyone in the east still told stories about the mad warlock of the Greyspine, and these were children who heard things in the dark. Who'd always heard things in the dark. Whose families had sent them to priests and herb-women and, once, to a man in Kessick who'd tried to beat it out of a nine-year-old with a belt.

That was Pim.

He didn't talk. He'd been at the Keep for four months, and he'd said perhaps twenty words, all of them to Ashka. He sat at the end of the front row with his knees pulled up and listened. Antho had never seen anyone listen like that. As if the dark were the only thing in the world that had ever been kind to him.

Antho knew exactly what that was like.

So he didn't push. He sat with Pim in the evenings, sometimes, at the mouth of the cave, and didn't say anything, and let the boy not say anything back. It was the best thing anyone had ever done for Antho, at that age. He'd been on the woodpile. At fifteen. Listening to voices. And his little brother had come out every evening and sat with him, and not asked, and not pushed, and just been there.

Lex had been ten.

*He's not here now*, said the four percent. *Is he.*

---

The letter was on the shelf above Antho's bed. He'd read it a hundred times. It wasn't long.

*Antho—*

*I've gone north. Alone. Don't follow. I know you'll want to, and I know you'll be angry, and you're right to be. But someone has to watch the door, and you're the only one who can. I've sent Ashka with you. Keep her close.*

*He's taking the bright ones. You're the brightest thing I know. Please stay where it's safe.*

*I'll write.*

*—L.*

*P.S. I'm sorry about the festival. I'm sorry about all of it. I'll explain when I'm back.*

He hadn't written. Three weeks, and nothing. Not a raven, not a goat, not a word.

Antho had been angry for the first week. Properly, coldly angry, the way he hadn't been angry since before Duskhold. He'd stood on the mountainside at the mouth of the Hollowmaw and looked north and thought about saddling Ashka and going after him and dragging him back by the ear.

*Don't follow*.

*Stay where it's safe*.

As if Antho were a child. As if he were the little brother. As if he hadn't held a breach in the world shut for six years with his own mind while Lex was learning to make horseshoes.

And then, the second week, the anger had gone, and something else had come in behind it, and that was worse. Because underneath the anger, if he was honest, if he made himself be honest the way he'd had to learn to be honest, so as not to drown, there was the other thing.

*He's right*.

*Someone has to watch the door.*

And it was always him. It was always going to be him. The brother who stayed. Lex went out into the world and lit it up. Lex walked into burning forges and Founders' temples and the Pale Warden's fortress, and came out the other side with a new class and a new hammer and a crowd cheering his name. And Antho stayed. On the woodpile. In the Hollowmaw. In the dark, with the door, keeping it shut.

He didn't resent it. Not really.

He just wished, sometimes, that someone would ask him.

---

"*She's eating the goat again*," said Teo.

Antho looked up from the book he was pretending to read. It was late morning, the students were at their chores, and Teo Brask was standing in the doorway of the library, dripping.

Teo was always dripping. He was fifteen, round-faced and earnest and homesick for the Tidewater marshes, and he heard Tide. It was the only thing he heard, and he heard it all the time. Water followed him. Puddles formed under his chair. Rain found him on cloudless days. He'd been at the Keep for a year and had never, as far as Antho knew, been entirely dry.

"Which goat?"

"*Correspondence.*"

Antho closed the book.

Correspondence was Malachi's goat. The one that had walked into the Hollowmaw three years ago with a letter tied round its neck, all the way from the Stillwater, across the Wastes. It had delivered the letter, eaten half of Antho's spare cloak and refused to leave. It now lived in a pen at the mouth of the cave, and every few weeks Malachi sent another letter, and Correspondence went and fetched it. Nobody knew how. Antho had stopped asking.

Ashka hated it.

He found them on the mountainside below the cave. Ashka was sprawled across half the slope like a fallen thundercloud. She was huge now. Twice the size she'd been at Duskhold. Her scales were the deep blue-grey of a storm sky, and when she breathed, little sparks of lightning crawled along her flanks and crackled in the grass. She had Correspondence's hind leg in her jaws.

Very gently.

Correspondence was standing on three legs on the slope, chewing a mouthful of grass and looking unimpressed.

"Ashka," said Antho.

The dragon rolled one great silver eye toward him. She didn't let go.

"Put the goat down."

She made a sound. It was a low rumbling growl that shook small stones loose from the slope and sent them clattering away down the mountain. It meant, as clearly as if she'd spoken: *The goat looked at me.*

"The goat looks at everyone. It's a goat. Put it down."

Ashka considered this. Then, very slowly, with enormous dignity, she opened her jaws and let Correspondence's leg slide out.

Correspondence finished its mouthful. Looked at the dragon. Looked at Antho. And walked over and began, placidly, to eat the hem of his coat.

Behind him, he heard Teo trying very hard not to laugh.

"Not a word," said Antho.

"*No, Master Antho.*"

"Not one."

"*No, Master Antho.*" A pause. A snort. A hiccup of suppressed laughter. "*It's just—Correspondence.*"

"Malachi named it."

"*I know. It's just. It's a* very *good name.*"

Antho looked down at the goat chewing his coat. And at the dragon sprawled across the hillside, watching the goat with an expression of profound and ancient offense. And at the boy behind him, dripping on the grass, red in the face from not laughing.

And something in his chest, for the first time in three weeks, eased.

"Yes," he said. "It is."

---

It came that night.

He was standing in front of the door, at the end of the count. *One thousand one hundred and eleven*. The cavern quiet around him. The lanterns burning gold on every wall. The clay lamp in its niche.

And the flame leaned.

He saw it. The small gold flame in his mother's lamp, which had burned straight up, steady and still, every night for three years. It leaned. Not much. A finger's width. Toward the door. Toward the black glass.

Antho went very still.

And then he heard it.

Very faint. So faint he wasn't sure, at first, that he'd heard anything at all. Coming through the black glass of the sealed door, from somewhere very far on the other side. A voice. A woman's voice. Low and a little rough, as if from long disuse.

Singing.

*If the dark comes calling, love, don't you mind.*
*A lantern's lit for the ones behind.*

He stopped breathing.

He knew that song. Everyone in the Greyspine knew that song. It was an old mountain lullaby, the kind every mother sang. He and Lex had sung it together in this cavern three years ago, with their mother's lamp between them, and every lantern in the mountain had caught fire.

But he knew *this* voice singing it.

Low and rough. A little flat on the high note. The way she'd always sung it, in the cottage at Harrow, in the dark, when he was small and frightened of the noises in the walls.

*If the dark comes calling, love, don't you fear.*
*There's a light in the window, and I'm right here.*

He didn't realize he'd moved until his hand was on the glass.

Cold. Then warm. Very faintly warm, at the center, where the voice was coming from. He pressed his palm flat against it. And the voice stopped.

And on the other side of the glass, something pressed back.

He felt it. Through the black smooth surface of the sealed door. A hand. Small. Cold. A woman's hand, pressing flat against the other side, palm to palm with his.

*[Oh*, *don't*,] whispered the Murmur. *[Don't, don't, don't. You know what this is. You know what it does. It finds the softest place in you and it puts its hand there.]* Its voice was suddenly urgent. Almost kind. *[It sang to you before. When you were fifteen. In your mother's voice. Do you remember? Do you remember what you did?]*

He remembered.

He remembered the woodpile. He remembered nights on the woodpile with his hands over his ears. He remembered a voice that sounded exactly like hers, calling to him from the dark under the trees, and walking out toward it barefoot in the frost, and his little brother's hand on his sleeve, pulling him back.

*[It's the Hollow,]* said the Murmur. *[It's always been the Hollow. It wears whatever face will open the door.]*

Antho stood with his hand on the black glass and his eyes closed.

The hand on the other side didn't move.

And then the voice spoke. Not singing. Speaking. So faint it was barely a breath against the glass.

"*Not yet, love.*"

He opened his eyes.

"*Not yet. Keep the lamp lit. Keep the door shut.*" A pause. The faintest pressure of a palm against his. "*He's coming. For what's under the floor. Don't let him have it.*"

And the hand was gone.

The glass was cold. Just cold. The lamp's flame straightened in its niche and burned straight up, steady and still, as if nothing had happened.

Antho stood there for a long time.

Then he went and sat down on the cold floor of the cavern with his back against the wall, and his arms around his knees, the way he'd sat on the woodpile at fifteen. And he stayed there until the morning, looking at the door, and didn't tell anyone.

---

He didn't tell anyone for a week.

He told himself it was because he wasn't sure. Because the Murmur was right: the Hollow had sung to him before, in that voice, when he was fifteen and half-mad and alone. Because his Clarity was at ninety-six and that left four percent of him that couldn't be trusted, and he didn't know which four percent had heard the song.

That was true. It was also a lie.

He didn't tell anyone because if he said it out loud, he'd have to decide whether he believed it. And if he believed it, then his mother was on the other side of the door. Alive. Or something like alive. And had been, all this time, while he sat on this side of it lighting lanterns.

And he didn't know what he would do with that.

So he taught. And he counted. And every night, at the end of the count, the lamp leaned, and the voice sang, and he put his hand on the glass and felt a hand press back. And every night she said the same thing.

*Not yet. Keep the door shut. He's coming. For what's under the floor.*

---

Seraphine came on the eighth day.

He felt her before he saw her. He always did now. A particular quality of quiet, coming up the mountain path. Not the quiet of Shade. The quiet of someone reading, very intently, while walking up a mountain and not looking where she was going.

He went out to meet her.

She was halfway up the switchbacks, on a mule, with a royal escort of four bored Keepers in grey trailing behind and a crate of books strapped to a second mule. She had a book open on the mule's neck in front of her. She was reading it. The mule was picking its way up the steep path entirely on its own initiative.

She looked up when he came round the bend. And her face, which had been frowning at the page, did a thing he'd never got used to. It *opened*. All at once. Like a window being thrown wide.

"*Antho*," she said.

He'd known her four years. They'd been something for three. He still didn't entirely understand how. She was the cleverest person he'd ever met, and she'd been tortured for a week in the Spire on Kell's orders and walked out of it with a pale scar down her face and her mind entirely her own. She was afraid of almost nothing, except spiders, and the sea, and being wrong about a date. She read faster than anyone alive. She had ink on her fingers always, and she wrote him letters every week, long ones, full of crossings-out and small furious drawings of people she'd met at court, and he kept every single one in a box under his bed, and read them when the four percent got loud.

He reached up and helped her down off the mule. She came down into his arms and stayed there.

"*You're thinner*," she said into his coat.

"I'm the same."

"*You're thinner and you haven't been sleeping. I can tell. You've got the look. The hollow-eyed one.*" She leaned back and looked up at him. Her spectacles were askew. "*What's happened?*"

He opened his mouth to say *nothing*.

And didn't.

"Come inside," he said. "I'll tell you."

---

He told her that night, in the library, with the door shut and a fire in the grate and the students asleep.

All of it. The lamp. The song. The hand on the glass. *Not yet. He's coming. For what's under the floor.*

She didn't interrupt. She sat in the chair across from him with her hands folded in her lap and her face very still, and listened the way she listened to everything. Completely.

When he'd finished, she was quiet for a long time.

"*Do you believe it's her?*" she said at last.

"I don't know."

"*That's not an answer.*"

"It's the only one I've got." He stared into the fire. "The Hollow used her voice before. When I was fifteen. It's how it got its hooks into me in the first place. I'd hear her, at night, calling from under the trees, and I'd go." His jaw tightened. "Lex used to pull me back. By the sleeve. He was ten."

"*And this time?*"

"This time it's not asking me to come." He looked at her. "That's what I keep thinking about. It's never once asked me to open the door. It's asking me to keep it *shut*." He rubbed his face. "The Hollow wouldn't do that. It's always wanted out. That's all it's ever wanted."

"*Unless it's clever*," said Seraphine quietly. "*Unless it knows that's the one thing you'd believe.*"

"Yes."

She was silent.

Then she reached down beside her chair, into the leather satchel she'd carried all the way up the mountain on her own back because she didn't trust the mule with it, and took out a roll of paper. Old paper. Yellow-brown and brittle, the edges scorched black.

"*I didn't come up here just to see you*," she said. "*Though I would have. I'd have come anyway. But I found this.*"

She unrolled it on the table between them.

It was a map. Or part of one. Most of it was burned away, a ragged black edge eating in from three sides. But what was left was clear enough. The long jagged line of the Greyspine, drawn in faded brown ink. The rivers. The passes. Wellscript, in a small precise hand, in the margins.

And in the middle of the eastern Greyspine, at the place where the Hollowmaw would be, a symbol. Drawn in red ink, very carefully.

An open hand. Palm up.

And above the palm, a keyhole.

"*It's from the Spire*," said Seraphine. "*The sealed vaults. Kell kept them locked for twenty years. When she fled, the Queen had them opened, and I've been cataloguing them ever since. Most of it's nonsense. Tax records. Old Sanction ledgers. Love letters from a High Warden two hundred years ago to a woman who wasn't his wife.*" Her mouth twitched. "*They were very bad love letters.*" She touched the scorched edge of the map. "*This was in a box with the Order's seal on it. And a note from the High Warden of the time. Dated four hundred years ago.*" She looked up. "*It said: Destroy. Do not read. The Founders' Key must never be made whole.*"

Antho stared at the red keyhole.

"*Someone tried to burn it*," said Seraphine. "*And didn't quite finish.*"

*Under the floor*.

*He's coming. For what's under the floor.*

"It's here," said Antho. Very quietly. "Isn't it. The third piece. Whatever Father said at the council. It's under the Hollowmaw."

"*I think so.*" Seraphine's hand found his across the table. "*I think that's why there was a breach here at all. Something under the mountain thinned the world. Something forged from the dark side of the lid.*" She swallowed. "*I think you've been sitting on it for nine years, Antho. Holding the door shut on top of it.*"

He didn't say anything.

He thought about six years of green fire. Of holding the breach with his own mind. Of the Murmur in his head every hour of every day. Of the voices. Of the woodpile. Of everything.

All of it. Because something was buried under the floor. And had been, for nine hundred years. And nobody had told him.

*Nobody ever tells you*, said the four percent. *Do they.*

---

"*There's more*," said Seraphine, after a while.

He looked at her.

"*In the capital*." She'd taken her hand back. She was twisting her fingers together in her lap, which was what she did when she was about to say something she didn't want to say. "*Since the festival. Since Lex left.*" She took a breath. "*Lord Chancellor Sorn has put a bill before the Queen's council. The Registration Act. Every Unbound sorcerer in Caldor to be registered with the Crown. Name, class, level, location. For their own protection, he says. So that if the unwriter comes for them, the Crown can guard them.*"

"That's not why."

"*No.*" Her mouth was a thin line. "*It's a list. A list of every bright thread in the kingdom, with addresses. Sorn's Ceiling Party wants it, and half the noble houses want it, and the Queen can't stop it on her own. She needs a majority of the council.*" Seraphine looked at him. "*Your father's been arguing against it. Every day. Very well. Very eloquently.*" She hesitated. "*And then going home at night to his rooms in the Spire and not coming out until morning.*"

"He always did keep odd hours."

"*Antho, it's not that.*" She leaned forward. "*There's something wrong with him. Since the festival. Since the baker. He's—he's frightened. I've never seen him frightened, not like this. He looks at the lanterns in the city at night as if he's counting them.*" She stopped. "*And Wren came back to Highcrest two days after Lex left.*"

Antho went still.

"*She was going after him. She told me. She had her boots on and her bow strung, and she was halfway out the window.*" Seraphine's voice was very careful. "*And your father caught her. And took her into his rooms, and shut the door, and they talked for an hour. And when she came out, she didn't go north.*"

"Where did she go?"

"*Nowhere. She's still there. In the Spire. She's sleeping in the corridor outside his rooms with her bow across her knees.*" Seraphine looked at him. "*She won't tell me why. She just says, someone has to.*"

Antho looked at the fire for a long time.

*Someone has to watch the door*.

*Someone has to*.

---

He went down to the door that night with Seraphine beside him.

He hadn't meant to bring her. He'd meant to tell her and leave it there, and go down alone the way he always did. But at the end of the count, when he turned to go down the last long passage into the bell, she'd taken his hand and come with him, without asking. The way Lex used to, on the woodpile.

They stood in front of the black glass together.

The lamp burned in its niche. Straight up. Still.

"*Will it happen with me here?*" whispered Seraphine.

"I don't know."

They waited.

The lanterns burned gold on every wall. Somewhere far above, at the mouth of the cave, Ashka shifted in her sleep, and a faint growl of thunder rolled down the passage. The cavern was very quiet.

And the flame leaned.

Seraphine's hand tightened on his. He heard her breath catch.

*If the dark comes calling, love, don't you mind—*

He put his hand on the glass.

The hand on the other side pressed back.

And this time, the voice didn't say *not yet*. It didn't sing. It spoke fast, and low, and urgently, the way you speak when you know you only have a moment.

"*Antho. He's found the second one. The bit. He's close. Your brother is leading him to it and doesn't know.*" A pause. A sound like something straining. "*When he has two, he'll come for the third. Here. He'll bring the dark with him. You can't hold the door against two pieces of the Key. Not alone. Not even you.*"

"Then what do I do?"

He hadn't meant to speak. It came out of him like a breath.

The hand on the other side of the glass went very still.

And then, so softly he almost didn't hear it:

"*Oh, my love. You've got so big.*"

He couldn't breathe.

"*Find the Ferryman*," said the voice. Fast again. Urgent. "*West. Past the Teeth. Where the world gets thin. The grey river. Ask him to take you to the Drowned Country. There's someone there who can help you. Someone the Hollow has been keeping for a long, long time.*" A pause. "*Take the dragon. She's been hearing her mother calling for a month. She hasn't told you, because she doesn't know how.*"

The pressure of the hand on the glass began to fade.

"*Wait—*" said Antho. "*Wait, Mam—*"

"*Keep the lamp lit.*"

And she was gone.

---

He stood with his hand on the cold black glass for a long time.

Seraphine didn't say anything. She stood beside him with her hand in his and her face wet and didn't say anything at all, and it was the kindest thing anyone could have done.

At last he took his hand away.

He looked at it. His scarred, long-fingered hand. Pale in the gold light.

"*You heard it*," he said. Not a question.

"*I heard a woman's voice*," said Seraphine. Carefully. Honestly. The way she said everything. "*I heard it through the glass. I don't know whose.*" She swallowed. "*But I heard it, Antho. It wasn't only in your head.*"

He closed his eyes.

*Four percent*, he thought. *And she heard it too.*

The Murmur didn't say anything. For the first time in years, it had nothing to say.

---

Ashka was awake when he came up to the mouth of the cave.

She lay across the slope with her great head lifted and turned to the west. Toward the Teeth. Toward the high jagged peaks at the far end of the Greyspine, black against the stars, where no road went and no one lived and the maps said only *Here the world grows thin*.

She was keening. Very low. So low it was more a feeling than a sound, a deep slow vibration that came up through the rock under his feet and into his chest. A sound he'd never heard her make. Lonely, and searching, and very old.

He went and stood beside her. He put his hand on her great storm-grey neck.

"How long?" he said.

She turned her head and looked at him. One huge silver eye. Lightning moved in the depths of it like fish in deep water.

He'd found her in a nest in the high rocks above the Hollowmaw, the first winter, still wet from the shell. He'd fed her and talked to her through six years of the dark, when she was the only living thing that would come near him. He knew her the way he knew his own hands.

She'd been hearing it for a month. And she hadn't told him. Because he'd been angry, and then sad, and then lost in the door, and she hadn't known how.

"I'm sorry," he said. "I wasn't listening."

Ashka put her great head down on the slope beside him, very gently, and huffed a breath of warm air that smelled of ozone and summer rain. And kept looking west.

---

He found Seraphine in the library at dawn, reading.

She looked up when he came in. She'd been crying again; he could see it. But her face was calm.

"*You're going*," she said.

"Yes."

"*West. To the Teeth. To find a ferryman on a river nobody's ever heard of, because a voice through a door told you to.*"

"Yes."

"*And leave the door.*"

That was the thing. That was the thing he'd been turning over all night, standing on the mountainside beside his dragon, looking west. *Keep the door shut*. *Watch the door*. *Someone has to.* Nine years. Nine years of staying. And now the voice that had told him to stay was telling him to go.

"I'll ask Roole for Keepers," he said. "As many as he can spare. And Brannoc and Hedda, if they'll come. The students know the door. Odile can feel the Hollow better than I could at her age." He swallowed. "And it won't be long. A week. Two."

"*And if he comes while you're gone?*"

Antho didn't answer.

Seraphine looked at him for a long time.

Then she closed her book. She stood up. She came round the table and put her inky hands on either side of his face, and looked up at him through her spectacles.

"*Do you know what I've been thinking, all night?*" she said. "*I've been thinking about you on the woodpile. At fifteen. Lex told me about it, once. How you'd sit there every night, listening to the voices, and not go. Not go into the trees. Even when it was her voice calling.*" Her thumbs moved on his cheekbones. "*Everyone always talks about the brother who went. Lex went to Avaloria. Lex went to Duskhold. Lex went north.*" She shook her head slowly. "*Nobody ever talks about the brother who stayed. Who sat on a woodpile in the dark for a year and held on with his fingernails because he had a little brother to look after. Who held a breach in the world shut for six years so nobody else would have to. Who stayed. Every time. When going would have been so much easier.*"

He couldn't say anything.

"*Staying isn't nothing, Antho*," said Seraphine. "*Staying is a thing you* do. *It's the hardest thing. Lex knows it. That's why he left you here. Not because he thinks you're weak. Because he knows you're the only one strong enough to stay.*" She took a breath. "*And now you have to go. Just this once. And that'll be hard too. In a different way.*"

"How do you know?"

"*Because I know you.*" She smiled. Small and wet and fierce. "*You count lanterns, Antho. Every night. One thousand one hundred and eleven. You can't bear to leave a single one of them alone.*" She kissed him. Once. Quickly. "*Go and find your ferryman. I'll count them for you while you're gone.*"

---

He left that afternoon.

Roole's Keepers wouldn't arrive for four days. Brannoc had sent a letter by raven saying he and Hedda were coming *and if anything happens to that door before we get there I will personally go into the Hollow and drag you back out by your ear, lad*. Odile had listened to Antho's instructions with her arms folded and her eyes narrowed, and then said, *I know, Master Antho, I've been listening to that door for a year, I could hold it in my sleep*, and then hugged him, very hard and very fast, and walked away before he could see her face.

Pim had come and stood beside Ashka while Antho strapped on the saddle. Not saying anything. Just standing there, small and thin and grave, with his hand on the dragon's great foreleg.

When Antho was done, Pim looked up at him.

"*Come back*," he said.

It was the first thing he'd ever said to Antho.

Antho knelt in the grass and looked at him. At the thin face. At the dark, careful, listening eyes.

"I will," he said. "I promise."

Pim nodded. Once. Then he turned and walked back up the slope to the mouth of the cave, and sat down on the rock beside Seraphine, and pulled his knees up, and watched.

Antho climbed onto Ashka's neck.

He looked back once. At the cave mouth. At the two figures sitting on the rock, one small and one not. At the great cleft in the mountain, glowing gold from within with one thousand one hundred and eleven lanterns.

And somewhere far below, at the bottom of the bell, a little clay lamp with a chip in its spout, burning.

*I'll be back, Mam*, he thought. *Keep it lit.*

Ashka spread her wings. Thunder rolled down the valley.

And the brother who stayed went west.
