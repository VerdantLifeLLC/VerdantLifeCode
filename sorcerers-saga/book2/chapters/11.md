# The Ferryman's Price

He felt it on the ninth night by the river.

He'd been sitting on the grey shingle, the way he'd sat every night since he came, with the Lantern beside him and Ashka asleep at his back and the Ferryman standing a little way off beside his long grey boat. He hadn't decided. He'd tried, every day, to decide. He'd walked up and down the shore of the river between the world and the Hollow, and turned it over and over, and every time he came to the edge of deciding, he'd remember the millpond. The willows. The gold evening light. Lex's freckled furious face. *One. Two. Three. Four. Five.*

And he'd stop.

*Nine days*, said the four percent. *Nine days of sitting on a riverbank doing nothing. You were always good at that.*

And then the sky went dark.

Not here. Not over the grey valley, where the sky was always the same flat colorless white. Somewhere else. Somewhere far away to the south. He felt it through the Bloodbond, through the thin frayed thread that ran from him across the whole width of the world to his brother: a shadow passing over the sun. Cold. A wind smelling of wet ash.

And then a sound. Not a sound. A *click*. Small and final. Like a lock turning in a door the size of the world.

Antho stood up.

Behind him, Ashka lifted her great head off the shingle. She'd felt it too. Her silver eyes were wide.

"*Two*," said the Ferryman. He hadn't moved. He stood beside his boat, leaning on his long pole, his eyeless face turned south. "*I felt the river shiver. Something has been joined that was broken.*" A pause. "*Fifty-five threads in my water now. Fifty-five. And the river is cold.*"

"Lex," said Antho. "Is he—"

"*Your brother lives. I would know, if he did not. All the dead of your blood come to my shore eventually.*" The rain-voice was very calm. "*But the one with the key is stronger now. Much stronger. And he will turn east.*"

*Toward the Hollowmaw. Toward Seraphine and Odile and Pim and Teo and nine children and a door and a little clay lamp*.

*He'll bring the dark with him. You can't hold the door against two pieces of the Key. Not alone. Not even you.*

Antho looked at the river.

At the far dark shore, a mile away across the grey.

He didn't decide. He realized, afterward, that he'd never really had to. It had been decided on the woodpile, at fifteen, when he'd sat in the dark with his hands over his ears and not gone into the trees, because there was a little boy in the house who needed him. Everything since had only been him finding out what he'd already chosen.

He turned to the Ferryman.

"Take it," he said.

---

The Ferryman didn't move for a moment.

Then he turned his long eyeless face toward Antho. And under the brim of the wide grey hat, something changed. Not a smile. The Ferryman didn't smile. Something like recognition.

"*You are sure.*"

"No," said Antho. "But take it."

"*It is the brightest thing you have.*"

"I know."

"*When it is gone, you will not know what you have lost. Only that something is missing. Like a tooth pulled in the night.*" The rain-voice was very gentle. "*You will reach for it, in the dark, when the voices are loud. And there will be nothing there.*"

"I know," said Antho. "Take it."

The Ferryman looked at him for a long time. Then he lifted one long grey hand, thin as a heron's leg, and laid it on Antho's forehead.

It was cold. Not like ice. Like the river.

"*Think of it*," said the Ferryman. "*One last time. Hold it in your hands. Then let go.*"

---

He thought of it.

The millpond. Below the Ashbourne mill. Summer. The air thick and gold and humming with midges. The willows trailing their long green hair in the water. The smell of mud and mint and cut hay from the meadow across the stream.

Lex. Ten years old. Skinny as a rake, all knees and elbows, burned brown from the summer, his hair sticking up at the back where he'd slept on it. Freckles. So many freckles. Standing on the stony bit of the bank with a flat grey stone in his fist and his tongue stuck out of the corner of his mouth in concentration.

Throwing. *Plunk*. Throwing. *Plunk*. Stone after stone after stone, straight down into the water like dropped bread.

*It's no good*, Lex had said. Near tears. *I can't do it. I'm rubbish. I'll never be able to do it.*

And Antho, fifteen, kneeling in the reeds beside him. The last summer before the voices. The last summer before everything. Taking his little brother's small brown wrist in his hand.

*No. Look. You're throwing it at the water. Don't throw it at the water. Throw it* across *the water. Low. Flatter. Like it's trying to fly under a door.*

*That doesn't make any sense*.

*It will. Try.*

And Lex trying. And the stone leaving his hand, spinning, flat and low, and touching the water, and *lifting*.

*One.*

Touching. Lifting.

*Two.*

Antho had stopped breathing.

*Three. Four.*

Lex's mouth falling open.

*Five.*

And sinking. A little ring spreading out on the gold water where it went down. And the heron lifting off the far bank in fright, great grey wings flapping. And Lex turning to him with his face lit up like the sun coming out—

*Did you see? Did you SEE? How many? Antho, how many?*

And Antho had looked at that face, that lit-up freckled face, and thought: *Five. Five times. I've never done more than four.*

And said: *Four. It was four. That was brilliant. You're brilliant.*

And picked him up and swung him round. Lex shrieking with laughter. The willows. The gold light. His little brother's arms around his neck.

And then. Years later. The Old Founders' Road. Lying on their backs in the dark under ten thousand stars. *It skipped five times. I counted. I always count. I told you four.* A long silence. And then Lex, beside him, laughing. Helpless. Laughing until he couldn't breathe, until he was rolling on the grass, until Antho started laughing too. *You absolute—you* liar. *Nine years, Antho! Nine years I've been telling people four!*

*I'm sorry*.

*Don't be sorry. Don't you dare be sorry. That's the best thing anyone's ever told me.*

He held it.

All of it. The gold light. The heron. The weight of a ten-year-old boy swung round in his arms. The stars. The laughter. He held it in his hands like a warm smooth stone, the way he'd held it every night in the dark for three years when the four percent got loud.

*Five*, he thought. *It was five.*

And let go.

---

It didn't hurt.

That was the strangest thing. He'd expected it to hurt. He'd expected it to feel like something being torn out of him. It didn't. It was like breathing out. Like setting down a heavy bag at the end of a long road and not noticing, for a moment, that your hands are empty.

The Ferryman took his hand away.

Antho stood on the grey shingle of the river between the world and the Hollow and blinked.

He felt strange. Light. A little dizzy. As if he'd stood up too fast. He looked at the river. At the far dark shore. At Ashka, watching him with her great silver eyes.

*Something's gone*, he thought.

He reached for it. In the dark. In the place where he kept the warm things.

There was a gap there. A smooth round hollow, the size of a stone. Warm, still, the way a bed is warm after someone's got out of it. But empty.

He didn't know what had been in it.

He knew it had been something good. He knew it had been something to do with Lex. He could feel the shape of his brother around the edges of the hollow, the way you feel the shape of a missing tooth with your tongue. A summer, maybe. Water? Something about water.

He couldn't find it.

He stood very still and tried, and tried, and there was nothing. Just the warm empty hollow. Just the shape of something he'd loved, and couldn't remember loving.

*[Oh*,] said the Murmur. Very softly. And for once, it didn't sound cruel. It sounded almost sad. *[Oh. That one.]*

"*It is done*," said the Ferryman. "*The fee is paid. For you and the storm-child both. Across, and back.*"

Antho looked at his hands.

Then he picked up the Lantern. And his staff. And walked down the shingle to the long grey boat.

---

The river was very quiet.

The Ferryman stood at the stern and pushed with his long grey pole, slow and steady, and the boat slid out into the grey. It didn't make a sound. No splash. No creak of wood. The mist parted in front of the bow like curtains and closed again behind the stern.

Antho sat in the middle of the boat with the Lantern on his knees. Its gold light pushed the grey back in a small circle, the size of the boat. Inside the circle, he was still himself. He held onto that.

Ashka didn't fit in the boat. She swam. Or rather, she *walked*, the way she'd walked into the river nine days ago, chest-deep in the grey mist, with her great wings folded tight against her sides and her head held high above the surface. But this time the grey didn't drink her. The fee was paid. The river let her pass. Her scales stayed storm-blue and the lightning crawled steady along her flanks, and she kept her silver eyes fixed on the far shore and didn't look back.

Halfway across, she stopped.

Antho saw her head come up. Saw her nostrils flare. Saw her whole great body go rigid in the mist.

And then he heard it.

Not with his ears. Through the hand he'd laid on the gunwale of the boat, through the grey water, through the river itself. A sound. Far away. Deep and slow and rumbling. Like thunder over mountains a hundred miles off.

A voice.

A dragon's voice, calling. Very old. Very tired. Calling a name.

Ashka screamed.

Not like before. Not grief. *Joy*. A great ringing cry that shook the mist around them and sent lightning leaping from her horns into the grey sky. And she plunged forward through the river, past the boat, toward the far shore, faster than Antho had ever seen her move.

"*She hears it again*," said the Ferryman behind him. Quietly. "*Her mother's voice.*" A pause. "*What the river takes, it takes. But a voice can be heard twice.*"

Antho watched his dragon go. Racing through the grey toward the dark, crying out, her whole body blazing with lightning. And he felt his throat close up.

He didn't know why.

He reached for the warm hollow in the dark. Empty.

But he watched her go, and his eyes stung, and he didn't know why.

---

The far shore was the color of the inside of his eyelids.

Not black. Antho had expected black. The Hollow had always been black to him: the black of the breach, the black of the Gloam, the black of the thing in his head. But this was different. A deep, soft, endless dark, faintly blue, faintly violet, like the sky an hour after sunset when the first stars are coming out. The shore was smooth dark stone. The sky above it was the same color as the stone. There was no horizon. The land and the sky simply met, somewhere far off, and became each other.

And it was *quiet*.

Not the hungry quiet of the breach. Not the whispering quiet of the Murmur. The quiet of a room where someone is sleeping. The quiet of snow falling at night.

He stood on the shore with the Lantern in his hand and felt the Murmur in him go very still.

*[Oh]*, it whispered. *[Oh. I remember now. I remember. This is where I was, before.]* A pause. *[It's so quiet.]*

"*This is not the Hollow*," said the Ferryman. He'd stepped out of the boat behind Antho, onto the dark stone. "*Not the deep of it. This is the shallows. The Drowned Country. Where the Hollow touches the edge of things, and the edge of things drowns.*" He lifted his long pole and pointed with it. Inland. Down a long slope of dark stone toward something that shimmered faintly. "*The storm-mother is down there. Under the water.*"

Antho looked.

There was a lake. A great flat stretch of it, dark and still, filling a valley between low hills of dark stone. And under its surface, faint and blurred, as if seen through very old glass, there were shapes.

Towers. Roofs. Streets. A whole city, drowned. Sunk beneath the dark water. And among the towers, deep down, something enormous. Coiled. Faintly, faintly glowing, with a dim blue-white light, like a lamp seen through fog.

Ashka was already at the water's edge. Pacing. Crying out. Her voice rang across the dark lake and the shape under the water *stirred*.

---

The water wasn't water.

Antho found that out when he waded in. It was like the river: thin, and grey-blue, and cold like forgetting. But thicker. Heavier. It pressed on him. It pulled at his edges, gently, the way the grey had in the valley beyond the Teeth.

He held the Lantern up. The gold light pushed the drowning back.

*[You could stay, you know*,] said the Murmur. Dreamy. *[It's very peaceful. No voices. No doors. No counting.]*

"Be quiet."

*[I'm only saying.]*

He went down. The lake bottom sloped away under his feet, smooth dark stone, and the not-water closed over his head, and he kept walking, down, with the Lantern held high and its little circle of gold around him like a bubble of air.

He could breathe. He didn't know how. He didn't think about it.

Ashka came down beside him. She had to swim: the not-water was too deep for her to walk, and she beat her great wings through it in slow heavy strokes like a heron flying in a gale. The lightning crawled along her flanks, lighting the drowned dark around them in flickering blue.

And they came down into the drowned city.

---

It was very old.

Older than Duskhold. Older than Avaloria. The towers were tall and thin and strange, built of a pale stone that had gone grey-green under the not-water. The streets were wide and paved with great slabs, cracked and tilted. There were statues in the squares, worn smooth, their faces gone. There were doorways with no doors. Windows with no glass.

And there were people.

Not living. Not the Drowned Dead either, the pale ghosts of Qel'Varran who'd lost their names. These were something else. Shapes. Faint and grey and drifting, like the thinned in the valley beyond the Teeth. They moved slowly through the drowned streets, going about errands that had ended a thousand years ago. A woman carrying a basket. A man leading a horse that wasn't there. Children, running, without sound, in a game that had no end.

They didn't look at him.

"*They do not know you are here*," said the Ferryman.

Antho turned. The Ferryman was walking beside him, down the drowned street. His long grey pole tapped on the cracked slabs. Antho hadn't heard him come.

"*They do not know anything*," said the Ferryman. "*This was a city, once. Before the Lattice. Before the Founders. The hungry thing came up out of the dark under it and drank it down, and it drowned here, at the edge of the Hollow, and it has been drowning ever since.*" He tapped his pole. "*Your ancestor sealed the hungry thing away. But the places it had already eaten stayed eaten.*"

"Can they be saved?"

The Ferryman was quiet for a long time.

"*I do not know*," he said at last. "*No one has ever tried.*"

Antho looked at a child running past him. A little girl, faint and grey, with her hair streaming out behind her, laughing without sound.

He opened the Lantern's shutter. Just a little. Just a crack.

The little girl stopped.

She turned. Slowly. And looked at the gold light spilling out across the drowned street. And came toward it. Slowly. One faint step at a time. Like the thinned in the grey wood. Like a child coming in from the cold.

She stood at the edge of the light, and held out her faint grey hands toward it.

*Hearthglow*, thought Antho. *Some of the lost don't want saving. They only want to sit by a fire for a while.*

He set the Lantern down on the drowned street.

"I'll come back," he said to the little girl. "I promise. I have to go and find someone. But I'll come back, and leave a light here for you."

She didn't answer. She didn't understand. She sat down at the edge of the gold light with her faint grey hands held out to it, and her face turned up, and Antho thought, maybe, though he couldn't be sure, that she smiled.

He picked up the Lantern again, and closed the shutter, and walked on. And she watched him go.

---

The storm-mother was in the square at the center of the city.

She was the biggest living thing Antho had ever seen.

Bigger than Sorrowmaw. Bigger than Thraxis. Bigger than anything. She lay coiled in the great drowned square like a sleeping cat, if a cat could be the size of a cathedral. Her scales had been storm-blue once; Antho could see that, in patches, under the grey-green bloom that coated them. Her wings were folded against her sides, vast, ragged, torn in a hundred places. Her head lay on the cracked flagstones of the square, longer than a barn, her great jaws closed, her eyes shut.

And she was *chained*.

Not with iron. With dark. Long ropes of it, thick as Antho's waist, black and soft and faintly writhing, like the shadows of chains. They came up out of the cracks in the flagstones all around her. Dozens of them. Wrapped around her neck, her legs, her wings, her tail. Holding her down.

And on the chains, all along them, things were feeding.

Antho stopped.

They were small. The size of a cat. Dark and soft and boneless, like leeches, like slugs. Hundreds of them. Clustered along the black chains wherever the chains touched her scales. And where they clustered, he could see it: little threads of blue-white light, being drawn up out of the great dragon's body, through the chains, into the soft dark things.

Lightning. They were drinking her lightning.

*[Drinkers*,] said the Murmur. Quietly. *[The Hollow's little mouths. I remember them. They go where the light is. They take it a little at a time. They never stop.]*

"*Eleven years*," said the Ferryman. "*Eleven years as the world counts. Down here, where time is thin, it has felt to her like three hundred. A storm-mother has a very great deal of lightning in her. But not endless.*"

Ashka had stopped too.

She hung in the drowned dark above the square, her wings beating slowly, staring down at the coiled shape below her. At the chains. At the feeding things. And Antho saw her shudder. All along her body. Like a horse shaking off flies.

And then the great dragon in the square opened one eye.

---

It was gold.

Not silver, like Ashka's. A deep molten gold, slit-pupiled, and very, very old. Dimmed. Clouded at the edges, like an old woman's eye. But gold, and glowing faintly, and fixed on the small storm-drake hanging in the dark above her.

And Antho *heard* her.

Not with his ears. With Grave-Speech. With the old cold hearing that had let him speak to the Drowned Dead, and to Thraxis in the black hall at Duskhold. It had never worked on the living before. But she was so close to the edge. So drained. So nearly gone.

Her voice came into his head like thunder over the sea.

*LITTLE ONE.*

Ashka cried out.

*LITTLE ONE. LITTLE SPARK. I HEARD YOU. ACROSS THE WATER. I HEARD YOU CALLING.*

And a picture. Thrown at him, huge and blazing, the way Thraxis had thrown his:

*A nest in the rocks. High. Cold. Lightning. Two eggs, blue as storm-clouds. One hatching. A tiny wet blue head, a tiny cry.*

*Seven winters of guarding them alone, after the Gloam took their father. Seven winters of storms and waiting.*

*And then: a crack in the mountain. A hole in the world, newly torn, breathing cold. Black hands like smoke, reaching out of it. Chains. Being dragged down into it. The nest, the eggs, left behind in the snow. One cracked and hatching. One still whole.*

*And then the dark. The Hollow. The drowned city. The chains.*

*Eleven winters. Three hundred years.*

"*Mother*," Antho whispered. He didn't know he'd said it. "*She's—Ashka's mother.*"

*NOT ASHKA'S MOTHER*, said the great dragon. *I DID NOT NAME HER. I NEVER SAW HER. SHE WAS STILL IN THE SHELL WHEN THEY TOOK ME.*

The gold eye moved. Slowly. From Ashka to Antho. And Antho felt it see him. All of him. Right down to the warm empty hollow in the dark where something had been.

*YOU NAMED HER*, said Vharuun. *YOU FOUND HER IN THE ROCKS. YOU KEPT HER WARM. YOU SANG TO HER.*

Her eye was very bright.

*I HEARD. IN THE DARK. FOR SIX YEARS. A MAN SINGING TO MY CHILD. BADLY.*

Antho's throat closed.

*THANK YOU*, said the storm-mother.

---

He freed her.

It took all night. If there was night in the Drowned Country. It took everything he had.

He went from chain to chain. With the Lantern open. With Shade. With Void Bolt and Shadowbind and every cold quiet thing he knew. The Drinkers fled from the gold light, squealing without sound, shriveling where it touched them. But the chains didn't care about light. The chains were the Hollow itself. The dark at the bottom of the world, twisted into rope.

He couldn't break them. He tried. He threw everything he had at the first one, and it didn't even dent.

So he did what he'd done for eight years in the Hollowmaw.

He *held*.

Not the chain. The *dark*. The deep cold Hollow-stuff the chain was made of. He put his scarred hands on it and reached into it with Shade, the way he'd reached into the breach, and *held it still*. The way you hold a dog that wants to bite. The way you hold a door shut. And while he held it, Ashka bit through it.

She had storm in her jaws. Lightning, crawling along her teeth. And the chain, held still, couldn't flow away from the bite the way it wanted to. It parted. Like a rope cut with a hot knife. And fell, and dissolved, and was gone.

One.

He moved to the next.

*[You can't do this*,] said the Murmur. *[There are forty of them. It's taking everything you've got. You'll burn yourself out. You'll drown here.]*

"Then I'll drown," said Antho, and put his hands on the next chain.

Two. Three. Ten.

His hands were bleeding. Not from cuts. From the cold. The Hollow-chains were colder than anything in the world, and holding them still was like holding ice that bit back. His fingers had gone black at the tips. He couldn't feel them.

Fifteen. Twenty.

The Lantern's light was getting dimmer. It drew on his mana, and he was running out. The gold circle around him shrinking. The not-water pressing in.

*Twenty-five*.

Ashka's lightning was fading too. She'd bitten through twenty-five chains of solid Hollow, and her jaws were bleeding, and the light along her flanks was faint and flickering. But she didn't stop. She looked at him, after every one, with her silver eyes. And waited. And when he put his hands on the next chain, she bit.

*Thirty*.

He couldn't see properly anymore. The drowned square was going grey at the edges. Like the thinned. Like the man with the rubbed-out face.

*Anthoren Vey*, he thought. *Anthoren Vey. Twenty-seven. I count the lanterns.*

*One thousand one hundred and eleven.*

*Thirty-five*.

He fell. His knees hit the cracked flagstones of the square. He got one hand on the next chain. Held. Felt Ashka's jaws close on it. Felt it part.

*Thirty-six*.

He couldn't get up.

There were four left. Around her neck. The thickest. The oldest. He could see them, black against the faded storm-blue of her scales, a few paces away. He couldn't reach them.

*Get up*, he told himself. *Get up. Get up. You held a breach for eight years. Get up.*

He couldn't.

And then a voice. Not the Murmur. Not Vharuun. Very faint, very far away. From somewhere deeper in the dark. Low and a little rough, as if from long disuse.

*Oh, my love. You've done so well.*

He lifted his head.

There was a light. Far off. Beyond the edge of the drowned square, beyond the drowned city, out in the deep dark where the Drowned Country went down into the real Hollow. A small light. Gold. Steady. The size of a candle flame. Like a lamp in a window, very far away across a field at night.

*Let me help*, said the voice.

And the four chains around Vharuun's neck *loosened*.

Not broke. Loosened. As if someone very far away, at the other end of them, down in the deep dark, had let go.

---

Vharuun rose.

Slowly. The way the Sunwell had risen, Antho thought later. The way an old man gets up from a chair. The four last chains fell away from her neck and slid back into the cracks in the flagstones like snakes going into holes. And the great storm-mother lifted her head off the floor of the drowned square for the first time in eleven years that had felt like three hundred.

And *breathed*.

It came up out of her in a great slow roll. Not fire. Not lightning. Thunder. A sound so deep it was barely a sound, a vibration that came up through the stone and the not-water and Antho's bones. And as it rolled out across the drowned city, the grey-green bloom on her scales cracked and fell away, and underneath, faint and patchy but *there*, they were blue.

Storm-blue. Ashka's blue.

She spread her wings. Ragged and torn, vast as sails. The not-water churned around them.

*LITTLE ONE*, she said. *COME HERE.*

Ashka came. Down out of the dark, slowly, her jaws bleeding, her lightning faint. And pressed her long narrow head against her mother's jaw. The way she'd pressed it against Thraxis's, in the black hall at Duskhold, three years ago, while her father died.

And Vharuun breathed on her.

Not everything. She didn't have everything. She'd been drunk on for eleven years that had felt like three hundred, and there was very little left. But some. A slow soft stream of blue-white light, pouring out of the old dragon's jaws into the young one's. And Ashka's flanks lit up. Her wounds closed. Her lightning came back, crawling bright along her scales.

And she made a sound Antho had never heard her make. A small, soft, rumbling purr. Like a kitten. Like the sound she made curled in his lap when she was the size of a cat.

He was lying on the flagstones of the drowned square. He couldn't get up. He watched the two storm-dragons pressed together, the huge old faded one and the young bright one, and the tears ran down the sides of his face into his ears, and he didn't know if they were his.

And far out in the dark beyond the drowned city, the little gold light was still burning.

---

"*Who was that*?" said Antho.

He'd got up eventually. He didn't know how long it took. Vharuun had breathed on him too, at the end, a little gentle huff of storm-air that smelled of rain on hot stone, and he'd felt strength come back into his hands. Not much. Enough to stand.

He stood at the edge of the drowned square and looked out into the dark, where the gold light had been. It was gone now. Just dark. Deep and soft and endless.

"*Who was that?*" he said again. "*The light. The voice. Who loosened the chains?*"

Behind him, the Ferryman didn't answer.

Antho turned. "You know. You know who it was."

The eyeless face looked at him from under the wide grey hat.

"*I have told you*," said the Ferryman, "*that I will not speak of what was paid to me freely. It is not mine to give.*"

"That's not an answer."

"*No*," the Ferryman agreed. "*It is not.*"

Antho stared at him. Then out at the dark again. Where the light had been.

*Oh, my love. You've done so well.*

*Let me help.*

*Not yet. Keep the lamp lit.*

*A small clay lamp. Burning.*

*THE WOMAN WITH THE LAMP*, said Vharuun.

Antho turned.

The storm-mother had lifted her great head and was looking at him with her one clouded gold eye. Ashka was curled against her side. And Vharuun's voice came into his head again, quieter now, like thunder very far away.

*THE WOMAN WITH THE LAMP. I HAVE SEEN HER. IN THE DARK. FOR MANY YEARS.*

Antho couldn't breathe.

*SHE COMES TO THE EDGE OF THE DROWNED COUNTRY, SOMETIMES. TO REST. SHE SITS AT THE EDGE OF THE DEEP AND LIGHTS HER LAMP AND SINGS.* A pause. *SHE SINGS TO THE DOOR. A LITTLE SONG. ABOUT A LANTERN IN THE WINDOW.*

"*Is she*—" He couldn't finish.

*SHE IS TIRED*, said Vharuun. *VERY TIRED. SHE HAS BEEN HOLDING SOMETHING FOR A LONG TIME.* Her gold eye moved, slowly, to the dark beyond the drowned city. Down. Toward the real Hollow. Toward the deep. *SOMETHING THAT WANTS VERY MUCH TO COME OUT.*

And then, very softly:

*SHE LOOKS LIKE YOU. AROUND THE EYES.*

---

They came back across the river at what might have been dawn.

Antho sat in the long grey boat with the Lantern on his knees and didn't speak. The Ferryman pushed with his pole, slow and steady, and the mist parted and closed. Behind the boat, two storm-dragons swam through the grey: one young and bright, one old and vast and faded, her ragged wings folded tight, her one good eye fixed on the shore of the world ahead.

The river let them through. The fee was paid.

Halfway across, Antho reached for the warm hollow in the dark. He didn't know why. He did it without thinking, the way you put your tongue to the gap where a tooth was.

Empty. Smooth. Warm.

He didn't know what had been there. He knew it had been about Lex. He knew it had been the best thing he had.

He'd given it for a storm-mother he'd never met, and eleven years of chains that had felt like three hundred, and four chains loosened by a light in the dark.

He thought it was probably worth it.

He wished he could remember what it was.

---

The Ferryman stood on the grey shingle as they climbed out of the boat. He didn't follow them up the shore.

"*Anthoren Vey*," he said.

Antho turned.

The eyeless face was turned toward him under the wide grey hat. And the unlit lantern at the top of the long grey pole swung gently in the wind off the river.

"*I have been the Ferryman of this river since before the world was made*," he said. "*I have taken a great many memories. Kings' crowns and lovers' kisses and the faces of children. I keep them all. That is what I am for.*" He paused. "*Yours is a good one. One of the best. I will keep it carefully.*"

Antho didn't know what to say.

"*If you ever wish to see it again*," said the Ferryman, "*come back to my shore. I cannot give it back. But I can let you look.*" A pause. "*That is not nothing.*"

"Thank you," said Antho.

The Ferryman inclined his long grey head. Then he turned, and stepped back into his long grey boat, and pushed off from the shore with his pole. And the mist closed around him, and he was gone.

---

They flew east.

Vharuun couldn't fly well. Her wings were too torn, her strength too drained. But she could glide, and she could ride the wind, and Antho had Gale. He sat on Ashka's neck with the Lantern hanging from the saddle and one hand stretched out toward the old dragon flying beside them, and *pushed*. Wind. Moving air. Lifting under her ragged wings, holding her up. It cost him. Every hour, it cost him. By the second day his hand was shaking and his face was grey.

But they crossed the grey valley. And the Teeth. And came down out of the high cold passes into the long green valleys of the western Greyspine, where the world was thick and real and full of color again, and Antho almost wept at the sight of an ordinary pine tree.

And on the fourth day, toward evening, they came over the last ridge and saw the Hollowmaw.

It was still lit.

He saw it from twenty miles off. The great cleft in the mountain, high on the slope, glowing gold from within. One thousand one hundred and eleven lanterns.

He let out a breath he felt like he'd been holding for three weeks.

*Still lit*, he thought. *Still here. Still home. He hasn't come.*

*Not yet*.

And then he saw the camp.

Below the Hollowmaw, on the long green slope of the valley, where there had been nothing but grass and goats and Correspondence's pen. Tents. Dozens of them. Grey tents, in neat rows. Cookfires. Horses. And Keepers, in their grey coats, drilling on the slope in long lines.

*Roole*, he thought. *Roole's Keepers. He sent them. As many as he could spare.*

There were more than he'd expected. Many more. Hundreds.

And as Ashka and Vharuun came gliding down out of the evening sky toward the mouth of the cave, the Keepers on the slope looked up and saw them, and shouted, and pointed. And a small figure came running out of the mouth of the Hollowmaw, down the slope, through the long grass. In a grey dress. With spectacles. Holding up her skirts with one inky hand and waving with the other.

Seraphine.

And behind her, smaller, slower, a thin dark-haired boy who never spoke, walking very carefully down the slope, with his eyes on the sky.

Antho slid off Ashka's neck before she'd finished landing. He nearly fell. He didn't care.

Seraphine reached him first. She didn't say anything. She threw her arms around him and held on, and he held on back, and she was saying something into his coat that he couldn't hear.

Then she leaned back and looked up at him. And her face changed.

"*Antho*," she said. "*What happened? Your face. There's something—something's different.*"

"I paid the Ferryman," he said.

"*With what?*"

He opened his mouth to tell her.

And didn't know.

He stood on the slope below the Hollowmaw with his arms around her and his mouth open, and reached for it, and there was nothing there. Just a warm smooth empty hollow, the shape of something he'd loved.

"*I don't know*," he said. "*Something about Lex. Something good.*" His voice cracked. "*I don't know what it was.*"

Seraphine stared at him.

Then she put her inky hands on either side of his face, very gently, and pulled his head down, and pressed her forehead against his. And held it there. And didn't say anything at all.

Behind them, the great old storm-mother Vharuun settled onto the slope below the Hollowmaw like a mountain lying down. And a hundred Keepers in grey coats stopped drilling and stared.

And Pim came up beside Antho, and looked up at the vast ragged faded dragon. And then at Antho.

"*You came back*," he said.

It was the second thing he'd ever said.
