# Prologue: A New Threat

Corin Bell was eleven years old the night his sister forgot her name.

They were in the high pasture above Harrowgate, in the far north of Caldor, where the hills ran up into the first grey shoulders of the Ironback range and the wind came down off the snow even in summer. It was late. The sheep were folded. The fire was banked. Corin was lying on his back in the heather with his hands behind his head, looking at the stars, and his sister was sitting beside him with her knees drawn up, singing under her breath.

Mella was sixteen. She'd been Kindled in the spring, at the old Stone in Harrowgate square, the first spring after the Keepers came through and took the flags off it, and the first spring after the smith with the gold eyes flew in on a dragon and laid his hands on it and broke every Ceiling it had ever held. Mella had put her hand on the Stone afterward, in front of the whole village, and the silver had come up and said *Shepherd, Uncommon*. And then a moment later, as if the Stone had thought about it, it had said something else.

*Weatherwise. Rare.*

She'd laughed for a week. She could feel storms coming now, a day before they came. She could smell snow on the wind three valleys away. She'd told Corin it was like having a second pair of ears, ears for the sky. She was going to go to Highcrest in the autumn, she said, to the Royal Observatory, where the Queen had said that any Unbound with a weather-class could come and learn.

She was singing a song about the sky now. Corin didn't know the words. He wasn't really listening. He was nearly asleep.

And then she stopped.

Not at the end of a line. In the middle of a word. Just stopped. As if someone had put a hand over her mouth.

Corin opened his eyes.

His sister was sitting very still beside him in the heather. She was looking at her hands. Turning them over. Looking at the palms, then the backs, then the palms again, the way you'd look at something you'd found on the road and didn't recognize.

"Mel?" said Corin.

She looked at him.

And he saw it. In her eyes. Something that wasn't there anymore. Like a lamp that had been burning in a window, and someone had walked past and pinched the wick.

"Who's Mel?" she said.

---

He thought she was joking. Of course he did. He laughed, and sat up, and punched her arm, and said *very funny*. And she looked at the place on her arm where he'd punched it, and then at him, with an expression of polite puzzlement, as if a stranger had punched her.

"I'm sorry," she said. "Do I know you?"

Corin stopped laughing.

"Mel. Mella. It's me. Corin. Your brother."

"I don't have a brother," said his sister. She said it kindly. Gently. The way you'd tell a lost child that you weren't his mother. "I don't think so. I don't—" She stopped. Her brow creased. She looked down at her hands again. "I don't think I have anyone," she said slowly. "I can't remember having anyone." She looked up at him. "What's my name?"

"*Mella*." His voice cracked. "*Mella Bell. You're sixteen. You live in Harrowgate. You're a Weatherwise. You're going to Highcrest in the autumn—*"

"Mella," she said, trying it. Like a word in a foreign language. "Mella. That's nice." She frowned. "It doesn't feel like mine."

And then she started to fade.

Not all at once. Slowly. Like a picture drawn in chalk, left out in the rain. Her edges went soft. Her colors went pale. He could see the heather through her hand. Through her arm. Through her face, which was still turned toward him with that polite, puzzled, kindly expression, as if she were waiting for him to explain something she didn't understand.

"*Mel*—"

He reached for her. His hand went through her shoulder like it was going through mist. Cold mist. Colder than the snow on the Ironbacks.

"*Mella! MELLA!*"

She was almost gone now. A shape in the starlight. A pale outline of a girl sitting in the heather with her knees drawn up.

And then she wasn't even that.

The heather where she'd been sitting was flattened. There was a dent in it. The shape of someone. And her shawl, the blue wool one their mother had knitted, lay on the ground where it had slipped off her shoulders.

And nothing else.

---

He ran down the hill to Harrowgate in the dark, screaming.

He hammered on the door of his own house until his father came out with a lamp, bleary and frightened. *Mella*, he sobbed. *Mella's gone, she's gone, she faded, she didn't know her name, she didn't know me, she just went—*

And his father looked at him with the same polite, puzzled, kindly expression his sister had worn in the last moment before she faded.

"Who's Mella?" said his father.

Corin stood on the doorstep in the dark and stared at him.

"Your daughter," he whispered. "Your *daughter*, Da. Mella. She's sixteen. She sleeps in the room at the top of the stairs."

His father looked at him for a long moment. Then he turned and looked back into the house. At the stairs. At the door at the top of them.

"That's the storeroom," he said slowly. "We keep the wool up there." He looked back at Corin, and his face was full of worry now. "Corin, lad, are you feeling all right? You've been out in the cold too long. Come in. Come in by the fire."

Corin pushed past him and ran up the stairs.

The room at the top of them was full of wool. Bales of it. Fleeces. Spindles. Baskets of carded roving. And nothing else. No bed. No chest. No little shelf with the clay bird Mella had made when she was seven. No dresses on hooks. No boots by the door.

As if she'd never been there at all.

He went back down. His mother was up now, in her nightgown, with her hair in a braid. She looked at him the way his father had. Worried. Kind. Puzzled.

He made himself look at her. He made himself ask.

"How many children do you have, Mam?"

His mother laughed. It was a soft, puzzled, loving laugh.

"Just you, sweetheart," she said. "Just you. You know that."

---

He was the only one who remembered.

He went round the whole village in the morning. Every house. He asked everyone. The miller. The smith. The old woman who kept bees. Mella's friends, the girls she'd been Kindled with in the spring. He asked them all. *Do you remember Mella Bell? Sixteen. Weatherwise. She was going to Highcrest.*

And every one of them looked at him with the same polite, puzzled, kindly expression. And said *no*.

He went to the Stone.

The old Stone in Harrowgate square. Grey and lichened and roughly the shape of a gravestone. The one Mella had put her hand on in the spring, and laughed, and laughed. He put his own hand on it. He wasn't sixteen. He wasn't Kindled. He didn't have a class or a panel or anything at all. But he put his hand on it anyway, and closed his eyes, and *asked*.

*Where is she? Where did she go?*

The Stone didn't answer. Of course it didn't. It was just a stone. But when he opened his eyes and took his hand away, there was something in the air in front of him. Just for a moment. A shape. Burned into the morning light like the afterimage of a lamp.

A keyhole.

Black. The size of his hand. Hanging in the empty air above the Stone with nothing around it. Like someone had taken a knife and cut a keyhole-shaped hole in the world.

And through it, very faintly, so faintly he would never be sure afterward that he'd heard it at all, a sound. A girl's voice. Singing a song about the sky. Getting further and further away.

Then the keyhole closed, and was gone, and it was only morning in Harrowgate square.

---

The Keepers came eleven days later.

Corin had never seen a Keeper. He'd heard about them. Everyone had. The old Wardens, the grey cloaks, who'd taken their silver lattice-pins off and become something else when the new Queen was crowned. Who went from village to village taking the flags off the Stones. Who wore a new pin now: a little lantern, silver, on the left breast.

There were two of them. A tall lean grey-haired man with a face like a hatchet, and a short wiry woman with a tracker's squint. They came up the road from the south on tired horses and stopped at the Stone in Harrowgate square and got down and looked at it for a long time.

Corin was sitting on the edge of the well. He'd been sitting there every day for eleven days. Waiting. He didn't know for what.

The hatchet-faced man noticed him first.

"You," he said. Not unkindly. "Boy. Have you seen anything strange here? In the last fortnight? Anything at all?"

Corin looked at him.

"My sister," he said. "She faded. And everyone forgot her. Everyone except me."

The hatchet-faced man went very still.

The short wiry woman beside him turned her head sharply.

"*Commander*," she said.

"I know, Fenn." The tall man crouched down in front of Corin, so their eyes were level. His eyes were pale blue and very steady. "What's your name, son?"

"Corin Bell."

"And your sister's?"

"Mella." Corin's voice cracked. "Mella Bell. She was sixteen. She was a Weatherwise. She was going to Highcrest in the autumn."

The tall man looked at him for a long moment. Then he looked over his shoulder at the woman, and something passed between them that Corin couldn't read.

"That's the ninth village," said the woman quietly. "Ninth this summer. Ninth that we know of."

"And the first time anyone's remembered," said the tall man.

He turned back to Corin.

"Corin Bell," he said. "I'm Commander Daxian Roole, of the Keepers. I'm going to ask you to show me exactly where your sister was sitting when she faded. And then I'm going to ask you to come with us. To Highcrest."

"Why?"

Roole looked at him. And something in his hatchet face softened.

"Because you're the only person in nine villages who remembers someone who's been taken," he said. "And there's a smith in the south with gold eyes who's going to want to talk to you very badly."

---

Before they left, Corin showed them the place in the high pasture.

The flattened heather. The dent in it, the shape of a girl sitting with her knees drawn up. The blue wool shawl, still lying there, damp with eleven days of dew.

Commander Roole knelt beside it. He didn't touch the shawl. He looked at the air above it. And Corin saw him draw in a breath, very sharp.

"*Fenn*," he said. "Look."

The short woman knelt beside him. She looked. Her squinting tracker's eyes went wide.

Corin looked too.

There was something in the air above the shawl. Faint. So faint he wouldn't have seen it if he hadn't been looking for it every day for eleven days. Not a keyhole. Not anymore. Just a scar. A thin black line, hanging in the empty air, the shape of a keyhole that had been cut and then healed over. Like the white line on a man's hand where a knife went in a long time ago.

"*Unwritten*," said Fenn softly. "That's what they're calling it in Highcrest. The Royal Archivist. She's been tracking the reports. She says it's like someone took a knife to the Lattice and cut a person's thread right out of it. Every record. Every memory. Everything that held them in the world." She swallowed. "Like they were never written in the first place."

Roole didn't say anything. He was staring at the scar.

"Commander?" said Fenn.

"I've seen this shape before," said Roole slowly. "Once. A long time ago. On an old Warden map in the Spire's sealed vault. The one the High Warden kept in her private office." He was very quiet for a moment. "It was drawn in the margin. Beside a note in her own hand."

"What did the note say?"

Roole stood up. He looked north. Up at the grey shoulders of the Ironbacks, and beyond them, to where the sky went pale and strange over a land that wasn't on any map in Caldor.

"*The Key*," he said.

---

That night, far to the east, beyond the healing Blightmarch, beyond the black ring of stones where Duskhold had stood, in the Sundered Lands where the Veil grew thin and strange and the ground did not always stay where you left it, an old man sat alone by a fire.

He was not as old as he had been.

That was the strange thing. Three years ago, when he walked out of Duskhold with nothing but nine stolen points of Authority and a note he couldn't bear to finish, he had been bent and grey and hollow, a man of sixty-one who looked eighty. Now he sat straighter. His hair was still grey, but there was dark in it again. The lines on his face had softened. His hands, holding a cup of something hot, were steady.

He had no staff. He didn't need one anymore.

At his belt, on a chain of dull black iron, hung a key.

Not a whole key. Part of one. The bow. The handle, the ring at the end of a key that your fingers hold to turn it. It was black. Not the black of iron, or Gloam, or anything Corin Bell would have recognized. The black of the space between stars. Of the dark before the first word. It was carved all over with Wellscript so fine you'd need a jeweler's lens to see it. And in its center, where a jewel might sit on a noble's key, there was a hole.

A keyhole. In the bow of a key. As if the key could open itself.

The man by the fire wasn't looking at it. He was looking at the flames. And in his mind, in the place under the air where his Authority burned, he was counting.

Not nine anymore.

Forty-three.

He had taken the ninth one that summer. A girl in the north, in a high pasture, singing about the sky. He hadn't known her name. He'd only known that her thread was bright and new and unbound, the way all the Unbound threads were, and that when the key's bow touched it, it came away from the net as easily as a loose thread from a hem. And the mana that had been in it came to him.

He told himself it didn't hurt them. He told himself they didn't feel it. They simply... stopped being written. Stopped being remembered. It wasn't death. It was something gentler than death.

He told himself a great many things, by the fire, at night.

*You're doing well, Elric*, said a voice in his mind. Old. Very old. Kind. The kindest voice he had ever heard. *Forty-three points. In three years. Without anyone giving you anything. You've earned every one.*

The old man who wasn't as old as he had been closed his eyes.

"*The Bit*," he said aloud. His voice was rough. "*Where is it?*"

*South*, said the voice. *Far south. In the glass, where the sun is too bright to look at.* A pause. *But you won't need to find it, Elric. Someone else is going to find it for you.*

"*Who?*"

The voice was silent for a long moment.

*My blood*, it said at last. Gently. *The young one. The smith. He'll go looking. He always goes looking.* Something in the kind old voice that might have been fondness. *He's so very like me.*

Elric Thorne opened his eyes.

He looked at the fire for a long time.

Then he reached down and touched the black bow of the key at his belt. And far to the south and west, in a village called Ashbourne, in a forge with twelve anvils, a young man with gold eyes woke suddenly in the dark with a feeling like a hand closing on his heart.

And didn't know why.
