# The Forbidden Realms

The world grew thin past the Teeth.

Antho had known it would. The maps said so, the old ones, the ones Seraphine had spread out on the library table the night before he left, peering at them through her spectacles with her nose almost touching the paper. *Here the world grows thin.* Every map. Every century. Written in the same place, at the far western end of the Greyspine, where the great range broke apart into a jumble of jagged black peaks like the teeth of some enormous broken jaw. Beyond them, nothing. Blank paper. Not even a guess.

"*Nobody's ever come back to draw it*," Seraphine had said. "*Or if they did, they didn't remember what they'd seen.*"

He'd thought she was being dramatic.

He'd been wrong.

---

It started with the color.

They crossed the last of the Teeth on the third morning, Ashka laboring up through thin cold air over a pass so high the snow on it had never melted, and came down the other side into a long valley. And the valley was grey.

Not grey like ash. Not grey like the Gloam-touched land had been, three years ago, dead and sick and rotting. Grey like a drawing. Like someone had sketched a valley in charcoal and forgotten to paint it. The grass was grey. The trees were grey. The sky over them was a pale flat white-grey, like an unpainted ceiling. There was a river at the bottom of the valley, and it was grey too, and it didn't sparkle.

Antho looked down at his own hand on Ashka's neck. It was still the right color. Pale, scarred, real. But at the edges, very faintly, where his fingers met the grey air, it seemed to blur. Like a drawing someone had started to rub out.

*[Oh*,] said the Murmur. *[Oh, I know this place.]*

He didn't answer it. But he lifted the Keeper's Lantern from the hook on Ashka's saddle and held it up, and the small steady gold flame inside it pushed the grey back a little. Not much. A circle the size of a cartwheel. Inside the circle, his hand stopped blurring.

Ashka flew on, westward, down the grey valley. Her storm-colored scales were going dull. He could see it. The blue-grey of her flanks fading, little by little, toward the same charcoal grey as everything else. The lightning that usually crawled along her sides had gone quiet. She was flying slowly. Heavily. As if the air were thick.

But she didn't turn back. She kept her great head pointed west, and kept making that low, lonely, searching sound in her chest, the one that came up through the saddle into his bones.

---

The Lattice went strange in the afternoon.

He'd been expecting that, too. He'd felt it before, at the Hollowmaw breach, years ago, and at Vhessa, and in the black hall at Duskhold: the places where the net was thin, where the silver threads of the world stretched and frayed and the Hollow showed through underneath. The panels went wrong in places like that. Words dropped out. Numbers scrambled.

But not like this.

Antho read it twice. Then he put the Lantern on his knee where he could see it, and watched its flame, and didn't look at anything else for a while.

*[It means,]* said the Murmur helpfully, *[that you might forget which bits of you are you.]*

"I know what it means."

*[I'm only saying. I've been here before. Well. Near here. Before I was in you. Before I was much of anything.]* A pause. A faint dry rustle. *[It's very restful, actually. Once you stop fighting it.]*

"Be quiet."

*[You always say that.]*

"You never listen."

*[True.]* The faintest hint of something like amusement. *[We are very alike, you and I.]*

Antho didn't answer. He looked at the Lantern. The small gold flame burned on, steady and still, and the four percent of him that wasn't clear grumbled and subsided.

The Keeper's Lantern. Lex had made it, with twelve Hush-runes on its glass and the whole of his heart in its making, and Malachi's eternal fire had finished it. *Voices can't act through you while it's lit.* That was what the panel had said. Antho had read it a hundred times since. Every time the four percent got loud.

He'd never told Lex what that meant to him. That his little brother had sat at an anvil for two days without sleeping and made him a way to be alone in his own head.

He should have. He should have told him a lot of things.

*He went north without you*, said the four percent. *He didn't ask.*

Antho looked at the flame and didn't answer.

---

They came down at dusk in a grey wood, because Ashka couldn't fly anymore.

She didn't say so. She couldn't. But she began to drop, slowly, her great wings beating heavier and heavier, the silver wing-frame Lex had forged for her creaking at the joints. And then she simply folded and glided, down and down, into a clearing among grey trees, and landed hard, and lay there with her sides heaving.

Antho slid off her neck and went round to her head.

"I know," he said. "I know. It's all right."

She rolled one great eye at him. It had gone grey. All the silver and lightning in it fading.

He put his hand on her nose. And did something he hadn't done in years: he *listened* to her. Not with his ears. With Shade. The deep cold quiet hearing that lived in the space between heartbeats. He listened to the shape of the silence inside his dragon. The places where she wasn't.

And found it.

A hole. Small. At the very center of her. Where the lightning lived. Where a storm-drake's storm comes from. As if someone had reached into her and pinched out a candle.

*[The grey drinks storm*,] said the Murmur. *[It drinks everything. Color. Warmth. Lightning. Names, if you stay too long.]* A pause. *[It's very old and very thirsty. It isn't wicked. It's just thin, and it wants to be full.]*

Antho kept his hand on Ashka's nose and pushed.

Not with Shade. With Gale. The second of his Wells, the one he'd only reached Answering in, the one that had come to him in the black hall at Duskhold the night Garrick died. Wind. Moving air. The breath of the world. He didn't know how to give her lightning. But he knew how to give her *air*. And air was where storms came from.

He pushed it into her. A long steady breath of cold wind out of his own chest and into hers, through his palm, through her scales, into the small hole at the center of her where the lightning had been pinched out.

And something sparked.

Faint. Tiny. A single blue-white thread of lightning, crawling up the side of her nose. Then another. She lifted her head. She sneezed. A great crackling sneeze that shook grey leaves off every tree in the clearing and singed the grey grass black in a fan-shape in front of her.

The black grass stayed black. Real black. Real color, in the middle of the grey.

Ashka looked at it. Then at him.

And made a sound he'd never heard her make before. Small, and hoarse, and a little embarrassed.

"You're welcome," said Antho.

He sat down against her side, in the circle of the Lantern's light, with the grey wood all around them. He felt very tired. Gale had taken more out of him than he'd expected. And he could feel the grey pressing against the edge of the Lantern's light, very patient, very thirsty, like water against a dam.

*Thirty-one percent*, he thought. *And it gets thinner the further west we go.*

---

The thinned came in the night.

He heard them before he saw them. Or rather, he heard where they weren't. Shade was very loud here, in the thin places. The silence of the grey wood was full of shapes, and some of the shapes were moving. Coming through the trees toward the clearing. Slow. Careful. A lot of them.

He stood up with the Lantern in one hand and his staff in the other. The staff of black wind-twisted pine he'd cut on the road out of the mountains, the first week he'd travelled with his brother, when he was still too proud to lean on people.

They came out of the trees.

Deer, at first. A herd of them. Or what had been deer. They were grey, like everything else, and *thin*. Not thin like starving. Thin like paper. He could see the trees behind them through their bodies. Their eyes were empty. Their hooves made no sound on the grey grass.

And they were *hungry*. He could feel it. Not for meat. For the Lantern. For its gold light. For the black scorch-mark on the grass. For Ashka's newly sparking scales. For Antho. For anything with color in it.

*[Thinned,]* said the Murmur. *[Things that came too close to the river. That stayed too long. The grey drank them down to almost nothing, and now they want it back. Anything they can get.]* A pause. *[They're not wicked either. They're just very, very empty.]*

Behind the deer came other things. A fox. A bear, huge and slow and grey as smoke. A flock of birds, flapping silently among the branches. And at the back of them, something that had been a man.

Antho's chest went tight.

It was a man. Or had been. Tall and thin and grey. Wearing the faint ghost-shape of a traveler's cloak. Holding the faint ghost-shape of a walking-stick. His face was blurred, rubbed out, a smudge where the features should have been.

And he was walking toward the Lantern with his grey hands held out. Like a man coming in from the cold toward a fire.

"*Please*," he whispered. It was barely a sound. Wind through dry grass. "*Please. Just a little. Just a little warm.*"

---

Antho didn't attack.

He thought about it. He had the power. Void Bolt would have gone through the thinned like a stone through paper. Shadowbind would have held them. He could have cleared the clearing in a few heartbeats.

But they weren't wicked. The Murmur was right. They were just empty. Hungry. Cold. Things that had come too close to something too big for them and been drunk down to nothing. And Antho had been something like that, once. For eight years. In a cave, in the dark, with the Hollow drinking him.

He looked at the grey man with the rubbed-out face.

*He came here looking for something*, thought Antho. *Like me.*

He set the Lantern down on the grass.

And opened its shutter.

Not all the way. Just a little. The gold light spilled out, wider, warmer, a circle the size of a cottage, and the thinned deer at the edge of it flinched and stopped. The grey man stopped too. He stood at the very edge of the light with his hands held out, trembling.

"Come and sit," said Antho quietly. "All of you. You can't have it. But you can sit by it."

The grey man didn't move.

Then, very slowly, he took one step forward. Into the light.

And didn't burn. Didn't flee. He stood in the gold light of the Keeper's Lantern with his grey hands held out, and Antho saw the light go *into* him. Not much. A little color. A faint flush on the smudge of his face. A faint brown in his cloak.

The man sank down onto the grass. Slowly. Folding up like a tired old man at the end of a long road. And sat there, at the edge of the light, with his empty face turned to the flame.

"*Thank you*," he whispered.

One by one, the others came. The deer. The fox. The great grey bear, lumbering into the light and lying down with a long silent sigh. The birds settled on the branches overhead. They didn't come close. They didn't try to take. They just sat, at the edge of the circle of gold, in the grey wood, and warmed themselves at a dead woman's light that her son had carried across the Teeth.

Ashka watched them with her head on her forelegs and lightning crawling faintly along her nose. She didn't growl. Antho didn't know why. Maybe she understood.

He sat down against her side again with his staff across his knees. And kept the shutter open all night. It cost him. The Lantern drew on his mana to burn this wide, a steady slow drain, like a cut that won't stop bleeding. By dawn he was grey himself with tiredness, and his hands were shaking.

But in the morning, when the pale flat light came up over the grey wood, the thinned were still there. All of them. Asleep, or something like asleep, curled up in the grass at the edge of the light.

And the grey man had a face.

Not much of one. Faint. Like a portrait in very weak watercolor. But there. An old man's face, lined and gentle, with a white beard. Asleep. Smiling.

Antho read it twice. He didn't feel anything about it. He was too tired to feel anything.

He shut the Lantern down to its usual small circle. He climbed onto Ashka's neck. And as they rose up out of the grey wood, heading west, he looked down and saw the old man with the faint white beard lift his head from the grass and watch them go.

And raise one faint, grey, slowly coloring hand. And wave.

---

They found the river on the fifth day.

He'd been expecting a river. The voice through the door had said so. *The grey river*. He'd imagined something like the Ashbourne millstream, or the wide slow Silverrun at Highcrest. Water. Banks. A river.

It wasn't.

It was a mist. A long wide slow-moving river of grey mist, a mile across, flowing from somewhere far to the north to somewhere far to the south, through a valley so wide and flat and grey that Antho couldn't see its edges. It moved like water. It rippled like water. When the wind blew across it, it made small grey waves that broke soundlessly on the grey shingle of the shore.

But it wasn't water. He could see that, the moment they came down on its bank. It was *thinness*. The world, worn so thin that it had stopped being solid and had become something else. Something in between. Something that flowed.

And on the far side of it, a mile off across the grey, there was another shore.

He could just see it. A dark line on the horizon. Darker than grey. Not black, exactly. More like the color of the back of his own eyelids at night. And above it, a sky that was the same color. Deep. Endless. Quiet.

The Hollow.

He knew it the way he knew his own heartbeat. He'd had it inside him for eight years. He'd held its door shut with his own mind. He'd heard it whispering every night of his life since he was fifteen. And there it was. A mile away. Across a river of grey mist.

*[Home*,] whispered the Murmur. Very softly. Almost reverent. *[Oh. Oh, I'd forgotten.]*

He held the Lantern up, and the Murmur went quiet.

There was a boat on the shore.

He hadn't seen it at first. It was grey, like everything else, and long and flat and low, like the punts the eel-fishers used in the Tidewater marshes. It was drawn up on the shingle a little way downstream. And standing beside it, leaning on a long grey pole, was the Ferryman.

He was tall. Very tall. Taller than the Barrow-King had been, taller than anyone Antho had ever seen. Thin as a heron. He wore a long robe of grey cloth, and a wide-brimmed grey hat pulled low over his face, and from the top of his long pole there hung a lantern.

The lantern was unlit.

Antho walked down the shingle toward him. His boots made no sound on the stones. Behind him, Ashka followed, her great head low, her nostrils flaring. She wasn't afraid. He could feel that through his hand on her neck. She was something else. Eager. Straining. Every line of her great body pointing across the river, toward the far dark shore.

The Ferryman turned his head as they approached.

Under the brim of the grey hat, there was a face. Long and narrow and grey, like weathered driftwood. And no eyes. Just smooth grey skin where eyes should have been, as if they'd been worn away by a thousand years of wind.

"*Anthoren Vey*," said the Ferryman.

His voice was like rain on a slate roof. Soft. Steady. Very old.

"You know my name."

"*I know every name that comes to my river. That is what I am for.*" The eyeless face turned toward Ashka. "*And the storm-child. Daughter of the old storm. She has been calling, has she not? Across the water. I have heard her. Every night for a month.*" A pause. "*Her mother hears her too.*"

Ashka made a sound. Low and urgent and pleading.

"Her mother's alive?" said Antho.

"*Her mother is in the Drowned Country. Across the river, and down. Under the water that is not water.*" The rain-voice didn't change. "*The Hollow has been keeping her there for eleven years, as your world counts them. Far longer, as she has felt them. Time is thin at the edge. Drinking her lightning. A little at a time. A storm-mother has a great deal of lightning in her. She has lasted very long.*"

"Then take us across."

The eyeless face turned back to him.

"*There is a price*," said the Ferryman.

---

Antho had known there would be. The voice through the door hadn't said so, but there was always a price. That was the first thing the Hollow had taught him, at fifteen, on the woodpile. Nothing comes free. Not out of the dark.

"Name it."

"*I take memories*," said the Ferryman. "*That is my fee. Every crossing. One memory, from each who crosses.*" He tilted his grey head. "*Not just any memory. That would be too easy. I take the one you would least wish to lose.*"

Antho went still.

"*The best thing*," said the Ferryman softly. "*The brightest. The one you carry closest. The one you take out in the dark, when the voices are loud, and turn over in your hands like a warm stone.*" The eyeless face was turned full toward him. "*I know which one it is. I can see it. It is very bright.*"

Antho didn't say anything.

"*A riverbank*," said the Ferryman. "*A summer evening. A small boy with a flat grey stone in his hand. You, fifteen, showing him how to throw it. Low, low, like you are trying to make it fly under a door.*" His voice was very gentle. "*And it skips. And you count. You always count. And it skips five times.*"

Antho's hand had closed on his staff so hard his knuckles hurt.

"*You told him four*," said the Ferryman. "*And then, years later, under the stars, you told him the truth. And he laughed. And it was the best night of your life.*" A pause. "*That is the price. For you and the storm-child both. One crossing there. One back. That memory, and no other.*"

---

He couldn't speak.

He stood on the grey shingle of the river between the world and the Hollow, with his dragon straining at his side and the Lantern in his hand, and he couldn't speak.

*Five times*.

He remembered it. All of it. The millpond below the Ashbourne mill. The willows. The low gold evening light. Lex at ten, skinny and freckled and furious at himself, throwing stone after stone after stone into the water with a great *plunk*. And Antho kneeling beside him in the reeds, guiding his small wrist. *Low. Flatter. Like it's trying to fly under a door.* And the stone leaving Lex's hand, spinning, and touching the water, and lifting, and touching, and lifting.

*One. Two. Three. Four. Five*.

And Lex's face.

And then, years later, in the dark, on the Old Founders' Road, the two of them lying on their backs under the stars. *It skipped five times. I counted. I always count. I told you four.* And Lex, beside him, laughing. Helpless. Laughing so hard he couldn't breathe. *You absolute—you* liar. *Nine years, Antho!*

That was it. That was the warm stone. That was the thing he took out in the dark when the four percent got loud. Not the battles. Not the breach closing, or Duskhold, or Morvane falling. Not the Queen's crown or the eleven thousand people cheering.

A stone that skipped five times. And his little brother laughing under the stars.

*[Pay it*,] whispered the Murmur. *[What's one memory? You've lost so many. You've lost years. What's one more?]*

He looked at the Lantern. The Murmur went silent.

But it wasn't wrong. That was the trouble. He *had* lost years. Eight years in the Hollowmaw that he barely remembered, gone grey and blurred like the thinned. His childhood, half of it buried under the Murmur. His mother's face: he could see the shape of it, but not the details anymore. Not her eyes. Not her smile.

He'd lost so much. He had so little left that was bright.

And this was the brightest.

"*No*," he said.

The Ferryman didn't move.

"Not that one. Take another. Take—" He stopped. Swallowed. "Take Duskhold. Take the breach closing. Take anything."

"*I take the one you would least wish to lose. That is the price. It is not mine to change.*"

"Then I won't pay it."

"*Then you will not cross.*"

---

Ashka screamed.

Antho had never heard her scream. Not when he found her, wet and furious and tiny in a nest of frozen rock. Not when her wing broke. Not at Duskhold, with Sorrowmaw's jaws closing on her. She'd roared, and growled, and thundered. Never screamed.

She screamed now. Long and terrible and grief-stricken, a sound like a storm tearing itself in half. And she threw herself at the river.

"*ASHKA—*"

He grabbed for her. Too late. She went past him in a great rush of grey scales and crackling wings, down the shingle, and into the grey mist. Not flying. Wading. Plunging into the river of thinness up to her chest, her great wings beating at it, pushing toward the far dark shore.

And the river *took* her.

He saw it. Saw the grey mist close around her chest and her shoulders. Saw the color drain out of her, all at once, the storm-blue of her scales fading to grey in the space of a heartbeat. Saw the lightning crawling along her sides gutter and go out. Saw her great silver eyes go flat and dull.

She stopped. In the middle of the grey, ten paces out from the shore. And stood there. Her wings slowly folding. Her head slowly lowering. As if she'd forgotten where she was going.

As if she'd forgotten why.

"*She did not pay*," said the Ferryman, behind him. Very quietly. "*The river takes what it is owed. One way or another.*"

Antho was already running.

---

The mist was cold.

Not cold like ice. Cold like forgetting. It came up over his boots and his knees and his waist, and everywhere it touched, he felt himself fading. Not his body. *Him*. His name. His face. The shape of himself, going soft at the edges, like the man with the rubbed-out face in the grey wood.

He held the Lantern up over his head. High. In both hands. The small gold flame burned against the grey.

*Anthoren Vey*, he thought. *Anthoren Vey. Twenty-seven. Twilight Warlock. Brother of Lex. Son of Seren. I count the lanterns. One thousand one hundred and eleven.*

The grey pressed in around the light. Thirsty. Patient. Very old.

*One thousand one hundred and eleven*, he thought, and kept walking.

He reached Ashka. She was standing chest-deep in the mist with her head drooping almost into it. Grey as stone. Her silver eyes were open, but they didn't see him.

He put his hand on her neck.

He couldn't feel her. Not with Shade. Not the shape of her, the familiar dragon-shaped silence he knew better than his own. There was just *grey*. Grey inside her. Grey where the storm had been.

"Ashka," he said.

Nothing.

"*Ashka*." Louder. He pressed his hand flat against her scales. "Ashka, it's me. It's Antho. Come on. Come back."

Nothing. Grey.

*She's gone*, said the four percent. *The river's got her. You're next.*

He felt it. Felt it coming up through his own legs, into his chest. His name going soft. *Anthoren. Anthoren V—*

He did the only thing he could think of.

He sang.

Not well. He'd never sung well. His voice was rough and low and had cracked on every high note since he was thirteen. He stood waist-deep in the river between the world and the Hollow, with the Lantern held over his head and his hand on his dragon's grey neck, and sang the song she'd hatched to.

Not the lantern song. The other one. The one he'd sung to a newly hatched drake in a nest of frozen rock, the first night, because it was the only thing he could think of to keep her warm. An old Greyspine herding-song. Nonsense words about a storm coming over the hill and a shepherd bringing the flock home.

*Oh the storm is coming, the storm is near,*
*bring them home, bring them home,*
*there's thunder on the mountain, but I'm right here,*
*bring them home, my love, bring them home.*

He sang it badly. He sang it with his voice cracking and the grey eating at his edges. He sang it the way he'd sung it that first winter, over and over, night after night, until the tiny wet furious thing inside his coat stopped shivering and bit him on the thumb.

*Bring them home, my love, bring them home*.

And under his hand, something sparked.

---

It was tiny. The size of a seed. A single blue-white spark, deep in the grey of her neck, under his palm.

He sang louder.

It spread. A thread of lightning, crawling up through the grey of her. Another. Her scales flickering. Blue. Grey. Blue again. Her great head lifting. Slowly. Her silver eyes—

They found him.

She looked at him. Ashka. His dragon. Out of a grey face, with lightning crawling back up her nose, and her eyes coming back silver.

And she made a sound. Small. Bewildered. The sound she'd made that first night in the rocks, wet and furious and new. The sound she'd made the first time she ever saw him.

"*There you are*," he said. His voice cracked again. "*There you are.*"

He turned her. It was like turning a ship. She was huge and slow and grey and confused, and he had to push her with his whole weight, his hand flat on her neck, singing all the while, step by step back through the grey toward the shore.

Ten paces. Five. The shingle under his boots.

They came out of the river onto the grey stones of the bank and both fell down.

---

He lay on the shingle for a long time.

Ashka lay beside him. He could feel her breathing. Slow and heavy. Her lightning was back, crawling faintly along her flanks. Her eyes were silver. She'd put her great head down beside his, so close her breath stirred his hair.

But something was wrong. He could feel it. In the dragon-shaped silence of her, where he listened with Shade. There was a hole in it. Small. At the very center.

"*The river takes what it is owed*," said the Ferryman.

He was standing over them, leaning on his long pole. The unlit lantern swaying gently.

"*She waded in without paying. So it took its fee.*" The eyeless face turned toward Ashka. "*It took the thing she would least wish to lose. The brightest thing.*"

Antho sat up. Slowly. "What did it take?"

"*Her mother's voice.*"

He went cold.

"*She has heard it every night for a month. Across the water. Calling her home. She has followed it across the Teeth, across the grey, all the way to my shore.*" The rain-voice was very gentle. "*And now she cannot remember what it sounded like. She knows she was looking for something. She does not remember what.*"

Antho looked at Ashka.

She was lying on the shingle with her head beside his. She lifted it, slowly, and looked out across the grey river. At the far dark shore. And he saw her frown, in the way dragons frown, a faint creasing of the scales above her eyes. Puzzled. As if she'd come into a room for something and couldn't remember what.

She looked at the far shore for a long time.

Then she put her head back down beside his, and closed her eyes.

She didn't keen. She didn't make that low lonely searching sound. She just lay there, quietly, beside him, the way she had when she was small.

As if there were nothing on the other side of the river at all.

---

He sat on the shore of the grey river until dark.

He didn't know what else to do. He sat on the shingle with his staff across his knees and the Lantern beside him and his dragon asleep at his back, and looked across the grey at the far dark shore, and thought.

*She paid it for me. She couldn't pay, so the river took it from her. Because I wouldn't.*

*Because I wouldn't give up a stone that skipped five times.*

The Ferryman hadn't left. He stood a little way off, beside his long grey boat, leaning on his pole. Waiting. Patient as the river.

"Can she get it back?" said Antho at last.

"*If she crosses. If she finds her mother. If she hears the voice again.*" The Ferryman didn't turn. "*What the river takes, it takes. But a voice can be heard twice.*"

"And if I pay. For both of us. Can she cross?"

"*Yes.*"

Antho closed his eyes.

"*You do not have to decide tonight*," said the Ferryman. "*The river is very patient. It has been here since before the world was made. It will be here when the world is gone.*" A pause. "*The price will not go down. But the time to pay it may run out.*"

"What does that mean?"

The eyeless face turned toward him.

"*It means that something is coming*," said the Ferryman softly. "*From the east. Through the world. Cutting as it comes. I can feel it in the river. Every thread it cuts falls into my water.*" He was silent for a long moment. "*Forty-eight. Forty-nine. Fifty.*"

Antho's head came up.

"*When it has what it seeks*," said the Ferryman, "*it will come for what lies under your lanterns. And then you will need the old storm. You will need her very much.*"

---

Much later, in the dark, with the river flowing grey and silent beside him and Ashka asleep at his back, Antho said:

"Have you ever taken anyone else of my blood across?"

The Ferryman was quiet for so long that Antho thought he wasn't going to answer.

Then:

"*Once.*"

Antho didn't move.

"*A woman*," said the rain-voice. "*Long ago. Not so long, for me. Long for you.*" A pause. "*She came down to my shore at night, with a lamp in her hand. A small clay lamp. Burning. She asked me to take her across.*"

Antho's heart had stopped.

"*She paid*," said the Ferryman. "*More than you can imagine. More than anyone has ever paid me.*" His eyeless face was turned toward the far dark shore. "*I will not tell you what. That was hers, and she paid it freely, and it is not mine to give away.*"

"*When*?" Antho's voice came out of him in pieces. "*When was this?*"

But the Ferryman only turned away, and leaned on his long grey pole, and looked across the river. And the unlit lantern on the top of it swung gently in the wind that came off the water. And he didn't say anything else.

Antho sat on the grey shore with his hands over his face.

*A small clay lamp*.

*Burning.*

And far, far away to the south, through the Bloodbond, so faint it was barely a whisper, he felt his little brother. Walking. Steady. Determined. Heading down into a country of sun and glass.

And behind him, a long way behind, but following, something cold.

*Lex*, he thought. *Lex. Turn around.*

But the Bloodbond was thin here, as thin as everything else, and he didn't know if his brother heard.
