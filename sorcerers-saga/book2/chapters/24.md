@@PART Part Two: Echoes of the Past
# Echoes of the Past

Fen Hollow was the first.

It was a small place. Smaller than Ashbourne. Thirty houses of grey stone and green turf, crouched in a fold of the land past the Blightmarch, where the moors gave way to marsh and the sky went on forever. There was a mill on a slow brown stream. A church with no spire. A green with a well. And on the green, a Lattice Stone.

Old. Older than the village. Grey, and pitted, and leaning a little to one side, the way old Stones did in the west, where the Order had never cared enough to straighten them. Its silver was faint. Lex could feel its threads from the edge of the village. Four hundred souls. Every one of them capped.

It was spring. Three months after the longest night. The marsh was full of frogs and the hedges were full of blackthorn and the air smelled of mud and new grass. And on the green at Fen Hollow, in the thin bright morning, the whole village had come out to see why a smith from Ashbourne had walked a hundred miles to stand beside their Stone.

He didn't stand there alone.

Ilse stood on his left. Ivo on his right. In their patched coats, green and brown, with their mouse-brown hair brushed for once and their faces very pale.

And Wren stood a little behind, with her bow on her back. Not holding his hand. Just there.

---

"*I don't know what to say*," Ilse had said, the night before. In the inn at the edge of the marsh, sitting on the end of the bed with Dorran's book in her lap. "*I don't know what to say to them.*"

"Say what he wrote," said Lex.

She'd looked down at the book. At the inside of the back cover. At the big clumsy careful hand.

"*He never said it out loud*," she said. "*Not once. In all the years. He just lifted things.*"

"Then you say it for him."

---

She said it.

Standing on the green at Fen Hollow, beside the old grey Stone, with four hundred people looking at her. A thief from Kessick. Twenty-four. Who had been capped at fourteen by a big man with a brand on his arm, and asked him *why did you put the light out*.

She held up the book.

"*There was a man*," said Ilse Marrow, "*who used to come here. A long time ago. Every spring, and every autumn. A Warden of the Kindling.*"

Some of the older people in the crowd went still.

"*A big man*," said Ilse. "*Bigger than anyone. With a shaved head and a broken nose. He stood beside this Stone, every Kindling-day, for twenty-two years. And when your children came up and put their hands on it, he put his hand on their shoulders. And pushed down.*"

The crowd was silent.

"*He wrote down every name*," said Ilse. Her voice cracked. She steadied it. "*Every child. Every Ceiling. Four thousand one hundred and nine of them. Twelve villages. This was the first.*" She opened the book. "*Aldo Cray. Bess Rook. Wat Lister.*"

A sound from the crowd. An old woman, near the front, with her hand over her mouth. A man beside her, grey-haired, staring.

"*He died*," said Ilse. "*In the winter. At the bottom of the world. Holding up a mountain, so we could get past.*" She swallowed. "*And before he died, he asked us to come here. Me and my brother. Because he capped us too. In Kessick. We were the first ones he ever felt go dim.*"

She turned to the back cover. And read it. In a clear, shaking voice, to the whole of Fen Hollow.

"*When they light up, tell them I'm sorry. Tell them the big man who put the lid on said he was sorry, and that he spent the rest of his life lifting things.*"

She closed the book.

Nobody said anything for a long time.

Then the old woman at the front came forward. Slowly. Leaning on a stick. She stopped in front of Ilse and looked at her for a long moment, with her faded eyes very bright.

"*Bess Rook*," she said. "*That's me. Spring Kindling. Ceiling twelve.*" She was quiet. "*I remember him. He had very gentle hands. For such a big man.*" She looked at the Stone. "*I always wondered why he looked so sad.*"

---

Lex put his hands on the Stone.

He'd done it two hundred and sixty times in Caldor. Once in Saffaran, the hardest of all. He knew the shape of it now the way he knew the shape of a hammer-swing. Reach in. Find the Ceilings. Lift.

He didn't need to ask this one. It was old and tired and it had been holding these lids down for nine hundred years, and when he reached into it, it practically *sighed*.

But he asked anyway. Because he always asked now. Since the Glass Desert. Since the Old Man of the Ironbacks. *Please*.

And lifted.

The light came up off the Stone like the sun coming up over the marsh. Silver and gold, pouring out through every thread in Fen Hollow. Four hundred threads, coming loose all at once. Lex felt them go. Felt the whole village take a breath.

And Ilse and Ivo stepped forward. The way Dorran had told them. *Stand beside the Stone. You were the first ones I ever felt go dim. You should be the first ones there when they light up.*

They put their hands on people's shoulders.

Bess Rook first. The old woman with the stick. Ilse put her hand on her shoulder, and Bess's eyes went wide, and her whole face lit up silver from inside, and she laughed. A cracked old woman's laugh, startled and delighted. Then a man. Then a girl of fifteen. Then a boy. One by one. As they came up to the Stone. Ilse on one side, Ivo on the other, with their hands on shoulders, the way a big man had once stood there with his.

Opening the lids.

Lex stood back and watched them. Wren came and stood beside him. She didn't take his hand. She just leaned against his arm.

"*He'd have hated this*," she said quietly. "*All these people. Crying. Thanking them.*"

"He'd have hated it," Lex agreed. "He'd have gone and hidden in the forge and hit something too hard."

Wren laughed. And wiped her eyes.

"*Eleven more*," she said.

"Eleven more."

---

But something was wrong with the Stone.

He noticed it at the end. When the light had faded and the village had gone to the church to ring the bell, and the twins were sitting on the edge of the well with Bess Rook between them, holding a hand each. Lex had stayed at the Stone. He had his palm on it still. Feeling the threads settle. The way you leave your hand on a horse's neck after a hard ride.

And he felt it.

A thread. Running *down*.

Not out. Every other thread in the Stone ran out, to a person, to a soul, to the four hundred and eleven people of Fen Hollow. This one ran down. Into the ground. Into the bedrock under the Stone. Thin and grey and very faint, so faint he'd never noticed it before. Not here. Not in two hundred and sixty-one other Stones.

*Ember*, he thought. *What's that?*

A long silence.

*[I don't know*,] said Ember slowly. *[I've never seen it. Lex, I've never* seen *it. And I've been in the Root with you for four years.]*

*It's always been there. In every Stone. I can feel it now I'm looking. It's like*—he searched for it—*like your own heartbeat. You never notice it until you stop and listen.*

He followed it. Gently. With the Root. A little way down.

It went a long way. Through the bedrock. Through the deep. Down and down. Thin and grey and steady. Carrying something. A trickle. A thin steady trickle of silver. Mana. From the Stone. From every thread in the Stone. A little from each. Running down.

*[A tenth*,] said Ember suddenly. *[Lex. It's taking a tenth. From every soul. Look. Every thread that runs out, there's a little bit of it running back, and down.]*

Lex pulled back. Fast.

He stood on the green at Fen Hollow with his hand on the old grey Stone and his heart hammering.

Because at the very bottom of the thin grey thread, a long, long way down, as far as he'd let himself follow it, he'd felt something.

Warmth.

Gold.

The faint, faint sound of breathing.

---

They did the twelve villages in a month.

Fen Hollow. Brackwater. Thornby, where Garret Shale's mother lived, and opened the door to find her son, unwritten and rewritten, sitting at her kitchen table eating porridge. Lex hadn't known. Ilse cried for an hour. Morrow's End. Saltmere. Little Ashby. All of them. Small grey places in the far west, where the Order had never cared and nobody had ever come.

Every time, Ilse read the back cover of the book.

Every time, someone in the crowd remembered him. *He had very gentle hands*. *He always looked so sad*. *He gave me a sweet, after, when I cried*. *He carried my little brother home on his shoulders because his legs had gone weak*.

Every time, Lex felt the grey thread running down.

He didn't say anything. Not to the twins. Not to Wren. He just felt it, every time, under every Stone. And every time, it was there. A tenth. Running down. Into the dark.

*[You have to tell someone*,] said Ember, on the last night. In the inn at Little Ashby. *[Lex.]*

*I know*.

*[Your father*.]

*I know*.

*[He has a lot to tell you too.]*

Lex lay on his back on the narrow inn bed with Wren asleep against his side and looked at the ceiling.

*I know*, he thought. *That's why I haven't gone yet.*

---

He went to the Hollowmaw at the end of the month. With Wren, on Ashka, who'd come to find them at Little Ashby with her silver eyes bright and lightning crawling along her flanks and a goat in her mouth (not Correspondence; a stranger's goat, which she'd stolen, and which Lex had to pay for).

Antho was waiting at the mouth of the cave.

He'd been there all winter. With Seraphine. With the students. With their father. He looked better than Lex had seen him in years. Not thinner. Not grey. His face had color in it. He'd cut his hair. He was wearing a new coat, dark blue, that Seraphine had obviously chosen.

"*You look*," said Lex, sliding off Ashka, "*almost respectable.*"

"*Seraphine made me*." Antho looked down at the coat. "*She said if I was going to teach nine children I should at least look like someone who owned a mirror.*"

"*Does she know you don't own a mirror*?"

"*She bought me one*." Antho's mouth twitched. "*I put it in the cold-room. Teo uses it to look at the fish.*"

And then, without warning, his brother pulled him into a hug. Hard. Brief. And let go.

"*Tell me about the stone*," said Antho.

Lex told him. Standing on the slope below the Hollowmaw in the spring sunshine, with the valley green below them and Ashka chasing a goat and Wren sitting on a rock grinning at them both. He told it well this time. He'd had a month of practise, in inns at the edge of marshes, telling it to the ceiling.

Antho listened. The way he always did.

"*Five*," he said, at the end.

"Five."

"*I saw it*," said Antho. "*From the water. At the bottom of the sea.*" He looked out at the valley. "*I didn't remember it. But I saw it.*" He was quiet. "*Every time you tell it, it gets a bit more like mine.*"

---

Their father was at the door.

He'd been there all winter too. Lex knew that. Antho had written. *He doesn't leave. He sleeps in front of it. On a blanket. The students bring him food. Pim sits with him every night.* And then, in a later letter: *The lamp doesn't lean anymore. Not since the longest night. She's gone, Lex. From the other side. Father doesn't know where.*

Arion was sitting on the black floor of the bell-shaped cavern with his back against the wall beside the door, and a blanket over his knees, and a book open on the blanket that he wasn't reading. The little clay lamp burned in its niche. Straight up. Steady.

He looked up when his sons came down the last of the passage. And his lean unchanged face did the thing it almost never did.

"*Lex*," he said. And then, quieter: "*Thank you. For bringing them back. The nine points. Antho gave them to me.*" He touched his chest. "*I'd forgotten what it felt like. To have them.*"

"Elric gave them back," said Lex. "Not me."

"*I know*." His father looked at the lamp. "*Antho told me. All of it. What he said at the end.*" He was quiet for a long moment. "*Tell him I went first. Tell him he was right. I wasn't ready.*" His voice dropped. "*I am now.*"

"*Old friend*," said Lex. "He said, tell him, old friend, I'm ready now."

Arion closed his eyes.

They stood there for a while. The three of them. In the gold light.

Then Arion opened his eyes, and folded the blanket, and closed the book. And looked at both his sons.

"*Sit down*," he said. "*I promised you something. Before you went to the Rift.*" He took a breath. "*I'm going to keep it.*"

---

He told them everything.

The Root. The way it had stopped him, at thirty-one, like a clock. The portraits in the secret room in the Spire: Wren had told Lex about them on the road north, after the Rift, quietly, as if she'd been holding it like a breath. *Arion Vey the scholar. Arion Vey the Warden. Arion Vey's son. Arion Vey's grandson.* The two hundred years of moving, and changing his name, and watching everyone he knew grow old and die.

"*Two hundred and thirty-one*," said Arion. "*Two hundred and thirty-two now. It was midwinter. The longest night.*" A faint, tired smile. "*I spent my birthday holding a door with nine children and a lamp. It was the best birthday I've had in a century.*"

Antho didn't say anything. He was sitting very still, with his hands folded in his lap and his grey eyes on their father's face.

Lex said: "*Aurelian told me*. At the Rift. He said he asked you. Two hundred years ago. Through the Root. Every night. To come down. To let him in."

His father went very still.

"*Yes*," he said at last.

"*What happened?*"

Arion was quiet for a long time. When he spoke, his voice was very low.

"*I was young*," he said. "*Thirty-five. Thirty-six. I'd had the Root for five years. I was the strongest sorcerer in Caldor, and I was frightened all the time, and I didn't know why.*" He looked at his hands. "*And I started hearing a voice. At night. When I was tired. In the deep gold, at the bottom of the Root.*"

"*Kind*," said Antho quietly.

"*Kind*." Arion nodded. "*So kind. It knew everything about me. Every fear. Every doubt. It said: you're carrying too much, Arion. Let me help. Let me carry it. Come down to me, and I'll take the weight, and you'll never be afraid again.*" He was silent. "*It said it was Aurelian. Our ancestor. The Root of the world. It said it was buried, and trapped, and lonely, and that if I came down and let it in, together we'd make the world safe forever.*"

"*And you said no*."

"*I said no*." Arion's mouth twisted. "*Not because I was wise. Not because I saw through it. Because I was* stubborn. *I've always been stubborn. My father said I'd say no to the sun if it asked me to get up in the morning.*" He shook his head slowly. "*It asked me every night for a year. And every night I said no. Not because I thought it was lying. Because I didn't want to be told what to do.*"

Lex almost laughed. He didn't.

"*And then it stopped asking*," said Arion. "*And I thought I'd won.*" He looked at the lamp. "*I hadn't. It had only decided to wait. For someone younger. Someone less stubborn. Someone who'd open the door himself.*" His eyes came to Lex. "*I sealed you. Both of you. When you were small. The class-masks. The Smith. The Hollow-touched. I told myself it was to hide you from the Order. And it was. But it was also to hide you from* that. *From the thing at the bottom of the gold. I thought, if they don't know they're Veys, it can't find them.*"

"*It found us anyway*," said Antho.

"*It found you anyway*." Arion's voice cracked. "*Through the breach. Through the Hollow. Through Elric. Through the Key.*" He bowed his head. "*Everything I did to hide you, it just went round.*"

---

Nobody said anything for a long time.

The lamp burned in its niche. The lanterns burned on the walls. Somewhere far up the passage, Lex could hear the students in the hall, Odile's sharp voice and Teo's laugh.

"*Mam's gone*," said Antho at last. Very quietly. "*From the door.*"

"*Yes*."

"*Where?*"

"*I don't know*." Arion's hands were clenched in his lap. "*The night after the longest night, the flame leaned. Once. And then never again.*" His voice was barely a whisper. "*I felt her go. Through the Root. Away from the door. Down. Into the deep.*" He looked up. "*I don't know why. I don't know where. She didn't tell me. She couldn't. The door sealed tighter when the Lockward went in. Too tight for a voice.*"

Lex looked at the lamp.

*She's been holding that door for eleven years*, he thought. *And the night it finally holds itself, she walks away from it. Into the dark.*

*Why?*

*[Because something needed her more*,] said Ember quietly. *[That's the only reason anyone in your family ever walks into the dark.]*

---

He told them about the grey thread.

He hadn't meant to. Not yet. Not today, with his father still raw from confession and Antho sitting so still. But it came out anyway. The way things came out of him. He'd never been able to hold anything in.

The Stone at Fen Hollow. The thread running down. A tenth. From every soul. Into the dark. And at the bottom, very far down, warmth. Gold. Breathing.

He watched his father's face while he told it.

And saw it go white.

"*The Tithe*," said Arion. Barely audible.

Lex stared at him. "You know about it?"

"*I've heard the word*." His father's voice was strange. "*Once. A long time ago. In a Founder's codex in the Spire's sealed vaults. A single line. In a margin.*" He swallowed. "*The tithe for the binding. A tenth of every thread, to keep the lid strong.*" He looked at Lex. "*I thought it meant something else. I thought it was a metaphor. I thought—*"

He stopped.

"*I thought it was for the binding*," said Arion Vey. "*That's what it said. To keep the lid strong.*"

"*And if it isn't*?" said Antho.

Their father didn't answer.

He didn't need to. Lex saw it on his face. The same thing he'd seen on Elric's face at the bottom of the Rift, when the kind voice turned its back and said *you'll be nothing at all*.

The face of a man realizing that something he'd believed his whole life had been a lie.

---

He dreamed that night.

He was sleeping in the hall of the Lantern Keep, on a pallet by the fire, with Wren curled against his back. He'd been tired. He'd been so tired, for months.

And he dreamed of a forge.

Not Ashbourne. Not the Hollowmaw. A great round forge on top of a white mountain, open to the sky. Stars overhead, so close and bright he could almost hear them. An anvil of gold. And a man at the anvil.

Tall. Fair-haired. In a smith's leather apron over a robe of white and gold, with his sleeves rolled up and his forearms scarred. Working a bar of iron. Light and fast and precise. *Tink. Tink. Tink*.

And singing.

Not well. His voice was rough, and he kept losing the tune. An old song. A working song, the kind smiths sang at the anvil to keep the rhythm. Lex knew it. Brannoc sang it. Badly.

The man looked up.

His eyes were gold. Root gold. Lex's gold.

But not like at the Rift. Not bright and warm and loving. These eyes were *tired*. Red-rimmed, as if he hadn't slept in a very long time. Sad. And when they found Lex, standing at the edge of the forge in his dream, they didn't light up.

They *flinched*.

"*Oh*," said the man at the anvil. "*Oh, no. Not you. Not one of mine.*"

---

Lex didn't move.

"*You're the one it's been talking to*," said the man. He'd set the hammer down. His scarred hands were shaking. "*The smith. My blood. I've felt it. Down here. Through the gold. Talking to you in my voice.*" His face twisted. "*Wearing my face.*"

"*Who are you*?" said Lex.

The man laughed. A short, broken sound.

"*I'm what's left*," he said. "*Of Aurelian Vey. Of the man who made the net. Who sang at this anvil, a long time ago, before.*" He looked down at his hands. "*It got into me. Eight hundred years ago. I went down to speak to it. To understand it. I thought—*" He stopped. "*It doesn't matter what I thought. I was wrong. I was so sure, and so wrong.*"

*So sure, and so wrong*. The Hearth had said that. In the Wellworld of Flame.

"*It wears me now*," said the man. "*Like a coat. And I'm—underneath. Buried. In my own tomb. At the Heart. Where the first thread goes down.*" His gold eyes found Lex's. "*It's eating me. A little at a time. For eight hundred years. There's not much left.*"

"*Why are you telling me this*?"

The man looked at him for a long time.

"*Because you're going to come*," he said. "*It's going to make you come. It always does. It'll write you a reason. A prophecy, or a promise, or someone you love, in the dark.*" His voice dropped. "*And when you come, it will be waiting.*"

"*Then I won't come*."

"*You will*." Very gently. "*You're a Vey. You always come.*" He picked up the hammer again. Looked at it. "*So listen. When you come. When you're standing at the Heart. When it's wearing my face and telling you to let it in.*"

He looked up.

"*Look for the flaw*," said the man who had been Aurelian Vey. "*Everything that's made has one. Even it. Even me.*" His tired eyes held Lex's. "*It didn't make itself. It made itself out of* me. *And I was never very careful with my folding.*"

---

Lex woke.

It was dark. The fire had burned low. Wren was asleep against his back, breathing slow and steady.

And Antho was sitting beside his pallet. On the floor. With his arms around his knees and his grey eyes on Lex's face.

"*You were talking*," said Antho. "*In your sleep.*"

Lex sat up. Slowly. His heart was hammering.

"*I dreamed*," he said. "*Of Aurelian. At a forge. On a white mountain.*" He swallowed. "*He said he was what was left. Buried under the thing that wears him. He said it was eating him.*"

Antho didn't say anything.

"*He said I'd come*," said Lex. "*To his tomb. At the Heart. He said it would make me come. And when I did, I should look for the flaw.*"

*[Lex*,] said Ember. *[Lex, it's a trick. It has to be. It's the same thing. It's the same face, the same voice, it's the thing at the door*—]

*I don't know*.

*[It's* luring *you. That's what it does. It told your father it was lonely and trapped. It told Elric he deserved more. It's telling you it's being eaten so you'll come and*—]

*I know, Ember. I know.*

He looked at his brother.

"*Was it the same*?" said Antho quietly. "*The voice. Was it the same as at the Rift?*"

Lex thought about it. Honestly. The way Malachi had taught him.

The voice at the Rift had been warm. Gold. Loving. *My boy. My good boy. Come home*. It had wrapped around him like a blanket.

The voice in the dream had been rough. Tired. Breaking. *Oh, no. Not you. Not one of mine*.

"*No*," he said. "*It wasn't the same.*"

"*What was different*?"

Lex was quiet for a long time.

"*At the Rift*," he said, "*it never once said it was sorry.*"

Antho looked at him.

"*This one did*," said Lex. "*He said he was wrong. He said he was so sure and so wrong.*" He swallowed. "*And he couldn't sing. He kept losing the tune.*"

His brother was quiet for a long time. Sitting on the floor by the dying fire, with his arms around his knees.

"*The thing in my head*," said Antho at last. "*For eleven years. The Murmur. The voice in the trees. It sang to me in Mam's voice.*" He looked at the fire. "*And it never once got the tune wrong.*"

They looked at each other.

*[Lex*,] said Ember. Very quietly. *[That doesn't prove anything.]*

*No*, thought Lex. *It doesn't.*

But he lay back down on the pallet, beside Wren, and looked at the ceiling. And thought about a tired man at a gold anvil, singing a working song badly, with his hands shaking.

*Look for the flaw*.

*Everything that's made has one*.

And far down, at the bottom of the gold, at the bottom of the Root, at the door, something warm turned over in its sleep. And breathed.

And waited.
