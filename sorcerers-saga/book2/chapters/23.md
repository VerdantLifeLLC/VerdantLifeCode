# Interlude: A Never-Ending Journey

On the other side of the door, it was always night.

Not dark. Seren Vey had learned that in her first year. The Hollow wasn't dark the way a cellar is dark, or a moonless field. It was dark the way the inside of your own eyelids is dark when you're very tired. Soft. Deep. Faintly blue, faintly violet, endless in every direction. You could see, if you wanted to. You just didn't, usually. There was nothing to see.

Except the lamp.

It sat on a ledge of black stone at the foot of the door. A small clay lamp, brown and round and plain, with a chip in its spout and a thumbprint in its base. Burning. Gold.

It was the same lamp. She'd worked that out in the second year. Not a twin. Not a copy. The *same*. The flame went through the door. One lamp, one flame, burning on both sides of the world at once, the way a candle in a window burns for the room and for the night outside.

She'd lit it eleven years ago, in the kitchen at Harrow, with her hands shaking and the hole in the world opening behind the hearth and her eldest son screaming on the stairs. She'd carried it into the dark with her.

And it had never once gone out.

Except the night of the Blade. When it had gone blue and died, and she'd screamed, and held, and held, and then felt her youngest son on the other side put his scarred hand on the lamp and *Speak* to it. And it had lit.

*Lex*. She'd felt him. Through the door. Through the flame. Big now. So big. Gold and hot and steady as a forge.

She'd sat down on the black stone afterward and wept for an hour.

---

She was very tired.

She knew that. She'd known it for a long time. Eleven years. Eleven years on the far side of a door, with her back against it and her hands flat on the black glass, holding.

Not holding it shut. People always got that wrong, in the stories. *She closed the door on herself*. As if a door, once closed, stayed closed. As if you could shut it and walk away.

You couldn't. Not this door. The Hollowmaw breach was the oldest crack in the world, older than the net, older than the lid. The Blade had been under it for nine hundred years, pinning it shut from the world's side. But from the Hollow's side, nothing held it. Nothing but whoever was willing to sit with their back against it and *push*.

For eight years, that had been Antho. She hadn't known. Not at first. She'd been lost in the shallows, wandering, half-mad, with her lamp. And then, standing on the far shore of a grey river with her way home in front of her, she'd felt it. A boy. On the other side. Holding the breach shut with his own mind, with green fire and Shade and his sanity. Her boy. Her eldest. Who'd been sixteen on the stairs at Harrow, screaming.

She'd come to the door. She'd put her back against it. And held from her side.

They'd held it together for eight years. Neither of them knowing.

And then the lamp. Three years ago. Her two boys, singing. And every lantern in the mountain catching fire. And the door sealing. And she'd felt Antho, through the glass, put his hand on it and say *Evening, Mam* to a lamp he thought was only a lamp.

She'd answered. He hadn't heard.

She'd kept answering. Every night. For three years.

---

There was something else she didn't tell anyone.

Not the boys. Not the voice in the Root that came to her every night, warm and low and tired, and said *Seren* as if her name were a prayer.

She didn't remember him.

She knew him. She knew his name. Arion Vey. She knew he was her husband, and the boys' father, and that he'd been asleep in a crystal for fifteen years and then awake in a tower for three. She knew his voice. She'd come to know it very well, these three years, through the Root, every night, in the dark. She knew he was clever, and stubborn, and kind, and afraid, and that he kept secrets the way other men kept dogs: carefully, and for company, and too many of them.

But she didn't *remember* him.

She didn't remember meeting him. She didn't remember marrying him. She didn't remember his face. She knew facts, the way you know facts about a stranger in a story. *They met in Highcrest. They married in spring. He went away often*. But the memories themselves, the warm bright living ones, were gone.

She'd paid them to the Ferryman.

---

Eleven years ago. The first winter.

She'd been in the dark a season by then. Three months, that had felt like three years. Wandering. Lost. She'd found the Drowned Country, the shallows at the edge of the Hollow, where the drowned cities were and the grey dead drifted through the streets. She'd sat by the edge of the deep with her lamp and sung to the dark, because it was the only thing she could think of to do, and the dark had listened, a little.

And then she'd found the river.

Grey. Wide. Slow. Flowing between the Hollow and the world. And on its shore, a tall thin figure in a grey hat, leaning on a long grey pole, with an unlit lantern swinging at the top of it.

*Seren Vey*, the Ferryman had said. *You want to go home.*

She'd wept. She hadn't been able to stop. Three months in the dark. Three months without her boys. And here was a boat, and a river, and the world on the other side.

*There is a price*, said the Ferryman. *The memory you would least wish to lose.*

She'd known what it would be. She'd known before he said it. Not the boys. The boys were her heart; she'd sooner have paid her heart. But the boys were also *hers*. She carried them. She'd carried them since before they were born.

The brightest thing. The warm stone she took out in the dark and turned over in her hands. The thing that was only *hers*, that nobody else had, that she'd chosen.

*A young man in a grey coat*, said the Ferryman softly. *On the steps of the Spire. In the rain. Who looked at you as if you were the first light he'd seen in two hundred years.*

She'd paid it.

And crossed.

---

She'd stood on the world's shore of the grey river, in the thin grey valley beyond the Teeth, with the Lattice humming faint and silver around her for the first time in three years. With her lamp in her hand. With the way home stretching east in front of her, through the grey, over the mountains, to wherever her boys were.

And she'd heard it.

Through the thin world. Through the Hollow behind her. From the east. Faint and terrible.

A boy screaming.

Not on the stairs at Harrow, this time. Somewhere else. Somewhere in the mountains. A cave. A crack in the world, newly torn, breathing cold. And a boy in front of it, sixteen, gaunt and wild-eyed, with green fire in his hands and the Hollow pouring into him through the crack, filling him, *drinking* him. Holding it shut with nothing but his own mind.

Alone.

Losing.

She'd stood on the shore of the grey river with her way home stretching out in front of her, and the price already paid, and every memory of the man she'd married already gone.

And she'd turned around.

---

The Ferryman had watched her walk back down the shingle.

*You have paid for one crossing*, he'd said. *One. If you go back now, I cannot take you over again. Not for that price. Not for any.*

*I know*, she'd said.

*You will be in the dark. Perhaps forever.*

*I know*.

*Why?*

She'd stopped at the water's edge. She'd looked back at him. At his long eyeless face under the wide grey hat.

*Because my boy is holding a door*, she'd said. *Alone. And I promised him, on the stairs at Harrow, that I'd keep it.*

And she'd walked into the grey river. Without paying. And the river had taken what it was owed, the way it takes from everyone who crosses without a fee. It had taken her way home. Every road back. She'd felt them go, one by one, like doors closing in a long corridor.

And she'd walked on, through the grey, through the shallows, through the Drowned Country, east. Toward the screaming. Following it through the dark for a month. Until she found the far side of the Hollowmaw breach.

And put her back against it.

And held.

---

Eight years. Then three.

And then the Key.

She'd felt the Blade go. On the night of the Blade. Felt it torn out from under the floor on the world's side, the old pin that had held the breach shut for nine hundred years. Felt the door bulge. Felt the whole weight of the Hollow come down on her back.

And she'd held. Somehow. With Antho's hand on the glass on the other side. And a boy she didn't know, holding Antho's wrist. And then Lex, lighting the lamp.

And then, three weeks later, a new hand on the glass. On the other side. Long-fingered. Lean. Warm.

*I'm here*, the voice had said. The voice from the Root. The voice she'd come to know so well in the dark, three years of nights. Not through the Root now. Through the door. Close. So close. *Seren. I'm here. I'm staying.*

She didn't remember his face.

She'd fallen in love with his voice anyway. All over again. In the dark. Without remembering why she had the first time.

She thought that was probably the strangest love story anyone had ever had.

---

The longest night had been the worst.

She'd felt the lid lift. Not here. Far away, at the bottom of the world, at the first knot. But the Hollow was all one thing, under everything, and when the lid lifted, *everything* pushed. Up. Through every crack. And the Hollowmaw was the biggest crack there was.

The door had bulged. Cracked. Screamed.

And on the other side, Arion. *Tonight I'm here*. With his hands on the glass and his forehead against it. And a crowd of small cold hands beside his: children, students, she could feel them, holding candles, holding on.

She'd held. They'd all held.

And then, at the bottom of the world, someone had turned the Key back.

She'd felt it. Felt the push stop. Felt the lid slam down, far away, and the whole Hollow *recoil*, like a hand snatched back from a fire. Felt the door stop bulging. Felt it settle.

And felt, faint and far, through the deep, a thread go up into the knot. Gold. Old. *Tired*.

*Elric*, she'd thought. And hadn't known how she knew.

She'd sat down on the black stone at the foot of the door, beside her lamp, and wept for him. For the man who'd sat up all night with Antho when he had the croup. She remembered that. She'd paid away her husband, but not the man who'd sung, badly, to her baby.

---

It came to her three nights after.

She knew it was coming. She'd known for eleven years that someday it would. She'd felt it in the deep, all that time, at the bottom of the Hollow. Patient. Waiting. Turning toward her sometimes, the way a sleeper turns toward a sound.

She was sitting by her lamp. Singing. The lantern song. *If the dark comes calling, love, don't you mind*.

And the dark in front of her *thickened*.

And there he was.

Tall. Fair. Beautiful, in a tired way, like a fine blade used too long. In a robe of white and gold with a smith's apron over it. With gold eyes. With her sons' eyes.

"*Seren*," said Aurelian Vey.

She didn't stop singing.

He waited. Politely. Standing in the dark a few paces away, with his scarred hands folded in front of him, while she sang the whole song through. All the verses. The one about the lantern. The one about the window. The one about the long road home.

When she'd finished, he smiled.

"*You always did have a lovely voice*," he said.

"*You've never heard my voice*," said Seren. "*You've heard my sons say good evening to my lamp.*"

"*I've heard you sing to the door for eleven years*." His gold eyes were very kind. "*Every night. I've been listening.*" He tilted his head. "*It's very soothing. I've grown quite fond of it.*"

---

He wasn't whole.

She saw that. She'd seen him before, through the deep, from far off. Always beautiful. Always perfect. Not tonight. Tonight, at the edges, his shape *flickered*. Like a reflection in disturbed water. His left hand, folded over his right, kept going faint, and then coming back, and then going faint again. And under the faintness, for a heartbeat at a time, there was nothing at all. A hole. A shape cut out of the dark.

*The Key*, she thought. *The bar. When the lid slammed shut, it caught him. Half in and half out.*

*It hurt him*.

"*It did*," said Aurelian Vey. As if she'd spoken aloud. "*It did hurt. I won't pretend. I've been hurt before, Seren, but never by someone I'd thought was mine.*" He looked down at his flickering hand. "*Elric. Of all people. Second Elric.*" He shook his head slowly. "*I underestimated him. I underestimated your son. I underestimated you.*" He smiled. "*That's three mistakes. I don't make many.*"

"*Go away*."

"*In a moment*." He came a step closer. "*I wanted to show you something first. I've been very lonely, these nine hundred years. And now I'm not.*" He turned his head. "*I've made some friends.*"

---

They came out of the dark behind him.

Four of them.

The first was a man. Tall and thin and stooped, in the long dark robe of a church choir-master, with a wide mouth and long pale hands. He was singing. Not aloud. Seren couldn't hear it. But she could see his throat moving, and his lips, and the air around him *shivered*, the way the air shivers over a fire. Wherever he looked, the dark bent toward him. Listening.

*The Cantor*, said a voice in her head. Not Aurelian's. Her own. Remembering something. A story. A village in the north, three hundred years ago, where the choir-master had sung so beautifully that the whole village had followed him into the hills one night and never come back.

The second was a woman. Short and round and motherly, in a grey apron, with her sleeves rolled up and her grey hair in a bun. A midwife's apron. A midwife's hands, strong and capable. She was smiling. A warm, kind, grandmotherly smile. And wherever she walked, the black stone under her feet went *soft*. Rotten. Like old fruit.

*Mother Sallow*. A plague village in the Vale, two hundred years ago. The midwife who'd stayed when everyone else fled. Who'd tended the sick until she was the only one left. And then had tended the dead.

The third was a knight. In armor. Black and silver, old-fashioned, with a great helm. But wrong. *Doubled*. Two shapes in one suit of armor, overlapping, as if two men had been poured into one mould. Four arms. Two swords. Two voices, Seren could hear them, faint, inside the helm, arguing. *Left. No, right. No, LEFT.*

*The Twinned Knight*. Two brothers. The Knights of the Ford, in the old ballads, who'd held a river crossing against an army, back to back, until the water rose and drowned them both. Holding each other. They'd never let go.

And the fourth.

A woman. Tall. Gaunt. In torn white robes gone grey. With ragged silver hair hanging in her face. And no face. Not a hole. Not a blank. Just—*unremembered*. Seren looked at it and couldn't hold it. Her eyes slid off. As if the face had been written in pencil and then rubbed out.

She stood behind the others. Very still. With her hands at her sides.

"*My Choir*," said Aurelian Vey fondly. "*Isn't it lovely? The Cantor. Mother Sallow. The Knights. And my Warden.*" He looked at the faceless woman. "*She hasn't got a name anymore. Nobody remembers it. So I've given her a new one. She guards my door now. Don't you, Warden?*"

The faceless woman didn't answer.

But Seren saw her hands. At her sides. Clenching. Very slightly. As if, somewhere, a long way down, something in her didn't agree.

---

"*They're going up*," said Aurelian. Conversationally. "*All four of them. Up into the world.*"

"*You can't*." Seren's voice was steady. She was proud of that. "*The lid's sealed. The Key's barred. My son made a bar, and your Elric set it, and you'll never*—"

"*Not through the lid*." Aurelian smiled. "*Oh, Seren. Did you think the lid was the only way?*" He knelt down. On the black stone. Across the little clay lamp from her. So his gold eyes were level with hers, with the flame between them. "*Do you know what the Tithe is?*"

She didn't answer.

"*Every Stone in the world*," said Aurelian Vey, "*every one my brother and sister helped me cut from the bones of the mountain, every one with a thread running into it, takes a little. A tenth. Of every soul's mana. Every day. Since the net was woven.*" His voice was very gentle. "*It runs down. Through the Stones. Through the threads. Down, down, to the bottom of the world. A tithe. For the binding. To keep the lid strong.*"

He smiled.

"*That's what I told them. My brother and sister. The other Founders. A tithe, for the binding.*" He tilted his head. "*It wasn't for the binding, Seren. It was for* me. *Nine hundred years. A tenth of everything. Every soul under the net. Feeding me.*"

Seren went cold.

"*Four thousand Stones in Caldor*," said Aurelian. "*Thousands more in the Sunreach and the north and the east. Every one with a thread running down. Down to me.*" His gold eyes were very bright. "*They're pipes, Seren. Every Stone is a pipe. Running from the world straight down to the bottom of the Hollow.*"

"*And pipes*," said Aurelian Vey softly, "*run both ways.*"

---

She understood.

She sat on the black stone with her back against the door and the lamp in front of her and felt the understanding go through her like cold water.

The Stones. Every village. Every town. Every city square. Harrowgate. Coldbrook. Kessick. Highcrest. Saffaran. The Stone at Hollow's Foot, below the Hollowmaw, where Arion had walked his sons out of the threads.

Pipes. Running down.

And something coming up.

"*Your sons have been very busy*," said Aurelian. "*Breaking Ceilings. Freeing threads. Four hundred and forty thousand of them. And every one they free shines brighter. Carries more mana. Pays a bigger tithe.*" He smiled. "*They've been feeding me, Seren. Every Stone they Unbind, they make the pipe wider.*"

"*No*."

"*Yes*." He stood up. "*And now the lid's shut. And I can't come up through the middle. So I'll come up through the edges. Through every Stone in the world. One at a time. Very slowly.*" He looked at his Choir. "*They'll go first. To get the pipes ready.*"

He turned to go. Then turned back.

"*Oh. And I've written a little poem*," he said. "*For your boys. A prophecy. They like prophecies, the Veys. They always have.*" He smiled. "*I'll make sure they find it.*"

"*What does it say?*"

"*It says come home*," said Aurelian Vey. "*It says the Root of the world is buried in my tomb, and only the heir can free it, and if he doesn't, the net will fail and everyone will die.*" He shrugged, very slightly. "*Some of that's even true.*"

"*They won't come*."

"*They always come*." He looked down at her with infinite kindness. "*That's the thing about your family, Seren. You always come. You came into the dark for a boy on the stairs. You came back across the river for a boy at the breach. Your husband sat in front of a door for three weeks because you were behind it.*" His gold eyes were very soft. "*They'll come for the same reason. Because someone they love is in the dark.*"

And he looked, very briefly, at the faceless woman standing at the back of his Choir. With her clenched hands.

And then he was gone. And the Choir with him. Into the deep. Going.

Up.

---

Seren sat by her lamp for a long time.

Then she turned around. And put her hands on the door. On the black glass. The way she'd done every night for eleven years.

"*Arion*," she said.

Nothing.

She pressed harder. Pushed her voice into the glass. Into the flame. Into the Root, deep down, where the thread ran that had once been tied to his.

"*Arion. The Stones. Tell them. The Stones, Arion. It's coming up through the Stones*—"

Nothing.

The glass was different. She felt it. Harder. Thicker. Sealed. When the lid slammed shut at the bottom of the world, when the Lockward went in, every crack in the Hollow had tightened. Including this one. The door that had been cracked enough for a voice to pass was cracked enough now for a flame. Only a flame.

She could hear him. Faintly. On the other side. His voice. Talking to her, the way he did. *Seren. Are you there? I can't hear you. Seren*.

He couldn't hear her.

---

She sat down on the black stone.

She looked at the lamp. At the little gold flame, burning on both sides of the door at once.

*They always come*, the thing had said. *Because someone they love is in the dark*.

*You came back across the river for a boy at the breach*.

She thought about that. For a long time. Sitting in the soft endless dark at the bottom of the world, with her back against a door she'd held for eleven years and a voice on the other side she couldn't remember falling in love with.

*He's right*, she thought. *They will come. They'll find his poem, and they'll come to his tomb, and they'll walk into whatever he's built for them. Because I'm in the dark.*

*Unless I'm not*.

She looked out into the deep. Into the soft blue-violet nothing, going on forever. Toward the place where the Choir had gone. Toward the bottom of the Hollow. Toward wherever the thing in her sons' ancestor's face lived.

And at the back of the Choir, a faceless woman with clenched hands.

*Nobody remembers her*, Seren thought. *Nobody but him. And he's given her a new name.*

*And I know what it's like. To be in the dark with no one to call you by your name.*

She stood up.

It was very hard. Her legs had forgotten how. Eleven years, sitting against a door. She stood with one hand on the black glass, swaying, and looked at the lamp.

*I can't take it with me*, she thought. *It has to stay here. It's the door. It's the flame on both sides. If I take it, the door goes dark.*

*But they'll keep it lit. On their side. Arion. Antho. The boy who doesn't talk.*

*They'll leave a light on*.

She knelt down. She cupped her hands around the little clay lamp, the way you'd cup your hands around a bird. She didn't pick it up. She just held her hands around it. And sang. Very softly. The last verse. The one about the long road home.

*If the dark's too long, love, and the way's too far,*
*look for the light, love, wherever you are.*
*I'll be walking toward it, however I can.*
*Leave a light in the window. I'm coming home.*

She stood up again. She took her hands away.

The flame burned on. Steady. Gold.

And Seren Vey, who had held the far side of a door for eleven years, and paid away her husband, and turned back across a river for her son, turned her back on the door for the first time.

And walked into the deep.

After the Choir. After the faceless woman with the clenched hands. After the thing in Aurelian's face.

The long way round.

---

On the other side of the door, in the bell-shaped cavern at the bottom of the Hollowmaw, Arion Vey sat with his hands on the black glass.

He'd been sitting there for three days. Since the longest night. Since the lid lifted and slammed shut. He'd felt the door settle. Felt it seal tighter. And since then, nothing. No voice. No *Arion*. Just the lamp in its niche, burning straight up.

The boy who didn't talk sat beside him. Pim. He'd barely left since the longest night. He sat with his knees drawn up and his small cold hand on Arion's sleeve.

And the flame leaned.

Arion felt his heart stop.

It leaned. Toward the door. The way it had leaned every night for three years, when she came. A finger's width. Toward the glass.

And then, slowly, it straightened again. And burned straight up.

He waited. An hour. Two. All night.

It didn't lean again.

"*She's gone*," said Pim.

Arion turned his head. The boy was looking at the lamp. His thin grave face very still. His dark careful listening eyes.

"*She was there*," said Pim. "*On the other side. I could hear her. Every night. Like a sound you stop noticing.*" He was quiet. "*And now she isn't.*"

It was the longest speech he had ever made.

Arion looked at the door. At the lamp. At the flame, burning gold and steady on both sides of a door that nobody was holding anymore.

*Seren*, he thought. *Seren. Where have you gone?*

He didn't get up.

He sat in front of the door with the boy beside him, and kept his hands on the glass. And when the lamp needed oil, though it never did, he filled it anyway. And every night, at the end of the count, he said good evening.

Leaving a light on.

Just in case.
