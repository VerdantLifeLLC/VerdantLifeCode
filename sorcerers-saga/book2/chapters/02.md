# The Festival of Sorcerers

Highcrest burned gold.

Lex saw it from Ashka's back, coming in from the south at dusk, with Antho on the dragon's neck in front of him and Corin Bell wedged between them, wrapped in his blue shawl, his eyes enormous. The whole city. Every terrace, every tier, every street and stair and rooftop, from the crowded Lower Town to the castle on the crag. Lanterns. Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. In every window, on every sill, hung from every eave and balcony and lamppost. The mountain city glowed in the blue summer dusk like a great coal in a banked fire.

And at the very top, beside the castle, the Spire. The white needle. With the Great Stone at its summit pulsing silver-blue against the evening sky, as it had for nine hundred years.

There was a lantern hanging from the top of it.

A real one. Enormous. Brass and glass, the size of a cart, hung from the very peak of the Spire on a chain, burning gold. Someone had climbed all the way up there to hang it. Lex had heard it was the Keepers. Roole's idea. The first year, they'd hung a small one. The second year, bigger. This year, the third Festival of Sorcerers, it was so big you could see it from Millbridge.

*A light left on for the lost.*

"*It's beautiful*," whispered Corin.

Antho turned his head on the dragon's neck. In the dusk, his grey eyes were very dark. Shade-dark. The sky between the stars.

"It is," he said quietly. "It's very beautiful." He looked at the boy. "Your sister would have liked it."

Corin's face crumpled. He didn't cry. He'd stopped crying, mostly, in the week since Ashbourne. He just held on tighter to his shawl.

Antho reached back without a word and put a scarred hand on the boy's shoulder, and left it there all the way down.

---

They landed in the castle courtyard.

The Queen had given permission, three years ago, for the dragon to land wherever she liked. It had caused a sensation the first time. Now the castle guards barely looked up. Ashka folded her vast storm-colored wings and settled onto the flagstones like a cat settling by a fire, and a stable-boy came running with a bucket of water the size of a bathtub, and she drank it in three long swallows and sneezed sparks at him in thanks.

Seraphine was waiting at the top of the steps.

Royal Archivist of Caldor. In a dark grey dress with the Queen's silver crown embroidered small on the collar, and new spectacles on her nose, uncracked, and ink on her fingers as always. The scars down the left side of her face had faded to fine silver lines. She stood very straight at the top of the castle steps with her hands clasped in front of her and watched the dragon land.

Antho slid off Ashka's neck. He walked up the steps. He didn't hurry. He stopped one step below her, so their eyes were level.

"Hello," he said.

"You're late," said Seraphine. "You said the twelfth. It's the fourteenth."

"There was a storm over the Fells. Ashka wanted to eat it."

"Of course she did."

They looked at each other. Neither of them moved. Lex, climbing down off the dragon with Corin, felt his face go warm and looked away.

When he looked back, Seraphine had her face pressed into Antho's coat and his arms were around her, and she was saying something into his chest that sounded very much like *I missed you, you idiot, I've been reading your letters until the paper wore through*, and Antho was laughing very softly into her hair.

*[Hm,]* said Ember.

*Don't.*

*[I didn't say anything.]*

*You were going to.*

*[I was going to say,]* said Ember with great dignity, *[that they write to each other every week. By raven, when Quill's willing. Seraphine keeps the letters in a box under her bed.]* A pause. *[Like Garrick's wife did.]*

Lex didn't say anything to that.

---

The council met that night in a small round room at the top of the castle's east tower.

The Queen's private room. Lex had been in it a dozen times in three years. Bookshelves on every wall. A fire. A round table, scarred and old, with no head and no foot, so nobody sat above anybody else. A window looking out over the whole golden city.

The Queen was already there when they came in.

Lysandra of Caldor was twenty-six now. Tall and slender, in a plain blue dress with no jewels, her dark hair bound up simply, a thin silver circlet on her brow that was the only sign she was anything more than a merchant's daughter. Her fine-boned face had grown thinner in three years. Her dark serious eyes had grown older. She was standing at the window with her back to the door, looking down at the lanterns.

She turned when they came in.

"Smith," she said. She always called him that. Never *Lexander*, never *Lord Vey*, though she'd offered him a title twice and he'd refused it twice. "Thank you for coming." Her eyes went to Corin, standing very close to Lex's side. "And this is the boy."

"Corin Bell," said Lex. "From Harrowgate."

The Queen crossed the room. She crouched down in front of Corin, in her plain blue dress, so their eyes were level. The way Roole had crouched in Harrowgate square.

"Corin," she said. "I'm told you remember your sister."

Corin stared at her. At the silver circlet. He swallowed.

"Yes, Your Majesty," he whispered.

"What was her name?"

"Mella."

"Mella," said the Queen. She said it carefully. Deliberately. As if writing it down. "Mella Bell. Weatherwise. Rare." She looked at him. "I'm going to remember that name, Corin. I'm going to have it written in the royal records. In ink. On paper. Where no one can unwrite it." She put her hand on his shoulder. "And I'm going to find out who took her."

Corin's face crumpled again.

The Queen stood up. She looked around the room. At Lex. At Antho and Seraphine, standing very close together by the bookshelves. At Roole, by the fire, with his arms folded. At Hedda Vorne, who had come up from Ashbourne on the cart with Lex's tools and refused to stay behind, and who was sitting in the best chair by the fire with her cudgel across her knees as if she owned it.

And at the man standing in the corner by the window.

Lean. Grey-eyed. Silver at the temples. In a long dark coat, with his hands clasped behind his back. A face that slid off your attention like water off oiled leather.

"Lord Advisor," said the Queen. "I believe you have a guess."

---

Arion Vey didn't answer at once.

He was looking at Lex. Lex looked back. Three years. Three years of first-of-the-months in the Ashbourne forge, of arguments with Brannoc in the Crooked Kettle, of evenings at the anvil, father and son, striking runes side by side. Three years, and Lex still couldn't read his father's face when his father didn't want it read.

He could read it now, a little. Enough to see that Arion was afraid.

"Yes," said Arion at last. "I have a guess."

He came to the round table. He didn't sit. He laid his hands flat on the scarred wood, the way he'd laid them on the black floor at Duskhold.

"Nine hundred years ago," he said quietly, "Aurelian Vey wove the Lattice. You know that. You know it was a net over the will of men, and a lid over the hunger beneath. What you don't know, what almost no one knows, because the Order spent three centuries burning every record of it, is that a lid needs a *lock*." He looked around the table. "And a lock needs a key."

Seraphine had gone very still.

"Aurelian forged it," said Arion. "With his own hands. On his own anvil. A key to the lid between the Lattice and the Hollow. So that if the binding ever failed, if the hunger ever grew too strong for the net, someone of the blood could open the lid just enough to reach down and *fix* it." His jaw tightened. "That's what he told the other Founders, anyway."

"And then?" said the Queen.

"And then he broke it." Arion's voice was flat. "Into three pieces. And hid them. In three places, far apart. And told no one where." He was quiet for a moment. "The Founders' records call it the Key of Shadows. Because he forged it from his own shadow. Literally. The old texts say he stood in the light of the first fire and cut his shadow off the ground with a blade of Wellscript and folded it on the anvil like steel." He looked up. "That's the kind of thing Aurelian could do."

"And someone found it," said Lex.

"Someone found one piece." Arion looked at him. "Three years ago. At the first Festival. You saw the panel. *A Key has been turned.*"

"Elric," said Lex.

His father closed his eyes.

"Yes," he said. "I think so. I've thought so for three years." He opened them. "I've been looking for him. Every month. With every Keeper Roole could spare, and every raven, and every scrap of Root I could reach. I've been through every Stone in the east. I've walked the edge of the Sundered Lands." He shook his head slowly. "Nothing. He's hidden himself. The way I taught him. The way we both learned in the Spire, forty years ago."

"*Three years*," said Antho. His voice was very quiet. Very cold. "You've known for three years. That Elric had a piece of a key that opens the lid on the thing in my head." He looked at their father. "And you didn't tell us."

"I wasn't *sure*—"

"You're never sure." Antho's grey eyes were very dark. "You weren't sure about Mam. You're *still* not sure about Mam. You weren't sure about the Vhessa breach until Morvane told us for you." His jaw worked. "I forgave you for keeping secrets, Father. On a plinth in Duskhold. In front of eleven thousand people. And you're still keeping them."

The room was very quiet. The fire crackled. Corin Bell had pressed himself against Lex's side.

Arion stood at the table with his hands flat on the wood and looked at his eldest son. His lean face was very still.

"Yes," he said at last. "I am."

"*Why*?"

"Because I was afraid," said Arion simply. "Because the last time I told anyone what I was afraid of, I tore a hole in the world. Because every time I've decided I knew best, I've been wrong, and someone I love has paid for it." His voice was very low. "And because I hoped. I hoped I'd find Elric first. Quietly. Before anyone else got hurt. Before either of you had to—" He stopped.

"Had to what?" said Lex.

His father didn't answer.

---

"The holes," said Seraphine. She'd taken out a ledger from somewhere, a thick one, bound in grey, and opened it on the round table. "Let me show you the holes."

She turned the pages. Rows and rows of her neat cramped handwriting. Villages. Dates. Numbers.

"I read the records every day," she said. "Every Stone in Caldor reports to the Spire's Great Stone. Every Kindling, every death, every level. I read them all. It's what I do." She pushed her spectacles up her nose. "In the spring, I started noticing gaps. A village where the Stone's thread-count dropped by one, overnight. No death recorded. No departure. No transfer. The thread just... wasn't there anymore. And when I went back through the old records, the thread wasn't there either. Not in last year's. Not in the year before. Not at the Kindling." She looked up. "As if the person had never been Kindled at all. Never been born."

"Unwritten," said Roole quietly.

"Unwritten." Seraphine turned another page. "I found nine. All in the north. All since spring." She looked at Lex. "You said eleven."

"I felt eleven. In the net. Last week."

Seraphine stared at him. Then she turned back to her ledger and started writing very fast.

"And they're all Unbound," said Lex.

Everyone looked at him.

"The ones I felt," he said. "The holes. They're all in the bright places. Where we broke the Ceilings." He looked at Antho. "Mella Bell was Unbound at the Harrowgate Stone in the spring. By us. The others—I'd bet every one of them was Unbound too." He swallowed. "Someone's going through the orchard, picking the ripest fruit."

"*Why*?" said Hedda Vorne from her chair by the fire. It was the first thing she'd said. "Why the Unbound?"

Arion was looking at the ledger.

"Because their threads are new," he said slowly. "Freshly freed. Loose, the way a new knot is loose before it's pulled tight. Easier to cut." His voice dropped. "And because they're *bright*. Unbound threads carry more mana than capped ones. Much more. No Ceiling holding them down. All that potential, flowing freely." He looked up. "If you cut one out of the net, the mana in it has to go somewhere."

"Into whoever cut it," said Antho.

Their father didn't answer. He didn't need to.

---

Wren came in through the window.

Literally. The council had been going for an hour, and they'd moved from the holes to the north, to the line of them Lex had felt running up toward the Ironbacks and beyond, and Roole had unrolled Kell's old map on the table with its keyhole in the margin. And then the window behind the Queen's chair, the one that looked out over the golden city, flew open with a bang that sent every candle in the room guttering.

And a small slim figure in a patched coat with a fox-red braid stepped in off the empty air above the city, windburned and grinning, and dropped lightly onto the floor.

"*Sorry I'm late*," said Wren Ashdown. "*The Sunreach is enormous. Did anyone know the Sunreach was enormous?*"

The Queen had half-risen from her chair with her hand on the dagger at her belt. Roole had his sword halfway out. Hedda had her cudgel raised.

Wren looked around at all of them. Then at Lex.

"Hello, Hammer," she said.

He was across the room before he knew he'd moved.

---

They found a corner. Later. After the council broke up, after Roole took Corin down to the kitchens to be fed, after Antho and Seraphine vanished somewhere together and the Queen went back to the window to look at the lanterns. A window seat in a corridor, looking out over the city.

Wren sat with her back against the stone and her knees drawn up and her boots on Lex's lap, the way she always sat. She'd taken her braid down. Her fox-red hair was a wild tangle around her windburned face.

"Five weeks," he said.

"I said three."

"You always say three."

"I always mean three." She leaned her head back against the window. "The Sunreach is *enormous*, Lex. It's all sand. Sand and sun and rocks and more sand. And the people are wonderful. And their queen has a scimitar the size of your hammer and rides a horse made of—I don't even know. It glows. It's made of light, or fire, or something." She laughed. "She wants to meet you. The smith with the gold eyes. Everyone in the Sunreach wants to meet you. They have Stones too. Thousands. Nobody's ever taken the flags off them."

"Thousands," said Lex. "More Stones."

"I told her you'd come. Someday." She looked at him sideways. "When you're not busy."

"I'm always busy."

"I know." She was quiet for a moment. "I heard about the holes. From Seraphine. Before I came in through the window." Her green eyes were very serious now. "The Unbound. Someone's cutting them out."

"Yes."

"You're going after them. Aren't you."

He didn't answer.

"You've got the line between your eyebrows," said Wren. "The thinking line. You've had it since I came through the window." She pressed her boot into his thigh. "You're going north. To where the holes go. To find whoever's cutting them."

"Yes."

"When?"

"Soon."

"Then I'm coming."

"No," said Lex.

Wren stopped.

---

She didn't say anything for a long moment. She just looked at him. Her face had gone very still.

"*No*?" she said.

"Wren—"

"*No*?" She took her boots off his lap. Slowly. She put her feet on the floor. "You don't get to say *no*, Hammer. You've never once in three years got to say *no*. That's not how this works."

"It's different this time."

"How?"

"*Because they're taking the Unbound.*" It came out louder than he meant. He made himself breathe. "Because whoever's doing this is going through the orchard picking the brightest ones. The ones who were freed. The ones whose threads are new and loose and full of everything the Ceiling held back." He looked at her. "You're a Mistwalker, Wren. Epic. Level fifty-two. Gale at Speaking. Unbound at a Stone by *my hands*." His voice cracked. "You're the brightest thread there is."

Wren stared at him.

"If whoever it is gets near you," said Lex, "if they put that key to your thread—you'll fade. Like Mella. And everyone will forget you. Your mam. Your brothers. Brannoc. The Queen. Everyone." He swallowed. "Everyone except me."

The corridor was very quiet. Out the window, the lanterns of Highcrest glowed gold.

"And you think," said Wren slowly, "that I'm going to let you go up there alone? Into whatever this is? While I sit in the castle and wait?"

"I think you're the person I'm most afraid of losing."

"*And you think I'm not afraid of losing YOU?*"

She was on her feet. Her fists were clenched. The wind had come up in the corridor from nowhere, a sudden cold gust that set the tapestries flapping and the candles streaming, and her hair was lifting around her face.

"You're the brightest thread there is, Lex. You're *Root*. You're the one who Unbound every one of them. If they want bright, you're the brightest thing in Aldermere." Her voice shook. "And you want to go up there alone. Like you always do. Like you went up the Hollowmaw alone, and into the Frostvault alone, and down the stair at Avaloria alone, and into the dark forge in the middle of yourself *alone*—"

"I wasn't alone in the dark forge. You were the bellows."

"*EXACTLY.*"

The wind slammed every shutter in the corridor.

They stood facing each other in the sudden silence. Wren breathing hard. Lex not breathing at all.

"I'm not asking you to protect me," said Wren. Her voice had dropped. "I've never once asked you to protect me. I'm asking you to *let me come*." Her green eyes were very bright. "*With* you. Not for you. You said that. On the cliff in the Stillwater. *With* you." Her voice cracked. "Did you mean it?"

Lex looked at her.

He thought about Mella Bell, laughing on the Harrowgate Stone. He thought about Corin, the only one who remembered. He thought about a blue wool shawl lying in the heather, damp with eleven days of dew.

He thought about Wren fading. Going soft at the edges like chalk in rain. Looking at him with polite, puzzled, kindly eyes and saying *do I know you?*

"I meant it," he said. "I still mean it."

"Then—"

"And I can't," said Lex. "Not this time. I'm sorry."

Wren looked at him for a very long time.

Then she turned and walked away down the corridor without a word, and the wind went with her, and the tapestries fell still.

*[Lex,]* said Ember, very quietly.

*Don't.*

*[I'm only going to say one thing.]*

*Don't, Ember.*

*[Your father did this,]* said Ember. *[Exactly this. On a stormy night in a cottage in Ashbourne. He decided what was best for the people he loved, and he didn't ask them, and he left.]* A pause. *[I know. I was there. I'm what he left on the anvil.]*

Lex didn't answer.

---

The festival reached its height at midnight.

The Queen spoke in the great square below the castle, at the foot of the stair that climbed up to the Spire. It was a tradition now. Three years old. The young Queen of Caldor standing on the steps in a plain blue dress with a lantern in her hand, speaking to her people.

Lex stood in the crowd. He didn't want to be anywhere near the steps. He didn't want to be seen. So he stood near the back of the great square, with his hood up, among the bakers and tanners and dockworkers of the Lower Town. And listened.

"*Three years ago*," said Queen Lysandra, "*a Ceiling was broken. For the first time in three hundred years.*"

The square was utterly silent. Ten thousand people. More. Holding lanterns.

"*Since then*," said the Queen, "*nine hundred and forty thousand Ceilings have been broken. In Caldor alone. Nine hundred and forty thousand of you have looked at your panels and seen a number with no end.*"

A roar went up. It shook the windows. It went up the stair and up the Spire and set the great lantern at its peak swinging on its chain.

Lex didn't roar. He was watching the edge of the crowd. Where a knot of people stood together near the stair, apart from the rest. Fine cloaks. Fine boots. Silver at their throats. And on their left arms, all of them, a band of grey cloth.

*[The Ceiling Party,]* said Ember. *[Sorn's people. Nobles and their followers. They wear the grey bands in protest. Grey for the Ceiling.]* A pause. *[There's Sorn himself. In the middle. The tall one with the silver beard.]*

Lex looked. A tall, broad, handsome man of sixty, with a silver beard trimmed to a point and a fine dark coat and a face like a carved statue of a wise old king. He was watching the Queen on the steps with an expression of polite, patient attention.

"*Some say*," said the Queen, "*that the Ceiling was a kindness. That it kept order. That it kept everyone in their place.*"

The knot of nobles near the stair didn't move. But Lex saw Lord Chancellor Varric Sorn smile very slightly.

"*I say*," said the Queen, and her voice rang across the square like a bell, "*that no one's place is decided for them by a flag on a Stone. I say that every one of you has the right to climb as high as your gift will take you. I say that the Ceiling was not a kindness. It was a theft.*"

The roar this time was so loud Lex felt it in his chest.

And under the roar, very faint, so faint he almost didn't feel it, something *turned*.

---

He felt it in the net.

A hole. Opening. Not far to the north. *Here*. In the square. In the crowd. Twenty paces from where he stood.

He spun around.

A man. A baker, by the flour on his apron. Middle-aged, round-faced, with a little boy on his shoulders holding a paper lantern. He was cheering with the rest of them. Shouting. His face red and happy.

And as Lex watched, he stopped.

In the middle of a cheer. Mid-word. As if a hand had been put over his mouth.

He looked down at his hands. Turned them over. Palms, backs, palms.

The little boy on his shoulders was still cheering. The woman beside him, his wife, by the flour on her apron too, was still cheering. Neither of them had noticed.

Lex used True Sight.

The world went gold. And he saw it. Saw the man's thread, bright and new, running out of him into the great net of the Lattice. *Unbound*. Lex could see the broken grey band, the Ceiling, rusted and hanging. He'd broken it himself. At the Lower Town Stone. The spring before last.

And he saw something else.

A second thread. Black. Thin as a hair. Running out of the man's chest, up into the air above the crowd. To a place in the empty air where the light was *wrong*. A shape. Hanging above the heads of ten thousand cheering people, black, the size of a man's hand.

A keyhole.

The black thread ran into it. And through it. And away.

And it was *pulling*.

The baker's thread was coming loose from the net. Lex could see it. Like a thread being drawn out of a hem, slowly, stitch by stitch. And as it came loose, the man was going *soft*. At the edges. Like chalk in rain.

His wife turned to him. Still smiling. Still cheering. She opened her mouth to say something to him.

And stopped.

She looked at him. At his face. And her expression changed. From joy to polite, puzzled, kindly confusion.

"I'm sorry," she said to her husband. "Do I know you?"

---

Lex moved.

He didn't think. He went through the crowd like a hot iron through wax, people stumbling out of his way, and reached the baker in three strides and grabbed his arm. And *reached*. With Root. With everything. Into the man's thread, the bright gold Unbound thread he'd freed himself, the one that was coming loose from the net stitch by stitch.

He grabbed it. And held on.

It was like grabbing a rope that a horse was pulling. The black thread was hauling on it. From the keyhole. From somewhere far away on the other side of the keyhole. Hauling, hauling, steady and strong and patient.

And Lex hauled back.

*[Lex, you can't—]* Ember. *[You can't win this. Whatever's on the other end, it's—]*

*I know.*

*[Then let go before it pulls you through too!]*

He didn't let go.

He held the baker's thread in both hands, in the place under the air, and pulled. And for one long moment, it held. The thread stopped coming loose. The baker stopped fading. His edges firmed up. His wife blinked.

And Lex looked up at the keyhole.

And saw through it.

Just for an instant. Like looking through an actual keyhole into a dark room. He saw a fire. A small one. In a strange land under a sky with too many stars. And sitting beside the fire, an old man in a grey cloak.

Not as old as he had been.

His hair was grey, but there was dark in it again. His face was lined, but the lines were softer. He sat straight. His hands were steady. And at his belt, on a chain of dull black iron, hung something black. The bow of a key. The ring at the end that your fingers hold to turn it. With a keyhole in its center.

His fingers were on it. Turning it.

He looked up. Through the keyhole. Across the impossible distance. Straight at Lex.

And smiled.

"*Hello, Lex*," said Elric Thorne.

---

The thread tore out of his hands.

Not slowly. Not stitch by stitch. All at once. Like a rope snapping. The black thread *yanked*, and the baker's bright gold thread came out of the net in a single rush, and out of Lex's grip, and up into the keyhole, and *through*.

And the baker faded.

All at once. In the space of a breath. Soft edges, pale colors, transparent, gone. His flour-dusted apron fell to the cobbles. The little boy on his shoulders fell too, and Lex caught him, and the boy stared up at him with a paper lantern in his hand and an expression of complete bewilderment.

"*Who are you?*" said the boy. "*Where's—*" He stopped. Frowned. "*I was sitting on something.*"

The woman was looking at the apron on the cobbles at her feet. Puzzled. She bent and picked it up. She looked at it.

"Someone's dropped this," she said. "It's got flour on it. Like mine." She looked around. "Is anyone missing an apron?"

Above the crowd, the keyhole closed. Healed over. A thin black scar in the air, already fading.

And nobody in the great square of Highcrest, out of ten thousand people cheering their Queen, had noticed a thing.

Except one.

Lex looked up. Across the square. Over the heads of the crowd. To the steps at the foot of the Spire, where the Queen stood with her lantern raised.

She wasn't looking at the crowd anymore. She wasn't speaking. She was looking straight at him. At the boy in his arms. At the woman holding the apron.

Her face was white.

*She saw it*, Lex thought. *She saw it. She remembers.*

And then he looked down at the boy in his arms. At the paper lantern. And he realized, with a cold lurch in his chest, that he didn't know the baker's name.

He'd Unbound him. The spring before last. At the Lower Town Stone. He'd held the man's thread in his hands not a minute ago. He'd seen his face.

He reached for the name. It wasn't there.

Not *forgotten*. Not the way you forget a name and it comes back later. *Gone*. Cut out. Like the thread. There was a hole in his memory the exact shape of a round-faced baker with a little boy on his shoulders, and the hole had no name in it at all.

*[Lex,]* said Ember. Its voice was shaking. *[Lex, I can't remember him either. I was there. I saw him. And I can't—]*

Lex stood in the middle of the great square with a stranger's child in his arms and ten thousand people cheering around him, and felt the cold go all the way through him to the bone.

*He's getting stronger*, he thought. *Mella, I remembered. This one, I can't. He's getting stronger.*

And somewhere very far away, beyond the end of every map, an old man by a fire took his fingers off a black key, and counted.

*Forty-four.*

---

He left at dawn.

He didn't tell anyone. He wrote a letter to his father, and one to Antho, and one to the Queen. Short ones. *I've gone north. Don't follow. I'll write.*

He wrote one to Wren. It was longer. He wrote it four times, and burned three, the way he'd once watched an old man burn letters by a candle in the dark, and stirred the ashes into a cup of water. The fourth one, he folded and left on the pillow in the room in the castle where she was sleeping. Where she'd gone to sleep without speaking to him.

It said: *I'm sorry. I meant it. With you. Not for you. I'm breaking my word. I know I am. I'll spend the rest of my life making it up to you. Please be here when I come back. —L.*

And under it, smaller: *I love you. I'm an idiot. I know.*

Then he went down to the castle courtyard in the grey light, where a stable-boy was asleep against a bucket and a vast blue dragon was curled on the flagstones like a cat by a fire.

Ashka opened one golden eye as he came near.

He put his hand on her warm blue scales.

"*Not you*," he said quietly. "*Antho needs you. Go home with him. To the Hollowmaw. Watch the door.*"

The dragon looked at him for a long moment. Then a picture came into his head, sharp and bright and full of reproach. *Same blood. Alone. Stupid. Alone again. STUPID.*

"I know," said Lex.

He shouldered Anvilsong, and walked out of the castle gate, and down through the waking city, and out through the north gate, and onto the road.

Alone.

*[You know this is a mistake,]* said Ember quietly, as the city fell away behind him into the morning mist.

*Yes.*

*[You're doing it anyway.]*

*He took a man in the middle of a crowd of ten thousand people. Through a keyhole. From the other end of the world.* Lex's jaw tightened. *And I couldn't hold on. And I can't remember his name.* He walked faster. *If he can do that to a baker in the square at Highcrest, he can do it to anyone. Wren. Antho. Brannoc. Anyone with a bright thread.*

*[And you think going alone will stop that?]*

*I think if I'm alone, he has fewer threads to pull on.*

Ember was quiet for a long time.

*[Your father thought that too,]* it said at last. *[Fifteen years ago.]*

Lex didn't answer.

He walked north. Toward the Ironbacks. Toward Harrowgate, and the high pasture where a blue shawl had lain in the heather. Toward the line of holes in the net that ran up into the strange pale sky beyond the end of every map.

Behind him, in the castle on the crag, a small slim figure with a fox-red braid stood at a window in a nightgown with a letter in her hand, and watched him go. She didn't cry. She'd stopped crying, mostly, years ago, on a mountain.

She just stood there, with the letter crushed in her fist, until he was out of sight.

Then she turned away from the window, and went to find her boots.
