# The Forbidden Lands

Teo Brask met them at the Teeth.

He came out of the east on Ashka's back on the morning of the fourth day, just as Vharuun was laboring up into the high cold passes with a dozen people clinging to her scales. A small round figure on a storm-drake's neck, waving both arms wildly, which nearly knocked him off.

"*Master Antho*," he shouted across the wind. "*Master Lex! I've got it! I've got it!*"

Ashka came in close, under the storm-mother's vast wing, matching her pace. Lightning crawled along her flanks. She looked very pleased with herself.

And Teo held up his hand.

Something small glinted in it. Silver. No bigger than a thumb.

And it was *ringing*.

Lex heard it over the wind. Over the thunder of the storm-mother's wingbeats. Over everything. A tiny, clear, sweet sound. Like a drop of water falling into a still pool. Over and over. Never stopping.

On Vharuun's back, behind Lex, someone made a sound like a breaking heart.

---

Malachi held the bell in both hands for a long time.

They'd come down on a ledge in the high Teeth, in the thin cold air, so Teo could climb across from Ashka. The boy had handed it over without a word. Just held it out, in his wet palm, ringing, and Malachi had taken it.

And it had kept ringing.

The old man sat on the cold rock with the little silver bell cupped in his gnarled brown hands, and looked at it. Tears were running down his face, into the creases, into his beard. He didn't wipe them away.

"*It rings when I'm sad*," he said. Barely audible. "*So he'd know to come and find me.*"

Nobody said anything.

"*It's been ringing for nine hundred years*," said Malachi. "*At the bottom of the sea. Every time I was sad. He couldn't hear it. He was in the net. And then he was in the dark.*" He turned it over. The tiny silver clapper swung. *Ting*. *Ting*. "*It never stopped. Not once. I was sad for nine hundred years.*"

"*The Undertow kept it*," said Teo. Very quietly. He was standing on the ledge, dripping, with his round face solemn. "*It said it was the loudest thing in the whole deep. It said everything else down there is silent, all the lost things, and this little bell never stopped ringing.*" He swallowed. "*It said it was glad to give it back. It said it had been listening to someone being sad for nine hundred years, and it was very tired of it.*"

Malachi laughed. Through his tears. A cracked, broken, astonished sound.

"*It said that*?"

"*More or less*." Teo went red. "*It doesn't use words. But that's what it meant.*"

---

"*Oriel said a bell*," said Lex, later. Sitting beside the old man on the ledge, with the wind howling past and the bell still ringing, very softly, in Malachi's lap. "*At the Heart. An old man with a bell.*"

"*Yes*."

"*What does it mean*?"

Malachi was quiet for a long time.

"*I don't know*," he said. "*I don't know, smith. I'm the youngest of seven. I was never the one who knew things.*" He looked down at the bell. "*But I know this. He made it so he'd hear it. Wherever he was. Whatever he was doing. If I was sad, it would ring, and he'd hear, and he'd come.*" His voice cracked. "*He always came. Every time. Until the Weaving.*"

He closed his gnarled hand around it. The ringing muffled. Didn't stop.

"*He's at the Heart*," said Malachi. "*Under the thing that wears him. The real him. What's left. You said so. Your dream. The man at the anvil who couldn't sing.*" He looked at Lex. His shifting eyes were wet and very steady. "*If there's any of him left at all. Any of my brother. And I bring this to him.*"

"*He'll hear it*."

"*He'll hear it*." Malachi opened his hand. The bell rang. *Ting*. *Ting*. "*And maybe, for one moment, he'll come.*"

---

They came down to the grey river on the sixth day.

Lex had heard about it. From Antho, a dozen times, in a dozen tellings. From Seraphine's notes. He'd thought he knew what to expect.

He hadn't.

It wasn't a river. It was the edge of everything. A mile-wide band of slow grey mist, flowing between the world and the dark, so flat and grey and silent that his eyes kept sliding off it. And on the far side, the Hollow. Deep and soft and blue-violet, like the inside of his own eyelids.

He felt the net thin as they came down the grey valley. Felt the Lattice go faint. Fainter. Like the Sundered Lands. Like the bottom of the Rift.

*[Nine percent*,] said Ember. *[Lex. I'm going to lose you again. Soon. On the other side.]*

*I know*.

*[I'll be at the door. Like last time. I'll be right there.]* A pause. *[Lex, the door's going to be right there too. On the other side. In the Hollow. The thing at the bottom of your gold. You're going to be walking toward it.]*

*I know*, thought Lex. *I've known since Malachi said every road goes to the Heart.*

---

The Ferryman was standing by his boat.

Exactly as Antho had described. Tall. Thin as a heron. In a long grey robe and a wide grey hat. Eyeless. Leaning on a long grey pole with an unlit lantern swinging from its tip.

He turned his long face toward them as they came down the shingle. All of them. Lex and Antho. Wren. Ilse, walking alone, her hands clenched. Kestrel and Tamsin. Seraphine with her notebook. Malachi with his ringing bell. Teo. Oriel on her mule. And behind them, filling the grey valley, two storm-dragons.

"*Anthoren Vey*," said the Ferryman.

His voice was like rain on a slate roof.

"*You came back*," he said. "*I said you would. They always come back.*"

"*I promised someone*," said Antho. "*In the Drowned Country. A little girl. I said I'd bring a light.*"

The eyeless face turned toward the Lantern in his hand.

"*So you did*," said the Ferryman. And then, after a long pause: "*She has been waiting. At the edge of your gold light, where you left her. She has not moved.*"

Antho's hand tightened on the Lantern.

---

"*There is a price*," said the Ferryman. "*For each who crosses. The memory you would least wish to lose.*"

Nobody moved.

Lex had known it was coming. Antho had told him. He'd lain awake at night on the road west, thinking about it. *What's my brightest thing?* He didn't know. Wren on the roof of the Spire. His mother's floury hands. Brannoc swinging him up onto the anvil at eleven and saying *this is yours now, lad*. The stone at the millpond.

He didn't want to give up any of them.

"*No*," said Malachi.

Everyone turned.

The old man had come down to the water's edge. He was standing on the grey shingle in front of the Ferryman, small and brown and barefoot, with the silver bell ringing in his hand.

"*Ferryman*," he said. "*You know me.*"

The eyeless face turned toward him. For a long time, the Ferryman didn't speak.

"*Malachi*," he said at last. "*Youngest of seven. Who stood beside his brother at the Weaving and said no.*" A pause. "*I know you. You have never crossed my river.*"

"*No*." Malachi held up the bell. "*But you know where we're going.*"

"*The Heart*."

"*The Heart*." Malachi's shifting eyes were very steady. "*To end the Tithe. To unmake the net. To stop the thing at the bottom of the world from drinking a tenth of every soul.*" He was quiet. "*Every thread it takes falls into your river first. Doesn't it? Every tithe. Every soul's tenth. On its way down.*"

The Ferryman was very still.

"*Eight hundred years*," said Malachi. "*You've had a tenth of the world pouring through your water. Down into the dark. Eight hundred years of other people's mana. Other people's light.*" His voice dropped. "*Your river was clean, before. I remember. I was there when it was clean.*"

The unlit lantern on top of the Ferryman's pole swung gently in the wind off the water.

"*It was*," said the Ferryman. Very quietly. "*It was clean. Before.*"

---

He didn't take a price.

Lex didn't understand at first. The Ferryman simply turned, and stepped into his long grey boat, and held out his pole.

"*Get in*," he said. "*All of you. All who are going to the Heart.*"

"*The fee*—" said Seraphine.

"*Is waived*." The eyeless face turned toward her. "*For this crossing. For this purpose.*" A long pause. "*I have been the Ferryman of this river since before the world was made. I have carried kings and beggars and lovers and the dead. I have taken a great many memories.*" The rain-voice was very soft. "*And for eight hundred years, my river has run grey with the Tithe. I would like it to run clean again before the world ends.*"

He looked at Malachi.

"*And I will take you further than the far shore*," said the Ferryman. "*Further than I have ever taken anyone. Down. Through the Drowned Country. Over the falls at the edge of the deep. To the Heart.*" The lantern swung. "*The river goes there. All rivers do, in the end. I have simply never been asked.*"

---

Antho was the last to get in.

He stood on the shingle with the Lantern in his hand, looking at the Ferryman. And the Ferryman looked back.

"*You said*," said Antho. "*When I paid you. You said if I ever wished to see it again, I could come back to your shore. You couldn't give it back. But you could let me look.*"

"*I did*."

"*Can I*?" Antho's voice was very quiet. "*Now. Before we go.*"

The Ferryman was silent for a long moment. Then he reached out one long grey hand, thin as a heron's leg, and laid it on Antho's forehead.

"*Close your eyes*," he said.

Antho closed them.

And Lex, standing in the grey boat, watched his brother's face change.

He didn't know what Antho saw. He'd never know exactly. But he saw Antho's face go soft. Young. Younger than Lex had seen it in eleven years. He saw his brother's mouth open, very slightly, as if in surprise. He saw his eyes, behind the closed lids, moving. Following something. Something skipping across water.

He saw Antho's lips move. Counting.

*One. Two. Three. Four.*

And then Antho's whole face lit up. From inside. Like a window when a lamp is lit. Like a small freckled boy on a riverbank turning round with the sun coming out on his face.

*Five*.

The Ferryman took his hand away.

Antho opened his eyes. He stood very still on the grey shingle. His face wet.

"*Oh*," he said. Very softly. "*Oh. He was so small.*"

He looked at Lex. Standing in the boat.

"*You were so small*," said Antho. "*And so furious. And then you turned round and your face*—" He stopped. Couldn't go on.

"*Is it back*?" said Lex. His voice cracked.

"*No*." Antho shook his head slowly. "*It's gone again. Already. Like a dream when you wake up.*" He put his hand on his chest. Over the warm empty hollow. "*But I saw it. From the inside. My own eyes.*" His mouth twisted. "*I counted, Lex. Out loud. You couldn't hear me over the heron.*"

He stepped into the boat.

And sat down beside his brother. And put his scarred hand on the back of Lex's neck.

And the Ferryman pushed off from the shore.

---

The Drowned Country was exactly as Antho had described it.

The not-water. The drowned city. Towers of pale grey-green stone. Wide streets of cracked slabs. Doorways with no doors. And the grey dead, drifting through it all, going about errands that had ended a thousand years ago.

The Ferryman poled them down a channel between the towers. The boat slid through the not-water without a sound. Above them, the soft blue-violet dark. Below, the drowned streets.

And in the great square at the center of the city, where Vharuun had lain chained for eleven years, a light.

Gold. Faint. Flickering. Like the last of a candle.

And around it, the dead.

---

Antho was out of the boat before it stopped.

He went down into the not-water of the square with the Lantern held high, the way he had the first time. Walking on the drowned flagstones. And the grey dead parted for him. Slowly. Like mist.

Lex followed him.

In the middle of the square, on the cracked flagstones, there was a little girl.

Faint and grey. Sitting cross-legged. With her hands held out toward something on the ground in front of her. A light. Very small. Very faint. Gold. The last ember of the Hearthglow Antho had left her, months ago, by the edge of the drowned square. She'd carried it here. Into the middle. And sat with it.

Around her, in a ring, other grey dead. Dozens of them. Sitting. Hands held out. Toward the dying light.

The little girl looked up.

She saw the Lantern. Saw it coming toward her through the drowned dark, gold and bright and steady. Saw the man holding it.

And she *smiled*.

Lex saw it. Clear as day. A faint grey face, a child's face, lighting up the way Antho's had on the shore.

"*I said I'd come back*," said Antho. Kneeling. In front of her. Setting the Lantern down on the drowned flagstones beside the dying ember. "*I'm sorry it took so long.*"

The little girl didn't answer. She didn't understand. She just held out her faint grey hands to the new light, and the gold of it poured over her, over all of them, the whole ring of the drowned dead, and they *leaned* into it. Like flowers toward a window.

"*I can't leave it*," said Antho quietly. To Lex. Not looking up. "*The Lantern. I need it. For the Heart.*"

"*I know*."

"*But I promised*."

Lex knelt down beside his brother. He unslung the pack on his back. The one he'd carried from the Hollowmaw, heavy with iron. And took out what was inside.

Lanterns. Six of them. Small, squat, iron. Like his father's. The last six from the Ashbourne Forge, that hadn't cracked on the night the Choir came up, because he'd made them heavier. Not Hush-runes. Something else.

*ARDENT*. The first rune he'd ever learned. The rune that had lit every lantern in the Hollowmaw.

He'd made them on the road west. At night. At a little anvil in the back of a wagon. For this.

He lit them. One by one. With his own fire. And set them down in a ring on the drowned flagstones, around the little girl and the gathered dead.

Gold light. Six little suns. Warm and steady.

*[Hearthglow*,] said Ember. Faint. Barely there. Six percent. *[He's not doing it. You are. With Ardent.]* A pause. *[Lex, that's—that's lovely.]*

The little girl looked at the six new lights. Then up at Lex. Then at Antho.

And very slowly, she reached out one faint grey hand. And touched Antho's sleeve.

Between finger and thumb.

Antho went absolutely still.

"*Oh*," he said. Barely a breath.

The little girl held his sleeve for a moment. Just a moment. And then let go. And turned back to the light, and held out her hands, and was quiet.

Antho knelt there for a long time.

When he stood up, he picked up the Keeper's Lantern. And looked at the six iron lanterns burning in a ring on the drowned flagstones. And the faint grey dead, sitting around them.

"*I'll come back*," he said. "*When this is done. And bring more. And see if anything can be done.*"

He didn't say it to anyone in particular. But the little girl looked up, once, as he walked away.

---

The Drowned Country ended in a waterfall.

Lex didn't know how else to describe it. The Ferryman poled them out of the drowned city and across a long flat expanse of not-water, grey and still, and at the far end of it the not-water simply *stopped*. Went over an edge. Fell. Down. Into a darkness so deep it had no bottom.

It made no sound. The not-water poured over the edge in a vast slow silent curtain, grey and shimmering, a mile wide. Falling. Forever.

"*The edge of the deep*," said the Ferryman. "*Beyond this, the shallows end. Beyond this is the Hollow itself.*" He turned his eyeless face toward them. "*Hold on.*"

And he pushed the boat over the edge.

---

They fell for a long time.

Lex didn't scream. He wanted to. He held onto the gunwale of the long grey boat with both hands and Wren's arm around his waist and his eyes screwed shut, and fell, and the not-water fell with them, a slow grey silent curtain on every side.

And the Lattice went out.

He felt it. All at once. The last faint silver threads of the net, which had been thinning since the Teeth, simply—stopping. Like a candle snuffed. One moment there, faint and far. The next, nothing.

*[Lex*—] said Ember. *[Lex, I'm*—]

And Ember was gone too.

He was alone in his own head. In the dark. Falling.

And at the bottom of him, at the bottom of the gold, at the door—

Warmth.

Getting closer.

---

They landed softly.

The boat simply stopped falling. Lex opened his eyes. They were floating on something. Not water. Not not-water. Something darker. Thicker. Like ink. Spreading out flat and black in every direction under a sky that wasn't a sky.

He looked up.

And saw the Forbidden Lands.

---

It was *empty*.

That was the first thing. Not dark. Not black. *Empty*. A vast nothing, going on forever. No ground. No sky. No horizon. Just the black ink under the boat, and above it, around it, in every direction, an absence. A place where things weren't.

And in the absence, *pieces*.

Floating. Like the slabs of earth in the Sundered Lands. But not earth. *Things*. Fragments. A piece of a forest, a dozen trees, their roots dangling into nothing. Half a house, its rooms open to the void, a table still set for dinner. A street. A hillside with sheep on it, grey and still. A church. A field of wheat. A ship.

Floating. Drifting. Slowly turning in the empty dark.

"*What are they*?" whispered Wren.

"*Eaten*," said Malachi.

He was sitting in the bow of the boat with the bell in his lap. Still ringing. Very softly. His shifting eyes had gone black, all the way. The soft black of the sky between the stars.

"*Everything the hunger ever ate*," he said. "*Before the Weaving. Before the net. Every village. Every forest. Every life.*" He looked out at the drifting pieces. "*It doesn't destroy. It* takes. *And it keeps what it takes. Down here. In the dark. Forever.*"

A little way off, a fragment drifted past. A kitchen. A small one. With a hearth, cold. A table. A chair. And on the chair, a shape. Grey. Faint. A woman. Sitting. Very still. With her hands in her lap.

Waiting for someone to come home.

---

And everywhere, falling, *threads*.

Lex saw them. Even without the Lattice. Even without the Root. Grey. Thin. Thousands of them. Millions. Coming down out of the emptiness above like rain. Like the grey threads he'd felt running down from every Stone in the world. Falling through the void, past the drifting pieces, all at a slant, all in the same direction.

Converging.

He followed them with his eyes. Far off. Very far. Where all the grey threads came together.

And there, in the middle of the empty dark, a light.

Gold. Steady. Bright. A pillar of it. Rising up out of nothing into nothing. Like the pillar in the Wellworld of Flame. Like the column of white fire in the Ashbourne hearth. Like the light that had poured up through the crack in the lid at the bottom of the Rift.

And at its foot, small at this distance, something that looked like a mountain.

White.

With a round open forge on top of it. Under stars too close and too bright.

"*The Heart*," said Malachi.

The bell in his lap rang louder.

---

They went toward it.

The Ferryman poled. The black ink under the boat didn't ripple. The drifting pieces of eaten worlds slid past on either side. A forest. A schoolroom. A bridge, with no river under it. A garden full of grey roses.

Nobody talked.

And as they went, things *noticed* them.

Lex felt it first. The way he'd felt the thinned in the grey valley, in Antho's telling. Attention. From the drifting pieces. From the grey shapes sitting in eaten kitchens and standing in eaten fields. Turning. Slowly. Toward the boat. Toward the light.

Toward the Lantern in Antho's hand. The only gold thing in all that grey.

"*They want it*," said Antho quietly. "*Like the thinned. Like the drowned. They want the light.*"

"*Can you give it to them*?"

Antho looked at the Lantern. Then at the drifting pieces. Thousands of them. Going on forever.

"*Not all of them*," he said. "*Not now.*" His voice was very low. "*Later. If there is a later.*"

---

The Knight was waiting at the foot of the white mountain.

Lex saw it from a long way off. A shape. Standing on the black ink at the place where the white mountain rose out of it. Tall. Black and silver. Four arms.

Waiting.

And as the boat came closer, he saw what it was holding.

Not swords. Not shields. Its four arms were wrapped around something. Holding it against its chest. Close. The way you hold a child.

Ivo.

Lex heard Ilse make a sound. A tiny sound. Like something tearing.

Ivo Marrow was alive. Lex could see him breathing. Held in the Twinned Knight's four black arms against its armored chest. His eyes were closed. His face was white. His mouse-brown hair was hanging over his forehead.

And he was *going in*.

Lex saw it. As the boat came closer. Saw where the Knight's black armor touched Ivo's body. His shoulder. His side. His arm. Where they touched, Ivo was *faint*. Blurred. Overlapping. Like a drawing someone had traced twice, slightly off. Like the two dead brothers inside the armor.

The Knight was pouring him in.

Slowly. A little at a time. Into the armor. Into the place where two brothers had been poured together for three hundred years.

To make three.

---

The Ferryman stopped the boat.

Fifty paces from the white mountain. On the black ink. Facing the Knight.

"*I can take you no further*," he said. "*The Heart is not my river. It is his.*" The eyeless face turned toward the white mountain. Toward the forge on its summit. Toward the pillar of gold. "*From here, you walk.*"

Nobody moved.

And then Ilse Marrow stood up in the boat.

She was very calm. Lex saw it. All the shaking had gone out of her. All the crying. She stood in the long grey boat on the black ink of the Forbidden Lands, in her patched green coat, with her hands open at her sides. Looking at the Twinned Knight. At her brother, in its arms.

"*He made it look like nothing*," she said. Very quietly. "*His wrist. Where I was holding on.*" She looked at her hands. "*And my hands believed him.*"

She stepped out of the boat.

Onto the black ink. It held her. Lex didn't know how. She walked on it the way Wren walked on the wind. Toward the Knight.

"*I'm a thief*," said Ilse Marrow. Walking. "*An illusionist. I make things look like what they're not.*" She didn't hurry. "*It's the only thing I'm any good at. That and stealing pies.*"

The Twinned Knight's helm turned toward her. Both heads. Two pairs of eyes glinting through the narrow slit.

"*The other one*," said one voice. Wonderingly.

"*The sister*," said the other. "*She came.*"

"*Of course she came*," said Ilse. She stopped. Ten paces from the Knight. Looking up at it. At her brother, faint and overlapping in its four black arms. "*You'd have come. Wouldn't you. If it was your brother.*"

The Knight was very still.

"*We'd have come*," said one voice. Slowly.

"*We did come*," said the other. "*Into the river. When it rose. We came for each other.*"

"*And drowned*."

"*And drowned*."

"*I know*," said Ilse. Very gently. "*I know the ballad. Everyone knows the ballad.*" She looked at Ivo. Then back up at the helm. "*Brannoc sang it in the forge. Badly. The Knights of the Ford. Who held the crossing back to back and wouldn't let go.*"

She took a breath.

"*He sang the last verse wrong*," said Ilse Marrow. "*Every time. Lex told me. He always forgot the last verse.*" She tilted her head. "*Do you remember it? The last verse? How it really goes?*"

The Twinned Knight didn't answer.

But its four black arms, holding her brother, went very, very still.

And behind Lex, in the long grey boat on the black ink at the bottom of the world, Malachi's little silver bell rang, once, very clearly.

Like a note given before a hymn begins.
