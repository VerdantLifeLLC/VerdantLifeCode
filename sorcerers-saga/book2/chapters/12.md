# The Queen's Advisor

Wren Ashdown had slept in a great many uncomfortable places.

Under hedges. In ditches. In the crook of an oak forty feet up, tied to the trunk with her own belt so she wouldn't fall out. On the back of a dragon at night over the Greyspine, with the wind screaming and a large warm smith holding onto her so she didn't slide off. In a cell in the Spire, once, for four days, on a floor so cold she'd stopped being able to feel her feet on the second.

The corridor outside Arion Vey's rooms was not the worst. But it was in the top five.

It was stone. Everything in the Spire was stone. Grey, cold, polished smooth by nine hundred years of Wardens' boots. The corridor ran the length of the fortieth floor of the great white tower, with tall narrow windows on one side looking out over the golden city, and doors on the other. Most of the doors were empty now. Since Kell fled, half the Order had gone with her, or gone home, or gone quiet. The fortieth floor had been the High Council's floor. Now it was mostly dust and echoes and Arion.

And Wren. Sitting on a folded blanket with her back against the wall beside his door, her bow across her knees, an arrow on the string, and her eyes on the corridor.

Fifty-three nights.

She'd counted. She'd picked that up from Antho, years ago. The counting. It helped, somehow. It made the nights into something with edges.

*Fifty-three nights*, she thought. *And not one assassin. Not one. Not so much as a cross look from a footman.*

*He's paranoid. He's an old man who's frightened of shadows, and he's got me sleeping on a floor for nothing, while Lex is out there somewhere with—*

She stopped that thought. She'd learned to stop that thought. It didn't go anywhere good.

---

She remembered the hour in his rooms.

Fifty-four days ago. The morning after the festival. She'd woken in the castle guest-room with Lex's note on the pillow beside her—*I'm sorry. I love you. Stay safe. Stay.*—and she'd read it twice, and said a word that would have made Brannoc blush, and pulled her boots on, and strung her bow, and gone to the window.

She'd had one leg over the sill. The wind already under her, already lifting. Windstep. She could have been over the Ironbacks by nightfall.

And a hand had closed on her arm.

Arion Vey. Standing in the doorway of the guest-room in his plain grey coat, with the dawn light on his lean face. She hadn't heard him come in. She never heard him come in.

"*Wren*," he'd said. "*Please. Give me an hour.*"

She'd almost pulled away. She'd almost gone anyway.

But she'd looked at his face. And it was Lex's face. Older, leaner, harder. But the same jaw. The same brows. The same line between the eyebrows, the thinking line, that Lex got when something was wrong.

And Arion Vey, Warden-Architect, the most powerful sorcerer in Caldor, was afraid.

So she'd given him an hour.

---

"*Someone is going to try to kill me*," he'd said.

They'd been in his rooms in the Spire. A big cold round room at the top of the tower, full of books and maps and instruments she didn't understand, with a fire that never seemed to warm anything. He'd sat in a chair by the fire. She'd stood by the window with her arms folded.

"*Before winter*," he said. "*I don't know who. I don't know how. But I know it's coming.*"

"*How?*"

"*Because it's what I'd do.*" His voice was very quiet. "*If I were Elric. Or Sorn. Or Kell. If I wanted the Registration Act passed, and the Unbound listed, and the Queen without her strongest voice on the Council.*" He looked into the fire. "*I'm the only thing standing between Sorn and his majority, Wren. Lysandra can't hold the Council alone. Not yet. She's too young, and the old houses don't trust her.*"

"*So hire guards.*"

"*I have guards. Roole's best. Twelve of them, in shifts.*" He shook his head. "*They're good men. But they won't see it coming. Whatever it is.*" He looked up at her. "*You will.*"

She'd stared at him.

"*You want me to stay*," she said slowly. "*Here. In the Spire. Guarding you. While Lex walks into—whatever's up there. Alone.*"

"*Yes.*"

"*No.*"

"*Wren—*"

"*No. Absolutely not. I'm going after him. He's an idiot and he left me a note on a pillow like some kind of—*" She'd stopped. Breathed. "*He thinks I'm the brightest thread he knows. He thinks if I come, Elric will use me against him.*"

"*He's right*," said Arion quietly.

She'd turned on him.

"*He's right*," Arion had said again. He didn't flinch. "*You're Unbound. You're Epic. You're a Mistwalker at level fifty-two with Gale at Speaking. Your thread shines like a beacon.*" His grey eyes held hers. "*And you're the thing Lex loves most in the world. If Elric gets near you, he won't need to fight my son. He'll only need to hold your thread and wait.*"

She hadn't said anything. She couldn't.

"*I can't stop you going*," said Arion. "*No one can stop you doing anything. I've seen that.*" Something moved at the corner of his mouth. "*But I'm asking you to stay. Not for me. For him. So that when he comes home, there's still something here to come home to.*"

And then, so quietly she almost didn't hear:

"*And because I'm afraid, Wren. I've never said that to anyone. Not in two hundred years.*"

She'd heard *two hundred*. She'd thought it was a figure of speech.

She'd stayed.

---

Fifty-three nights.

She sat in the corridor outside his door with her bow across her knees, and listened to the Spire breathing around her. The creak of the old stone settling. The wind moaning in the windows. The faint footsteps of the guard shifts changing, three floors down.

And from behind Arion's door, very faintly, the sound of his voice.

He talked at night. She'd noticed it the first week. Not to anyone. There was no one in there. She'd checked, the first time, slipping the lock with a hairpin and peering in. He'd been sitting in his chair by the cold fire, alone, with his eyes closed and his lips moving.

She couldn't hear the words. Not usually. Just the murmur of his voice, low and steady, going on for an hour or two hours at a time. Like someone talking to a sick person by their bedside.

Some nights, she caught a word. A phrase.

*Hold on.*

*Not much longer.*

*I know. I know, love. I know.*

She'd never asked him about it. It wasn't the kind of thing you asked.

But she'd thought about it. Every night. Lying in the corridor with her bow across her knees.

*Love*.

---

The letter came on the fifty-fourth morning.

She was on the roof when the courier found her. She went up there at dawn, every dawn, after the night watch, to stretch her legs and feel the wind. The Spire's roof was a flat circle of white stone, eight hundred feet above the city, with a low parapet and nothing else. No one went up there except her and the pigeons.

The courier was a girl of perhaps twelve, out of breath from the climb, in a tinker's patched coat. She held out a folded square of paper, sealed with a blob of candle-wax.

"*From the Bitter Hills*," she said. "*For the red-haired lady who sleeps on the floor.*" She looked Wren up and down. "*That's you, right?*"

"*That's me*."

"*He said you'd be grumpy*." The girl grinned. "*He said to tell you he's sorry and he loves you and you were right.*" She considered. "*He said that last bit twice.*"

Wren took the letter.

She didn't open it until the girl had gone. Then she sat down on the parapet of the Spire, with her back to the eight-hundred-foot drop and her feet dangling over the roof, and broke the seal.

*Wren—*

*I'm an idiot. You said so in your note and you were right.*

She read it through once, fast, the way she read everything. Then again, slowly. Then a third time, very slowly, one word at a time.

*I love you. I'm saying it now. Properly.*

She sat on the parapet of the Spire with the letter in her hands and the dawn wind in her hair and laughed. Out loud. And then, without any warning at all, she was crying. Great ugly gulping sobs, the kind she hadn't cried since she was eight and her mother had gone into the dark and not come out for a year.

The pigeons all flew away.

*Idiot*, she thought. *Idiot, idiot, stupid great lump of a smith. Two months, and you write me this, and you're halfway across the world where I can't hit you.*

She folded the letter very small and put it inside her shirt, against her heart.

*There's a fire-witch who's even angrier than you are*.

*Well*, she thought. *We'll see about that.*

---

There'd been another letter in the courier's bag. For Arion. The same blob of candle-wax. The same handwriting.

She took it to him.

He was at his desk, in the big cold round room, writing. He always seemed to be writing. Speeches for the Council. Letters to the Queen. Notes in a small hand in the margins of old books. He looked up when she came in.

"*From Lex*," she said, and held it out.

His face changed. Just for a moment. Like a door opening and closing. He took the letter. He broke the seal.

She watched him read it.

It was short. She could see that from across the desk. A few lines. She watched his eyes move down them. And she watched his face go white.

Not pale. *White*. All the color draining out of it in a heartbeat, like water from a cracked jug. His hand, holding the letter, went very still.

"*What*?" said Wren. "*What is it? Is he hurt?*"

Arion didn't answer.

He read the letter again. Then he folded it, carefully, along its creases. And held it over the candle on his desk. And watched it burn.

"*Arion.*"

"*He's not hurt*," said Arion. His voice was very quiet. "*He's alive. He's going south. To the Sunreach.*" He watched the last of the paper curl and blacken. "*He's found the second piece. Or knows where it is.*"

"*That's not what made you go white.*"

He looked at her. A long look.

"*No*," he said. "*It isn't.*"

She waited.

"*He asked me a question*," said Arion at last. "*He asked if I knew something. Something about the Root.*" He looked down at the ash on his desk. "*I didn't. I swear to you, Wren, I didn't know.*" A pause. "*But I should have guessed. I should have guessed two hundred years ago.*"

There it was again. *Two hundred years*.

Wren didn't ask. Not yet.

But she looked at him. Properly. The way she looked at a forest when something in it was wrong. *Don't look for the deer. Look for where the forest is wrong.* And she saw it. Saw what she'd been not-seeing for fifty-four nights.

He hadn't changed.

She'd known him for three years now. Since Duskhold. Since he came out of the crystal. And in three years, Arion Vey had not changed by a single hair. Not a line on his face. Not a grey thread in his dark hair. Lex had grown a beard and shaved it off and grown it again. Antho had gone from a gaunt wild-eyed wreck to a lean grave man. The Queen had gone from a princess to a monarch, and it showed around her eyes. Wren herself had a scar on her chin now, and a little crease beside her mouth that hadn't been there at nineteen.

Arion Vey looked exactly as he had the day he stepped out of the crystal at Duskhold. Forty-five, perhaps. Lean and dark and tired.

Exactly the same.

---

The Council met on the fifty-sixth day.

Wren went. She always went. She stood at the back of the High Council chamber in her patched coat with her bow on her shoulder, and the old lords and ladies looked at her sideways and muttered, and she ignored them.

The chamber was round, like everything in the Spire. A great domed hall with tiers of carved wooden benches rising up on every side. At the bottom, in the center, a round table of black stone, and around it the twelve chairs of the High Council. The Queen's chair, carved with the lion of Caldor. Arion's, plain grey, at her right hand. The rest held by the great houses of the realm.

And at the Queen's left hand, in a chair of red wood carved with grapevines, Lord Chancellor Varric Sorn.

Wren had disliked him on sight, three years ago, at the first festival. She disliked him more every time she saw him. He was perhaps fifty, tall and thin and elegant, with silver hair swept back from a high forehead and a neat pointed silver beard. He wore black velvet, always, with a single grey armband on his left sleeve. He had a beautiful voice. Deep and warm and reasonable. He used it the way Wren used her bow.

"*Majesty*," he was saying. "*My lords. I do not ask this lightly.*"

*Yes, you do*, thought Wren.

"*Fifty-five of our people*," said Sorn. "*Gone. Unwritten. Their families do not even remember their names.*" His voice dropped, grave and sorrowful. "*And every one of them was Unbound. Every one. Freed from the Ceiling by our young Forgeheart, with the best intentions in the world, and left shining in the dark like a lamp in a window for anything that hunts to find.*"

Murmurs, around the tiers. Nods.

"*I do not blame Lex Vey*," said Sorn. "*I want to be very clear on that. He is a hero. He freed us from the Pale Warden. He is young, and brave, and did what he thought was right.*" He spread his long white hands. "*But a lamp left burning in a window draws moths. And now it draws something worse.*"

He looked around the chamber.

"*The Registration Act does not cage anyone*," he said. "*It does not Ceiling anyone. It only asks that every Unbound sorcerer in Caldor be known to the Crown. Their name. Their class. Where they live.*" His voice was very gentle. "*So that when the unwriter comes, the Crown can stand between him and our people.*"

*So that the Crown has a list*, thought Wren. *Of every bright thread in the kingdom. With addresses.*

*And so that whoever holds the list knows exactly where to go.*

Arion stood up.

He didn't raise his voice. He never did. He stood at the black stone table in his plain grey coat with his hands flat on its surface, the way Lex stood at an anvil, and the chamber went quiet.

"*My lord Chancellor*," he said. "*How many of the fifty-five were registered with the Crown before they were taken?*"

Sorn's face didn't change. "*None, my lord. That is precisely the point—*"

"*How many were registered with the Order? On the Stones? In the Lattice records?*"

A pause. "*All of them, I should think. Every Kindled person is.*"

"*All of them*," said Arion. "*Every one of the fifty-five was written in the records of the Lattice. With their name, and their class, and where they lived.*" He looked around the chamber. "*And the man who took them found every single one.*"

Silence.

"*He doesn't need a list, my lords*," said Arion quietly. "*He has one. The Lattice is a list. Every Stone in Caldor is a list.*" He looked at Sorn. "*A second list does not protect anyone. It only gives a second copy to whoever wants it.*" A pause. "*I wonder who that might be.*"

The chamber erupted.

Wren watched Sorn's face. While the lords shouted, while the Queen called for order, while the old Duke of Harlowe stood up and shook his fist. She watched the Lord Chancellor's elegant face, under his silver hair.

And for one moment, just one, she saw something move in it. Not anger. Something colder.

He looked at Arion, across the black stone table, the way Wren looked at a deer before she loosed.

---

The Act failed. Six to five, with the Queen abstaining as custom required. Arion's vote, and the Queen's four loyal houses, and the Duke of Harlowe, who had changed his mind halfway through Arion's speech.

"*It'll come back*," said Elowen Dunmore, walking with Wren down the long spiral stair of the Spire after the session. "*Sorn'll bring it back. Next month. And the month after. Until he has his six.*"

Elowen was Garrick's daughter. Twenty-eight, broad-shouldered and square-jawed, with her father's steady grey eyes and his habit of saying exactly what she thought. She was Knight-Captain of the Queen's Guard now, and she wore Oathkeeper on her back, the great plain sword that had been her father's. Wren had liked her from the first day. She was one of the few people in Highcrest who didn't look at Wren sideways.

"*I know*," said Wren.

"*And your father-in-law-to-be has just made himself the most hated man in the room.*"

"*He's not my—*" Wren stopped. Scowled. "*He's not anything to me. He's Lex's father.*"

Elowen looked at her sideways. "*Mm*."

"*Don't* mm *me, Dunmore.*"

"*I didn't say anything.*" Elowen's mouth twitched. "*Only that you've slept outside his door for fifty-six nights, and you carry a letter around inside your shirt that you think nobody's noticed, and every time anyone says the word* smith *you go red.*"

Wren went red.

"*There*," said Elowen. "*Like that.*"

"*I will put an arrow in you.*"

"*You'd miss*," said Elowen comfortably. "*You'd be thinking about him.*" She clapped Wren on the shoulder with one big hand. "*Get some sleep, Ashdown. Real sleep, in a bed. I'll put my best men on his door tonight.*"

Wren didn't. She never did.

That was the night they came.

---

She heard it at the turn of the night.

The dead hour. Three hours after midnight, when the Spire was at its quietest, when even the guards three floors down had stopped talking and were only leaning on their spears. She was sitting in the corridor outside Arion's door with her bow across her knees and her eyes half closed. Not asleep. She never slept, on watch. Just resting. Listening.

And the Spire's breathing changed.

It was nothing. A creak. Not the usual creak of stone settling. A creak with a *weight* to it. A footstep, very soft, on old stone. Somewhere down the corridor. In the dark, past the last window, where the moonlight didn't reach.

Wren didn't move. She didn't open her eyes all the way. She slowed her breathing. In. Out. Like a sleeper.

And she *listened*.

Not with her ears. With Gale. The wind in the corridor. The tiny drafts that moved along the floor and under the doors and through the cracks in the old stone. She'd learned it at the Stillwater, from Malachi, three years ago. *The wind touches everything, lightfoot. Listen to what it touches.*

It touched something. Three somethings. Coming down the corridor in the dark, very slowly, very softly. Pressed against the wall. Moving the way she moved when she hunted. Toe first. Weight forward.

And it touched something else. Something cold. Something that moved through the air of the corridor the way a stone moves through water, pushing the drafts aside.

Metal. Chains. Heavy, fine, silver-black links. She knew that feel. She'd felt it once before, in Hedda Vorne's shop in Highcrest, three years ago, coiled around Hedda's body like a snake. Cutting her off from everything. Every skill. Every stat. Every scrap of magic. Leaving her a level-one nobody, slumped against a counter.

Severance chains.

Wren opened her eyes.

---

There were three of them. In grey. Not Keepers' grey. A darker grey, like ash, with hoods pulled low and cloth over their faces. They were ten paces away and closing, and the one in front had a coil of silver-black chain over his shoulder, and the other two had short swords, and none of them had expected her to be awake.

She shot the one with the chain first.

She didn't stand up. She didn't need to. She drew from sitting and loosed, and the arrow took him through the thigh and pinned him to the floor, and he went down with a shriek and the chain spilled off his shoulder onto the stone with a sound like a bag of coins.

The other two came at her.

She was on her feet before the first one reached her. She didn't draw again; there wasn't time. She swung the bow, two-handed, like a staff, and cracked him across the side of the head with the upper limb, and he staggered. The second one lunged. Short sword, low, at her belly. She stepped back. Not on the floor.

On the *wind*.

Windstep. Up. Into the air of the corridor, three feet off the ground, the draft under her boots holding her like a step on a stair. The sword went under her. The man stumbled forward, off balance, his face turning up toward her in shock.

She kicked him in it.

He went down. She came down on top of him, landed on his back, drove both knees into his spine, and heard the breath go out of him in a great *oof*. She had her knife out and at his throat before he could get it back.

"*Don't*," she said.

He didn't.

The one she'd hit with the bow was getting up. Swaying. Groping for his sword on the floor. She looked at him.

"*Really?*" she said.

He looked at her. At his friend, under her knees, with a knife at his throat. At the other friend, pinned to the floor by an arrow through the thigh, moaning.

He ran.

She let him. She could have shot him. She didn't want to leave the man under her knees. She heard him go clattering away down the corridor, toward the stair. And then she heard him stop.

And heard Elowen Dunmore's voice, from the stairwell, say very calmly: "*Evening.*" And then a meaty thud. And then nothing.

---

Arion's door opened.

He stood in it in his shirtsleeves, with a candle in his hand. He hadn't been asleep either. She could see that. He looked down at the corridor. At the man pinned to the floor by an arrow, moaning. At the coil of silver-black chain lying on the stone. At Wren, kneeling on the back of an assassin with a knife at his throat.

His face didn't change.

"*Severance*," he said quietly. Looking at the chain.

"*They were going to put it on you*," said Wren. "*In your sleep. And then—*" She didn't finish.

"*And then I'd be nobody*," said Arion. "*No Authority. No Root. No Prerogatives. A level-one man in a nightshirt.*" He looked at the chain for a long moment. "*Very easy to kill. Or to carry away.*"

Elowen came up the corridor from the stair, dragging the third man by the collar like a sack of grain. He was unconscious. Behind her came four of her guards, swords out, looking furious and embarrassed.

"*Got past us on the thirty-eighth floor*," said Elowen grimly. "*Came up the outside of the tower. On ropes. From the roof.*" She dropped the unconscious man at Wren's feet. "*The roof, Ashdown. Eight hundred feet. Who comes up the outside of the Spire on a rope?*"

"*Someone who knew there'd be a guard on the stair*," said Wren.

She was looking at the man under her knees. She reached down with her free hand and pulled the cloth off his face.

He was young. Twenty, perhaps. Ordinary. A face you'd pass in the street. And on the sleeve of his ash-grey coat, half hidden under the cuff, a band of grey cloth.

And pinned to the band, very small, a silver brooch. In the shape of a key.

---

The man with the arrow in his thigh talked. Eventually. In a cell in the lower Spire, with Elowen standing over him and Wren sitting on the edge of the cot, turning an arrow over and over in her fingers.

He didn't know much. He'd been hired in the Lower Town, in a tavern, by a man in a mask. Fifty crowns. Fifty more when the job was done. The chain had been given to them in a sack. The rope, the hour, the route up the outside of the tower, all of it written on a paper he'd burned.

"*And the brooch?*" said Wren.

He wouldn't look at it.

"*The Concord*," he said at last. Very low. "*They said we were the Concord now. That when it was done, there'd be a new order. No more Unbound. No more wildcasters. Everything back the way it was.*" He swallowed. "*They said the Ceiling would come back. That the Key would bring it back. That the world would be safe again.*"

"*Who said?*"

"*I don't know. The man in the mask. I don't know.*" He was shaking. "*He said the night of the masks. When it's done, the night of the masks, everything changes.*"

Elowen and Wren looked at each other.

"*The night of the masks*," said Elowen slowly. "*Sorn's masquerade. At his house on the Crown Terrace. The whole court's invited. The whole Council. The Queen.*" Her square face had gone very still. "*It's in five days.*"

---

Wren went back up to the fortieth floor at dawn.

She didn't sit down in the corridor. She knocked on Arion's door. And when he said *come in*, she went in, and shut the door behind her, and leaned against it with her arms folded.

He was in his chair by the cold fire. He looked up at her. He looked very tired.

"*I want to know*," she said.

"*Know what?*"

"*Everything.*" She didn't move from the door. "*Two hundred years. You keep saying it. I thought it was a figure of speech. It's not, is it.*" She stared at him. "*You haven't aged a day since Duskhold. Not a hair. Not a line. Three years.*" Her voice was very flat. "*How old are you, Arion?*"

He was quiet for a long time.

Then he got up. Slowly. And went to the far wall of the round room, where a tall bookcase stood, crammed with old leather spines. He put his hand on one of the books. Pulled it. Not out. *Down*.

And the bookcase swung open.

---

There was a room behind it.

Small. Round. Windowless. Lit by a single Lattice-lamp on a hook, silver and steady. It smelled of dust and old paper and lavender.

And on the walls, portraits.

Dozens of them. Hung frame to frame, from floor to ceiling, all the way round. Old oil paintings in gilt frames, cracked and darkened with age. Charcoal sketches on yellowed paper. A miniature on ivory, no bigger than her palm. A great dark canvas taller than she was.

All of them the same man.

Lean. Dark-haired. Grey-eyed. Tired. In a dozen different costumes from a dozen different centuries. In the white and silver robes of a High Warden, two hundred years out of fashion. In the plain brown coat of a country scholar. In the black velvet and white ruff of a courtier, from the reign of some king Wren had only read about in Seraphine's books. In armor. In rags. In the grey of the Order.

Arion Vey. Over and over. Never older. Never younger. The same face, staring out of every frame.

Wren stood in the doorway of the little round room and didn't breathe.

She went to the biggest one. The great dark canvas. A man in the white and silver robes of a High Warden, standing beside a Lattice Stone, one hand resting on it. Behind him, through a painted window, a city on a mountain. Highcrest. But wrong. Smaller. No Spire. Where the Spire should have been, there was scaffolding. Half a tower, white, rising out of a forest of wooden poles.

There was a brass plate at the bottom of the frame. She bent to read it.

*WARDEN-ARCHITECT ARION VEY*
*upon the laying of the first stone of the Spire*
*in the reign of King Osric the Second*

Osric the Second.

She knew that name. Seraphine had made her read the king-lists once, on a long boring night on the Old Founders' Road, as a cure for Wren's insomnia. It had worked. But some of it had stuck.

*Osric the Second. Two hundred years ago. More.*

She turned around.

Arion stood in the doorway of the little round room. In his shirtsleeves. With the silver lamplight on his face. The same face as the man in the painting, standing beside a Lattice Stone while they built the Spire.

"*Two hundred and thirty-one*," he said quietly. "*I'll be two hundred and thirty-two at midwinter.*"

---

She sat down on the floor.

She didn't mean to. Her legs just went. She sat on the dusty floor of the little round room with her back against the wall under a portrait of Arion Vey in a white ruff, and stared at him.

He came and sat down too. On the floor. Across from her. With his back against the opposite wall, under a charcoal sketch of himself in rags.

"*The Root*," he said. "*It keeps you. As long as it needs you.*" He looked at his hands. "*I took it when I was thirty-one. From my father, when he died. And I stopped. Just—stopped. The way a clock stops.*" He was quiet. "*I didn't know, at first. It took ten years before I noticed. Twenty before anyone else did.*"

"*And then?*"

"*And then I moved. Every thirty years or so. Changed my name. My face, a little. Hair. Beard. Spectacles.*" Something that might have been a smile. "*Arion Vey the scholar. Arion Vey the Warden. Arion Vey's son. Arion Vey's nephew. Arion Vey's grandson.*" He gestured at the portraits. "*I kept these because I was afraid I'd forget which one I was.*"

"*Does Lex know?*"

"*No.*"

"*Antho?*"

"*No.*" He looked at her. "*Seren knew. No one else. Not Elric. Not in forty years. He thought I was a man who'd been lucky with his looks.*" His mouth twisted. "*He used to tease me about it. Arion, you vain old peacock, what's your secret.*" He was quiet. "*That's part of why he hated me, I think. In the end. That I never got older. That I'd always be there, first, at the front, while he got old and grey and slow behind me.*"

Wren stared at him.

"*Will they?*" she said. "*Lex. Antho. They've got the Root too. Will they stop?*"

Arion was silent for a long time.

"*I don't know*," he said at last. "*I've been wondering that since Duskhold. Since I came out of the crystal and saw them both with gold in their eyes.*" He looked at her. "*I hope not. I've never hoped for anything so much.*"

"*Why?*"

"*Because it isn't a gift, Wren.*" His voice was very low. "*It's a sentence. You watch everyone you love grow old and die. You stand at the front while the world goes by. You wait.*" He looked at the portraits. "*And something at the bottom of the gold watches you while you wait. And whispers.*"

Wren went very still.

"*That's what Lex asked you*," she said. "*In the letter. Isn't it.*"

Arion closed his eyes.

"*He asked if I knew that something was watching through the Root. Through his eyes.*" His voice was barely a breath. "*And I didn't. I swear to you I didn't. But I've heard it whisper. For two hundred years. At night. When I'm tired.*" He opened his eyes. "*I always thought it was my own mind. My own loneliness. I thought every man who'd lived as long as me would hear voices, at the end.*"

"*And now?*"

"*And now my son has found a door at the bottom of the Root*," said Arion Vey, "*with something on the other side of it. Looking out.*" He looked at her. "*And I'm two hundred and thirty-one years old, and I'm more frightened than I've ever been in my life.*"

---

She didn't ask about the voice at night. The one she heard through his door. *Hold on, love. Not much longer.*

She thought about asking. She almost did. She opened her mouth.

And then she looked at him. Sitting on the dusty floor of a secret room, surrounded by two hundred years of his own face, with his hands lying open and empty in his lap. And she thought about what Lex would say. Lex, who couldn't lie. Lex, who always asked.

And she thought about what Antho would say. Antho, who knew what it was to have a door inside you.

*Not today*, she thought. *He's told me one thing. That's enough for one morning.*

She got up. She held out her hand.

He looked at it. Then took it. She pulled him to his feet. He was lighter than she expected.

"*Five days*," she said. "*Sorn's masquerade. The night of the masks. Something's going to happen there.*"

"*Yes.*"

"*You're going.*"

"*Of course I'm going.*" He looked at her. "*I'm the Queen's advisor. If I don't go, Sorn knows I know.*"

"*Then I'm going too.*"

"*Wren—*"

"*In a mask*," she said. "*And a dress, if I have to. I've never worn a dress to a party. I'll probably fall over.*" She narrowed her eyes. "*But I'll be there. With a knife in my garter and my bow behind the curtains. And if anyone so much as looks at you sideways, I'll put an arrow in them before they finish blinking.*"

Arion looked at her for a long moment.

And then he laughed. Properly. For the first time in fifty-six days. A short, startled, helpless laugh, the kind that comes out of you before you can stop it.

"*Seren*," he said, "*would have liked you very much.*"

Wren didn't know what to say to that. So she didn't say anything. She just nodded, once, and turned and walked out of the little round room full of portraits, and back out into the cold round room with the fire that never warmed, and back out to the corridor.

She sat down on her folded blanket. She put her bow across her knees.

Fifty-seven nights.

She took the letter out of her shirt and read it again.

*Stay with Father, if that's where you are. Something's wrong with him, I think, and he'd never say so.*

*Oh, Hammer*, she thought. *You have no idea.*

---

Two days later, a raven came.

Not Quill. A Keeper's raven, sleek and grey, with Roole's seal on the message tied to its leg. Elowen brought it up the stair at a run and found Wren on the roof, and thrust it into her hands without a word.

*Ashdown —*

*Lex is back in Caldor. Crossed the Scald four days ago with a party of six. Coming north fast on Sunreach horses. Says the Key is in two pieces now and the old man has both. Says he's coming to Highcrest before he goes to the Hollowmaw.*

*Antho's back at the Keep. He's brought a dragon. A big one. You'll have to see it to believe it.*

*Kell's been seen in the north with three hundred men in grey. Ash-grey. Not mine.*

*Watch the masquerade. Watch everything. I'm sending you Fenn and twenty Keepers.*

*— D. Roole*

Wren read it twice. Then she looked out from the roof of the Spire, south, across the golden city and the green river valley and the long blue hills, toward the Scald and the Sunreach and the road that came up out of them.

*Coming north fast*.

*Coming to Highcrest*.

She didn't realize she was smiling until Elowen said, very dryly, behind her:

"*You've gone red again, Ashdown.*"
