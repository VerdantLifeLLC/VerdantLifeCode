# What Wil Did Not Say

Wil did not sleep, the nights after the fire. I knew because I did not sleep either, and every night, when I climbed out onto the roof of Merra's house to sit in the cold and stare at the black hole in the skyline where the Eolian had been, he was already there.

He sat on the ridge of the roof with his bandaged hands resting on his knees, palms up, the way the physician had told him to keep them. He did not look at me when I came up. He did not say anything. I sat down beside him and did not say anything either.

We did that for two nights. On the third, he spoke.

"Sim was downstairs today," he said. "With her."

I did not ask who. There was only one *her*, those days.

"He read to her," said Wil. "For three hours. Poetry. That dreadful Eld Vintic stuff he likes, with all the rhymes." He was quiet for a moment. "She laughed at one of them. The first time she's laughed since. It was a very small laugh. It hurt her; she coughed for a long time afterward. But she laughed."

"That's good," I said.

"Yes." Wil looked down at his hands. "It's very good."

---

The wind came across the rooftops, cold and damp, smelling of river and ash. Down in the street below, a cart went by, its wheels loud on the cobbles. Somewhere a dog barked, and stopped.

"I sat with her all yesterday," said Wil. "And the day before. And the day before that. I didn't read to her. I don't know any poetry. I just sat." He turned his hands over, carefully, so that the bandaged palms faced down. "She was asleep, mostly. Sometimes she woke up and looked at me and didn't say anything. And then she went back to sleep."

I waited.

"And then Sim came in today," said Wil, "with his book. And she woke up, and saw him, and her whole face—" He stopped.

He did not finish. He did not need to.

---

I had not known. That is the truth, and I am ashamed of it. I had known Wil for years by then. I had eaten with him, drunk with him, studied with him, fought beside him. I had trusted him with my life more than once. And I had never known.

I looked at him now, sitting on the ridge of the roof in the cold, with his dark Cealdish face very still and his burned hands resting on his knees. And I thought about him going down the cellar stairs of the burning Eolian, into the smoke and the dark, alone, when everyone else was running the other way. I thought about him kneeling beside her and begging her to let go. I thought about him taking her wrists, and pressing his own hands against the hot stone, and pulling.

I thought about what it must have cost. Not his hands. The other thing.

"How long?" I said quietly.

Wil did not answer for a long time.

"Since the first term," he said at last. "Since the first day she came into the Archives. She had a stack of books on Eld Vintic grammar, and she dropped them all on the stairs, and I helped her pick them up." He almost smiled. "She said thank you. And she smiled at me. And that was that."

"Wil—"

"And then she met Sim," said Wil. "A span later. In the same stairwell. He dropped *his* books." Wil almost smiled. "On purpose, I think. He'd seen me do it."

"She looked at him," said Wil, "the way she looked at him today. Downstairs. The first time she ever saw him." He shrugged, a small careful movement that did not jar his hands. "And he looked at her the same way. And I thought, well. There it is."

---

"You never told her," I said.

"No."

"Why not?"

Wil turned his head and looked at me. His dark eyes were very calm.

"Because she was happy," he said simply. "And Sim was happy. And they were happy together, in that stupid, sweet, ridiculous way that makes everyone around them want to throw things." He looked back out at the rooftops. "What would telling her have done? Made her feel guilty. Made Sim feel awkward. Made everything worse, for everyone, so that I could say something I wanted to say." He shook his head. "No. It was better not to."

"That's very noble."

"It's not noble." Wil's voice was flat. "It's just arithmetic. Three people unhappy, or one. I can count, Kvothe. I'm Cealdish." He paused. "And I wasn't unhappy. Not really. Not most of the time. I got to be her friend. I got to sit with her in the Archives and argue about Eld Vintic declensions. I got to see her laugh." He looked down at his hands. "That was enough. It was more than enough."

"And now?"

He did not answer for a long time.

"Now I've got hands that won't close properly," he said at last, "and she's alive, and she's laughing at Sim's terrible poetry." He smiled, very slightly. "I'd call that a fair trade."

---

We sat there for a long time. The wind blew. The stars came out, one by one, cold and far away. In the black gap in the skyline where the Eolian had been, something small was glowing. An ember, still smoldering deep in the rubble, three days after the fire.

"Kvothe," said Wil.

"Yes?"

"You'll never tell her." He did not look at me. "Or Sim. Ever."

"Wil—"

"Promise me."

I looked at him. At his calm dark face and his bandaged hands. I thought about all the things I had never told anyone. About Auri, and the Cellarage, and the stone that said no. About my mother's name. About Denna, and the cord on her wrist, and what I had begun to suspect about her patron.

Everyone has a door they keep closed. Even Wil. Especially Wil.

"I promise," I said.

He nodded once. Then he stood up, carefully, keeping his hands away from his body, and went back down through the window into the house, and I heard him lie down on the floor in the corner, where he had been sleeping all week, and go very still.

He did not sleep. I could tell by his breathing. But he lay there, and after a while, so did I.

---

I kept the promise. I have kept it all these years. I am breaking it now, a little, by telling you; but Wil is a long way from here, and Sim and Fela are married, and happy, and there is nothing in this story that can hurt them now.

And I think Wil would forgive me. I think he would want someone to know. Not so they would pity him. He would hate that. But so that somewhere, in some book, it would be written down: that there was a man once who went into a burning building for a woman who did not love him the way he loved her, and never said a word, and called it a fair trade.

The Amyr would have called that *ivare enim euge*. For the greater good.

I call it the bravest thing I ever saw.

---

"He builds bridges now," the innkeeper said to Chronicler. "Did I tell you that? Sim wrote me. Wil stayed at the University. He's Kilvin's right hand. Kilvin taught him the big work, the heavy work, the kind that doesn't need fine fingers. He built a new bridge over the Omethi." He smiled faintly. "He says his hands are too clumsy for fine work now. But they're very good at holding up heavy things."

Chronicler looked up from his page. "Did he ever marry?"

The innkeeper was quiet for a moment.

"Not that I know of," he said. He looked at the fire. "But Sim says Wil eats supper with them every Cendling, in their rooms above the bookbinder's, and argues with Fela about Eld Vintic declensions until the candles gutter. And that Fela says he's the best man she knows."

He picked up his cup.

"I think," he said, "that would be enough for him. I think it's always been enough."
