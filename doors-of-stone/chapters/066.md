# The Maer in the Hills

The greystone Remmen sent us to stood alone on the crown of a bare green hill, with nothing around it for a mile in any direction but grass and sky and wind. We stepped out of the twilight beside it on a grey afternoon in late autumn, and the first thing I smelled was smoke.

Not woodsmoke. Not the smell of cookfires, or of a farmer burning brush. The other kind. The smell of a village burning. The smell of war.

I stood on the hilltop and turned in a slow circle, looking. The hills rolled away on every side, green and brown and dun, rising toward a line of higher, rockier hills in the south and east. There were no roads that I could see. No houses. No people.

But to the north, on the horizon, there was a smudge of dark smoke against the grey sky. And to the west, another. And to the east, far off, a third.

"Where are we?" said Bast. He was shivering. The wind was cold, and he was not used to wind.

"Vintas," I said. "Somewhere in the south. The hills beyond Severen." I looked at the rocky hills to the southeast. "Levinshir is that way. I think."

"And the Maer?"

"Remmen said he was hiding in the hills to the south." I looked at the smoke on the horizon. "Somewhere."

---

It took us two days to find him.

We walked south through the empty hills, keeping to the valleys and the stream beds, away from the ridgelines where we might be seen. Bast kept his glamour close around us, so that from a distance we looked like two more shadows among the rocks. We saw no one on the first day. On the second day, we saw soldiers.

They were riding along a road in a valley below us, twenty or thirty of them, in the black and silver livery of the house of Jakis. They were riding hard, heading west. We lay flat in the heather at the top of the valley and watched them pass. None of them looked up.

"Hunting," said Bast softly, when they had gone. "They were hunting someone."

"The Maer, probably."

"Did they find him?"

"I don't know."

We followed the road west, a long way behind the soldiers. We followed it until dusk, when it came to a place where the valley narrowed between two high rocky shoulders of hill. And there, in the narrowest part of the valley, we found the soldiers.

They were dead. All of them. Lying in the road and among the rocks on either side, with arrows in them. Their horses were gone.

I stood at the edge of the killing ground and looked at the arrows. They were long and grey-fletched, the kind of arrows the Maer's archers used. The kind I had seen in Severen, in the practice yards below the palace.

"Ambush," said Bast.

"Yes."

"The Maer's people?"

"Yes." I looked up at the high rocky shoulders on either side of the valley. "They're close."

---

They found us before we found them.

We were climbing the eastern shoulder of the valley in the last of the light, picking our way up among the rocks, when a voice spoke out of the darkness above us.

"Stop. Hands where I can see them."

We stopped. We held up our hands.

A man came down out of the rocks. Then another, and another. Five of them, with bows drawn and arrows nocked. They wore no livery, only rough brown clothes and cloaks of grey wool, but they moved like soldiers.

The one in front was big and grizzled, with a broken nose and a scar across his chin. He looked at me for a long moment in the dusk. Then his eyes went wide.

"God's teeth," he said. "It's the Ruh."

I knew him. It took me a moment. He was one of the Maer's guards from Severen. Not one of the ones who had ridden with me to Renere. Another one. I had seen him on the palace walls, and in the practice yards.

"Corrin," I said. "Is Corrin with you?"

"Corrin's dead." The big man lowered his bow slowly. "Died on the walls when they breached the south gate. Took five of them with him." He was staring at me. "They said you were dead too. They said you'd run off into the Fae and never come back."

"I came back."

"So you did." He looked at Bast. His eyes narrowed. "Who's that?"

"A friend."

"He's got a funny look about him."

"He's a funny friend," I said. "Take us to the Maer."

---

The Maer's camp was in a hidden valley high in the rocky hills, a narrow cleft between two great shoulders of grey stone with a stream running down the middle of it. There were perhaps three hundred people there. Soldiers, mostly, the remnants of the Maer's army. But also servants from the palace, and townsfolk from Severen who had fled when the city fell, and a few families from the villages round about who had nowhere else to go.

They were living in caves and under rock overhangs and in a few ragged tents. There were small fires, carefully shielded so that their light would not be seen from the valleys below. There was very little food. Everyone was thin and grey and tired.

The Maer was sitting beside one of the fires, at the mouth of a shallow cave near the head of the valley. He was wrapped in a grey soldier's cloak, and there was a bandage around his left arm, and his face was hollow and hard in the firelight. He looked ten years older than when I had last seen him.

He looked up as I came into the firelight. For a long moment he did not move.

Then he stood up, slowly, and came around the fire, and took me by the shoulders, and looked at me.

"Kvothe," he said. His voice was rough.

"Your grace."

"You are alive."

"Yes, your grace."

He looked at me for a moment longer. Then he pulled me into an embrace. It was brief and hard and very tight, the embrace of a man who has lost a great deal and has just found one small thing he thought was gone.

"She is gone," he said, into my shoulder. "Meluan. They took her. When the palace fell. I was on the south wall. I could not reach her." His voice cracked. "I could not reach her."

"I know," I said. "I know, your grace. I'm going to get her back."

---

Stapes was there. He came out of the cave a little later with a pot of something that might have been soup, and when he saw me, he stopped and stared, and nearly dropped the pot. Then he recovered himself, and set the pot down very carefully on a flat stone by the fire, and bowed.

"Sir," he said. "Welcome back."

"Thank you, Stapes."

"Would you care for some soup?" He hesitated. "It is mostly water, I'm afraid. And a little barley. And something one of the soldiers caught, which I believe was a rabbit."

"I'd love some soup."

He served it to me in a battered tin cup. It was terrible. It was mostly water and a little barley and something that might have been a rabbit. It was the best thing I had eaten in a very long time.

Stapes stood beside me while I ate. He did not say anything. But once, when he thought I was not looking, he reached up and wiped his eyes with the back of his hand.

---

And then, a little later, as the fire burned low and the Maer was telling me about the fall of Severen, there was a commotion at the lower end of the valley. Voices raised. Someone running.

A boy came pelting up the valley toward the fire. A soldier's boy, a runner, no more than twelve.

"Your grace!" he gasped. "Your grace, there's people coming up the lower path. A whole crowd of 'em. With wagons."

The Maer was on his feet at once, his hand on his sword. "Soldiers?"

"No, your grace. Not soldiers. They're—" The boy hesitated. "They're *singing*, your grace."

I heard it then. Faint, drifting up the valley on the night wind. A fiddle. Badly played. Every third note flat. And a woman's voice, rough and sweet as honey from a wild hive, singing "Tinker Tanner."

I stood up.

---

It was Hallow's Company.

They came up the lower path in the dark, three wagons painted in bright colors gone dull with dust, pulled by tired horses. Tessit was leading the first horse, with his battered hat pulled low. Mirra was walking beside him. Brin was sitting on the seat of the first wagon, sawing away at his fiddle. Illa was walking beside the second wagon, singing.

And behind them, there were others.

Many others. More wagons, carts, people on foot. Refugees. Families from the villages. Old men and women, children, a few young men with farm tools in their hands and hard frightened faces. They came up the path behind Hallow's Company like a river behind a boat, fifty of them, a hundred, more.

"We found them on the roads," said Tessit, when he saw me. He did not seem surprised to see me. Ruh are rarely surprised by anything. "Wandering. Nowhere to go. The king's men burned their villages." He shrugged. "We heard the Maer was in the hills. We thought he might have room."

The Maer was staring at them. At the long line of ragged frightened people coming up the path in the dark behind the Ruh wagons.

"We have no food," he said. "We can barely feed ourselves."

"We have some," said Mirra. "Not much. But some. We'll share." She looked at the Maer with her round cheerful face gone grave. "That's what people do, your grace. When things are bad. They share."

The Maer looked at her for a long moment. Then he looked at me.

"Ruh," he said.

"Ruh," I said. "One family, your grace. Under the sky."

He looked back at Mirra. And then, slowly, he bowed. A real bow. The kind of bow a Maer gives to a lord.

"You are welcome," he said. "All of you. Whatever we have is yours."

---

Nell found me by the fire later that night.

I did not recognize her at first. She had grown. She was taller, and less thin, and her hair was braided in the Ruh fashion, and she was wearing a dress of bright red wool that Mirra must have made for her. She came up to the fire and stood looking at me with her enormous eyes, and I looked back at her, and for a long moment neither of us said anything.

Then she said, "You're the man from the well."

"I am."

"You pulled me up."

"I did."

She looked at me for a long moment. Then she came around the fire and sat down beside me, very close, and leaned against my arm.

"I have something for you," she said. "From the pretty lady."

I went very still.

"What pretty lady?"

"The one with the dark hair. The one who came to our farm and looked at Mama's cloth." Nell reached into the pocket of her red dress. "She was in the king's camp. When we played there, five days ago. Tessit said we had to play for the king, or the soldiers would take our wagons. So we played." She held out her hand. "And after, when we were packing up, the lady found me. She knew me. She remembered me from the farm." Nell's voice was very small. "She cried. She held me and cried and said she was sorry. Over and over. She said she didn't know."

I did not say anything. I could not.

"And then she gave me this," said Nell. "And she said, 'Give it to the red-haired man. The one who pulled you out of the well. You'll see him again. I know you will.'" She opened her hand. "And I did. See you again. Just like she said."

In her small palm, there was a cord. Braided from three strands of dark hair. Knotted.

---

I read it by the light of the fire, with Nell asleep against my arm and Bast sitting on my other side and the Maer across the fire, watching me.

*Nine days to the door*, it said. *From the day I tie this. Four days gone now, maybe five.*

*The king has the box. He wears it. Around his neck, on a chain. Never takes it off.*

*The lady is in the camp. Weak. The baby is well. They keep them close to the king.*

*The old man says: the key will come. He says: when the key comes, we will not need the baby.*

*He says: if the key does not come, we will use the baby at the dark of the moon.*

*The old man says you know the door's name now. He says someone gave it to you. He smiled when he said it.*

*He says: he will come. He always comes.*

And then, after a gap, a knot I had never seen. It took me a long time to puzzle it out. It was made of pieces I knew—*hand*, *name*, *hold*, *keep*—twisted together in a new way.

When I finally understood it, I sat very still.

*I can hold it. If you need me to. I know how.*

And then, at the very end, the knot that said *forgive*.

---

I folded the cord very carefully and put it inside my shirt, against my skin, next to the knot from the Grey Man.

Across the fire, the Maer was watching me.

"What does it say?" he asked quietly.

"It says we have four days," I said. "Maybe five. Until the dark of the moon."

"And then?"

"And then they open the door. With me, if I come. With your daughter, if I don't."

The Maer went very still. His hollow face was grey in the firelight.

"My daughter," he said.

"Yes, your grace. Meluan had a daughter. Eleven days ago, or near enough. They're both alive. They're both in the king's camp."

The Maer did not say anything for a long time. He sat very still by the fire, with his bandaged arm in his lap, and he looked at the flames.

"A daughter," he said at last. His voice was very soft. "I have a daughter."

"Yes, your grace."

He closed his eyes. His face did something I had never seen it do before. It crumpled, just for a moment, like paper in a fist. Then it smoothed out again.

He opened his eyes. He looked at me across the fire. And his eyes were very hard.

"How many men do you need?" he said.
