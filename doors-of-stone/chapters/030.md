# The Game Explained

I did not sit.

I stood beside the table with Caesura in my hand, point down, and I looked at him. He looked back at me. After a moment he shrugged, as if it did not matter to him one way or the other, and placed a white stone on the board. The corner point. His opening move. The same one he always made.

"You can play standing," he said. "Some people prefer it. It makes them feel they're in control."

"I'm not here to play."

"Everyone is here to play. Everyone, everywhere, always. The only question is whether they know it." He folded his hands on the head of his stick. "Ask your questions, then. I'll answer what I can."

Behind me, I heard Denna get to her feet. I heard her footsteps on the mossy stone, coming closer. She stopped a little way off, to one side, where she could see both of us. I did not look at her. I could not.

"Who are you?" I said.

"You know who I am. I'm Bredon. I've been Bredon for a long time now. Longer than you would believe." He smiled. "Before that, I had other names. None of them would mean anything to you."

"Master Ash."

"That's what she calls me. I let her. It's a small joke between us." He glanced at Denna, and his face was fond. "She thinks it's because I'm grey. It's not, entirely."

"You serve the Chandrian."

He raised an eyebrow. "Serve? No. I don't serve them. They're rather crude, most of them, and very limited. Cinder in particular. He has all the subtlety of a thrown brick." He shook his head. "No. I don't serve the Seven. I serve the one they serve. There's a difference."

"Haliax."

Bredon's face went still. Just for a moment. Then the smile came back.

"You've been reading," he said. "Good. I like a well-read opponent." He placed another stone on the board. "Yes. Haliax. Although that's not his name either, not really. That's the name that was put on him. The name that was *cursed* into him." His voice changed, very slightly. Something harder came into it. "His name was Lanre. He was the greatest man who ever lived. He was betrayed by the people he loved and served, and he was punished for it with a curse so terrible that it has lasted for longer than the stars have had names. He cannot die. He cannot sleep. He cannot forget. He cannot so much as rest his head for a single moment without the weight of what was done to him crushing down on him like a mountain."

"What was done to him? He destroyed Myr Tariniel. He killed thousands."

"Did he?" Bredon looked up at me. "Were you there?"

"Of course not."

"Then how do you know?"

"Because the story says so."

"Ah." He smiled. "The story. Yes. The story that was written by the man who cursed him. The story that has been told and retold for thousands of years, by people who were not there and did not know and never bothered to ask whether it was true. The story that the Dellers kept on a cloth, and the Amyr kept in a book beneath the Archives, and your parents were trying to sing." He placed another stone. "That story."

I stood very still.

"You knew about my parents."

"Of course I knew. I've known about you for a very long time, Kvothe. Since before you were born." He looked at me with his kind cold eyes. "I knew your mother."

---

The garden was silent. The lantern swung gently on its branch, casting shifting shadows across the table and the board and Bredon's face. The black water of the pool was perfectly still.

"You're lying," I said.

"I'm not. I told Denna, and I'll tell you: I don't lie. It's inelegant." He sat back in his chair. "I knew Netalia Lackless when she was a girl. I was a friend of her father's, in those days. I used to visit the Lackless house, and play tak with the old lord, and tell stories to his daughters by the fire." He smiled faintly. "She was a clever child. Much cleverer than her sister. She asked questions. Too many questions. She wanted to know about the box, and the door, and the old rhyme. She wanted to know everything."

"And you told her?"

"Some of it. Enough to make her curious. Not enough to make her afraid." He shrugged. "I thought, at the time, that she might be useful. A clever girl of the line, who knew enough to be interested but not enough to be careful. I thought I might guide her toward the door. Gently. Over many years." His smile faded. "But she was cleverer than I thought. She saw what I was doing. Not all of it, but enough. And she ran. She ran away from her house and her name and her family and everything she knew, and she hid herself among the Edema Ruh, where no one would ever think to look for a lady of the line."

I thought about my mother, pale and shaking, walking me away from the fire. *It's not a kind song. You must never sing it again.*

"She hid from you," I said.

"From me. From the Seven. From everyone." Bredon nodded. "And she was very good at it. It took us years to find her. Years. By the time we did, she had a son."

He looked at me.

"Cinder was supposed to bring her to me," he said quietly. "Her, and the boy. Alive. That was the plan. She would come to the door, and I would explain things to her, and in time, perhaps, she would understand." He shook his head slowly. "But Cinder has no patience. And your father had been singing about Lanre, the true story, the old one, very loudly and very well. And Cinder lost his temper." He paused. "By the time I heard, it was over. Everyone was dead. Everyone except you."

"You're saying you didn't want them killed."

"I'm saying it was a waste. A terrible, stupid, wasteful thing." His voice was very quiet. "I don't like waste, Kvothe. It's inelegant. And your mother was a remarkable woman. I was fond of her." He looked at me. "I'm fond of you, too. I have been since the first day you sat down across a board from me. You play like her. Did you know that? You play exactly like her. Too clever by half, and too proud to see what's in front of you."

---

My hand tightened on Caesura's hilt. I felt the anger rise in me again, hot and red and roaring. I wanted to raise the sword. I wanted to bring it down.

I did not. I breathed. I let the anger rise, and I let it pass.

"Why didn't Cinder kill me?" I said. "That night. He had me. He could have. Why didn't he?"

Bredon smiled. It was a slow, satisfied smile, the smile of a man watching a stone placed exactly where he wanted it.

"Because Haliax told him not to," he said. "Haliax came, at the end, and saw you. A little red-haired boy, alone in the woods, with his mother's eyes. The last of the line." He paused. "And Haliax said *he is needed*."

The words fell into the garden like stones into water.

*He is needed.*

"Needed for what?" I said. But I already knew. I knew the way you know the answer to a riddle the moment before you hear it.

"You know what for," said Bredon gently. "You went down the stairs. You read the book. You know what you are." He placed another stone on the board. "You are the key, Kvothe. Or rather, you are the hand that turns it. Only the line can open the keeper's door. Only a child of Loeclos, with the key of stone in hand, speaking the door's true name." He smiled. "Your mother would never have done it. She knew too much, and she was too afraid. But you? You're young. You're curious. You're hungry. You want to know what's behind every door you see." He looked up at me. "And you're a namer. Do you know how rare that is? A child of the line who can speak true names? It hasn't happened in a thousand years. We thought it might never happen again."

"I'll never open it."

"Won't you?"

"Never."

"Hm." Bredon looked at the board. "You said you'd never go down the stairs beneath the Four-plate door, too. I imagine. At some point." He placed a stone. "And yet."

---

"Why?" I said. My voice was hoarse. "Why do you want it opened? What's behind it that's worth all this?"

Bredon was quiet for a long time. He sat looking at the board, at the pattern of black and white stones, his old spotted hands resting on the silver head of his stick.

"There is someone behind it," he said at last. "Someone who was put away. Someone very powerful. Someone who can shape things. Change things. Make new things out of nothing, and unmake old things into nothing." He looked up at me. "Someone who could unmake a curse. Even a curse like the one on Lanre. Especially a curse like that."

"You want to free the Thief so he'll break Haliax's curse."

"I want to free the one you call the Thief so he can give my master what he has wanted for longer than the world has had a name." Bredon's voice was very soft. "Rest. Peace. An ending. That's all Lanre has ever wanted. To be allowed to die." He paused. "Is that so terrible? To want to help a man die, when he has been suffering for ten thousand years?"

"And the rest of the world? What happens to it, when the Thief is free?"

Bredon smiled. It was a very small smile, and very sad, and very old.

"Some stories have to end," he said, "so that others can begin."

---

Denna made a sound. A small choked sound, like the one she had made when he held her wrist. I turned to look at her.

She was standing a few feet away, beside the black pool. Her face was white. Her wrist was cradled against her chest, the knotted cord still glowing faintly red. Her eyes were fixed on Bredon, and they were full of something I had never seen in them before.

It was understanding.

"The song," she said. Her voice was barely a whisper. "The song isn't to free Lanre. Not from the lie. It's to—" She stopped. "It's to weaken the curse. So that when the door opens, and the Thief comes out, he can break it more easily." She looked at Bredon. "That's it, isn't it? That's what I've been writing. A key. Another key. A key for the curse."

Bredon looked at her with great fondness.

"You always were clever," he said. "Much cleverer than you give yourself credit for. That's why I chose you."

"You used me."

"I taught you. I gave you everything you have. Your voice, your harp, your knots. Your *name*." His voice was gentle. "You were a nameless girl sleeping in ditches when I found you. Now you're the finest singer in three kingdoms. Is that using?"

"Yes," said Denna. "Yes, it is."

She turned and looked at me. And for a moment, just a moment, I saw the whole of her. Not the careful smile or the practiced charm or the shifting names. Her. The girl beneath. Frightened and furious and grieving and utterly, utterly alone.

"Kvothe," she said. "I'm sorry. I'm so sorry."

"It's not your fault."

"It is. It's all my fault." She shook her head. "I didn't know. But I should have. I should have seen." She looked at the cord on her wrist. "I think I did see. I just didn't want to."

---

Bredon rose from his chair. He leaned on his stick and looked at the two of us, standing in his garden in the lantern light, and his face was full of something that might have been affection, or might have been the satisfaction of a craftsman looking at a finished piece.

"You're both very young," he said. "You think that knowing a thing changes it. That if you understand the shape of the board, you can somehow step off it." He shook his head. "You can't. No one can. The board is the world. And the game has been going on for a very long time."

He picked up his stick and tapped it once, lightly, on the mossy stones.

"Denna will finish the song," he said. "She'll finish it because she has no choice. The knot on her wrist will see to that. And she'll sing it, in every great hall from here to Atur, and people will weep, and the lie will grow weaker." He looked at me. "And you, Kvothe. You'll go to the door. You won't mean to. You'll swear you never will. But you'll go. Because it's what you are. Because you can't see a door without wanting to open it."

"You're wrong."

"Perhaps." He smiled. "We'll see."

He turned and began to walk away across the garden, toward a small door in the far wall that I had not noticed before. His stick tapped on the stones.

"You could stop me," he said, without turning around. "You have a sword. I'm an old man. It would be easy." He paused at the door. "But you won't. Because you still don't know enough. You don't know what I know, or who else knows it, or what will happen if I die. You're afraid that killing me will make things worse. And you're right to be afraid." He opened the door. "That's the beautiful game, Kvothe. It isn't about winning. It's about making sure your opponent is always, always too afraid to move."

He stepped through the door. It closed behind him.

I stood alone in the garden with Denna, beside the black pool, under the swinging lantern. The tak board sat on the table between us, its stones laid out in their half-finished pattern.

I looked down at it. And I saw, for the first time, the shape of what he had been building. Stone by stone. Move by move. All along the edges of the board, quietly, while I was paying attention to the center.

It was a door.
