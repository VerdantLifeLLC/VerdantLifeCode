# How the Ruh Came to the Roads

Tessit told the story on our sixth night with Hallow's Company, when the fire was low and the children were asleep and there was nothing left in the cider jug but the dregs.

It is an old story. Every Ruh knows it. My father told it to me when I was five, and his mother told it to him, and so on back as far as anyone can remember. There are a hundred versions. This is Tessit's. I have always liked it best.

---

*Once,* said Tessit, *before there were kingdoms, and before there were roads, the Ruh had a home.*

*It was a city. A great one. Built of white stone, on a high mountain, where the air was clean and the sky was close. And the Ruh were its singers. Not its lords, not its soldiers, not its builders. Its singers. They sang in the great hall, and in the gardens, and at the fountains where the children played. They sang the city's history, so it would be remembered. They sang the city's laws, so they would be kept. They sang every birth and every death and every marriage, so that nothing that happened in that city would ever be forgotten.*

*And the city loved them for it. And they loved the city.*

*And then, one night, the city burned.*

---

Across the fire, I had gone very still. Tessit did not notice. Or if he did, he did not stop.

*No one knows why,* said Tessit. *The songs don't say. Some say it was a war. Some say it was a betrayal. Some say a man the city loved went mad with grief and brought it down. The songs don't say, because the ones who knew the songs died in the fire. Almost all of them.*

*Almost.*

*A few of the singers lived. A handful. They were out on the roads, that night, carrying songs to the towns around the mountain, the way the singers always did. And they saw the fire from far off, on the mountaintop, like a torch. And they came running. And by the time they got there, there was nothing left. Only ash, and stone, and silence.*

*And a man with one eye, standing in the ruins.*

---

I did not breathe.

*He was a great lord,* said Tessit. *A namer, some say. Or a king. The songs don't agree. But he was standing in the ashes of the city with blood on his face, and he looked at the singers who had come running up the mountain, and he said:*

*"The city is gone. The hall is gone. The gardens and the fountains are gone. There is nothing left for you to sing to."*

*And the eldest of the singers, an old woman with a voice like a river, said: "Then we will sing to the roads."*

*And the man with one eye looked at her for a long time. And then he said, "Yes. That is what the world will need. Go out onto the roads. Carry the songs with you. And never stop. Never build another city. Never make another home. Because the things that hunt songs will hunt you, and a city can be burned, but a road cannot."*

*And the singers went down the mountain, and out onto the roads. And they never stopped. And they never built another city. And their children were born in wagons, and their grandchildren, and their grandchildren's grandchildren. And they became the Edema Ruh.*

*Edema,* said Tessit. *In the old tongue. It means "those who remember."*

*And Ruh. It means "the road."*

*The road that remembers.*

---

The fire crackled. Nobody said anything for a long time.

"That's not how my father told it," I said at last. My voice sounded strange.

Tessit looked at me across the fire. "How did he tell it?"

"He said the Ruh were cursed. For stealing a song from a king. He said the king cursed them to wander forever, and never have a home."

Tessit smiled faintly. "That's the version for the villages. For the gorgio. They like to hear that we're cursed. It makes them feel better about hating us." He poured the dregs of the cider jug into his cup and looked at them. "My mother told me the other one. The true one. She said her mother told her it was a secret. For the family. Not for anyone else."

"Why tell me?"

Tessit looked at me for a long moment. His lean brown face was very still in the firelight.

"Because you're Arliden's boy," he said. "And Arliden was trying to find that city. Did you know? That was what his song was about. The one he never finished. He'd heard the true version from my mother, one summer, when our troupes camped together. And he got it into his head that it was real. That there'd been a white city, and a fire, and a man with one eye." Tessit shook his head slowly. "He spent years looking. Asking old men in taverns. Reading old books in forgotten libraries." He drank his cider. "My mother said it would get him killed. Looking for things that should stay lost."

I did not say anything.

"She was right," said Tessit quietly. "Wasn't she?"

I looked into the fire. At the red coals and the grey ash.

"Yes," I said.

---

I did not tell Tessit, that night, that the city was real. That I had heard the whole story from an old man in a broken lighthouse, a boy who had hidden under a slab of white marble while it burned. That the man with one eye was still alive, in a round tower in the ruins, keeping watch.

I did not know that last part yet. That would come later.

But I lay awake that night in the back of the third wagon, among the sacks and the costumes, and I thought about my people. About the Ruh. About a handful of singers coming down a burning mountain and out onto the roads, and never stopping. About the thing that hunts songs, and a city that can be burned, and a road that cannot.

*Edema Ruh.*

*The road that remembers.*

I had always been proud to be Ruh. I had been proud in Tarbean, and at the University, and at the Maer's court, where they sneered at me for it. I had been proud the way you are proud of a scar.

That night, for the first time, I understood what I was proud of.

We were not cursed. We were not thieves or beggars or tinkers. We were the last singers of a city that burned. And we had been carrying its songs down every road in the world, for ten thousand years, so that no one would forget.

I thought about Skarpi, hiding under a slab of marble. I thought about my father, looking for the city in old books. I thought about my mother, singing harmony beside him by the fire.

And I made a promise, lying in the dark among the sacks, that I would finish my father's song. The one he never finished. The one about the white city.

I did. It cost me more than I can tell you. But I did.

And when I sang it, in the Eolian, with the candles burning blue, I sang it for the Ruh. For the road that remembers.

I do not think anyone in that hall understood. Except, perhaps, one. A shadow in the center of the room, made of forgetting.

I think he understood very well.
