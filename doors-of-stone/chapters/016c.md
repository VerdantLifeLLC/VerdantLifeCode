# Interlude: Knuckles

"Your hands," said the innkeeper. "What happened to them?"

Chronicler looked down. He had been writing for so long that he had forgotten about his hands, except as things that held the pen. Now he looked at them. The knuckles of his right hand were scabbed and swollen, purple and yellow at the edges, and there was a split across the first knuckle that had broken open again with the writing and was seeping a little, staining the edge of the page.

"Nothing," he said. "I fell."

"You fell on your knuckles." The innkeeper's voice was mild. "Only the right hand. Only the first two knuckles. In a straight line." He looked at Chronicler with his green eyes. "I have seen a great many men who fell, scribe. And I have seen a great many men who hit something. Those are not the same."

Bast, in his backward chair, grinned.

Chronicler felt his face go hot. He put down his pen and folded his hands in his lap, under the table, where no one could see them.

"It's not important," he said.

"Everything's important," said the innkeeper. "You said so yourself, on the first night. The small things hold up the big ones." He leaned back in his chair. "I've been talking for two days. You've been listening. I think I'm owed a story."

---

Chronicler did not answer for a long time. He looked at the fire. He looked at his satchel, leaning against the leg of the table, with its pens and its inks and its stack of blank paper.

"It was in Rannish," he said at last. "Nine days ago. On my way here."

The innkeeper waited.

"There's a square," said Chronicler. "In the middle of the town. With a Tehlin church on one side. And when I came through, there was a crowd in the square, and a fire. A bonfire. In the middle of the day." He was quiet for a moment. "They were burning books."

Bast's grin went away.

"Not many books," said Chronicler. "Rannish is a small town. There aren't many books to burn. A few prayer books that the priest said were heretical translations. An almanac with the wrong feast days in it. Some broadsheets. Songs, mostly. The kind that tinkers sell for a penny." He looked at his hands under the table. "And a book of stories. Old ones. A collection. It had belonged to a schoolmaster who'd died that winter, and his widow had given it to the church, and the priest had read it and decided it was full of pagan filth."

"Was it?" said the innkeeper.

"It had the story of Lanre in it," said Chronicler. "The old version. The one where he's not wholly a villain. And the story of Taborlin, and the story of the Ruh leaving their city, and a dozen others." He was quiet for a moment. "It was a very good book. I could see that, even from the edge of the crowd. Hand-copied. Old. The kind of thing that there might only be one of, anywhere in the world."

---

"I went up to the priest," said Chronicler. "I didn't mean to. I meant to walk on. I had somewhere to be. I'd been told—" He glanced at Bast. "I'd been told there was someone in Newarre I needed to see, and I shouldn't dawdle." He looked back at the fire. "But I went up to him anyway. And I asked him, very politely, if I could buy the book. Before he burned it. I said I'd pay whatever he liked."

"And?"

"And he said no. He said it was the devil's work and it would go into the fire like the rest." Chronicler's voice was very even. "And I said that it was a history. That it was the only record of those stories in that form. That whatever he thought of them, they were *old*, and they were true in their way, and someone should keep them. Even if it wasn't him." He paused. "And he said that the only true stories were in the Book of the Path, and everything else was lies, and lies should burn."

"And then he threw the book in the fire," said the innkeeper quietly.

"And then he threw the book in the fire."

---

The common room was very still.

"I hit him," said Chronicler. "I didn't decide to. I've never hit anyone in my life. I'm a scribe. I've spent my whole life sitting at desks, copying things. But I watched that book go into the fire, and the pages start to curl, and I hit him. In the face. As hard as I could." He almost laughed. "It was a very bad punch. I'd never thrown one before. I think I hurt my hand more than his face."

"And the crowd?"

"The crowd didn't like it." Chronicler looked down. "They pulled me off him. And they held me. And some of them hit me back. Not too badly. A few kicks. A few punches. The priest had them stop, after a while. He said I was a madman, and I should be driven out of town, and not harmed further, because Tehlu is merciful." His mouth twisted. "So they drove me out of town."

"And the book?"

Chronicler was quiet for a long time.

"I got some of it," he said.

---

He reached down to his satchel. He opened it and took out something wrapped in a clean cloth. He set it on the table and unwrapped it, very carefully.

It was a handful of pages. Old pages, soft and yellowed, the edges black and crumbling where the fire had caught them. Perhaps a dozen of them. Loose, with the stitching burned away.

"When they pulled me off him," said Chronicler, "I fell against the edge of the fire. And I put my hand in it." He turned his right hand over, showing the palm. It was red and shiny and blistered. "And I grabbed what I could. I didn't look. I just grabbed. And then they were dragging me away and I held on." He looked at the pages. "This is what I got. Twelve pages. Out of three hundred."

The innkeeper leaned forward. He looked at the pages without touching them. His face was very still.

"What's on them?" he said softly.

Chronicler turned the top page toward him. It was written in a small, faded, careful hand, in old-fashioned letters. The top of the page was burned away. But the rest was clear.

*—and the singer went down into the dark place beneath the city, where the door was, and sang to it. And the door listened, as doors do. And it is said that as long as someone sings to it, it will not open. But the singers are all gone now, and no one remembers the song—*

The page ended there. The rest was ash.

---

Nobody said anything for a very long time.

The innkeeper sat looking at the burned page. His hands were flat on the table. His face showed nothing at all. But Chronicler, watching him, saw his left hand move. Very slightly. Toward the page. And stop. And curl, slowly, into a fist.

"Where did you say this book came from?" said the innkeeper at last. His voice was very quiet.

"A schoolmaster in Rannish. He'd had it from his father, the widow said. And his father from his. It was very old."

"Twelve pages," said the innkeeper.

"Out of three hundred."

The innkeeper was quiet for a long moment. Then he looked up at Chronicler. And something in his face had changed. Something in his eyes. For a moment—just a moment—they were not the eyes of an innkeeper at all.

"You put your hand in a fire," he said, "for a book you'd never read. About stories you didn't know were true. Because a priest said lies should burn."

Chronicler did not answer.

"And you hit him," said the innkeeper. "A scribe. Who's never hit anyone in his life."

"It was a very bad punch," said Chronicler.

The innkeeper looked at him for a long moment. And then, very slowly, the corner of his mouth moved.

"Skarpi chose well," he said.

---

He did not say anything else about it. He sat back in his chair, and after a while he said, "Where was I?" in his ordinary voice, and went on with the story.

But later that night, when the telling was done for the day, and Chronicler had gone up to his room, there was a knock on his door. And when he opened it, there was no one there. Only a small clay pot on the floor of the passage, with a wooden lid. And a folded note, in a quick, neat, slanting hand.

*For the burn. And the knuckles. Rub it in twice a day. It will sting.*

*You'll want your hand. There's a great deal left to write.*

*—K.*

Chronicler used it, twice a day, as instructed. It stung like the devil.

By the next morning, his hand did not hurt at all. But the knuckles stayed scabbed and dark, the way knuckles do, and every time he looked down at them, holding the pen, he remembered a fire in a square in Rannish. And twelve pages out of three hundred.

And the song that no one remembers anymore.

---

He gave the pages to the innkeeper, on the last morning. He did not say why. He only held them out, wrapped in their clean cloth, and the innkeeper looked at them for a long time, and then took them.

"There were three hundred," said the innkeeper softly.

"I know."

"Someone should write the rest down. Before it's gone." He looked at Chronicler. "All the rest. Every story anyone still remembers. Before the priests burn them and the tinkers forget them and the last old man who knows them dies."

"That's a great deal of writing."

"Yes." The innkeeper tucked the pages inside his shirt, against his chest. "You'd better take care of your hands."
