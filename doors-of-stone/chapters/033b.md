# Lady Tamsin's Cats

Lady Tamsin Aerwen had thirty-one cats, and she knew the name and pedigree and temperament of every one of them, and she liked all of them better than she liked most people.

"Cats," she told me, on the first afternoon, "are honest. They want exactly three things. Food, warmth, and to be left alone when they choose and fussed over when they choose. They never pretend to want anything else." She stroked the grey tom in her lap, a vast shaggy creature named Admiral. "Courtiers want the same three things. But they lie about it."

She was the first of the Maer's three friends I went to see in Renere, though I will tell you of the others in their place. It was in her rooms, in my first days in that city, that I learned the things that would make sense of what I saw at the king's bedside. I came to her in the old wing of the palace on the Maer's letter, the afternoon I arrived. She had read the letter twice, holding it very close to her bright old eyes, and then she had tossed it into the fire and watched it burn.

"Alveron always did write too much," she said. "Sit down, boy. Have a cake. Not that one; Pudding's been at it."

I did not ask which cat was Pudding. I took a different cake.

---

She told me things. That was her gift, it turned out. Not power, not money, though she had a great deal of both. Knowing things. She had been at the Vintish court for sixty years, since she was a girl of fourteen sent to wait on the old queen, and she had spent those sixty years listening. At keyholes and behind curtains and across dinner tables. She knew who had married whom and why, who had poisoned whom and how, whose children were not their husband's and whose husbands were not their children's father's.

And she knew about the Jakis family.

"New money," she said, with profound contempt, of a house that had held its barony for two hundred years. "Old Jakis's grandfather bought the title from a king who needed a war paid for. They've been buying ever since. Land, offices, marriages." She fed Admiral a piece of cake. "The present duke's grandfather married a girl from the eastern hills. Very poor. Very old family. That was the last thing the Jakises didn't have, you see. Old blood. They bought some."

"What family?"

Lady Tamsin looked at me over Admiral's shaggy head.

"Nobody remembers," she said. "A little branch of something older. Some cousin of a cousin of the Lacklesses, four hundred years back. Poor as mice. They'd have sold their daughter to a tinker if the tinker had offered more." She smiled thinly. "That's where Ambrose gets his looks. Not from the Jakis side. The Jakises all look like well-fed pigs."

I sat very still.

"A cousin of the Lacklesses," I said.

"So they say. It's in the genealogies, if you care to look. Nobody does. It's a very thin thread." She stroked the cat. "Why? Does it matter?"

"It might."

Lady Tamsin looked at me for a long moment with her bright old eyes.

"Everything matters, in this court," she said. "Eventually. That's the first thing you learn." She set Admiral down on the floor. He stalked off, offended. "What else do you want to know?"

---

"There's an old man," I said. "At court. He plays tak. Bredon."

Something changed in Lady Tamsin's face. It was very small. A tightening at the corners of her mouth.

"Bredon," she said. "Yes. I know Bredon."

"How long has he been at court?"

She did not answer right away. She got up, slowly, leaning on her cane, and crossed the room to a tall cabinet in the corner, crowded with cats. She shooed three of them off the lower shelf and opened a drawer and took out a small book bound in faded red leather. She brought it back and sat down and opened it on her lap.

"My diary," she said. "From my first year at court. I was fourteen. I wrote down everything." She turned the brittle pages carefully. "Here."

She held it out to me. I read the line her finger was pointing to. It was written in a round, careful, girlish hand, sixty years old.

*The old gentleman with the silver stick beat the prince at tak again tonight. He is very kind to me. He says I have a clever face. His name is Master Brand.*

I looked up.

"Brand," I said.

"Brand," said Lady Tamsin. "Sixty years ago. An old man then. White hair. A stick with a silver head. He taught the old king to play tak when the old king was a boy." She took the diary back and closed it. "He left after a year or two. I forgot about him. And then, forty years later, an old man came to court with a stick with a silver head, and said his name was Bredon, and taught the prince to play tak."

"The same man?"

"I was fourteen, the first time. I was fifty-five, the second. My eyes weren't what they had been." Lady Tamsin's voice was very quiet. "But I remember a face. I've always remembered faces. It's how I've survived." She looked at me. "He said I had a clever face. Both times. The same words."

The room was very quiet. Even the cats seemed still.

"He was at the Maer's court," I said. "For eleven years."

"And before that, here. And before that, somewhere else, I expect." Lady Tamsin set the diary on the table. "I've never told anyone. Who would believe me? A mad old woman with thirty cats, saying the old tak player hasn't aged a day in sixty years." She smiled thinly. "They'd put me in a tower."

"I believe you."

"Yes." She looked at me. "I rather thought you might. Alveron says you believe a great many strange things. And that you're usually right."

---

She told me one more thing, before I left.

"The king's hair," she said. "Has anyone told you about the king's hair?"

"No."

"They will. Kelm will, if you go to him; he frets about it." She looked at me.

"I was the one who noticed it first. Before the physicians. I visit Roderic every morning; I've known him since he was in swaddling." She was quiet for a moment. "The morning it started, the tangles, there was a girl in his rooms. Very early. Before the valet. A singer. The king's nightingale, they were calling her, though she'd only been at court a month. She'd come to sing for him because he couldn't sleep."

I did not move.

"Dark hair," said Lady Tamsin. "Very pretty. Very sad, I thought. She sat beside his bed and sang to him, and when I came in she was braiding a thin dark cord into the hair at his temple. A charm for sleep, she told me, very prettily. Her patron had given it to her. An old Yllish thing." Lady Tamsin looked at me. "She looked up at me, and she had such a look on her face. As if she'd just realized something. As if she was frightened of her own hands."

"What happened?"

"She left. That same day. Nobody's seen her at court since." Lady Tamsin picked up another cake and looked at it. "The old man was her patron, they said. Bredon. He took her away."

I sat very still in Lady Tamsin's warm crowded room with the cats all around me, and I thought about a girl sitting beside a dying king's bed, stroking his hair, singing. And looking down at her own hands.

*It feels like the knot is holding on to something.*

I did not know, then, sitting in that room, what I would see the next morning, bent over the king's pillow with my lute. I only knew that something cold had settled into my stomach and would not leave.

"Thank you, my lady," I said.

Lady Tamsin looked at me for a long moment. Then she held out the cake.

"Eat something, boy," she said. "You've gone the color of Admiral."
