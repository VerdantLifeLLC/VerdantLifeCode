# The Hollow Market

I walked for what felt like a day through the silver forest, and the light did not change.

That is one of the strangest things about the Fae, and one of the hardest to get used to. There is no sun. There is no moon. There is no day or night, no morning or evening, no passage of time written on the sky. There is only the twilight, soft and shadowless and endless, glowing from every direction at once. You walk, and you walk, and you grow tired and hungry and thirsty, and you look up at the sky to see how late it is, and it is exactly the same as it was when you started.

I had learned, the first time, to measure time by my own body. By hunger and thirst and weariness. By the number of times I stopped to rest. By the aching of my feet. It was not very accurate. But it was better than nothing.

By that measure, I walked for a day. Perhaps a little more. I ate some of my bread and cheese when I grew hungry, and drank from my waterskin when I grew thirsty, and did not touch the streams I passed, though they ran clear and cold and sang softly over their stones. I did not touch the fruit that hung from some of the trees, though it was very beautiful, and smelled of honey and summer.

And I did not know where I was going.

---

That was the problem. I had thought about it a great deal, on the road to the Eld. I had told Elodin I would figure it out. But now that I was here, standing in the silver forest under the endless twilight, I realized that I had no idea how.

The Fae is not like the mortal world. It does not have maps. Or rather, it has maps, but they are not maps of places. They are maps of stories, of feelings, of intentions. Roads in the Fae go where they want to go, not where they were built to go. A path that leads north one day might lead south the next. A grove that is an hour's walk from here might be a year's walk from there. Distances stretch and shrink according to rules I did not understand.

The first time I had been here, I had not needed to know any of that. Felurian had kept me in her glade, and when I left, she had shown me the way out. I had never had to find my own way.

Now I did. And I did not know how.

I stopped at the edge of a clearing and sat down on a fallen log and put my head in my hands.

*You'll figure it out*, I had said. Of course I would. I always did.

---

"You look lost," said a voice.

I looked up. There was a woman standing at the edge of the clearing, on the far side, under a tree whose leaves were the color of old copper. She was tall and thin and very pale, with long hair the color of dead leaves and eyes like pools of dark water. She was wearing a dress that seemed to be made of spider silk, grey and shimmering. She was smiling.

"I am lost," I said. There did not seem to be any point in denying it.

"Ah." She tilted her head. "A mortal. Lost in the twilight. How very traditional." Her smile widened. "Where are you trying to go, little mortal?"

I hesitated. I remembered the rules. The ones Felurian had taught me, and the ones I had learned on my own. *Never give your name. Never eat their food. Never make a bargain you do not understand.*

"North," I said. "To the mountains. The Stormwal."

"The Stormwal." She looked at me with her dark-water eyes. "That's a long way. Through some very unfriendly country." She paused. "Why would anyone want to go there?"

"I have business there."

"Business." She laughed, a sound like dry leaves blowing. "Mortals always have business." She came a few steps into the clearing. Her feet made no sound on the moss. "I could help you, you know. If you asked nicely."

"How?"

"There's a market," she said. "Not far from here. The Hollow Market. Everything in the twilight is for sale there, if you know how to ask. Maps. Roads. Directions." She smiled. "I could take you there."

"What would it cost?"

"The market? Oh, it costs nothing to go. Only to buy." She tilted her head. "And taking you there? That would cost you nothing either. I'm feeling generous today. I like the look of you." Her eyes moved over me, slowly. Over my face, my hair, my cloak. They lingered on the cloak. "That's a very fine shaed you're wearing."

"It was a gift."

"From whom?"

"Felurian."

The woman went very still. For a long moment, she did not speak. Then she smiled again, but it was a different smile. More careful.

"Felurian," she said. "Well. You are an interesting mortal, aren't you?" She was quiet for a moment. "All right. I'll take you to the market. For nothing. As a courtesy to the lady who made that cloak." She turned away. "Follow me. And don't step off the path."

---

The Hollow Market was in a hollow. That was the first thing I noticed, when we came to it. A great round hollow in the forest floor, like a bowl pressed into the earth, a quarter of a mile across. The sides of the bowl were steep and grassy and dotted with tents and stalls and awnings of every color. The bottom was a great open space, crowded with people.

Or with things that looked like people. Some of them looked very like people indeed, tall and fair and beautiful, dressed in silks and velvets and cloth of gold. Some of them looked only a little like people, with too-long fingers or too-bright eyes or skin the color of bark or moss or stone. And some of them did not look like people at all. I saw a creature like a great grey heron, standing on one leg beside a stall full of glass bottles, haggling with the stallkeeper in a voice like a creaking gate. I saw something small and furry and many-legged scuttling between the stalls with a basket on its back. I saw a woman made entirely of leaves, rustling softly as she walked.

The noise was incredible. Voices in a hundred languages, rising and falling, laughing and shouting and arguing. Music from a dozen different places, pipes and drums and strings, all playing different tunes at once. The clink of coins, the rustle of cloth, the creak of wood.

And the smells. Spices and perfumes and roasting meat and fresh bread and flowers and incense and things I could not name. Some of them were wonderful. Some of them were terrible. Some of them were both at once.

"The Hollow Market," said the woman with the dead-leaf hair. "Everything in the twilight is for sale here." She looked at me sidelong. "Everything. Remember that."

"I'll remember."

"Good." She smiled. "Well. I've done my courtesy. Good luck, little mortal." And she turned and walked away into the crowd, and was gone.

---

I spent what felt like hours wandering through the market.

I saw things I cannot describe, and things I would not describe if I could. I saw a stall selling bottled dreams, each one a different color, swirling and shimmering behind the glass. I saw a stall selling memories, pressed flat between sheets of paper like flowers. I saw a stall selling voices, and another selling shadows, and another selling years. A woman offered to sell me a year of my own life back, one I had lost somewhere, and I did not ask her how she had got it.

I saw a stall selling names.

It was a small stall, at the edge of the market, in the shadow of a great old tree. It was hung with strips of cloth, dark and pale and every color between, and on each strip there was writing. Knots, some of them. Some of them were letters I could not read. Some of them were not writing at all, only shapes, twisted and folded into patterns that made my eyes ache.

The stallkeeper was very old. So old that I could not tell whether it was a man or a woman, or whether it had ever been either. It sat on a stool in the shadows behind the stall, wrapped in a great grey cloak, and its face was a mass of wrinkles so deep that its eyes were lost in them.

"Names," it said, as I passed. Its voice was like the rustle of dry paper. "Names for sale. Old names, new names, true names, false names. Names to hide behind. Names to wear. Names to give away." It looked at me from the depths of its wrinkles. "You look like a man who needs a new name."

I stopped.

"Why do you say that?"

"Because your old one is so heavy." The stallkeeper tilted its head. "I can see it on you. Bowing your shoulders. Bending your back. It's a very heavy name. Very old. Very dangerous." It paused. "Very valuable."

I went cold.

"It's not for sale," I said.

"Everything is for sale," said the stallkeeper. "Here." It reached out one long grey hand and touched one of the strips of cloth hanging from its stall. "I could take it off you. Your name. Just for a while. Lighten your load. Give you another one to wear in its place. Something light. Something easy." It smiled, a horrible toothless smile. "And when you want it back, you come here and buy it."

"And the price?"

"Oh, the price is very small. Very small indeed." The stallkeeper leaned forward. "I'd only want to *read* it. While I held it. Just once."

I looked at the strips of cloth hanging from the stall. At the names written on them. At the knots, twisted and folded and tied.

I wondered how many of them had been sold here. How many of them had never been bought back.

"No," I said. "Thank you."

The stallkeeper shrugged. "Suit yourself." It settled back on its stool. "But you'll need it, someday. I can see that too. A day will come when that name is too heavy to carry. And you'll need somewhere to set it down." It smiled its toothless smile. "When that day comes, remember where I am."

I walked away. I did not look back.

But I heard its words for a long time afterward, echoing in my head. *A day will come when that name is too heavy to carry.* I heard them the way I had heard Elodin's. *A name can be put down. You need someone to hold it for you.*

---

I found the mapmaker near the bottom of the hollow.

It was a small, neat stall, hung with scrolls and rolls of parchment and lengths of string. The stallkeeper was a small, neat creature about the height of a child, with a round face and round spectacles and a round belly, and skin the color of a ripe acorn. It was sitting behind its stall with a quill in its hand, drawing something on a long strip of parchment, and it did not look up as I approached.

"Roads," it said, in a small precise voice. "Maps. Directions. Routes. Paths. Ways." It dipped its quill. "Where to?"

"North," I said. "To the Stormwal. The mountains. In the mortal world."

The mapmaker stopped drawing. It looked up at me over its spectacles.

"The Stormwal," it said. "Through the twilight."

"Yes."

"That's a very long way."

"I know."

"Through some very unfriendly country."

"So I've been told."

The mapmaker looked at me for a long moment. Then it set down its quill and folded its small acorn-colored hands on the stall in front of it.

"There's only one road that goes there from here," it said. "The Moon's Road. It runs north, through the Deep Wood, past the Long Lake, and out through the Northern Gate into the mortal mountains." It paused. "It also runs past the Grove."

I did not have to ask which grove. I knew from the way it said the word.

"The Cthaeh's grove."

"Just so." The mapmaker nodded. "The Sithe watch that road. Very closely. Anyone who goes near the Grove, they shoot. No questions. No warnings. No mercy." It looked at me over its spectacles. "And anyone who's *been* to the Grove before—anyone who's spoken to what lives there—they shoot on sight, wherever they find them. Because once you've heard it speak, you're poisoned. You're a danger to everyone around you. You're a plague."

I did not say anything. I did not have to. The mapmaker looked at my face and nodded slowly.

"Ah," it said. "You've been there."

"Yes."

"And you want to go past it again."

"I have to."

The mapmaker was silent for a long time. Then it sighed, a small precise sigh.

"Well," it said. "I'll sell you the road. That's my business. But I'll tell you this, for free, because I've never sold a road to a dead man before and I'd rather not start." It leaned forward. "Go quietly. Go quickly. Don't stop. Don't speak. Don't listen. And if you hear it calling you—and you will—don't answer." It paused. "And pray the Sithe are looking the other way."

"What's the price?"

The mapmaker looked at me. Then at my lute case.

"A song," it said. "A new one. One that's never been sung in the twilight before." It smiled faintly. "We get so few new songs here. Everyone's been singing the same ones for ten thousand years."

I looked at it for a long moment. Then I took out my lute. My left hand was still stiff and cold, even under the warmth of the shaed. But I could move my fingers a little now. Enough.

I played it "Tariniel."

Not all of it. Just the first part. The white city on the last day of its life. The towers and the gardens and the fountains. The children playing.

When I finished, the mapmaker was very still. Its round spectacles had fogged, and it took them off and wiped them on its sleeve.

"Well," it said. Its small precise voice was not quite steady. "Well. That's a new one." It put its spectacles back on. "Yes. That will do."

It reached under its stall and took out a length of string. Silver string, very fine, wound on a small wooden spool. It held it out to me.

"Tie one end to your wrist," it said. "The other end will find the road. Follow it. Don't let go." It paused. "And good luck, singer. You'll need it."

I took the spool. I tied the end of the silver string around my good wrist. The other end lifted from the spool, all on its own, and floated in the air like a strand of spider silk caught in a breeze. It pointed north.

I thanked the mapmaker. I put away my lute. And I began to follow the string.
