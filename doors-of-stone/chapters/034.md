# Renere

Renere is the oldest city in Vintas, and it wants you to know it.

It sits in a bowl of low green hills where three rivers meet, and it has been sitting there for so long that no one remembers who founded it. There were kings in Renere before there was a Vintas, and kings before that, and before that, the stories say, there were things that were not kings but ruled anyway. The city has been burned three times and flooded twice and besieged more times than anyone has bothered to count, and every time it has been rebuilt on top of itself, so that the streets are layered like the pages of a book, the new on top of the old on top of the older still.

You feel it, walking through Renere. You feel the weight of all that history pressing up through the cobblestones. The houses lean together over the narrow streets as if they are whispering secrets. The churches are so old that their stone has gone black and smooth as river rock. And at the center of it all, on a low hill in the middle of the bowl, the royal palace squats like a great grey toad, all towers and battlements and heavy walls, looking down on the city with an air of patient menace.

I rode into Renere on a grey afternoon, with rain threatening and my two guards riding silent behind me. I had never been there before. I did not like it.

---

The Maer had given me the names of three people at court whom he trusted. The first was a lady named Tamsin Aerwen, an elderly widow who had been a lady-in-waiting to the old queen and knew everyone and everything. The second was a lord named Petre Ashford, a quiet, bookish man who held a minor post in the treasury and was, the Maer said, the most honest man in Renere, which was not saying a great deal. The third was a physician.

"His name is Kelm," the Maer had told me. "He trained at your University, many years ago. He is one of the king's physicians, though not the chief. He is a good man, and a careful one, and he does not believe that the king's illness is natural." He had paused. "He has written to me twice this spring. Very guarded letters. He is frightened."

The next morning, I went to see Kelm.

He had rooms in a narrow house near the palace, on a street full of apothecaries and bookshops. His door was answered by a servant who looked at my letter of commission for a long time before letting me in. The rooms beyond were small and cluttered and smelled of herbs and vinegar and old paper. Kelm himself was a small grey man in his fifties, thin and stooped, with ink-stained fingers and a pair of spectacles perched on the end of his nose. He looked, more than anything, like a Master of the University. He had the same air of distraction, the same sense that most of his mind was somewhere else.

But when he looked at me, his eyes were sharp.

"You're the Maer's man," he said. "The Ruh."

"Yes."

"The one who caught Caudicus."

"Yes."

He nodded slowly. "Good. Good. I heard about that. Clever work. Very clever." He gestured me to a chair. "Sit, sit. Would you like tea? I have tea. Somewhere." He looked around vaguely at the clutter. "Or wine. I think I have wine."

"I'm fine, thank you."

"Good. Good." He sat down across from me and folded his hands. "You've come about the king."

"Yes."

"The Maer thinks he's being poisoned."

"The Maer thinks he's being killed," I said. "Not necessarily by poison."

Kelm looked at me for a long moment over the top of his spectacles.

"No," he said quietly. "Not by poison. I've tested everything. Every dish, every cup, every bottle of medicine. I've tested his bedclothes and his candles and the water in his washbasin. I've even tested the ink he signs his letters with." He shook his head. "Nothing. Not a trace. Whatever is killing him, it isn't anything I can find."

"But he is being killed."

"Oh, yes." Kelm's voice was very flat. "Yes, he is. I've been a physician for thirty years, and I know what natural illness looks like. This isn't it. He was a strong man, Roderic. Strong as an ox. He hunted every day and drank every night and never had so much as a head cold. And then, last autumn, he simply began to fade." He took off his spectacles and rubbed his eyes. "Not all at once. Slowly. A little weaker every day. A little thinner. A little paler. As if something were drawing the life out of him, one drop at a time."

"Are there any other symptoms? Anything strange?"

Kelm put his spectacles back on and looked at me. He hesitated.

"There is one thing," he said at last. "It's probably nothing. The other physicians laugh at me when I mention it."

"Tell me anyway."

"His hair," Kelm said. "It's been growing. Very fast. Much faster than it should. And it tangles. Every morning, his valet combs it out, and every evening, it's tangled again. Knotted. Snarled up in these little tight knots, like a bird's nest." He shook his head. "The valet cuts them out, and they come back. Every day. The chief physician says it's a symptom of the fever. But I've never seen a fever do that. Not in thirty years."

I sat very still.

"Master Kelm," I said. "I need to see the king."

---

It took three days.

Kings, even dying ones, are not easily seen. There are forms to be followed and permissions to be sought and a great many people to be flattered, bribed, or ignored. I had the Maer's letter of commission, which opened some doors. I had Kelm, who opened others. I had Lady Tamsin Aerwen, a tiny, wrinkled, bright-eyed old woman who seemed to find the whole thing enormously entertaining and who opened more doors in an afternoon than I could have opened in a month.

"The king is dying," she said, when I explained what I wanted. "Everyone wants to see him. Half the court is camped outside his door, waiting for him to die so they can be the first to kiss Andrian's ring." She cackled. "Andrian, of all people. The boy can't tell a sword from a spoon. Still. He's the heir, and he'll be king, and everyone wants to be his friend." She looked at me with her bright eyes. "But you don't want to kiss anyone's ring, do you? You want to *look* at the king. Not bow to him. Look."

"Yes, my lady."

"Why?"

I hesitated. Then I told her a part of the truth. "Because I think someone is killing him. And I think I might know how."

Lady Tamsin looked at me for a long moment. Her face, which had been bright and merry, went very still.

"Do you now," she said softly. "Do you, now."

She opened the last door the next morning.

---

The king's bedchamber was a great dim room hung with heavy red curtains, lit by a dozen candles and a fire that roared in a hearth big enough to roast an ox. It was stiflingly hot. It smelled of sickness and incense and something else, something sour and wrong that I could not quite place.

There were people everywhere. Physicians in black robes, conferring in low voices by the window. Priests in white, murmuring prayers at the foot of the bed. Servants, courtiers, guards. A pair of ladies in magnificent dresses, weeping delicately into lace handkerchiefs. A young man with a weak chin and a fine velvet coat, standing by the fire with his hands clasped behind his back, looking bored and frightened in equal measure. Prince Andrian, I assumed.

And in the center of the room, in a great four-posted bed hung with red velvet, the king.

I had seen dying men before. I had seen them in the streets of Tarbean, and in the Medica, and in the Eld. I thought I was prepared for it. I was not.

Roderic of Vintas had been a big man. You could see that in the frame of him, the width of the shoulders and the length of the bones. But there was almost nothing left on that frame now. His skin was stretched over his skull like paper over a lantern, yellow and translucent. His eyes were sunk deep in their sockets. His hands, lying on the red coverlet, were claws.

And his hair.

It spread out across the pillows around his head like a dark stain. It was long, much longer than a man's hair should be, long enough to reach his waist if he had been standing. It was grey and dark in streaks. And it was knotted.

Not tangled. Knotted. I saw it the moment I came close enough to see clearly. Among the long grey strands, scattered through it like burrs in a dog's coat, there were small tight knots. Dozens of them. Some were only a few hairs, twisted around each other. Some were larger, whole locks drawn into intricate patterns that made my eyes ache.

I knew those patterns. I had seen them on the Dellers' cloth, and on the cord around Denna's wrist. I had tied some of them myself, in the Grey Man, with Denna's hands guiding mine.

They were Yllish.

---

Kelm had arranged for me to be introduced as a musician, sent by the Maer to play for the king's comfort. It was not unusual. Dying men often asked for music. I had my lute with me, and I sat down on a stool at the foot of the bed, and I played.

I played softly. Old songs, gentle ones. Lullabies and love songs and the slow sad ballads that people sing at wakes. The priests frowned at me. The physicians ignored me. The weeping ladies stopped weeping and listened. Prince Andrian turned from the fire and looked at me for a long moment, then turned back.

And while I played, I looked at the knots.

It was hard. They were small, and there were so many of them, and they were half hidden in the tangled mass of hair. But I had spent months learning to read the Dellers' cloth, and before that, days learning from Denna. I knew what to look for.

Most of the knots were meaningless. Or not meaningless, exactly, but empty. Decoys. Knots that looked like writing but said nothing, the way a child's scribble looks like writing but is not. They were there to confuse. To hide the real ones.

But the real ones were there. I found them, one by one, as I played. Three of them, woven deep into the hair near the king's scalp, so close to the skin that no valet's comb could reach them. They were tiny, and very tight, and very old-looking, as if they had been there for a long time.

The first said *ending*. I knew that one. It was one of the first Denna had taught me.

The second said *slowly*. Or something like it. *A little at a time.* *Drop by drop.*

And the third was a name.

I could not read it. It was too complex, too long, a whole cluster of knots woven together into something that was almost a sculpture. But I knew what it was. I knew it the way I had known my own name when the Sleeper spoke it. It was the shape of a man. It was Roderic.

Not the name *Roderic*. The name that was him.

Someone had written his true name into his hair, in knots. And beside it, they had written *ending*, and *slowly*.

And his hair was doing what it said.

---

I finished the song I was playing. I began another. My fingers moved on the strings without my thinking about them. I was not thinking about the music. I was thinking about the knots.

Who had tied them? Not Bredon. He could not have gotten close enough. The king's bedchamber was guarded day and night, and the king's hair was combed every morning by his valet. Whoever had tied those knots had been close to the king. Very close. Close enough to touch his hair without anyone thinking it strange.

A valet. A barber. A lover. A physician.

I looked around the room. At the black-robed physicians by the window. At the priests. At the servants. At the weeping ladies.

At Prince Andrian, standing by the fire.

He was looking at me again. Our eyes met, across the room, and he did not look away. There was something in his face I had not expected. Not boredom. Not fear.

Knowledge.

He knew. He knew what the knots were. And he knew that I knew.

I held his gaze for a long moment. Then he turned away, back to the fire, and I looked down at my lute and kept playing.

---

When I finished, a physician came and told me that the king needed to rest, and that I should go. I rose and bowed and gathered up my lute. As I passed the fire, Prince Andrian spoke without turning around.

"You play very well," he said.

"Thank you, your highness."

"My father always loved music. He used to have musicians play for him every night. Before." He was quiet for a moment. "You looked at his hair."

I stopped. I did not answer.

"Everyone looks at his hair," said Andrian. "It's grotesque, isn't it? Like something from a story. But you looked at it differently. You looked at it as if you could read it."

He turned from the fire and looked at me. He was a young man, only a little older than me, with a weak chin and pale eyes and a pleasant, forgettable face. But his eyes were not forgettable. They were very frightened, and very steady.

"Can you?" he said.

I looked at him for a long moment. I thought about lying. I thought about it very seriously.

"Yes," I said. "Some of it."

"What does it say?"

"It says he's dying. Slowly. On purpose."

Andrian closed his eyes. For a long moment he did not move. Then he opened them again.

"Can you stop it?"

"I don't know. Maybe. If I could get close enough. If I had time."

"You'll have time," said Andrian. He glanced at the physicians by the window, the priests at the foot of the bed. "I'll see to it. Come back tonight. After midnight. There's a door in the east wall of the palace garden, behind the yew hedge. I'll leave it unlocked."

"Your highness—"

"Please." His voice cracked, very slightly. "He's my father. He's not a good man, or a wise one, or even a very kind one. But he's my father. And I don't want him to die like this." He looked at me. "Please."

I looked at him. At his weak chin and his frightened eyes.

"After midnight," I said. "I'll be there."

---

I did not know, then, that I would never keep that appointment. I did not know that Prince Andrian of Vintas, heir to the throne, would be dead before midnight came.

I only knew that someone had written a king's name in his hair, and that the knots were tied by a hand I thought I recognized. Small and precise and very neat, with a particular way of doubling back on the loops that I had seen before. On the Dellers' cloth. On the cord she wore in her hair.

On the knot she had left for me on my table, in the Maer's palace, the morning she went away.
