# Nails

Kilvin taught me to make nails.

It was the day after the chest. I did not understand, then, why he did it. I did not understand much of anything. The chest was finished and sitting in the corner of his workshop under a canvas cover, and everyone had gone home to sleep after the long night: Wil with his scarred hands, and Fela with her two stones, and Devi yawning and grumbling and smelling of alchemy. Sim had fallen asleep on a bench by the door and nobody had had the heart to wake him.

I did not sleep. I did not feel tired. I sat on a stool by the cold forge with my hands in my lap and watched the grey light come up in the windows of the Fishery, and did not think about anything.

Kilvin came in at dawn. He was carrying a sack. He set it down on the anvil with a heavy clank and looked at me for a long time with his dark, deep-set eyes.

"Get up," he said.

I got up.

---

He lit the forge. I watched him do it. I did not help; I did not know I was supposed to. He built the fire carefully, the way he did everything, laying the coal just so and working the bellows with slow steady strokes until the heart of the forge glowed orange, then yellow, then white.

Then he opened the sack. It was full of iron. Short rods of it, as long as my hand and as thick as my little finger, dull grey and flaking with rust. Hundreds of them.

"Nail stock," said Kilvin. "Good iron. Cheap iron. From the smith on Tanner's Row." He picked one up and held it out to me. "Take it."

I took it. It was cold and rough and heavy in my palm.

"Today," said Kilvin, "you will make nails."

I looked at the rod of iron in my hand. Then at him.

"Why?" I said.

It was a real question. I did not know. I did not know why anyone did anything, then. I did not know why the sun came up or why people talked or why I was standing in a workshop with a rod of iron in my hand. I only knew that he had told me to get up, and I had, and now he was telling me to make nails.

Kilvin looked at me for a long moment. Something moved in his big bearded face.

"Because your hands need something to do," he said. "And nails are very simple. And very useful. And there are never enough of them."

---

He showed me.

He took a rod of iron in his tongs and thrust it into the heart of the forge, and worked the bellows, and waited. When the end of the rod glowed orange, he drew it out and laid it on the anvil and struck it with his hammer. Four blows. Quick and even. Turning the rod a quarter-turn between each. And the end of the rod drew out into a point, square and sharp.

Then he set the rod into a hole in a heavy iron plate—the nail header, he called it—and nipped it off with a chisel a finger's length from the point, and struck the stub that stood up from the plate. Three blows. And the stub mushroomed out into a head, flat and round.

He knocked the plate against the anvil, and a nail fell out onto the stone floor, glowing dull red, hissing faintly.

The whole thing took less time than it takes to tell.

"There," said Kilvin. "A nail." He handed me the tongs. "Now you."

---

I made a bad nail. Of course I did. I held the rod in the fire too long, and it burned, and threw off sparks. I struck it too hard, and the point bent. I nipped it off too short, and the head split when I struck it. What fell out of the header onto the floor was a twisted, lumpy, useless thing, more like a knot of iron than a nail.

I looked at it. I did not feel anything.

"Again," said Kilvin.

I made another. It was also bad. Not as bad as the first; the point was straight, this time. But the head was lopsided, and the shank had a kink in it.

"Again," said Kilvin.

---

I made nails all that day. From dawn until well after dark. Kilvin stood beside me the whole time, with his arms folded across his chest, and said nothing except *again*. He did not correct me. He did not show me a second time. He did not tell me what I was doing wrong. He only watched, and said *again*, and handed me another rod of iron from the sack.

And slowly, very slowly, the nails got better.

I do not know how. I was not thinking about it. I was not thinking about anything. I held the rod in the fire and drew it out and struck it and turned it and struck it and nipped it and struck it, over and over, the way you breathe. And somewhere in the doing, my hands began to learn.

They learned how long to leave the iron in the fire: until the color was just so, orange shading to yellow at the very tip. They learned how hard to strike: not hard at all, really, only firm and even, letting the weight of the hammer do the work. They learned the quarter-turn between each blow, and the angle of the chisel, and the rhythm of the whole thing. *Heat. Four. Turn. Nip. Three. Knock.*

By noon, one nail in three was usable. By dusk, two in three. By the time the moon came up over the rooftops of the Fishery, and Kilvin finally put his big hand on my shoulder and said *enough*, there was a pile of nails on the floor beside the anvil as high as my knee. And most of them were good.

---

I stood beside the forge, with the hammer still in my hand, and looked at them. My arm ached. My hands were blistered. My face was black with soot and running with sweat.

I did not feel anything. I want to be honest about that. It was not a moment of triumph. I did not look at the pile of nails and feel proud, or pleased, or even tired.

But I felt my hands. That was the thing. For the first time since the chamber beneath the Lackless house, I felt my hands. They were aching and blistered and hot, and they hurt, and they were *mine*. They had done something. They had learned something. And they were still humming with it, the rhythm of it, *heat four turn nip three knock*, the way your body goes on rocking after you step off a boat.

Kilvin was watching my face.

"There," he said quietly. "You feel it."

I did not answer. I looked down at my hands.

"The hands remember," said Kilvin. "Even when the rest forgets. Even when the name is gone. The hands remember what they have done." He took the hammer from me, gently, and set it on the anvil. "This is what I wanted to show you. Before you go."

---

He sat down on the stool by the forge. I had never seen Kilvin sit down in his own workshop. He was always standing, always moving, always bent over some project or another. But he sat now, heavily, with his big scarred hands on his knees, and looked at the pile of nails.

"When I was young," he said, "younger than you, I was very sick. A fever. In the mountains, in Ceald, where I was born. It burned for eleven days. When it broke, I did not know who I was." He was quiet for a moment. "Not for long. A span. Perhaps two. But I did not know my name, or my mother's face, or the village I was born in. I did not know anything."

I looked at him.

"My father was a smith," said Kilvin. "He did not know what to do with me. He was not a gentle man, or a clever one. He did not know how to help. So he did the only thing he knew." Kilvin nodded at the anvil. "He put a hammer in my hand, and a rod of iron, and he said: *make nails.*"

"And you remembered?"

"Not at once." Kilvin shook his head slowly. "But my hands did. They had made nails since I was six years old. They knew how. And while my hands were making nails, the rest of me could rest. Could be quiet. Could wait." He looked at his hands. "And one day, in the middle of a nail, I remembered my mother's face. And the next day, my name. And the day after that, everything else."

---

We sat by the forge for a long time. The coals ticked and settled. Outside, somewhere in the dark, a cat was yowling.

"You are going somewhere quiet," said Kilvin at last. "Somewhere far away. With your chest and your Fae friend and no name." He looked at me. "It will be very quiet. And very long. Years, maybe. That is what Elodin says."

"Yes."

"You will need something to do with your hands." Kilvin's voice was very gentle, for him. "It does not matter what. Nails. Bread. Polishing cups. Mending a roof. It does not matter. Only that your hands are busy. Only that they are making something. Even small things." He paused. "Even nails."

"Why?"

Kilvin looked at me for a long moment.

"Because the hands hold the door open," he said. "When the rest is closed. They keep a little space. So that when the name comes back, it has somewhere to come back *to*."

---

He gave me the nails. All of them. The whole knee-high pile, swept into a canvas bag and tied at the neck. It was very heavy. I did not know what to do with it.

"Take them," said Kilvin. "Wherever you go. Use them. Build things." He almost smiled, in his beard. "They are not very good nails. But they are yours."

I took them.

They went into the cart, beside the chest, and they came with me all the way across the Commonwealth, and into an empty inn at a crossroads. And I used them. Every one. I used them to mend the roof, and the stable door, and the shutters, and the loose boards on the stairs. I used them to build the shelves behind the bar, and the frame for the sword above it, and the bench outside the kitchen door where Bast sat in the evenings.

The Waystone Inn is held together by Kilvin's nails. The bad ones and the good ones. Made by a man with no name, in a single day, in the Fishery, with a Cealdish master standing beside him saying *again*.

---

"Are they still there?" said Chronicler. "The nails?"

"Every one." The innkeeper looked around the common room. At the bar, and the shelves, and the stairs. "I used the last of them the spring before you came. On the stable door. It had come loose again." He was quiet for a moment. "I remember thinking, when I drove it in: that's the last one. I've used them all up. And I didn't know why that made me sad."

"And now?"

The innkeeper looked down at his hands. They were resting on the table. Long-fingered and red-knuckled, with calluses on the fingertips from the lute and a thin pale scar running around the left wrist.

"Now I think Kilvin was right," he said. "The hands held the door open. Seven years. Nail by nail." He turned them over, palms up. "And when the name came back, it had somewhere to come back to."

He was quiet for a long moment.

"I should write to him," he said. "When this is over. I should tell him. He asked me to write when the candle burned blue." He almost smiled. "I suppose it has. I suppose I owe him two letters now."
