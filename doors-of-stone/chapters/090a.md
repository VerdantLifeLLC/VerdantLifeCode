# What Elodin Took Back

Elodin came again on the fourth night. Alone.

I was sitting by the window in the top room of the Grey Man, looking out at the lights of Imre. The others had gone to bed, or gone home. Denna was asleep in the next room; I could hear her breathing through the wall, slow and even. Bast was somewhere on the roof. He did not sleep much, those days. He said the city was too loud.

I was not doing anything. I was sitting. That was most of what I did, in the grey. Sit, and look at things, and not feel anything about them.

There was a tap on the window. I looked up. Elodin was crouched on the sill outside, balanced on the balls of his bare feet, three stories above the street, grinning at me through the glass like a gargoyle.

I opened the window. He climbed in.

---

He did not say anything at first. He walked around the room, touching things. The back of a chair. The edge of the washstand. The iron latch of the door. He picked up a candle stub from the table and smelled it, and put it down again. He looked out the window for a long time, at the rooftops.

Then he sat down on the floor, cross-legged, facing me.

"Do you remember the snow?" he said.

I looked at him. I did not know what he meant. I did not remember much of anything, then. But something moved in me, very faintly, at the word. Like a stone shifting at the bottom of a deep pool.

"No," I said.

"No. Of course you don't." Elodin looked at his hands. "It was on the roof of the Rookery. Years ago. Your last winter with us. There were four of you. We were learning the name of snow." He was quiet for a moment. "You said it forgets. Or pretends to."

I did not say anything.

"And I told you," said Elodin, "that someday you would want very much to forget something. To cover it over. To make everything smooth and white, as if it had never happened. And that you would find, if you were unlucky, that you could." He looked up at me. "And I told you not to."

---

The candle on the table guttered in the draft from the window. Elodin did not close it. He sat on the floor with his dark eyes on my face.

"I was wrong," he said.

I looked at him.

"I don't say that often," said Elodin. "It's not that I'm never wrong. I'm wrong all the time. Constantly. Magnificently. But I don't usually notice, and when I do, I don't usually care." He smiled faintly. "But I care about this one. So I came to say it. Even though you won't remember I did."

"Wrong about what?"

"About the snow." He looked out the window. "I told you not to cover things over. I told you it was dangerous. I told you it was the worst thing a namer could do." He was quiet. "Because that's what I did. When I was seventeen. I tried to change my name, and when it went wrong, I tried to forget. To cover it all over. To make myself smooth and white and new." He looked at his hands. "It broke me. Three years in Haven. You know that. I told you."

I did not know it. But I nodded.

"So I thought it was always wrong," said Elodin. "Always. For everyone. I thought the only honest thing was to carry it. All of it. Every broken piece. Even if it cut you." He shook his head slowly. "I was very proud of that. Of carrying it. I made it into a kind of philosophy. I told my students. I told you."

"And now?"

Elodin was quiet for a long time.

"Now I've seen the cord," he said. "On your wrist. What she did. How she did it." He looked at me. "And I've seen you. Sitting here. Grey and quiet and whole. Not broken. Not torn in half. Just—put down. Folded up. Covered over, very gently, with snow."

He reached out and touched the back of my left hand, very lightly, just above the knotted cord.

"She didn't try to change your name," he said. "That's the difference. That's what I didn't understand, when I was seventeen. I tried to make myself into something else. She didn't. She just held what you were. Held it very carefully. So that you could stop carrying it for a while."

---

"Is that good?" I said.

It was a strange question. I knew it as I said it. It was the kind of question a child asks. But I did not know any other kind, then.

Elodin looked at me for a long moment.

"It's snow," he said. "It's what snow does. It covers things. It makes the world quiet. It lets things rest, underneath, through the long cold months, until the ground is ready." He was quiet. "And then it melts. And everything's still there. Just the same as before."

"It doesn't really forget," I said. "It only pretends."

I did not know where the words came from. They came up out of the grey from somewhere very deep, the way the chord had come out of my hands in the farmhouse. Something my body remembered, even if I did not.

Elodin's face went very still.

"Yes," he said softly. "That's right. That's exactly right." He was quiet for a long moment. "You said that. On the roof. Do you know that? Those exact words."

"No."

"No." He smiled. It was a strange smile, and very sad, and very fond. "But you still know it. Somewhere. Down at the bottom, where the truth lives." He took his hand away from mine. "That's what I came to tell you. That's all. That I was wrong, and snow isn't always the enemy. Sometimes it's the only thing that keeps the seeds alive through the winter."

---

He stood up. He went to the window. He climbed up onto the sill and crouched there, balanced, with the night behind him and the cold wind stirring his hair.

"Master Elodin," I said.

He looked back at me.

"Will I remember?" I said. "Any of it? When it melts?"

Elodin did not answer at once. He looked out at the rooftops of Imre, dark and silver in the starlight. Far off, across the river, the University was a black shape against the sky. The Archives, windowless. The Rookery, with its round room at the top.

"You'll remember everything," he said at last. "That's the terrible thing about snow. It doesn't take anything. It only waits." He looked at me. "You'll remember the good and the bad. The things you did and the things you didn't. All of it. All at once, when the thaw comes." He was quiet. "It's going to be very hard, Kvothe. Harder than anything. Harder than the forgetting."

"Then why wait?"

"Because the ground's not ready." Elodin's voice was very gentle. "Not yet. You'd drown. Everything you are would come back all at once, and there's too much of it, and it's too loud, and it would drown you. You need time. Years, maybe. To let the ground get ready. To make a little space in yourself where it can come back and have somewhere to stand."

He looked at me for a long moment.

"Make the space," he said. "That's all. That's all you have to do. While you're waiting. Be small. Be quiet. Polish things." He almost smiled. "Auri's right about that. She usually is. Make a small, quiet, clean space. And then, when the thaw comes, there'll be room."

---

He turned to go. Then he stopped, crouched on the sill, and looked back one last time.

"And Kvothe," he said. "When it comes. The thaw. When it all comes back." His dark eyes were very bright. "Don't try to carry it all by yourself. That was my other mistake. I thought I had to. I thought that was what made it honest." He shook his head. "It's not. It's just what makes it heavy."

"Who should I carry it with?"

Elodin looked at me. Then he looked up, at the roof above the window, where Bast was sitting in the dark, listening. I had not known he was listening. Elodin had.

"Someone will come," he said. "Someone always does. Someone to listen. Someone to write it down." He smiled. "Stories are very good at carrying heavy things. That's what they're for."

And then he was gone. Off the sill and into the dark, over the rooftops, barefoot, laughing at something only he could hear.

---

I did not remember that conversation. Not for seven years. It went into the snow with everything else.

But when it melted, it was one of the first things that came back. Before Denna. Before my parents. Before my own name. I was sitting at a table in an inn at a crossroads, with a scribe across from me and his pen moving on the page, and I heard, very clearly, a voice from very far away, on a windowsill in Imre, three stories above the street:

*Stories are very good at carrying heavy things. That's what they're for.*

And I understood, then, why I had said yes.
