# The Price of a Lock

I did not say anything for a long time.

I sat in the round tower at the center of the dead white city, with a cup of cold tea in my hands and the fire crackling in the hearth, and I looked at the oldest man in the world, and I did not say anything at all.

Beside me, Bast had gone very still.

"Explain," I said at last. My voice was very quiet.

Selitos set down his cup. He folded his long hands on the table.

"The keeper's door," he said, "is two names braided together. The name of the door, and the name of the line. That is what makes it open to the line. That is what makes it dangerous." He looked at me. "If you could take the name of the line out of the door—unbraid it—then the door would be only a door. A name of stone, like the other six. It would answer to no one. No key could open it. No blood. No voice."

"Then how—"

"Only the line can unbraid it." Selitos's voice was very steady. "Only one of the line, standing before the door, with the key in hand, speaking the door's name. Not to open it. To *un*-speak their own part of it. To take the name of the line back out of the stone." He paused. "And to do that, they must give it up. Their share of it. The part of the line's name that lives in them."

"Their own name."

"Their own name. Or the part of it that is the line. Which, in a namer of the line—in someone like you—is most of it." He looked at me. "You would have to take your name out of yourself, singer. And leave it with the door. Or somewhere else. Somewhere away from you. And you would have to never pick it up again."

---

The fire crackled. The wind moaned in the ruins outside.

"What would happen to me?" I asked.

Selitos was silent for a long time.

"You would be less," he said at last. "Not empty. Not dead. But less. You would be a man without a name. You would walk and speak and eat and sleep. You would remember things, some of them. But you would not be *you*. Not really. You would be the shape of a man, walking around a hole."

"Could I name?"

"No. Naming comes from the name. Without it—" He shook his head. "No naming. No sympathy, probably, or very little. Sympathy needs the Alar, and the Alar needs a self to hold it. No music, perhaps. Or music without the fire in it." He looked at me with his one good eye. "You would be ordinary, singer. The most ordinary man in the world. That is the price."

I looked down at my hands. At my good right hand, and my cold left one, with the white print of Cinder's fingers on the wrist.

"And the door?"

"The door would be locked. Forever. Or as close to forever as anything is." He paused. "Unless someone spoke your name again. At the door. With the key in hand."

I looked up.

"The name does not vanish," said Selitos. "Names cannot be destroyed. Not true ones. They can only be set down. Put somewhere. Kept." He leaned forward. "If you gave up your name at the door, it would have to go somewhere. And wherever it went, it would still be the key. The last key. The only key. If anyone ever found it, and carried it to the door, and spoke it there, with the stone in their hand—the door would open. And there would be no line left to close it again."

"So the name has to be hidden."

"The name has to be hidden. Kept. Held. By someone you trust, in something that will hold, somewhere no one would ever think to look." Selitos's voice was very quiet. "And you would have to live the rest of your life without it. Knowing that it was out there. Knowing that if anyone ever found it, everything you gave up would be for nothing."

---

"Can it be done?" I asked. "Has anyone ever done it?"

Selitos looked at me for a long time.

"Once," he said.

"Who?"

"Your mother."

---

I did not move. I could not.

"Not all the way," said Selitos quietly. "She did not go to the door. She did not have the key; her father still kept it then, and after him, her sister. She could not have unbraided the line from the stone." He paused. "But she knew enough. From her family. From the old stories. From—" He hesitated. "From the man you call Bredon, who told her too much when she was a girl, because he thought she would be useful."

"What did she do?"

"She changed her name." Selitos looked into the fire. "Not the name she was called by, though she changed that too. Netalia to Laurian. She changed the other one. The deep one. She could not take the line out of it. But she could hide it. Bury it. Wrap it in another name, like a stone wrapped in cloth, so that the door would not know her. So that the line would not answer." He was silent for a moment. "It is why they could not find her, for so long. It is why she could hide among the Ruh for twelve years. The door could not feel her. The key could not feel her. She had made herself into someone else."

"And me?"

"And you were born to someone else," said Selitos. "To Laurian, not to Netalia. Your name was shaped in her, and she had wrapped her own name so well that yours grew up wrapped too. Hidden. Quiet." He looked at me. "That is why they did not find you, either, until it was too late. Until you were already born and already grown and already singing."

"Then how did they find us? At the end?"

Selitos looked at me with his one good eye. It was very sad.

"Your father's song," he said. "It was very good. Very true. And when a true song is sung, it rings out like a bell. It is heard." He paused. "They did not find your mother. They found the song. And your mother was singing it."

---

I got up from the table. I did not mean to. I simply found myself on my feet, and then walking, and then standing at the narrow window of the tower, looking out at the ruins of the white city.

The sun was going down behind the peaks. The white stone was turning gold, then red, then the color of old blood. The shadows of the broken walls stretched out long across the snow.

Behind me, I heard Selitos's chair scrape. I heard his bare feet on the stone floor, coming toward me. He stopped beside me at the window.

"I am sorry," he said.

"You didn't kill them."

"No. But I made the door, and the line, and the key. I made the curse. I made the Amyr, and the Singers, and all the rest of it." He looked out at the ruins. "Everything that happened to your family happened because of things I did, a very long time ago. Things I thought were right." He was silent for a moment. "I have had a long time to think about whether they were."

"And?"

"And I still do not know." He smiled faintly. "That is the curse of a long life, singer. You never stop doubting."

---

We stood at the window for a long time, watching the sun go down.

"Selitos," I said at last. "If I do it. If I lock the door forever. What happens to Lanre?"

He did not answer right away.

"He goes on," he said at last. "As he is. Forever. Cursed. Remembering. Unable to die." He was quiet for a moment. "He wants the door opened so that the Thief will unmake him. If the door can never be opened, he can never be unmade. He will walk the world until the world ends. And then, perhaps, a little while after."

"That's cruel."

"Yes."

"You made it that way."

"I did." Selitos looked at me. "I told you. I have had a long time to doubt."

"Is there no other way? To end the curse? Without opening the door?"

Selitos was silent for a very long time. The sun sank behind the peaks. The ruins went grey, then blue, then dark.

"There is a way," he said at last. "Perhaps. There is always a way, with names. But I do not know it. I have looked, for a very long time. I have read every book in this tower, and every book in every other place I could reach. I have not found it." He looked at me. "Perhaps you will. Someday. You are cleverer than I was, at your age. And you have something I never had."

"What?"

"A voice," said Selitos. "The song you sang. It did something I never could. It made him *remember*. Not just the pain. All of it. Lyra's name, in a voice that did not lie." He was quiet for a moment. "A curse is a name spoken in anger. Perhaps it can be answered by a name spoken in—something else."

"In what?"

He smiled. It was a very old smile, and very tired, and very sad.

"If I knew that," he said, "I would not still be here."

---

That night I did not sleep. I sat by the fire in the round tower with my lute in my lap and did not play it. Bast slept in a chair beside me, curled up with his hooves tucked under him. Selitos sat across the fire in his own chair, with his one good eye closed and his stone eye open, glinting in the firelight. I did not know if he was sleeping. I did not know if he ever slept.

I thought about what he had told me.

*You would have to give up your name. Leave it somewhere. Never pick it up again.*

*You would be the shape of a man, walking around a hole.*

*You would need someone to hold it for you. Someone you trust. In something that will hold.*

I thought about Elodin, standing at the gate of Haven with his forehead against the bars. *A name can be put down. It's very rare. Very difficult.*

I thought about the Cthaeh, whispering in the dark. *When you set down your name, who will you trust to hold it?*

I thought about my mother. Wrapping her name in another name, like a stone wrapped in cloth. Hiding among the Ruh for twelve years. Singing my father's song.

I took out Denna's knot. The one from the Grey Man. *Road. Promise. A story told many times, and changes in the telling.* And the tiny knot at its heart that held her name.

I held it in my hand for a long time, looking at it in the firelight.

*The girl with the knots*, the Cthaeh had said. *She's the only one who could. The only one in all the world who knows how to tie a name into a cord so that it holds.*

I closed my hand around it.

And across the fire, very quietly, Selitos said, "Yes."

I looked up. His one good eye was open. He was looking at me.

"Yes," he said again. "That would work. If she were willing. If she could do it." He was silent for a moment. "If you could bear to let her."

"How did you—"

"I see names, singer." He touched his stone eye. "And yours is very loud tonight. It has been thinking."

I looked down at the knot in my hand.

"Is it the Cthaeh's path?" I asked. "If I do this? If I give my name to her? Is it what it wants?"

"I do not know," said Selitos. "No one knows. That is the nature of the thing." He looked at me with his one good eye. "You will have to choose. And bear what comes after."

"That's what everyone keeps telling me."

"That is because it is true." He closed his eye again. "It is the only truth there is, in the end. Everything else is just a story."

---

In the morning, before we left, Selitos gave me the door's name.

Not the name of the keeper's door. The name of one of the others. The one beneath the Archives. The one I had stood before, in the Chamber of Watches, with Devi's hand in mine.

"So that you will know what it sounds like," he said. "A door's name. So that when you stand before the other one, you will recognize it." He looked at me. "Do not speak it. Ever. Not even in a whisper. Just hold it. Let it sit in you. Let it teach you the shape."

He leaned forward and spoke it into my ear.

I will not tell you what it was. I could not, even if I wanted to. It was not a word. It was not even a sound, exactly. It was more like a feeling. A weight. A great slow deep patient *no*, older than any stone I had ever heard, braided through with something else. Something that was not *no*. Something that was almost a *yes*, held tightly, held down, held back.

It was the shape of a prison. The shape of something holding something else, forever, against its will.

I felt it settle into me. Into my bones. Into the part of me where names live.

When I opened my eyes, Selitos was looking at me.

"Now you know," he said.

"Yes."

"Go, then. Go south. The war is waiting. And the door." He paused. "And the girl."

He walked us to the edge of the plaza. At the great arch on the east side, he stopped.

"This is as far as I go," he said. "I cannot leave the city."

"Will I see you again?"

He looked at me with his one good eye. Then he smiled, very faintly.

"Perhaps," he said. "If you ever find the other way. The one that ends the curse without opening the door." He paused. "If you ever find it, come back. And sing it to me. I would like to hear it very much."

He raised one long hand, in something that was almost a blessing.

"Go," he said. "Singer. Key. Kvothe." He paused. "Go and choose."

We went.

When I looked back, from the top of the pass, he was still standing under the great arch at the edge of the plaza, very tall and very still, with the sun on his white hair and his stone eye glinting.

He was watching us go. He watched until we were out of sight.

I think, sometimes, that he is still watching.
