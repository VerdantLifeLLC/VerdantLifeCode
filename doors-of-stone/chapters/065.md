# The Twilight Court

We could not go back the way we had come. The Sithe had forbidden it, and I believed them. But we could not go south through the mortal world either. It was two months' hard travel from the Stormwal to Vintas, over mountains and through wilderness and across a country at war. By the time we arrived, it would be over. One way or another.

"There's another way," said Bast.

We were sitting in the shallow cave where the scrael had come for us, on the far side of the high pass, eating the last of my bread. The ring of greystones stood on the bare shoulder of the mountain below us, grey against the snow.

"The greystones go more places than one," he said. "If you know how to ask them. The Moon's Road is only one road. There are others." He hesitated. "My father's court has roads of its own. Old ones. The Telwyth Mael have always had a fondness for the mortal mountains. It's where we came from, in a way." He looked at his hooves. "If I asked the stones to take us to my father's court, they'd do it. And from my father's court, there are roads to everywhere."

"Your father's court," I said. "Where you ran away from. Where the Sithe said you shouldn't go back to, because you might be poisoned."

"I know." Bast looked at me. "But you'd be with me. And you're taking me to the mortal world, like they said. We'd only be passing through." He paused. "And my father would want to know. That I'm alive. That I didn't—" He stopped.

I looked at him for a long moment.

"You want to see him," I said.

Bast looked away. "Maybe," he said quietly. "A little."

---

The greystones took us to the Twilight Court.

It was not like the Moon's Road, or the Hollow Market, or anywhere else I had been in the Fae. It was a valley, deep and narrow, cut between two great cliffs of dark grey stone. The cliffs rose up so high on either side that the twilight sky above was only a thin ribbon of violet. A river ran along the valley floor, dark and swift and silent. And along the banks of the river, and climbing up the cliffs on either side, there was a city.

Not a city of stone, like Myr Tariniel. A city of wood and vine and living things. Houses grown out of great trees, their branches twisted into walls and roofs. Bridges of living vine, stretching from cliff to cliff across the valley. Stairs cut into the stone and overgrown with moss and flowers. Lanterns hanging everywhere, from every branch and bridge and eave, glowing soft gold and green and blue in the endless twilight.

It was beautiful. It was one of the most beautiful places I have ever seen. And it was very quiet. As if the whole city were holding its breath.

"It's always like this," said Bast softly, as we walked along the riverbank. "Quiet. My father likes it quiet." He looked around at the living houses, the glowing lanterns. His face was strange. "I'd forgotten how quiet."

People saw us as we passed. Fae people. Some of them tall and fair, some of them small and quick, some of them with hooves like Bast's, or horns, or eyes like a cat's. They stopped what they were doing and stared at us. At Bast, mostly. Some of them whispered. Some of them bowed. One old woman, sitting on the step of a house grown out of a willow tree, burst into tears and covered her face with her hands.

Bast did not look at any of them. He walked straight ahead with his chin up and his jaw tight, and he did not stop until we came to the end of the valley, where the two great cliffs came together and the river poured out of a cave in the rock.

There was a house there. Bigger than the others. Grown out of a tree so old and so huge that its trunk was wider than the Eolian, and its branches reached up the cliffs on either side like arms. There were stairs spiraling up around the trunk, and windows glowing gold in the bark, and at the top of the stairs, a great door of living wood.

Bast stopped at the foot of the stairs. He looked up at the door.

"Wait here," he said. "Please. Let me go first."

I waited.

---

He was gone a long time. I sat on a root at the foot of the great tree and watched the river pour out of the cave, dark and swift and silent. People came and went along the riverbank behind me. Some of them stared at me. None of them spoke.

At last, the great door at the top of the stairs opened, and Bast came out. His face was wet. He came down the stairs slowly and stopped in front of me.

"He wants to see you," he said.

---

Remmen, Lord of the Telwyth Mael, received me in a round room at the heart of the great tree. The walls were living wood, smooth and golden and faintly warm, and there were no windows. The only light came from a pool in the center of the floor, a round pool of still dark water that glowed softly from below, like a lantern under ice.

Remmen was sitting beside the pool on a low bench. He was tall and lean and dark-haired, like Bast, with the same fine-boned face and the same deep blue eyes. But he was older. Much older. There were lines around his eyes, and his hair was touched with silver at the temples. His feet, where they showed beneath the hem of his long green robe, were hooves.

He looked at me for a long time without speaking. His face was very still.

"You are the mortal," he said at last. His voice was deep and soft and very tired. "The one who stood between my son and the Sithe."

"Yes, my lord."

"Why?"

I told him what I had told Bast. About being twelve, and alone, and having no one to stand in front of me.

He listened in silence. When I was done, he looked down at the glowing pool for a long time.

"His mother was mortal," he said. "Did he tell you?"

"Yes."

"She left. When he was very small. He never understood why." Remmen was quiet for a moment. "I never told him. I could not bear to."

I did not say anything.

"She left because she was dying," said Remmen softly. "Mortals die, in the twilight, if they stay too long. Slowly. So slowly you do not notice, at first. They fade. They grow thin and pale and quiet, like a flower kept too long out of the sun." He looked into the pool. "She stayed with us for thirty mortal years. Longer than any mortal should. Because she loved us. Because she could not bear to leave him." He paused. "And then one day she looked in a mirror and saw what she had become, and she knew that if she stayed another year, she would die. And that he would watch her die. And that he would never forgive himself, because it was his birth that had made her stay so long."

The pool glowed softly. The living walls were very still.

"So she left," said Remmen. "Without saying goodbye. Because if she had said goodbye, she would not have been able to go." He looked up at me. "She went back through a greystone in the mountains, into your world, and she lived there for another eleven years. In a little village in the Stormwal foothills. She married a shepherd. She had two more children." He smiled faintly. "She was happy, I think. I hope. I watched her, sometimes, in the pool."

"Did you ever tell Bast?"

"No." Remmen's voice was very quiet. "I could not. He would have gone to find her. And she would have seen him, and it would have broken her heart." He was silent for a moment. "And then she died. Twelve mortal years ago. Of a fever, in the winter. And after that, there seemed no point in telling him at all."

---

I looked at him for a long time.

"He went to the Cthaeh," I said, "to ask it why she left."

"I know. He told me." Remmen closed his eyes. "It would have told him the truth. And it would have told it in the way that would hurt him most. That she left because of him. Because his birth trapped her. Because loving him was killing her." He opened his eyes. "And he would have carried that forever."

"You should tell him," I said. "The truth. The whole truth. Not the Cthaeh's version. Yours."

Remmen looked at me. His deep blue eyes were very sad.

"You are very young," he said. "You think the truth always helps."

"I think the truth told with love helps more than the truth told with cruelty," I said. "And the Cthaeh's already whispered to him. Once. In the net. It's already planted something." I paused. "You can plant something too. Something better."

Remmen was silent for a very long time.

"Perhaps," he said at last. "Perhaps you are right." He looked down at the pool. "I will think on it."

---

He showed me the pool, after that. It was a scrying pool, old and deep, that could look into the mortal world if you knew how to ask it. He asked it to show me Vintas. Severen. The war.

It showed me.

I knelt at the edge of the glowing water and looked down into it, and I saw Severen. The city on the cliff, with the Maer's palace at the top. But it was not the Severen I remembered.

The walls were broken. There were great gaps in them, black and jagged, like missing teeth. The low city at the foot of the cliff was burned; only blackened timbers and tumbled stone remained. Smoke rose from the high city in a dozen places. And over the gates of the Maer's palace, where the Maer's banner had always flown, there was another banner.

Black and silver. The arms of the house of Jakis.

"No," I whispered.

"It fell three spans ago," said Remmen quietly. "By your world's reckoning. They breached the walls with sympathy and fire, and the low city burned, and the high city was taken street by street." He looked at me. "Your Maer escaped. With some of his people. They are in the hills to the south, hiding."

"And his wife?"

Remmen touched the surface of the pool. The image shifted. I saw a camp. A great army camp, sprawling across a green valley. Tents and banners and cookfires. Soldiers everywhere. And in the center of the camp, a great pavilion of black and silver silk.

"She was taken," said Remmen. "In the fall of the palace. She was very near her time. She gave birth in the king's camp, eleven days ago." He looked at me. "A daughter."

I stared into the pool. At the black and silver pavilion.

"Three spans," I said. "Severen fell three spans ago."

"Yes."

"I was only in the twilight for—" I tried to count. The Hollow Market. Felurian. The Sithe. The Moon's Road. "A span. Maybe two."

"Time runs differently here," said Remmen gently. "You know that. You must have known it."

I had known it. I had known it the first time, with Felurian, when I came out of her glade after what felt like days and found that months had passed. I had known it, and I had gambled anyway. I had thought the Moon's Road would be faster than the mortal roads. And it had been. But not fast enough.

"How long?" I said. "How long since I left Imre?"

Remmen was quiet for a moment.

"Nearly five months," he said.

---

I knelt there beside the pool for a long time. I did not move. I did not speak.

Five months. While I walked through the silver forest and bought a road for a song and argued with the Sithe and sang to the mountain and drank tea with Selitos. Five months, in the mortal world. The siege, and the fall, and Meluan taken, and her daughter born in the camp of the man who would use her blood to open a door.

*A child of the line*, Skarpi had said. *It will be many years before anyone knows.*

But Bredon did not need to know. Not really. A newborn of the line, with pure blood, not yet named, not yet grown. Not a namer, perhaps. But perhaps she did not need to be. Perhaps a newborn's blood, and a newborn's cry, and the key of stone in a king's hand, would be enough to wake the door.

Or perhaps they were only waiting. For me. With my aunt and her daughter as bait.

"Where are they going?" I said. My voice was hoarse. "The king's army. Where are they going?"

Remmen touched the pool again. The image shifted. I saw the army moving. A great column of men and horses and wagons, winding through green hills. And at the head of the column, a banner of black and silver. And beneath the banner, riding a tall black horse, a young man in black with a crown on his head.

Ambrose.

And beside him, on a grey mare, a girl with dark hair, and an old man on a donkey, leaning on a stick.

"South and east," said Remmen. "Toward the hills."

"Toward Levinshir," I said.

"I do not know your mortal names for places." Remmen looked at me. "But there is an old house in those hills. Very old. Older than the kingdom that surrounds it. I have seen it in the pool, many times. It has a door beneath it." His deep blue eyes were very steady. "They will be there in nine days."

---

Bast found me an hour later, sitting on a root at the foot of the great tree, staring at the dark river.

He sat down beside me. He did not say anything for a long time.

"My father told me," he said at last. "About my mother. Why she left." His voice was very quiet. "He said you told him to."

"I suggested it."

"He said you told him the truth told with love helps more than the truth told with cruelty." Bast looked at the river. "He said he'd never thought of it like that."

"Did it help?"

Bast was quiet for a long time.

"Yes," he said at last. "I think so. It hurts. It hurts a lot." He wiped his eyes with the back of his hand. "But it's a different kind of hurting. It's not—" He struggled for the word. "It's not *poisoned*. If that makes sense."

"It does."

We sat there together by the river for a while.

"He told me about the war too," said Bast. "About Severen. And the baby." He looked at me. "Nine days."

"Nine days."

"Can we get there in nine days?"

"Your father says there's a greystone in the hills near the Lackless house. An old one. He can send us there." I looked at the river. "We'd arrive in the mortal world tomorrow, by our reckoning. But I don't know how long that will be there. A day. A span. It's hard to say, going back."

"Then we should go now."

"Yes."

Bast did not move. Neither did I.

"Reshi," he said. "You know what's waiting there. Don't you? At the door."

"Yes."

"Bredon. And Ambrose. And Cinder, probably. And the Seven. And the thing behind the door." Bast's voice was very steady. "And you're the key. You're the one they need. And you're walking right into it."

"Yes."

"Why?"

I looked at him. At his deep blue eyes, wet with tears for his mother, and his fine-boned face, and the silver circlet on his brow.

"Because my aunt is there," I said. "And her daughter. And Denna." I paused. "And because if I don't go, they'll open it anyway, with the baby. And there won't be anyone there who knows how to close it."

"And you do? Know how?"

I thought about Selitos. About the door's name, sitting in my bones like a stone. About the price.

"Yes," I said. "I know how."

Bast looked at me for a long time. Then he stood up. He brushed off his clothes. He held out his hand.

"Then let's go," he said. "Reshi."

I took his hand. He pulled me to my feet.

And we went to find the greystone.
