# Pike

I found Pike on my second day in Tarbean, on the steps of a church in Hillside, begging.

I did not know him at first. Why would I? The last time I had seen him, he had been fourteen, big for his age, with a broad flat face and small mean eyes and a pair of fists he used on anyone smaller than himself. He had been the leader of a pack of older boys who ran the alleys near the docks. He had caught me, the first winter I was in Tarbean, in an alley behind a tannery, and he and his friends had held me down and taken my father's lute and smashed it against a wall.

I had been twelve. It was the last thing I had of my father's. I watched them break it, and I lay in the alley afterward with blood in my mouth, and I did not cry. I was past crying, by then.

I had hated him for a long time. Longer than I like to admit. I had imagined, many nights, on the rooftops of Tarbean, what I would do to him if I ever found him again, when I was bigger. I had imagined it in great detail.

And now here he was. On the steps of the church of Tehlu in Hillside, with a tin cup in front of him.

He had no legs.

---

They had been taken off above the knee. Both of them. The stumps were wrapped in filthy rags and tucked under him on a little wooden board with iron wheels, the kind that legless beggars push themselves around on with their hands. His hands were thick and callused and black with the dirt of the streets. His broad flat face was the same, but thinner, and covered in a patchy beard. His small eyes were the same too. But there was nothing mean in them now. Only a kind of dull patience, like the patience of an old dog.

I stood at the bottom of the church steps and looked at him, and I did not know him.

He looked up at me. He held up his cup.

"Spare a copper, sir?" he said. "For a poor cripple? Tehlu bless you."

I knew his voice.

---

I did not move. I stood at the bottom of the steps with my hands at my sides and looked at him, and I felt it rise up in me. The old hate. The old rage. The boy in the alley with blood in his mouth, watching his father's lute splinter against a wall.

*Here he is*, it said. *Here he is, at last. Helpless. Alone. No one to hold you down this time.*

I had a knife on my belt. I had Caesura on my back. I had spent months learning the Ketan from the Adem, and I could have killed him before he knew I had moved.

I did not move.

"What happened to your legs?" I said.

He blinked up at me. People did not usually ask. They usually dropped a coin, or did not, and walked on.

"Cart," he said. "Ran over 'em. Down by the docks. Six years back." He shrugged, the way you shrug about the weather. "Surgeon took 'em off. Said they'd rot otherwise. Took my money too. All of it." He held up his cup again. "Spare a copper?"

"Six years ago," I said.

"About that."

It would have been not long after I left Tarbean. After I stole a purse and bought passage and walked out of the city toward the University with my head full of dreams. While I was learning sympathy and playing at the Eolian, Pike had been lying in an alley by the docks with his legs crushed under a cart.

"Did anyone help you?" I said.

He looked at me for a long moment. Something moved in his small eyes.

"Trapis," he said. "The old man. With the cellar." He looked down at his hands. "Carried me there himself. Couldn't have weighed more than a sack of feathers, the old fellow, but he carried me. Put me on a cot and fed me soup for three months." He was quiet for a moment. "Never asked for nothing. Never asked what I done."

I stood very still.

"He died," I said. "Yesterday."

Pike looked up at me. His face went slack.

"Trapis?"

"Yes."

Pike did not say anything for a long time. He sat on his little board on the church steps with his cup in his hand, and his face did something complicated, and then he looked away down the street.

"Good man," he said at last. His voice was rough. "Better than me. Better than most." He wiped his nose with the back of his hand. "Better than I deserved."

---

I sat down on the steps beside him.

I do not know why. I did not decide to. I sat down on the cold stone beside the boy who had smashed my father's lute, and I looked out at the street with him, at the carts going by and the people hurrying and the gulls wheeling over the rooftops.

"Do you remember a red-haired boy?" I said. "About twelve. Came to Tarbean about nine years ago. Played a lute."

Pike was quiet. Then he turned his head and looked at me. At my face. At my hair.

I watched him remember.

His face went white under the dirt and the beard. His hands tightened on the edge of his board. He did not try to push himself away; he could not have gone fast enough, and he knew it. He only sat very still, and looked at me, and waited.

"You," he said.

"Me."

"You've come to—" He stopped. He swallowed. "Go on, then. Get it over with. I'll not fight you." He laughed, a short ugly sound. "Can't fight anybody, now."

I looked at him. At his thin face and his stumps wrapped in rags and his black callused hands.

"Why did you do it?" I said. "The lute. Why?"

Pike was quiet for a long time.

"'Cause you had something," he said at last. "And I didn't." He looked at the street. "You came into Waterside with your red hair and your lute, and you were starving same as the rest of us, but you had that lute. You'd sit in doorways at night and play it, soft, and everybody'd stop and listen. Even me." He was quiet for a moment. "I hated you for it. Hated you so bad I couldn't breathe. 'Cause you had something beautiful, and nobody could take it off you. Not the cold, not the hunger, not anybody." He looked down at his hands. "So I took it."

I did not say anything.

"Didn't make me feel any better," said Pike. "Thought it would. Didn't." He shrugged. "Nothing ever did. Not till Trapis."

---

We sat on the church steps for a long time. A priest came out of the church and looked at us, a red-haired man with a sword and a legless beggar sitting side by side on his steps, and frowned, and went back in.

At last I stood up.

Pike looked up at me. He was still waiting. For the blow, or the knife, or the curse.

I reached into my pack. I did not have any money; I had given it all to Mae, the day before. But I had something. A small thing I had bought in the market that morning with the last copper I found in the bottom of my pack, because it reminded me of something, and I did not know what.

It was a wooden whistle. A child's toy. Painted red, with the paint worn off at the mouthpiece.

I put it in his cup.

Pike looked down at it. Then up at me.

"What's that for?" he said.

"Music," I said. "Something beautiful. That nobody can take off you."

He stared at me. His small eyes were very wide.

I turned and walked away down the street. I did not look back.

But at the corner, before I turned it, I heard something behind me. Very faint, over the noise of the carts and the gulls. A thin wavering sound. Three notes, badly played, going up.

I stood at the corner and listened to it for a long time.

Then I went on to look for Skarpi. And I found, as I walked, that the old hate was gone. Not buried. Not locked behind the doors of sleep. Gone. Like a stone taken out of a shoe you have been walking on for nine years without knowing why it hurt.

I have never missed it.
