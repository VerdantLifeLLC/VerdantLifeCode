# The Girl Who Tied the Moon

*Bast told me this story through the window slit of my cell in the Tower of Thorns, on the longest night of that winter. I did not understand it, then. I did not understand anything. But I remembered it, the way I remembered the tapping from the cell below, and the lock of a baby's hair in blue silk. And when my name came back, it came back with it.*

*He said it was a story the Telwyth Mael tell their children. He said his mother told it to him, when he was very small, before she went away.*

---

Once, *said Bast*, before the Thief, before the war, before the twilight, there was a girl who could tie anything.

She lived in a village by the sea. A small one. Fishing boats and nets drying on the rocks and gulls crying all day long. And she was the daughter of a net-maker, and she had learned to tie knots before she could walk. By the time she was six, she could tie a net faster than her father. By the time she was ten, she could tie a net so fine a minnow couldn't slip through, and so strong a whale couldn't break it.

But that wasn't the strange thing about her. The strange thing was that her knots held more than fish.

When she tied a knot and thought about the sun, the knot was warm. When she tied a knot and thought about the rain, the knot was wet. When she tied a knot and thought about her mother's voice, singing in the kitchen, and put the knot to her ear, she could hear it. Faint. Far away. Like a song in another room.

She could tie *anything*. Anything she could hold in her mind, she could hold in a cord.

---

Her father was frightened of it. He told her to stop. He told her it was unnatural, and people would talk, and no good ever came of anything unnatural. So she stopped. Or she said she did. She tied ordinary nets for ordinary fish, and she smiled and nodded when her father spoke, and at night, in her little bed under the eaves, she tied other things.

She tied the smell of the sea after a storm. She tied the color of the sky at dawn. She tied the sound of the gulls, and the feel of sand between her toes, and the taste of her grandmother's honey cakes. She kept them all in a box under her bed, a box full of little knotted cords, and on nights when she was sad or lonely or couldn't sleep, she took them out and held them and was not sad anymore.

And one night, when she was sixteen, she looked up out of her little window under the eaves, and saw the moon.

---

The moon was very beautiful in those days, *said Bast*. More beautiful than now. Because in those days, the moon was whole. She was always full. She never waxed or waned. She hung in the sky every night like a great silver coin, round and bright and perfect.

And the girl looked at her, and thought: *I want to keep that.*

Not to own it. Not to take it. Just to keep a little of it. The way she kept the smell of the sea and the sound of the gulls. A little silver thread of moonlight, tied in a cord, in her box under the bed. So that on dark nights, when the clouds were thick and the moon was hidden, she could take it out and hold it and still see her.

So she took a cord. The finest she had. Spun from her own hair. And she held it up to the window, in the moonlight, and she began to tie.

---

It was the hardest knot she had ever tied. The moon did not want to be tied. She was too big, and too bright, and too far away. The cord kept slipping. The light kept sliding off it like water off oil.

But the girl was stubborn. She tied, and tied, and tied, all night long. Loop over loop over loop. Until her fingers bled. Until the sky began to grey in the east.

And just before dawn, just as the moon was setting, the knot held.

She felt it. A little tug. A little catch. And there, in the cord, very small, very faint, was a thread of silver light. A little piece of the moon.

She put it in her box, with the others. And she went to sleep, very tired and very happy.

---

And the next night, *said Bast*, the moon was not whole.

There was a little piece missing. A thin sliver, on one edge, like the paring of a fingernail. Hardly anything. You had to look very hard to see it.

But the moon had never had a piece missing before. And she was very frightened.

She looked down at the world. She looked everywhere. In every village and every city, every forest and every sea. Looking for the piece of herself that was missing. And at last she found it. In a little box under a little bed in a little room under the eaves, in a fishing village by the sea.

And she came down to get it.

---

The girl woke in the night, and the moon was in her room.

Not all of her. She was much too big. But a little of her. A silver light, very bright, filling the little room under the eaves from floor to ceiling. And a voice. Very soft, very sad, very far away.

*You have something of mine*, said the moon. *May I have it back?*

The girl sat up in bed. She was very frightened. But she was also very stubborn.

"I only took a little," she said. "Just a thread. So I could keep you. On dark nights."

*I know*, said the moon. *And it was a very beautiful knot. The most beautiful anyone has ever tied. But I cannot be whole without it. And if I am not whole, I cannot be what I am.*

"What are you?"

The moon was quiet for a long time.

*I am the one who is always there*, she said. *Even when the clouds are thick. Even when you cannot see me. I am always whole, and always there, and always watching over you. That is what I am.* She was quiet again. *But now there is a piece of me missing. And I am not always whole. And so I am not always there. And so I am not what I was.*

The girl looked at the box under her bed. At the little cord with the thread of silver light in it.

And she understood.

---

She took out the cord. She held it up. And she began to untie it.

It was harder than tying it had been. Much harder. The knot did not want to come undone. It had caught the moonlight, and it wanted to hold on to it, the way all her knots held on to things. Her fingers bled again. She wept. But she did not stop.

And just before dawn, just as the moon was setting, the knot came loose. And the thread of silver light slipped out of the cord, and floated up, out the little window under the eaves, into the sky.

And the moon was whole again.

---

*Thank you*, said the moon. Very softly. From very far away. *That was very brave. And very kind. Most people would have kept it.*

"I wanted to keep it," said the girl. She was crying. "I wanted to keep it more than anything."

*I know*, said the moon. *That is why it was brave.*

And then, just before she set, she said one more thing.

*Because you gave it back, I will give you something. Not a piece of me. You cannot hold a piece of me. But a promise.* The light in the little room grew very soft. *Whenever you are lost, look up. And I will be there. Whole. Always. Watching. And you will know the way home.*

And the moon set. And the girl sat in her little bed under the eaves, with the empty cord in her hands, and watched the sun come up over the sea.

---

Bast was quiet for a long time, at the window slit. I lay on my pallet in the dark and did not say anything.

*That's the end*, he said at last. *That's the whole story. My mother told it to me every night, for a year. Before she left.*

*Then the Thief came*, I said. I did not know why I said it. It came up out of the hole in my chest from somewhere very far down. *And stole the moon. And she was never whole again.*

The window slit was very quiet.

*Yes*, said Bast at last. His voice was very strange. *Yes. That's the other half. The half my mother never told.* He was quiet for a moment. *How did you know?*

*I don't know.*

*You know*, said Bast softly. *Somewhere. You know everything. You just can't reach it.* He was quiet for a long time. *That's why I told you the story, Reshi. The girl who tied the moon. And gave it back.*

*Why?*

Bast did not answer for so long that I thought he had gone. Then, very softly, through the slit in the stone:

*Because someone tied you. And someday, she's going to have to give you back.*

---

I did not understand. I lay on my pallet in the grey dark of my cell and held my left wrist in my right hand and ran my fingers over the knots, and I did not understand anything.

But I remembered the story. Every word of it. And years later, at the top of the stairs of an inn at a crossroads, kneeling in front of an open chest with a cord of dark hair in my hands, I remembered it again.

I remembered the girl, weeping, untying the knot. I remembered the thread of silver light, floating up out of the cord.

And I thought: *she gave it back*.

Not the girl in the story. The other one. The one who tied me. She had taken my name out of the air in a chamber beneath an old house, and held it, and kept it safe for seven years. And then, when it was time, she had sent a tinker down the road with a single knot. The one that would let it go.

She wanted to keep it. I know she did. More than anything.

That is why it was brave.
