# Tempi's Question

Tempi found me that afternoon, on the rocks above the Maer's cave, where I had gone to be alone.

He did not announce himself. The Adem never do. I was sitting on a flat grey boulder with my knees drawn up, looking out over the hills toward the south, where a smudge of smoke on the horizon marked the king's camp. And then I became aware that he was sitting on the boulder beside me, cross-legged, with his hands resting on his knees, looking at the same smudge of smoke.

I did not know how long he had been there. I did not ask.

"Tempi," I said.

He nodded. His hands moved on his knees. *Greeting. Gentle.*

We sat in silence for a long time. The wind moved in the grass below us. Far off, a hawk was circling, very high, a small dark cross against the grey sky.

"You are going to kill a king," said Tempi at last.

---

It was not a question. The Adem do not ask many questions; they think it rude to demand an answer from someone who may not wish to give one. But it was not quite a statement either. It hung in the air between us, waiting, the way Tempi's words often did.

"I don't know," I said. "Maybe. If I have to."

Tempi did not say anything. His hands were still.

"He has Meluan," I said. "The Maer's wife. My aunt. And her child. He's taking them to the Lackless house, to open the door. And if he opens it—" I stopped. I did not know how to explain the door to Tempi. I did not know how to explain the Thief, and the crack, and the things that were pressing against the other side. "It would be very bad," I said at last. "For everyone. For the whole world."

"And to stop this, you will kill him."

"If I have to."

Tempi was quiet for a long moment.

"Is it of the Lethani?" he said.

---

I did not answer at once. I had known he would ask. I had been asking myself for days, ever since the Maer had told me about Severen, and the king's army, and Meluan in the black and silver tent. I had asked myself in the twilight of the Fae, and on the long road down from the greystone, and on the rocks the night before, staring up at the stars.

I had not found an answer.

"I don't know," I said. "I thought you might tell me."

Tempi looked at me. His pale eyes were very calm.

"I cannot tell you," he said. "The Lethani is not a thing one person can tell another. You know this. It is a thing you must find. In yourself. In the moment." His hands moved. *Gentle reproach.* "You have learned this. At Haert. From Shehyn. From Vashet. From me."

"I know. But this is different."

"How is it different?"

"Because it's a king." I heard my own voice rise, and I pulled it down again. "Because it's not a bandit on a road, or a man with a sword coming at me. It's a king, sitting in his tent, surrounded by his guards. And I'm going to walk in there with a lie on my face and a knife in my boot, and I'm going to—" I stopped. "It's murder, Tempi. Whatever else it is. It's murder."

Tempi did not answer. He looked out at the smudge of smoke on the horizon.

---

"When I was young," he said at last, "younger than you, I went on my first contract. A merchant in Vintas. He had hired the Adem to guard his caravan through the mountains. There were bandits. We fought them." He was quiet for a moment. "I killed a man. My first. He was young, like me. He had a sword, and he came at me, and I killed him. With my hands." He looked at his hands, resting on his knees. "It was of the Lethani. I knew it. I did not doubt."

"But?"

"But the merchant," said Tempi, "was carrying slaves. In the last wagon. Under the cloth. Children. From the south. To sell in Vintas." His voice did not change. "The bandits knew. They had come to free them. The man I killed was the brother of one of the children."

The wind moved in the grass. The hawk turned, high above us.

"I did not know," said Tempi. "When I fought. I did not know. I thought I was doing the Lethani. I thought I was right." He was quiet for a long moment. "Afterward, I went to Shehyn. I told her. I asked her if I had done the Lethani, in killing that man. And she said to me: *You did the Lethani you knew. But the Lethani you knew was very small.*"

---

I did not say anything for a long time.

"What happened?" I said at last. "To the children?"

"I freed them." Tempi's face did not change. "That night. I broke my contract. I took them from the wagon and I brought them to the bandits' camp, in the mountains, and I gave them back." He paused. "The Adem do not break contracts. It was a great shame. I was sent back to Haert. I was not allowed to take another contract for three years." His hands moved. *Acceptance. Old pain.* "It was of the Lethani. The breaking. I knew this, too. But it did not bring back the young man with the sword."

"Why are you telling me this?"

Tempi turned his head and looked at me.

"Because you ask if killing the king is of the Lethani," he said. "And I cannot tell you. But I can tell you this: whatever you decide, you will decide with the Lethani you know. And the Lethani you know may be very small." He looked back at the smoke. "You do not know everything about this king. You do not know everything about the door. You do not know everything about yourself. No one does. That is the way of things."

"Then how do I decide?"

"You decide," said Tempi, "and then you carry it."

---

I looked at him. His face was calm and still, the way Adem faces always are. But his hands, resting on his knees, had curled very slightly. *Old pain.*

"That's not very comforting," I said.

"No." Something moved at the corner of his mouth. It was the closest thing to a smile I had ever seen on him. "The Lethani is not for comfort. It is for knowing what to do." He was quiet. "Comfort is for afterward. For friends. For fires at night." He looked at me. "That is why I came."

"To comfort me?"

"To be here afterward." Tempi's pale eyes were very steady. "Whatever you do. Whether it is of the Lethani or not. Whether you know or not." His hands moved. *Family. Promise.* "You will need someone to sit with. After. When you are carrying it. I have carried such a thing. I know its weight." He paused. "It is lighter, with someone to sit with."

---

I did not say anything. I could not. I sat on the boulder with my knees drawn up and looked at the smudge of smoke on the horizon, and my eyes stung, and I did not know if it was the wind.

After a while, Tempi reached out and put his hand on my shoulder. The Adem do not touch often, except in fighting and in love. It was a very light touch. Barely there.

"You are my student," he said quietly. "Whatever you do tomorrow, you will still be my student. That does not change."

"Even if I kill a king?"

"Even if you kill a god," said Tempi. "Even if you break the world." He took his hand away. "It is not what you do that makes you my student. It is that I taught you. And I did. And that is finished, and cannot be unfinished."

---

He stayed with me until the sun went down. We did not talk much more. We sat on the boulder and watched the light fade over the hills, and the smoke on the horizon turn from grey to black to a faint red glow, as the king's camp lit its fires for the night.

When it was dark, he stood up.

"Tomorrow," he said, "I will be at your left side. Vashet at your right. We will get you to the tent." His hands moved. *Certainty.* "What happens in the tent is yours."

"Thank you, Tempi."

He looked down at me. In the dark, I could not see his face. But I heard something in his voice that I had never heard before, in all the months I had known him. Something very like tenderness.

"Do not thank me," he said. "Come back. That is thanks enough."

And he went away down the rocks, silent as a shadow, toward the fire where Bast was waiting.

---

He was at my left side, the next night. He did what he said he would do. He got me to the tent.

And afterward—long afterward, after the door, and the grey, and the Tower, and the hanging that was not a hanging—he was the first of the Adem to come to Hallow's Company, in the western hills, when word reached Haert that I was alive. Before Vashet. Before anyone.

I did not know him. I did not know anyone, then. I sat on the tailboard of a wagon with my hands in my lap and looked at the lean pale-eyed man standing in the road, and felt nothing.

He did not seem to mind. He sat down beside me on the tailboard. He did not say anything. He sat with me for three days, from dawn until dark, and did not say a single word, and then he got up and bowed to me, the Adem way, and went back north to Haert.

Mirra asked me, afterward, who he was. I did not know. I only knew that for three days, sitting beside him, something in me had been a little lighter.

I understand now. He had kept his promise. He had come to sit with me. After. While I was carrying it.

Even though I did not know what I was carrying. Even though I did not know him.

It is lighter, with someone to sit with.
