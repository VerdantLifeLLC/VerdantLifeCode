# The Trial of the Kingkiller

They came for me at dawn, on the first day of summer.

Four guards, in the new livery of the Penitent King: plain grey, with a single white thread running down the breast like a line of ash. They unlocked the iron door of my cell and stood in the doorway and looked at me, sitting on my pallet with my back against the wall.

"Up," said one of them. "It's time."

I got up. I did not ask what it was time for. I did not care.

They put chains on me again. Wrists and ankles. They were careful with the left wrist; they put the iron above the cord, not on it. I do not know whose order that was. I have always suspected it was the Penitent King's.

Then they led me down the long winding stair of the Tower of Thorns. Down and down and around, past a hundred narrow window slits, each one showing a different grey slice of the grey city below. My legs were weak. I had not walked more than six paces at a time in eight months. I stumbled on the stair, and the guards caught me, and held me up, and did not say anything.

At the bottom of the stair, there was a door. And beyond the door, the courtyard of the palace. And beyond the courtyard, the great hall.

---

I remember the noise first.

It came at me like a wave as they led me through the great double doors. Voices. Hundreds of them. Thousands. A roar of sound, like the sea breaking on rocks. It filled the hall and echoed off the high timbered ceiling and pressed against my ears like a hand.

Then, as I came in, it stopped.

All at once. Like a candle snuffed. The whole great hall went silent, and in the silence, I heard my chains clinking on the stone floor. And my own breathing. And somewhere, very far away, a bird singing outside a window.

The hall was full. Fuller than the Eolian had been, the night I sang. There were people on benches along both walls, and people standing behind the benches, and people crowded in at the doors and hanging from the windows. Lords and ladies in fine clothes. Merchants and guildsmen. Priests in white. Soldiers in grey. Common folk, packed shoulder to shoulder at the back, craning their necks to see.

And at the far end of the hall, on a dais, beneath a great banner of plain grey cloth with a single white thread running down it, there was a throne.

The Penitent King sat on it.

He wore grey. Not the rich grey of silk, but plain grey wool, like a monk's habit. There was a crown on his head, a simple circlet of silver. His feet, resting on a cushion before the throne, were bare. His face was hollow and grey and very still.

Beside him, on a smaller chair, sat a woman with white hair.

I did not know her. But she was looking at me across the length of the hall, and her face was wet, and when she saw me look back at her, she put her hand over her mouth.

---

They led me down the length of the hall, between the benches. People stared at me as I passed. Some of them hissed. Some of them made signs against evil. One of them, an old woman in black, spat on the floor at my feet.

They stood me in the center of the floor before the dais. They took the chains off my ankles, but not my wrists. They stepped back and left me standing there alone.

The Penitent King looked down at me from his throne. His face did not change.

A man stood up from a table at the side of the dais. He was old and lean and grey, in a long black robe, with a chain of office around his neck. A magistrate, I learned later. The high justice of Vintas. He held a long scroll in his hands, and he unrolled it, and he began to read.

---

I do not remember what he read. Not most of it. It was long, and dry, and full of words I did not know. *Regicide*. *Treason*. *High crime against the crown and the realm*. He read for a long time, and his voice droned on, and I stood in the center of the floor with my chained wrists and let it wash over me like rain.

I remember the end of it.

"—that on the night of the dark moon, in the autumn of last year, in the cellars of the house of Lackless near the town of Levinshir, the accused, Kvothe, called the Ruh, called the Arcane, did with malice and forethought strike down and slay His Majesty Ambrose, first of that name, King of Vintas, with a blade of foreign make, and did thereby commit the highest crime known to the laws of this realm." The magistrate lowered his scroll. He looked at me over it. "How does the accused answer?"

The hall was silent.

I looked up at him. I did not answer. I did not know how.

"The accused will answer," said the magistrate. "Did you, or did you not, kill the king?"

I thought about it. I thought about a young man in black velvet, standing at a door, with a jeweled knife at a woman's throat. I did not remember him. I did not remember the door, or the woman, or the knife. But I remembered the sword. I remembered the feel of it going in. It was the only thing from that night that I remembered clearly. It was in my hands, and my arms, and my shoulders. It was in my body, the way the Ketan was in my body. Below the grey. Below the hole where my name had been.

"Yes," I said.

A murmur went through the hall. Like wind through leaves.

"You admit it?"

"Yes."

"Freely? Without coercion?"

"Yes."

The magistrate looked at me for a long moment. Then he looked at the Penitent King.

"Your majesty," he said. "The accused has confessed."

The Penitent King did not move. He did not speak. He sat on his throne with his bare feet on the cushion and looked at me, and his face was like a stone.

"Nevertheless," said the magistrate, "the law requires that witnesses be heard. So that the realm may know the manner of the crime." He turned to the hall. "Let the witnesses come forward."

---

There were many of them. More than I expected. They came up one by one from the benches and stood beside me before the dais and told what they had seen.

A soldier came first. A young man, barely more than a boy, with a thin frightened face. He had been in the ring of torch-bearers, he said, in the chamber beneath the Lackless house. He had seen the Ruh come down the stair. He had seen the Ruh walk up to the king. He had seen the Ruh draw his sword and strike.

"Did you see the king do anything?" asked the magistrate. "Before he was struck?"

The young soldier hesitated. "He was at the door, your honor. With the lady. The Maer's wife." He glanced at the dais, at the woman with white hair. "Her Majesty, I mean. He had her hand on the door."

"And?"

"And he had a knife." The soldier's voice dropped. "At her throat."

A murmur went through the hall. Louder this time.

"Go on," said the magistrate.

"And the Ruh said stop. And the king said the door wanted all of her. And he—" The soldier stopped. He swallowed. "And the Ruh killed him."

"Before or after the king moved the knife?"

The soldier was quiet for a long time.

"Before," he said at last. "Just before."

---

An officer came next. The lean, careful man who had spoken to the duke on the doorstep of the Lackless house. He told the same story. He told it more carefully, with more words and fewer feelings. But it was the same.

"The king was not himself," he said. "He had not been himself for months. Since the old man came to court. Since he began to wear the box." He looked at the Penitent King. "I served his father for twenty years, your majesty. I knew the boy from a child. He was proud. He was cruel, sometimes. But he was not a murderer. Not until the end."

"And at the end?"

The officer was quiet for a moment.

"At the end," he said, "I believe he would have killed her. I believe the Ruh saved her life."

The murmur in the hall was very loud now. The magistrate had to raise his hand for silence.

---

Then Meluan came down from the dais.

She walked slowly. She was very thin, and she leaned on the arm of a servant, a small precise man in grey with a calm, pleasant face. Her hair was white as snow. Her face was lined and pale, the face of a woman twenty years older than she was. But she walked with her head high.

She stood beside me before the dais. She did not look at me. She looked at the magistrate.

"I am Meluan," she said. "Of the house of Lackless. Queen of Vintas, now, God help me." Her voice was very steady. "I was in the chamber. I was at the door. I was the one with the knife at her throat." She paused. "Ask me your questions."

The magistrate asked. She answered. She told it all, in a clear quiet voice that carried to every corner of the silent hall. The camp, and the tent, and the long ride to the Lackless house. The cellar stair. The door. The king cutting her palm and pressing it to the stone. The door drinking her. Her hair going white. The knife.

"He said I was nothing," she said. "He said I was just a woman. Just a vessel. Just blood. He said the door wanted all of me." Her voice did not waver. "And he put the knife to my throat. And I felt it cut. Here." She touched her throat, where there was a thin pale line, very faint. "And then he fell. And I lived."

She turned, at last, and looked at me.

"He saved my life," she said. "My nephew. My sister's son. He saved my life, and my daughter's, and the lives of everyone in this hall. He closed the door. He paid for it with his own name." Her voice cracked, just slightly. "And you want to hang him for it."

The hall was utterly silent.

"I know the law," said Meluan. "I know what it says. A king is a king, and whoever kills one must die. I know." She looked at the magistrate. "But I want you to know what you're hanging him for. I want every one of you to know. So that when you tell the story—and you will tell it, all of you, to your children and your grandchildren—you tell it true."

She turned away. She walked back to the dais, slowly, on the servant's arm. She sat down in her chair beside the Penitent King. She did not look at me again.

But I saw her hand. It was resting on the arm of the throne. And the Penitent King's hand was resting on top of it. And his knuckles were white.

---

Denna was the last.

I did not know she was there until she stood up. She had been sitting on a bench at the side of the hall, near the dais, in a plain grey dress, with her dark hair pulled back. There were guards on either side of her. She stood up, and they stood up with her, and she walked out into the center of the floor.

I did not know her. I want to be clear about that. I did not know her name, or her face, or what she was to me.

But the cord on my wrist began to hum the moment she stood up.

She came and stood beside me. Very close. Close enough that I could have touched her, if my hands had not been chained. She looked at the magistrate.

"My name is Denna," she said. "Or Dianne. Or Dinael. Or a dozen others. It doesn't matter which." She paused. "I was the king's singer. I was there, in the chamber. I saw everything." She looked up at the Penitent King. "I've already told the truth once. At the duke's feast. You've all heard about it, I'm sure." A faint smile. "I'll tell it again, if you like. But it'll be the same."

"Tell it," said the magistrate.

She told it.

She told it differently from the others. Not as a soldier tells it, or an officer, or a queen. She told it the way a singer tells it. With the parts in their places. With the fear and the cold and the silver light. With Bredon at his tak board and the Seven in the shadows and the Thief's voice behind the door. She told them things none of the others had told. Things they had not seen, or had not understood, or had been too frightened to say.

The hall listened. Some of them wept. Some of them made signs against evil. Some of them sat very still with their faces closed, the way people sit when they are hearing something they do not want to believe.

When she came to the end—to the name, and the cord, and the door closing—she stopped. She turned and looked at me.

"He doesn't remember any of it," she said quietly. "Not me. Not his friends. Not his own name. He gave it all up. For you. For all of you. So that the thing behind that door would never come out." She looked back at the magistrate. "That's what you're hanging. A man with no name. A man who gave his name away to save you."

She stepped back. She did not return to her bench. She stood beside me, very close, with her shoulder almost touching mine.

The guards did not try to move her.

---

The magistrate looked at me for a long time.

"The accused has heard the witnesses," he said at last. "Does the accused have anything to say? In his defense? Or otherwise?"

The hall was silent. Every face turned toward me. The lords and ladies on their benches. The common folk crowded at the back. The priests and the soldiers and the guildsmen. The Penitent King on his throne, with his white-knuckled hand on his wife's. Meluan, with her white hair and her wet face.

Denna, beside me.

I looked at them. At all of them. I tried to think of something to say.

There was nothing in me. Nothing at all. I was a grey man in a grey hall, in grey chains, with a hole where my name had been.

But I felt the cord on my wrist. Humming. Very softly. The way a string hums when another string nearby is plucked.

And I found, somewhere very far down, at the bottom of the hole, beneath all the grey, something. Not a word. Not a thought. Just a shape. The shape of something true.

I opened my mouth.

"I killed a king," I said.

My voice was flat. Toneless. Like a lute with no strings. But it carried to every corner of the silent hall.

"I don't remember why. I don't remember him. I don't remember the door, or the woman, or the knife. I don't remember my own name." I looked at the magistrate. "But I remember the sword. I remember my hand on it. I remember it going in."

I looked down at my chained hands.

"I did it," I said. "It was mine. My hand. My choice. Whatever it cost, and whatever it saved." I looked up. "I'm not sorry. I don't know enough to be sorry. But I'm not going to pretend it was someone else."

I was quiet for a moment.

"Hang me if you have to," I said. "It's the law. I understand the law. But tell it true. When you tell it. Tell it the way she told it." I nodded at Denna. "Not a hero. Not a monster. Just a man at a door, with no time left, who chose."

---

The hall was silent for a very long time.

Then, from somewhere at the back, among the common folk crowded at the doors, a voice began to sing.

It was a woman's voice. Rough and untrained. Very quiet. I could barely hear it.

It was singing about a white city on the last day of its life.

The guards turned. The magistrate turned. The lords and ladies on their benches turned in their seats, craning to see.

Another voice joined it. A man's, this time. Then another. Then another. Scattered through the crowd at the back of the hall, one here, one there. Not many. A dozen, perhaps. Singing very quietly, very badly, out of tune and out of time.

But singing.

*Tariniel*. The song the Penitent King had banned. The song that got your tongue cut out, if the wrong people heard you singing it.

They were singing it in the great hall of the palace of Renere, in front of the king himself.

The magistrate raised his hand. He opened his mouth to call for silence.

"No," said the Penitent King.

It was the first word he had spoken all day. The magistrate stopped. He turned and looked up at the throne.

The Penitent King was sitting very still, with his bare feet on the cushion and his hand on his wife's. His hollow grey face was wet.

"Let them sing," he said quietly.

---

They sang the whole of it. Or as much as they knew. It took a long time. They did not know all the words, and they stumbled, and some of them stopped and started again. But they sang. And no one stopped them.

When they finished, the hall was silent again.

The magistrate cleared his throat. His voice, when he spoke, was not quite steady.

"Your majesty," he said. "The court must rule."

The Penitent King looked down at me from his throne for a long, long time.

"I know," he said.

He stood up. He stood on the dais in his plain grey robe, with his silver crown and his bare feet, and he looked out over the hall.

"The accused has confessed," he said. "Freely. Before witnesses. To the killing of a king." His voice was very steady. "The law is clear. It has been clear for a thousand years. It does not care why. It does not care what was saved, or what was lost. It cares only that a king is dead, and a man's hand did it."

He looked down at me.

"Kvothe, called the Ruh," said the Penitent King. "Called the Arcane. Called the Kingkiller. I find you guilty of regicide. And I sentence you to be hanged by the neck until you are dead, in the great square of Renere, at dawn, three days from now."

The hall was silent.

Beside me, Denna made a small sound, and put her hand on my arm.

"And may God," said the Penitent King, very quietly, "have mercy on us all."
