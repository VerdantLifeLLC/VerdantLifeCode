# The Players

The king's army was camped in a broad green valley two days' march from the Lackless house, beside a slow brown river, beneath a sky the color of old pewter. There were thousands of tents. They spread out across the valley floor like a field of grey mushrooms, rank on rank, with cookfires smoking between them and banners snapping in the wind. There were horse lines and wagon parks and latrines and a smithy, ringing with the sound of hammers. There were soldiers everywhere, thousands of them, in the black and silver of the house of Jakis and the colors of a dozen lesser lords.

And in the center of it all, on a low rise above the river, there was a great pavilion of black and silver silk.

We came down into the valley at sunset, three wagons painted in bright colors gone dull with dust, pulled by tired horses. Tessit was leading the first horse. Brin was sitting on the seat of the first wagon, sawing away at his fiddle. Illa was walking beside the second wagon, singing "Tinker Tanner."

And I was sitting on the tailboard of the third wagon, with a borrowed fiddle in my lap and a borrowed hat pulled low over my eyes and hair the color of mud.

---

Bast had done it that morning, in the Maer's valley. He had sat me down on a rock and stood behind me and put his hands on my head and murmured in that quick sly language of his, and I had felt something shift. A kind of tingling, starting at my scalp and running down my neck. When he was done, he had handed me a scrap of mirror that Mirra had lent him, and I had looked into it and seen a stranger.

My hair was brown. Not a rich brown, or a warm one. A dull, flat, forgettable brown, the color of old leaves. My face was the same, but somehow not. My nose was a little longer. My chin a little softer. My eyes were not green anymore, but grey, like a rainy sky.

I looked like no one. I looked like any one of a thousand Ruh boys, wandering the roads with a fiddle and a battered hat.

"It won't last forever," Bast had said. "A day. Maybe two. And it'll fade if you get angry, or frightened, or do anything too much like yourself." He had looked at me critically. "So try not to be yourself. For once."

"I'll try."

"You won't," he had said, and grinned. "But try anyway."

---

The soldiers stopped us at the edge of the camp. A dozen of them, in black and silver, with pikes. Their sergeant was a heavy, sour-faced man with a scar across his lip.

"Ruh," he said. It was not a question. "What do you want?"

"To play," said Tessit. He took off his battered hat and held it in front of him, humble and hopeful. "We played for the king five days ago, sir. On the road north of here. He liked it. We thought maybe he'd like to hear us again."

The sergeant looked at him. Then at the wagons. Then at Illa, walking beside the second wagon, singing. His sour face softened slightly.

"I remember you," he said. "You had that girl who sang the one about the lady and the lord and the bath." He looked at Illa. "That you?"

Illa smiled at him. It was a very good smile. "That's me."

The sergeant grunted. "All right. Go on through. Set up by the river, near the king's tents. He'll want to hear you after supper." He looked at the wagons again, and his eyes passed over me without stopping. "And no stealing. Anything goes missing, we'll hang the lot of you."

"We never steal, sir," said Tessit, with great dignity.

The sergeant snorted. But he waved us through.

---

We set up on a flat stretch of grass beside the river, a stone's throw from the black and silver pavilion. Tessit and the others unhitched the horses and set out the stage: a few planks laid across trestles, with a painted cloth hung behind for a backdrop. Mirra built a fire. Brin tuned his fiddle, badly. Kellen and Wat began to juggle knives, very fast and very close to each other's heads, and a crowd of soldiers gathered to watch.

I sat on the tailboard of the third wagon with my borrowed fiddle and looked at the pavilion.

It was huge. Black silk walls, rising up into a peaked roof, with silver embroidery along every seam. Silver banners hung from the poles at every corner, snapping in the evening wind. There were guards at every entrance. Many guards. Big men in black armor, with halberds.

And around the pavilion, in a ring, there were smaller tents. Black and silver too, but plainer. For the king's household. His servants, his guards, his favorites.

One of them had a guard at its door who was not in black armor. He was in grey. Plain grey, with no insignia. And he was standing very still.

I looked at that tent for a long time. At its plain black walls, and the guard in grey, and the small window in its side, covered with a flap of silk.

As I watched, the flap moved. A hand pushed it aside. A woman's hand, pale and thin.

And then a face appeared in the window. Pale and thin and very tired, with dark hair pulled back and dark shadows under the eyes.

Meluan.

She looked out at the river. At the Ruh wagons, and the fire, and the soldiers gathered around to watch the juggling. Her face did not change. But her hand, on the edge of the window flap, tightened.

She was looking at the wagons. At the Ruh. The people she had hated all her life, because one of them had taken her sister.

Then she let the flap fall, and was gone.

---

The king came out after supper.

The soldiers had been gathering for an hour, more and more of them, until there were hundreds crowded around the little stage by the river. They were sitting on the grass and standing in rows and climbing on wagons to see. They were eating their suppers from tin plates and drinking from leather jacks and laughing and shouting. The fire was high and bright. Kellen and Wat had finished their knife-juggling and Illa had sung two songs, and the crowd was warm and happy and a little drunk.

Then the flaps of the black and silver pavilion were drawn back, and the crowd went quiet.

Ambrose came out.

He was dressed in black, as he always was now. Black velvet, black silk, black leather, cut close and fine. There was a circlet of silver on his head, simple and plain, and it suited him better than I wanted it to. He walked out of the pavilion with his head high and his hand on the hilt of his sword, and his guards fanned out around him, and the soldiers on the grass began to cheer.

And around his neck, on a silver chain, there was a box.

Small and dark and smooth, about the size of a loaf of bread. It hung against his chest like a pendant. No lid. No lock. No seams.

I felt it from across the grass. I felt it the moment he stepped out of the pavilion. A humming, deep and low, like a string plucked somewhere far off. Its long slow *no*, saying itself over and over.

And beneath it, fainter, braided through it like a thread through cloth, the thing I had heard in Meluan's green room. The name that belonged to my family.

It knew me. I felt it know me. I felt it turn, the way a compass needle turns, toward me.

I kept my head down. I kept my borrowed hat low over my borrowed eyes. I held my borrowed fiddle and did not look up.

Beside me on the tailboard, Bast went very still.

"Reshi," he breathed. "Your hair. It's—"

I looked down. At the ends of my hair, where they hung over my shoulders. They were not brown anymore. Not quite. There was a tinge of red in them. Just a tinge. Like the first light of a fire, glimpsed through smoke.

"Stop it," Bast hissed. "Whatever you're feeling. Stop it. It's breaking the glamour."

I closed my eyes. I thought about the Adem. About Vashet, on the hillside at Haert. *Be still. Let it rise. Let it pass.*

I let it rise. I let it pass.

When I opened my eyes, my hair was brown again.

---

Ambrose sat down in a great carved chair that his servants had brought out and set on the grass in front of the stage. His guards stood around him. His favorites clustered at his sides. Lords and ladies in rich clothes, glittering with jewels, laughing and whispering.

And among them, at his left hand, a girl in black silk, with a harp in her lap.

Denna.

She did not look at the stage. She did not look at anyone. She sat very still beside the king's chair with her harp in her lap and her hands folded on the strings and her eyes on the ground. Her face was pale and composed and utterly empty.

And behind her, leaning on his stick, Bredon.

He was looking at the stage. At the Ruh. At the wagons. His face was mild and pleasant and interested, the face of an old man enjoying an evening's entertainment. His eyes moved slowly over each of us. Tessit, and Mirra, and Brin with his fiddle, and Illa, and Kellen and Wat.

And me.

His eyes stopped on me. Just for a moment. The barest pause.

Then they moved on.

I did not breathe. I sat on the tailboard with my borrowed fiddle and my borrowed hat and my borrowed face, and I did not breathe.

He had not seen me. He could not have. Bast's glamour was good. I looked like no one.

But he had paused.

---

We played.

Tessit played the lute and Brin played the fiddle and I played the other fiddle, badly, on purpose, the way Brin played. Illa sang. Mirra sang. Kellen and Wat did tumbling tricks between the songs, and the soldiers roared and whistled and threw coins onto the stage.

We played "Tinker Tanner" and "Jenny Nettle" and "The Lady and the Lord and the Bath." We played "Leave the Lantern" and "Aloine at the Well." We played a bawdy song about a miller's daughter that made the lords and ladies around the king's chair titter behind their hands.

And all the while, I watched.

I watched Ambrose, lounging in his carved chair with the box on his chest, laughing at the bawdy songs. I watched his guards, standing around him with their halberds. I watched the lords and ladies. I watched Bredon, leaning on his stick behind Denna's chair, smiling.

And I watched the plain black tent with the guard in grey at its door.

Somewhere out there, in the dark beyond the firelight, twelve Adem were moving through the camp. Silent as shadows. I could not see them. No one could. But I knew they were there. I knew that Vashet was leading them, and that Tempi was at her side, and that they were moving toward that tent.

We played "The Wind's Daughter," a sea shanty I had learned on Hessa Tern's ship. The soldiers stamped their feet and sang along with the chorus.

The guard in grey at the door of the plain black tent was very still.

We played "Lanre Turned." A traditional song, very old, one that every Ruh knows. It is not true, and it is not false. It is just a song. The soldiers liked it. Ambrose did not. He scowled and waved his hand, and Tessit stopped playing at once and bowed and moved on to something else.

The guard in grey at the door of the tent swayed slightly. Just slightly. As if a gust of wind had caught him.

Then he folded up, very neatly, and sat down on the grass beside the tent door, and did not move again.

No one noticed. Everyone was watching the stage.

I began to play faster.

---

We were halfway through "The Ballad of Sir Savien Traliard" when Bredon stood up.

He did it slowly, leaning on his stick, the way an old man stands. No one paid him any attention. He was only an old man, getting up to stretch his legs, or to find a privy, or to get another cup of wine.

He did not go to find a privy. He walked around the back of the king's chair, and along the edge of the crowd, and toward the plain black tent.

My fingers faltered on the fiddle strings. I missed a note. Beside me, Illa glanced at me sharply and sang a little louder to cover it.

Bredon walked to the tent. He looked down at the guard in grey, sitting on the grass beside the door. He looked at him for a long moment.

Then he turned and looked at the stage.

He looked straight at me.

And he smiled.

---

I stopped playing. I could not help it. My hands stopped on the strings, and the fiddle went silent, and I sat there on the tailboard of the wagon and looked at him across the firelit grass, and he looked back at me, and he smiled.

He knew. He had known from the moment he saw me. The pause. The barest pause, when his eyes found me on the tailboard. He had known, and he had let me sit there, and play, and watch, and think I was hidden.

And now he stood at the door of the tent where my aunt and her daughter were kept, and he smiled at me, and he raised his stick an inch off the ground in his small courtly salute. The way he always did, at the start of a game.

*Your move*, it said.

Beside me, Bast hissed. "Reshi. Your hair."

I did not look down. I did not need to. I could feel it. The glamour was breaking. Peeling away like old paint. I could feel the red coming back into my hair, bright and fierce, like a fire catching.

I stood up. I set down the fiddle. I took off the borrowed hat.

And in the firelight, in front of the king and his court and eight thousand soldiers, my hair burned red.

---

The crowd went silent. All at once, like a candle snuffed.

Then someone said, "That's him."

And someone else said, "The Ruh. The kingkiller."

And someone else, somewhere, began to shout.

Ambrose was on his feet. His carved chair had gone over behind him. He was staring at me across the firelit grass, and his face was white, and then red, and then something else entirely. Something I had seen on his face once before, a long time ago, in the courtyard of the University, when I called the wind.

It was fear.

"Take him!" he shouted. His voice cracked. "Take him! Take him alive!"

The guards around him surged forward.

And that was when Devi's surprise went off.
