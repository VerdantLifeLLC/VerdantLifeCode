# The Piper in the Ice

On the first day after the storm, following the silver string up toward the high pass, we found a man frozen in the ice.

Bast saw him first. We were crossing a long, steep field of old blue ice below a cliff, roped together, with our boots bound in strips of blanket to keep them from slipping. The wind had dropped. The sky was hard and clear and very blue, and the light off the ice was so bright it hurt to look at, so that I was walking with my eyes half closed, watching the rope ahead of me and nothing else.

Bast stopped. The rope went slack.

"Reshi," he said. His voice was strange. "Look down."

I looked down.

---

He was lying about three feet beneath the surface, on his back, with his arms folded across his chest. The ice was so clear above him that he might have been lying under glass. I could see every detail of him. The weave of his clothes. The stitching on his boots. The small white scar on his chin.

He was young. Younger than I had thought, at first glance. Twenty, perhaps. Twenty-two. He had a narrow, clever face and dark hair cut short, and his eyes were closed, and he did not look dead. He looked as if he had lain down on the ice for a moment to rest, and fallen asleep, and would wake in a moment and sit up and brush the frost off his coat and go on his way.

He was wearing clothes I did not recognize. A long coat, cut in a fashion I had never seen, of some dark cloth that had kept its color under the ice. Blue so deep it was almost black. There were patterns stitched along the hem and the cuffs, in silver thread. Circles, and lines, and small angular shapes that were almost letters. And in his folded hands, held against his chest, there was a pipe.

Not a smoking pipe. A musical pipe. A long slender flute of pale bone, with seven holes along its length.

"How long has he been here?" I said. My voice sounded very small in the bright silence.

Bast did not answer at once. He was crouching on the ice, looking down, with an expression on his face I had never seen before. Something between grief and awe.

"A long time," he said at last. "A very long time, Reshi. Look at his coat. Look at the stitching." He touched the ice above the dead man's hem with one fingertip. "Those are namer's marks. The old kind. From before the war." He looked up at me. "He's from Tariniel."

---

We did not move for a long time. The sun climbed. The light on the ice grew brighter.

"The war," I said. "The Creation War. That was—"

"Thousands of years ago." Bast looked down at the man in the ice. "Yes."

"He can't have been here that long. He'd have—the ice would have—"

"The Stormwal keeps things," said Bast. "That's what it's for. That's why they built the white city here, in the first place. Because the mountains remember. Nothing rots, up here. Nothing changes." He was quiet for a moment. "My mother told me that. When I was very small. She said that if you climbed high enough into the Stormwal, you could find things from the beginning of the world. Lying in the ice. Waiting." He looked at the dead man's face. "I thought it was a story."

I knelt down on the ice beside him. I looked at the young man's face. At the small white scar on his chin, and the long clever fingers folded around the bone flute.

"He was a musician," I said.

"He was a namer," said Bast. "They were the same thing, then. Or nearly. Before the war, the singers and the namers were one people. They called things by singing to them. The pipe was how he spoke." He was quiet. "My mother said the pipers of Tariniel could play the name of the wind so sweetly that it would come and lie down at their feet like a dog."

---

"What happened to him?"

Bast did not answer. He was looking at the dead man's chest, where his hands were folded. At the bone flute.

I looked too. And I saw what Bast had seen.

There was a crack in the flute. A long thin crack, running down its whole length, from the mouthpiece to the last hole. And the dead man's hands were not just folded over it. They were clenched. The knuckles white, even after all that time. Holding the broken flute together.

"He was running," said Bast quietly. "From the city. When it fell. When Lanre came, and the city burned, and the people ran up into the mountains." He looked up at the cliff above us, at the notch of the high pass beyond it. "He was running this way. Toward the pass. And something caught him. Or the cold caught him. Or he just got tired." He looked back down. "And he lay down here, on the ice. And he held his flute together. And he died."

"Why hold it together? If it was broken?"

Bast was quiet for a long moment.

"Because it was his name," he said. "Or part of it. The pipers put a piece of their name into their pipes. Like the Adem put a piece of theirs into their swords. And if the pipe broke—" He stopped.

"Then the name broke."

"Yes."

I looked at the young man's face. Peaceful, and clever, and very young. With his broken name held together in his hands.

---

I thought, then, of many things. I thought of my mother, who had wrapped her name inside another name so that the door could not find her. I thought of Elodin, who had tried to change his name at seventeen and broken himself for three years. I thought of Denna, with a cord around her wrist, and the things her patron had taught her to tie.

I thought of a stone in my pocket that said no.

And I thought: *this is what happens. This is what happens to namers when the world ends. They run, and they hold their names together as long as they can, and then they lie down in the ice.*

I did not know, then, that I would be one of them. That I would lie down, in my own way, in my own ice, for seven years. With my name held together in a box.

I did not know. But something in me did, I think. Because I found that I was crying, kneeling on the ice above that young man from the beginning of the world. And I did not know why.

---

Bast let me cry. He did not say anything. He crouched beside me on the ice with his hand on my shoulder, and waited.

When I was done, I wiped my face and looked up at him.

"We should do something," I said. "For him. We can't just leave him here."

"We can't dig him out," said Bast. "The ice is too hard. And even if we could—" He hesitated. "He's been here since the beginning, Reshi. This is his grave. The mountains made it for him. It wouldn't be right to take him out of it."

"Then what?"

Bast looked at me for a long moment. Then he looked at the lute on my back.

"You could play for him," he said.

---

I took out my lute. My fingers were stiff with cold; I had to blow on them for a long time before they would move. The strings were stiff too, and out of tune from the storm, and I spent a long time tuning them, kneeling on the ice with the sun burning on my back and the wind very soft and very cold around my ears.

Then I played.

I did not play a song. I did not know any songs from Tariniel. Nobody did; they had all been lost when the city fell, the way the city had been lost, the way the young man in the ice had been lost. So I did not play a song. I played what I heard.

I listened to the ice. I listened to the wind, very soft, moving across the bright blue field. I listened to the cliff above us and the pass beyond it and the great white silence of the mountains all around. And I listened, very hard, to the young man in the ice. To his clever face and his dark coat with the silver stitching and his broken flute. And I tried to hear what he would have played. If he had reached the pass. If he had lived.

I do not know what I played. I cannot remember it now. I have tried, many times, and it does not come. It was not a song that was meant to be remembered. It was meant to be played once, on that ice, for that man, and then let go.

But I remember that Bast wept while I played. And I remember that when I finished, and the last note faded out into the enormous white silence, there was a sound from the ice.

---

It was very small. A single clear note, high and sweet, like a bird calling once at dawn. It came from beneath the ice. From the place where the young man lay, with his broken flute in his hands.

Bast and I looked at each other.

"Did you—" I said.

"Yes," said Bast. His face was wet. "Yes, Reshi."

We looked down. The young man lay just as he had lain. His eyes closed, his hands folded, his broken flute held against his chest. Nothing had changed.

But the crack in the flute was gone.

I do not know how. I did not do it. I am as sure of that as I am of anything. I did not call the name of the flute, or the ice, or the young man. I only played what I heard.

But the flute lay whole in his hands. Pale and smooth and unbroken, from the mouthpiece to the last hole. And his hands, which had been clenched so tightly, were loose now. Resting on it gently. The way you rest your hands on something you love, when you are no longer afraid of losing it.

---

We did not speak for a long time. Then Bast stood up and coiled the rope, and I put my lute away, and we went on across the ice toward the cliff, and the pass beyond it.

At the edge of the ice field, I looked back. The sun was very bright on the blue ice. I could not see the young man anymore. The glare hid him.

"Bast," I said. "What do you think it means?"

He did not answer for a while. He was looking back too.

"I think," he said slowly, "that he was holding his name together for a very long time. All by himself. Since the beginning of the world." He was quiet. "And I think he didn't have to anymore. Because someone came and heard him."

"That's all?"

"That's everything, Reshi." Bast turned away from the ice and looked up at the pass. "That's all anybody wants. Mortal or Fae or namer from the beginning of the world. For someone to come, and hear them. And let them put it down."

---

I thought about that, later. Many times.

I thought about it when a woman with dark hair tied my name into a cord and put it in a box. I thought about it through seven grey years behind a bar, holding myself together, alone, with nothing in my hands.

And I thought about it when a scribe came up the road with his bloody knuckles and his satchel full of paper, and sat down at my table, and asked me to tell him the story. And listened.

Someone came, and heard. And let me put it down.

That's everything.
