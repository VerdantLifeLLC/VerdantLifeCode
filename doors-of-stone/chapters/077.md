# Interlude: A Draft Under the Door

The innkeeper stopped speaking, and the Waystone was cold.

Chronicler noticed it first as a stiffness in his fingers. He had been writing for so long, so fast, that his hand had become a thing apart from him, a machine of bone and ink that went on moving while the rest of him sat listening. But now it slowed. It fumbled. He looked down and saw that the ink on the tip of his pen had gone thick, almost jelly-like, the way ink goes in a cold room in winter.

He looked up. His breath was smoking in the air.

The fire in the hearth was still burning. He could see it, bright and yellow, crackling over the logs Bast had piled on an hour before. But it was not giving off any heat. It was as if the fire were a painting of a fire, hung on the wall.

"Reshi," said Bast. His voice was very quiet.

The innkeeper did not answer. He was sitting very still at the table, with his hands folded in front of him, looking at the window. The shutters were closed. But around their edges, where they did not quite meet the frame, there was frost. White and feathery, creeping inward across the dark wood like the fingers of a slow hand.

"Reshi," said Bast again. "It's here. It's close."

"I know."

"You have to stop. You have to stop telling it. Right now. Every word you say, it gets closer. It can *hear* you. It can hear your—" He stopped.

"My name," said the innkeeper. "It can hear my name coming back." He did not look away from the window. "I know, Bast. I've known since the first night."

---

Chronicler set down his pen. His hand was shaking, and it was not only from the cold.

"What's here?" he said. "What's close?"

The innkeeper turned his head and looked at him. In the yellow, heatless firelight, his face was very pale, and the bruises on it were very dark. His eyes were green and steady and very old.

"You know what," he said. "You've heard the whole story. Or most of it." He glanced at the frost on the shutters. "The cold is his sign. Ferule's. Cinder's. When he's near, the fire burns and gives no heat, and water freezes in the cup, and the breath goes white."

Chronicler looked down at the cup of tea by his elbow. There was a skin of ice on it.

"He's coming here," he said. His voice sounded very far away. "Now. Tonight."

"Not yet. Not tonight, I think. He's still out in the woods. Beyond the fields. He's been there all day." The innkeeper nodded toward the window. "That smoke you saw this morning. That was his. Did you notice how it leaned? Toward the inn. Toward me." He was quiet for a moment. "He's waiting. He's waiting for it to be strong enough. For me to be loud enough. And then he'll come."

"Loud enough?"

"My name." The innkeeper looked down at his hands. At the thin pale scar that ran around his left wrist like a bracelet. "It's been very quiet, for a long time. So quiet no one could hear it. Not him. Not the Seven. Not the thing behind the door." He touched the scar with one finger. "But I've been telling the story. For three days. And every word of it is a piece of me. And every piece I tell, I get back."

He looked up at Chronicler.

"Stories are names too," he said. "Did you know that? The oldest kind. Elodin told me once, a long time ago, that the best way to learn the name of a thing is to hear its whole story. Every piece of it. From the beginning." He smiled faintly. "I didn't understand him, then. I thought it was one of his riddles."

Chronicler stared at him.

"You've been—" he said. "All this time. Three days. You've been—"

"Remembering," said the innkeeper. "Yes. Putting myself back together. A piece at a time. Out loud." He looked at the frost creeping across the shutters. "And it's working. I can feel it. Can't you, Bast?"

Bast did not answer. He was standing by the window with his arms wrapped around himself, staring at the frost. His face was very white.

"I can feel it," he whispered.

---

"Then stop," said Chronicler. He heard his own voice rise, and could not stop it. "If it's bringing him here—if every word you say brings him closer—stop. Don't tell any more. I don't need the rest. I'll write what I have. I'll leave tomorrow, and I'll—"

"No," said the innkeeper.

"*Why not?*"

The innkeeper looked at him for a long moment. Then he looked at Bast, at the window. Then at the sword above the hearth, hanging on its two pegs beneath the plaque that said FOLLY.

"Because I have to finish it," he said. "I said three days. It's the third day. And a thing said three times becomes true." He was quiet for a moment. "And because if I stop now, halfway, with my name half back and half gone—" He looked at Chronicler. "Do you remember what the tinker said? This afternoon?"

Chronicler remembered. *Some stories are very heavy. But they're heavier still if you drop them halfway.*

"If I stop now," said the innkeeper, "I'll be neither. Not Kote. Not Kvothe. Something in between. Something torn." He smiled faintly. "Elodin knows what that's like. He spent three years in Haven learning. I'd rather not."

"And if you finish?"

The innkeeper did not answer right away.

"Then I'll be myself again," he said at last. "Or close enough. And he'll come. And we'll see." He picked up his cup of tea, and looked at the skin of ice on it, and set it down again. "Either way, it ends tonight. One way or another. I've known that since the first day, too."

---

Bast turned from the window.

"Reshi," he said. "Let me go out. Let me go out there and find him. Before he comes. I can find him. I can—"

"No."

"I can *hold* him. Delay him. Give you time."

"No, Bast."

"*Why not?*"

The innkeeper looked at him. His face was very gentle.

"Because he'd kill you," he said simply. "And I'd have to finish the story knowing that. And I don't think I could."

Bast stared at him. His deep blue eyes were very bright. Then he looked away.

"Then what do I do?" he said. His voice cracked. "What do I *do*, Reshi? I can't just stand here. I can't just stand here and watch him come."

"You can bar the door," said the innkeeper. "And put more wood on the fire, even if it doesn't warm anything. And make another pot of tea. And sit down, and listen." He paused. "That's all any of us can do, right now. Listen."

Bast looked at him for a long moment. Then he went to the door and dropped the heavy bar into its brackets. He went to the hearth and piled on more wood. He went into the kitchen and came back with a fresh pot of tea, steaming, and set it on the table.

The steam from the pot rose into the air, and froze, and fell back onto the table as a fine white dust.

Bast sat down in his backward chair. He folded his arms on the back of it. He did not say anything else.

---

The innkeeper poured the tea. It was hot for a moment, in the cups. Then it was not.

He wrapped his hands around his cup anyway, as if for warmth. He looked at Chronicler.

"Are you ready?" he said.

Chronicler looked down at his pen. At the thickened ink on its tip. He dipped it in the inkwell. The ink there was thick too, almost solid. He stirred it with the nib until it loosened.

"Yes," he said.

"Good." The innkeeper nodded. "Because this next part is the worst of it. The part after. When I was nameless." He looked down at the scar on his wrist. "I don't remember all of it. Not clearly. You'll have to forgive me for that. It's like trying to remember a fever dream. Pieces. Flashes. Colors and sounds, but not much sense." He was quiet for a moment. "But I remember enough."

He closed his eyes.

"The first thing I remember," said Kvothe, "is the grey."

---

Outside the Waystone Inn, the night was very still. No wind stirred the trees at the edge of the fields. No dog barked in the town. No owl called.

The frost crept across the fields. It crept across the road. It crept up the steps of the Waystone, and across the porch, and up the door, white and feathery, like the fingers of a slow hand.

And beyond the trees, at the edge of the woods, a blue light burned. Small and steady and far away.

Waiting.
