@@PART PART FOUR: NAMELESS

# Grey

The first thing I remember is the grey.

Not the color. The world. Everything in it. The stone of the chamber floor beneath my cheek, and the torches guttering on the floor, and the faces that bent over me. The silver light still glowing faintly in the crack down the center of the door. Denna's dark hair, and her dark eyes, and the blood on her lips. All of it grey. Not the grey of stone, which has a color of its own, rich and deep and various. The grey of ash. The grey of a picture drawn in charcoal and left out in the rain.

I had not known, until that moment, how much of the world I saw with my name.

You do not know it either. No one does. You look at a candle flame and see yellow, and you think the yellow is in the flame. It is not. Not all of it. Some of it is in you. Some of it is the part of you that knows fire, that has always known it, that leaps up in recognition when it sees a flame. The part of you that has a name, and knows that fire has one too.

Take away the name, and the flame is still there. It still burns. It still gives light. But it is only light. Only heat. Only a thing, burning, that means nothing to you at all.

That is what I remember. Lying on the floor of that chamber, with the cord of my name tied around my wrist, looking at a world that had gone to ash.

---

They carried me up the stair.

I remember that too, in pieces. Hands under my arms. The stone steps going past beneath me, one after another, grey and endless. Someone breathing hard close to my ear. Someone else saying *careful, careful, mind his head*.

I remember the cellars of the Lackless house. Great dark rooms full of broken furniture and empty wine racks and the smell of damp. I remember rats, their eyes glinting in the torchlight, scattering from our path. I remember the kitchens, cold and bare, with a great black iron range and a long table scarred by centuries of knives.

And I remember the hall.

It was the great hall of the Lackless house. A long, high room with a timbered ceiling and tall windows at one end, and a hearth at the other so big you could have stood up in it. There were banners hanging from the rafters, rotted and faded, so old that their colors were gone and their devices unreadable. There were portraits on the walls, dark and cracked, of men and women in the clothing of other centuries.

They laid me down on the long table in the center of the hall. I lay on my back and looked up at the rotting banners, and I did not know what they were. I knew they were banners. I knew they had once had colors, and meanings, and names. But I did not know what any of that meant. It was like looking at writing in a language I had never learned.

Someone was holding my hand. My left hand. The one with the cord around the wrist.

I turned my head and looked at her.

She was sitting on a bench beside the table, bent over me. Her face was grey. Her hair was grey. Her eyes were grey. Everything was grey. But there was something in her face that made me want to keep looking at it. Something that tugged at me, the way a hook tugs at a fish.

I did not know her. But I wanted to.

"Can you hear me?" she said.

I nodded.

"Do you know where you are?"

I thought about it. I did not.

"Do you know who I am?"

I looked at her for a long time. At her grey face, and her grey eyes, and the tears running down her cheeks. I felt the cord on my wrist, tight against my skin. I felt something in it, very faint. Like a voice in another room.

"You tied it," I said. My own voice sounded strange to me. Flat. Toneless. Like a lute with no strings. "The cord. You tied it."

She made a sound. Half a laugh, half a sob.

"Yes," she said. "Yes. I tied it."

"Why?"

She looked at me for a long moment.

"Because you asked me to," she said. "A long time ago. Before you knew you were asking."

---

The others came.

I remember them coming into the hall, one by one, out of the dark. Some of them walking. Some of them limping. Some of them being carried.

A small fair woman with spectacles, carrying a satchel. She came and stood beside the table and looked down at me and said something sharp and angry and full of grief, and I did not understand it.

A dark man with burned hands, who stood at the foot of the table and did not say anything at all, only looked at me with a face like a closed door.

A girl with dark hair and a pale face, who knelt on the floor beside the bench and put her hand flat on the stone and closed her eyes.

A thin young man with straw-colored hair, with a bandage tied across half his face. Blood had soaked through the bandage over his left eye. He stood at the edge of the group and stared at me, and his one visible eye was very wide.

And a slender young man with dark hair and deep blue eyes, who came and stood at the head of the table and looked down at me, and whose face, when he looked at me, did something I did not understand.

I did not know any of them. Not their names, not their faces, not what they were to me. They were grey shapes in a grey room, making sounds I did not understand.

But the young man with the blue eyes reached down and touched my hair, very gently, the way you touch a sleeping child. And his hand was shaking.

"Reshi," he said.

The word meant nothing to me. But I felt the cord on my wrist tighten, very slightly. As if something inside it had heard.

---

They talked, around me. I lay on the table and listened to them talk, and did not understand most of it.

Some things I understood. Words, here and there. The way you understand words in a foreign language that are almost like words in your own.

*Army*, I understood. *The king's army. Outside. Thousands of them.*

*Dead*, I understood. *The king is dead. Everyone knows. The ones who ran up the stair told them.*

*Coming*, I understood. *They're coming. The officers. The duke. They're coming here.*

*Kingkiller*, I understood.

That word, I understood very well. I did not know why. It was only a word. But when they said it, something moved in the hole in my chest. Something heavy and cold, like a stone sinking in dark water.

---

The small fair woman with spectacles was arguing with the dark man with burned hands.

"We can't stay here," she said. "The whole army's outside. When they find out the king's dead in the cellar, they'll tear this place apart."

"We can't move him either. Look at him. He doesn't even know where he is."

"Then we carry him."

"Through eight thousand soldiers?"

"Through the tunnel. The way we came."

"With the wounded? With the lady? She can barely stand. Sim can barely see." The dark man shook his head. "We'd never make it."

"Then what do you suggest?"

The dark man did not answer. He looked at me, lying on the table.

The slender young man with blue eyes was still standing at my head. He had not moved. He had not taken his hand from my hair.

"I can glamour him," he said quietly. "Make him look like someone else. Like one of the soldiers. We can walk out through the front door."

"All of us?"

"Not all of you. I can't hold that many. Not after tonight." His voice was very tired. "Him. Maybe one or two others."

"And the rest of us?"

The blue-eyed young man did not answer.

---

That was when the small dark woman in red came into the hall.

She came from the stair that led down to the cellars. She walked very straight, very quiet, and her face showed nothing at all. There was blood on her red clothes. There was a sword in her hand.

She came to the table and looked down at me.

I did not know her. But I knew her hands. I did not know how. I watched her hands, hanging at her sides, and I felt something stir in the hole in my chest. As if I had seen those hands before, a thousand times. As if they had taught me something.

Her hands moved. A small gesture. I did not know what it meant. But the cord on my wrist tightened again, very slightly.

"The Adem will hold the door," she said. Her voice was flat. "The front door of this house. While the rest of you go out through the tunnel. We will hold it until you are gone."

"Vashet," said the dark man with burned hands. "There are eight thousand of them."

"There were eight thousand at the camp by the river. Tempi held his piece of it." She did not look at him. She was looking at me. "We will hold ours."

"You'll die."

"Perhaps." Her hands moved. Something I did not understand. "It is the Lethani."

---

I sat up.

I do not know why. I did not decide to. My body did it on its own, the way a body will sometimes move before the mind has caught up. I sat up on the long table in the grey hall, with the cord of my name tied around my wrist, and I looked at the woman in red.

"No," I said.

Everyone went still.

The woman in red looked at me. Her face did not change. But her eyes did.

"No," I said again. My voice was still flat and strange. I did not know where the word was coming from. I only knew that it was right. "No one else dies. Not for me. Not tonight."

"Then how—"

"I'll go out," I said. "Through the front door. To the army. To the duke."

The whole hall was silent.

"They want the kingkiller," I said. "I'm the kingkiller. I'll go to them. And while they're busy with me, the rest of you go out through the tunnel. With the lady. With the wounded." I looked at the woman in red. "No one else dies."

"Reshi," said the blue-eyed young man. His voice was very small. "Reshi, you don't know what you're saying. You don't know who you are right now. You can't—"

"I don't need to know who I am," I said, "to know what's right."

I looked down at the cord on my wrist. At the knots. Glowing very faintly. Silver, like moonlight on snow.

"Somebody told me that," I said slowly. "A long time ago. I don't remember who." I looked up. "Choose. And bear what comes after."

The woman in red went very still. Her hands, at her sides, were motionless.

Then they moved. One gesture. Very small.

I did not know what it meant. But I found that my own hands were moving to answer it. Clumsily. Badly. Like a man speaking a language he has forgotten, whose mouth remembers the shapes of the words even when his mind does not.

The woman in red looked down at my hands. Something moved in her face. Not an expression. Something smaller.

"Yes," she said quietly. "That is the Lethani."

---

They argued. Of course they argued. The small fair woman shouted and the straw-haired young man wept and the dark man with burned hands stood very still with his jaw clenched. The girl with dark hair knelt on the floor with her hand on the stone and did not say anything.

And the one who had tied the cord—the one with the grey face and the tears—sat on the bench beside the table and held my hand and did not let go.

In the end, they could not stop me. I do not think they could have, even if I had been myself. Maybe especially then.

The blue-eyed young man was the last to give up. He stood at the head of the table with his hands clenched and his face white, and he looked at me for a long time.

"Then I'm coming with you," he said.

"No."

"I owe you a life. The old kind. I'll pay it."

"Then pay it by living," I said. "By going with them. By making sure they get out."

He stared at me. His deep blue eyes were very bright.

"That's not fair," he said. "You don't even know who I am. You can't just—you can't just *tell* me what to do, when you don't even know who I *am*—"

"I know you'd die for me," I said. "I can see it on you. That's enough."

He looked at me for a long moment. Then his face crumpled, and he turned away, and he put his hands over his face, and his shoulders shook.

---

The one who had tied the cord did not argue. She did not shout or weep or plead. She only sat on the bench and held my hand.

When the others had begun to gather themselves, to lift the wounded and help the lady with white hair to her feet and make for the cellar stair, she leaned close to me.

"I'm coming with you," she said.

"No."

"Yes." She looked at me. Her grey eyes were very steady. "Not because I owe you. Not because I love you, though I do." She touched the cord on my wrist. "Because of this. Because I tied it. Because if anyone tries to take it off you, or pull on it, or use it, I need to be there. I'm the only one who knows how it's tied. I'm the only one who could undo it."

"They'll hang you."

"Maybe. Maybe not. I was the king's nightingale. His favorite singer. I've got friends at court." She smiled faintly. It did not reach her eyes. "And even if they do, it doesn't matter. This matters more." She held my wrist. "You matter more."

I looked at her. At her grey face and her grey eyes and her grey hair, and the tears on her cheeks, and the blood on her lip.

I did not know her. I did not know her name, or what she was to me, or why the sight of her face made the hole in my chest ache.

But I knew that the cord on my wrist was humming, very faintly, when she touched it. The way a string hums when another string nearby is plucked.

"All right," I said.

---

We walked out of the great hall together, she and I, through the dark rotting corridors of the Lackless house, toward the front door.

Behind us, the others went down the cellar stair, toward the tunnel and the dark and the long way out. I did not look back. I did not know them. There was nothing to look back at.

But at the top of the stair, the young man with blue eyes stopped and turned. I felt him. I felt his eyes on my back. And I heard him say, very softly, in a voice that cracked in the middle:

"I'll find you, Reshi. Wherever they take you. I'll find you."

I did not turn around. But the cord on my wrist tightened, just a little.

And I found, to my surprise, that my eyes were wet. Though I did not know why.

---

The front door of the Lackless house was great and black and iron-bound, and very old. I put my hand on it. It was cold.

On the other side, I could hear them. The army. Thousands of voices, rising and falling like the sea. Horses. Drums. Someone shouting orders.

The woman beside me took my hand. My right hand, the one without the cord.

"Ready?" she said.

"I don't know what that means."

She laughed. It was a broken sound, and a real one.

"Neither do I," she said. "Not really. No one ever does."

I pushed the door open.

The morning was grey. The sky was grey. The hills were grey. And spread out across the slopes below the house, as far as I could see, there were soldiers. Thousands of them. In grey armor, under grey banners, with grey faces turned up toward the door.

They saw us. They went quiet. All of them, all at once, like a candle snuffed.

And then someone, somewhere, began to shout.

*Kingkiller.*
