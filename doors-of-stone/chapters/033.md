# The Maer's Commission

I told the Maer the truth.

Not all of it. I did not tell him about the garden, or Bredon's confession, or what I had heard about my parents' deaths. I did not tell him about the night Denna spent in my rooms. Some things are not for Maers.

But I told him that his wife had given me the Lackless box, and that I had kept it under my pillow, and that someone had taken it in the night. I told him who, and I told him why, as best I understood it. And I told him that I thought the person she had taken it from me to protect me from now had it within his reach.

The Maer listened to all of it without interrupting. He sat behind his desk in his long study, with the tall windows behind him and the whole of Severen spread out below, and his face did not change at all. When I had finished, he was silent for a long time.

"Bredon," he said at last.

"Yes, your grace."

"Bredon has been at my court for eleven years. He was at my father's court before that. He taught my cousin to play tak. He gave me a book of poems on my fifteenth birthday." The Maer's voice was very flat. "He has dined at my table a hundred times."

"I know, your grace. I'm sorry."

"You are sorry." He looked at me. "You are sorry that a man I have trusted for most of my life is an agent of something so old and so dangerous that I did not believe in it until this moment. You are sorry that my wife gave away her family's most precious possession to a boy she has known for a year. You are sorry that the boy then lost it to a girl he loves, who is bound by a knot to the man who wants it most." He paused. "Is that the sum of it?"

"Yes, your grace."

The Maer looked at me for a long time. Then, to my utter astonishment, he laughed.

It was not a happy laugh. It was short and sharp and bitter, the laugh of a man who has just been dealt a very bad hand of cards and can see no way to play it. But it was a laugh.

"God's teeth," he said. "When you do a thing, Kvothe, you do not do it by halves."

---

He rose from his desk and went to the window and stood looking out, his hands clasped behind his back. It was a gesture I had seen him make many times, when he was thinking.

"My wife," he said. "Does she know?"

"Not yet. I came to you first."

"Good. I will tell her." He was quiet for a moment. "She will be very distressed. Not about the box. About you. She has been—" He hesitated. "Since you spoke with her, she has been different. Lighter. As if something she had been carrying for a very long time has been set down." He turned from the window and looked at me. "She told me, you know. About her sister. About you."

I did not know what to say.

"You might have told me yourself," said the Maer. "It would have been the proper thing."

"I know, your grace. I'm sorry."

"Stop saying you are sorry. It is becoming tiresome." He came back to his desk and sat down. "You are my wife's nephew. That makes you my nephew, by marriage. Did you think of that?"

"No, your grace."

"No. I did not think you had." He steepled his fingers and looked at me over them. "It changes things. Not a great deal, perhaps. You are still a Ruh, and still a bastard of sorts, and no one at court will ever accept you as anything else. But it changes things between us. You are family now. And I do not abandon family."

I stared at him. I did not know what to say. I had come into his study expecting anger, or cold dismissal, or worse. I had not expected this.

"Your grace," I said. "I lost the box."

"You did not lose it. It was stolen from you. By someone who loved you, if your account is true, and who was trying to protect you." He shook his head. "That is not a crime. That is a misfortune. And misfortunes can be mended."

"How?"

"By getting it back." He leaned forward. "And to do that, we need to know where Bredon has gone, and what he intends. And I think I may know where to begin."

---

He told me.

King Roderic was dying. There was no longer any doubt about it. The wasting sickness had taken him slowly through the winter and faster through the spring, and now he lay in his bed in the royal palace at Renere, too weak to rise, too weak to speak more than a few words at a time. His physicians gave him a month. Perhaps two.

When he died, the crown would pass to his heir. His son, Prince Andrian, a young man of twenty with a weak chin and a strong fondness for horses. But Andrian was unmarried and had no children. And after him, the line of succession ran through a tangle of cousins and uncles and nephews, many of whom were now dead.

"Four heirs have died in two years," the Maer said. "Andrian is the only one left between the throne and the next four in line. And of those four, three are either very old, very young, or very foolish." He looked at me. "The fourth is Ambrose Jakis."

"Fifth in line," I said slowly. "But if Andrian dies—"

"Then he becomes fourth. And if one or two of the others should have an accident—" The Maer spread his hands. "He becomes second. Or first."

I sat very still. I thought about Ambrose on the steps of the Mains, in the snow. *When I'm king.* I thought about his smile. The secret one.

I thought about Bredon, standing behind him.

"Bredon is putting Ambrose on the throne," I said.

"I think so. Or trying to." The Maer's face was grim. "I have suspected for some time that the deaths were not natural. I have had my own people watching. But they have found nothing. No poison, no assassins, no bribed servants. Nothing at all. Every death looks exactly like what it appears to be. A fever. A fall. A hunting accident." He shook his head. "Whoever is doing this is very, very good."

"It's not poison," I said.

The Maer looked at me sharply. "How do you know?"

"Because I know what Bredon can do." I thought about the knot on Denna's wrist, glowing faintly red. *It could stop my heart. Just like that.* "He ties knots, your grace. Old ones. Yllish knots. The kind that don't just mean things, but *do* things. He tied one on Denna's wrist that could kill her if she disobeyed him." I swallowed. "I think he's been tying knots for the heirs."

The Maer was silent for a long time.

"That," he said at last, "is the most absurd thing I have ever heard."

"Yes, your grace."

"If anyone else had said it to me, I would have had them thrown from the cliff."

"Yes, your grace."

"But you are not anyone else." He sighed. "And I have learned, over the last year, that the world is considerably stranger than I had thought." He looked at me. "Can you prove it?"

"I don't know. Maybe. If I could get close to the king. If I could see him. If there's a knot on him somewhere, I think I could find it."

"And then? If you find it?"

"Then maybe I can untie it." I paused. "Or at least I'll know. And we'll know who's next."

---

The Maer looked at me for a long time across his desk. I could see him thinking, weighing, calculating. He was a very clever man, the Maer. Cleverer than me, in many ways. He had survived the Vintish court for thirty years by being clever.

"The king's court is not a safe place," he said at last. "Not for anyone, and certainly not for you. You are known to be my man. You are known to be Ruh. You are known, in certain circles, to have killed a number of bandits in the Eld and to have done something unpleasant to my former arcanist." He paused. "And Ambrose Jakis will be there. He is often at the palace now, since his father's rise. He will know you."

"I know."

"He hates you. That much is known even in Severen."

"I know."

"If he is what we think he is, if he is Bredon's piece, then he will want you dead. And at the king's court, with his father's power behind him, he will be in a position to arrange it."

"I know, your grace."

The Maer looked at me. Then he sighed and shook his head.

"You are going to go anyway," he said. "Aren't you?"

"Yes, your grace."

"Because of the girl."

I did not answer. I did not have to.

The Maer was quiet for a long moment. Then he opened a drawer in his desk and took out a sheet of heavy cream paper and a pen. He began to write.

"You will go as my envoy," he said, without looking up. "My personal representative to the royal court. It is a common enough thing; I send someone every season to pay my respects and carry my letters and keep my ear to the ground. No one will think anything of it." He wrote a few more lines. "You will carry letters of introduction to certain people at court whom I trust. There are not many. You will carry a purse sufficient to your station, which is to say considerably more than you are used to. And you will carry this."

He signed the paper, and dripped wax onto the bottom of it, and pressed his seal into the wax. Then he held it out to me.

I took it. It was a letter of commission. It named me as the Maer's envoy, with all the rights and privileges that entailed. And at the bottom, above his seal, there was a line I had not expected.

*The bearer is to be accorded every courtesy due to a member of my house.*

I looked up at him.

"Your grace," I said. "I can't—"

"You can. You will." He did not smile. But there was something in his eyes that was almost warm. "My wife would never forgive me if I sent her sister's son into a nest of vipers without every protection I could give him. And neither, I find, would I."

---

Meluan came to see me that evening, as I was packing.

She did not knock. She simply opened the door of my rooms and came in and closed it behind her and stood with her back against it, her hands resting on the great round swell of her belly. She was very pale. Her eyes were red.

"Alveron told me," she said.

"I'm sorry."

"Don't." She came into the room and sat down on the edge of my bed. She looked tired. More tired than I had ever seen her. "Don't be sorry. It wasn't your fault."

"I should have kept it safer."

"You kept it under your pillow. That's where I kept it." She smiled faintly. "It didn't help me either. Not really. It was only luck that I woke that night. Only luck that I screamed." She shook her head. "It was never safe. Not anywhere. I think I always knew that. I just didn't want to know."

I sat down beside her. For a long time, neither of us spoke.

"The girl," Meluan said at last. "The one who took it. Do you love her?"

"Yes."

"Does she love you?"

I thought about the knot on the table. *Forgive. Keep. Safe.*

"Yes," I said. "I think so."

Meluan nodded slowly. "Then she'll keep it safe. As best she can." She looked at me. "Your mother loved your father. And she ran from everything to keep him. To keep you." She was quiet for a moment. "People do strange things for love. Foolish things. Brave things. Sometimes they're the same thing."

She reached out and took my hand.

"Find her," she said. "Find her, and find the box, and bring them both home. And then come back here, to Severen. To us." She squeezed my hand. "You have a family now, Kvothe. Don't forget that."

I looked at her. At her pale tired face, and her red eyes, and her hand in mine.

"I won't," I said.

---

I left for Renere the next morning. The Maer gave me a horse, a tall grey gelding with a steady temper and an uncanny tolerance for my lute. He gave me two guards, hard-faced men who said little and watched everything. He gave me a purse so heavy I had to carry it in two parts.

Stapes saw me off at the gates. He stood very straight in the grey morning light, his hands folded, his face calm.

"Good luck, sir," he said.

"Thank you, Stapes."

He hesitated. Then he stepped forward and said, very quietly, so that the guards could not hear:

"Sir. When you are at court. The old gentleman. Bredon." He paused. "He always took his wine with a little honey. Very particular about it. And he always, always sat with his back to the wall." He looked at me. "I thought you might like to know."

I looked at him for a long moment. Then I smiled.

"Thank you, Stapes," I said. "That's very useful."

He bowed. "Safe travels, sir."

I rode out through the gates and down the long cliff road toward the plain. The sun was coming up over the river valley, turning the fields to gold. Somewhere to the north and east, three days' ride away, the royal city of Renere lay waiting, with its king dying in his bed and its heirs dying one by one and Ambrose Jakis smiling his secret smile.

And somewhere out there, beyond that, beyond everything, Denna was riding with a box in her saddlebag and a knot on her wrist, going somewhere I did not know.

I lit Kilvin's candle that night, in an inn at the edge of the plain. It burned yellow. I watched it for a long time.

Then I took out the little knot she had left me, the one that said *forgive*, and held it in my hand until I fell asleep.
