# Behind the Door

I did not answer.

I want that written down. I want it recorded somewhere, in plain ink on plain paper, that on that night, in that room, with that voice whispering my name from the other side of a door of stone, I did not answer. It is one of the few things I did right, in all the long story of my foolishness. I would like it to be remembered.

The voice was soft. It was patient. It was kind, the way a mother's voice is kind when she calls a child in from the dark. It spoke my name, and it was not the name *Kvothe*. It was the other one. The one the Sleeper had spoken, the one I did not know but recognized the way you recognize your own face. It spoke my name as if it had always known me, and it said, without words, *come*.

I stood very still. I thought of Auri, kneeling by her shelf with a pebble in her hand. *If the door speaks to you, don't answer.* I thought of the Sleeper, with his heavy grey eyes. *Do not come to the door. Whatever they tell you. Whatever they offer.*

I held Devi's hand so tightly I felt the small bones grind together. I did not answer.

After a long time, the voice stopped.

The breathing went on. Slow and deep and patient. But the whisper was gone, and the room was silent again, and I felt something let go of me that I had not known was holding on.

I let out my breath. My whole body was shaking.

"Did you hear it?" I said. My voice sounded thin and strange.

"Hear what?" said Devi.

I looked at her. She was looking at me with an expression of confusion and growing alarm. She had not heard it. She had heard nothing at all.

I looked at Fela. She was looking at me too. Her face was white, and her eyes were very wide.

"I heard it," she whispered. "It said something. I couldn't understand it. But it was talking to you."

"Yes."

"What did it say?"

I did not answer that either.

---

We did not go near the door. None of us did. We stayed at the edge of the room, as far from it as we could get, and we went to the table in the center of the circle.

It was a plain table, square and solid, carved from the same grey stone as everything else. The book lay in the center of it. It was very large, as large as the great ledgers in Lorren's office, and bound in something that was not leather. It was pale and smooth and faintly translucent, like old horn. There was no title on the cover. There was only a single sigil, pressed into the surface, a sigil I recognized.

It was a tower, burning.

I had seen that sigil before. In the Maer's library in Severen, in an old book on the history of the Aturan church. It was the mark of the Amyr. The Ciridae. The highest of their order, who wore that sign tattooed on their bodies and answered to no law but their own.

Devi reached for the book. I caught her wrist.

"Don't," I said. "Don't take anything."

"I'm not going to take it. I just want to read it." She pulled her wrist away and looked at me. "We came all this way, Kvothe."

She was right. I let go.

She opened the book.

---

It was written in Aturan. Old Aturan, very formal and very archaic, the kind of Aturan that the scholars of the empire had used for law and scripture. It was difficult to read, but not impossible. Devi and I had both studied it. We bent over the book together in the red light of my lamp, and we read.

It was a record. A history, of sorts, though not like any history I had ever read. It had been written by many hands over many years, each entry dated and signed with a name I did not recognize. Some entries were long, pages and pages of dense script. Others were only a line or two.

*The door holds. No change. —Tessaly, watching.*

*The door holds. The Sleeper stirred in the night but did not wake. —Aradin, watching.*

*The door holds. There was a sound behind it at the third hour, like a great beast turning over in its sleep. —Mirren, watching.*

On and on, for hundreds of pages. The same words, over and over, like a litany. *The door holds.* A long patient record of men and women who had come down the stairs into the dark to sit in this room and listen to the breathing on the other side of the stone and make sure that nothing changed.

And then, near the front of the book, before the litany began, there was something else.

It was an account. Written in a different hand from the rest, older and more careful. It had no date and no name. It began simply:

*This is the truth of the doors, set down so that those who keep them may know what they keep.*

I read it. Devi read it beside me. Fela stood behind us, looking over our shoulders, and read it too.

I will not tell you all of it. Some of it I did not understand, then or later. Some of it I have forgotten, or tried to. And some of it is not mine to tell. But I will tell you what I learned that night, standing in the dark at the bottom of the world with my friends beside me and the breathing of something vast and terrible in my ears.

---

There had been a war, once. Long ago, before the Aturan Empire, before any empire. A war between those who could name the world and those who could shape it.

The namers were those who understood the world as it was. Who could hear the true names of things and speak them, and by speaking them, command. The shapers were something else. They did not understand the world. They changed it. They made new things, things that had never been, things that had no true name because they had no true nature. They made them out of nothing, out of will and want and the raw stuff of creation.

One of the shapers was greater than all the rest. The account did not give his name. It called him only *the Thief*, because of the thing he had stolen: the moon. He had loved her, the account said, and she had not loved him, and so he had taken her. He had torn a hole in the sky and made a place beyond the world where he could keep her, and he had pulled her into it.

But he could not hold her. Not all the way. She was too much a part of the world, too much a part of the sky. And so she was caught between. Half in the world, half out of it. Waxing and waning. Coming and going. Never able to rest.

The war that followed had destroyed nearly everything. Cities had burned. Whole peoples had vanished. The namers and the shapers had torn at each other until the world itself began to come apart at the seams.

And at the end of it, when the shapers were defeated and the namers were few and broken and the world lay in ruins, those who were left had done a thing.

They had taken the Thief and put him away.

Not killed him. They could not kill him. He was too strong, and too strange, and there was no name in the world that could unmake him, because he had made himself into something that had no name. So they had done the only thing they could. They had shut him away, beyond the world, in the place he had made to hold the moon. And they had sealed the way behind him with doors of stone.

Seven doors, the account said. Seven doors in seven places, each one a face of the same prison. Seven locks on the same box. And each door had its keeping, and each keeping had its keepers, and the keepers were bound to watch the doors until the end of the world.

*The first door lies beneath the house of Loeclos,* the account said, *and it is the keeper's door, for it alone may be opened, and only by the line of Loeclos, with the key of stone. The line was chosen because it was the Thief's own line, and his blood knows his prison. So it is both the lock and the danger. While the line holds, the door holds. But if the line should come to the door with the key in hand, and speak its name, the door will open.*

*Therefore let the line be kept in ignorance, and let the key be kept apart, and let the name be kept close.*

I stopped reading. I could not see the page anymore. My eyes had gone blurry and my hands were shaking and there was a roaring in my ears like a great wind.

The Thief's own line.

My mother's line.

My line.

---

"Kvothe?" Devi's voice seemed to come from very far away. "Kvothe, what's wrong?"

I did not answer. I could not. I stood there with my hands on the edge of the stone table and the book open in front of me, and I tried to breathe.

The line of Loeclos. Chosen because it was the Thief's own blood. His blood knows his prison.

*The door holds while the line holds.*

That was why the Chandrian had come for my mother. Not only for the songs. For the blood. For the line. She had run away from her family, from the house and the name and the knowledge that went with them. She had gone out onto the roads with a red-haired man who made songs, and she had had a son.

And they had found her.

But they had not killed me.

I had always wondered about that. Why they had killed everyone in the troupe, every man, woman, and child, and left me alive. I had told myself it was luck. That I had been off in the woods, playing, and they had simply not noticed me. That Haliax had called them away before Cinder could finish the job.

But I remembered, now, standing in that dark room with the book of the Amyr open in front of me. I remembered Cinder, crouching in front of me in the firelight, his black eyes fixed on mine. I remembered the smile on his face, and the way he had looked at me.

He had not looked at me like a man who had forgotten a child in the woods. He had looked at me like a man who had found exactly what he was looking for.

---

"Kvothe." It was Fela. Her hand was on my shoulder, cold and firm. "Kvothe, we need to go."

I looked at her. Her face was white and strained, and her eyes were fixed on the door.

"The stone," she said. "It's getting louder. The seal I asked. It won't hold much longer. It's telling me. We have to go now."

I looked at the door. It had not changed. Grey stone, no handle, no hinges, no lock. The breathing went on beyond it, slow and deep.

But something had changed. I could feel it. The air in the room had grown heavier, thicker, the way the air grows before a storm. The breathing was a little faster. A little less deep.

Something on the other side of the door was waking up.

"Yes," I said. "Yes. We have to go."

I closed the book. My hands were shaking so badly I almost could not do it. I closed it and stepped back from the table, and Devi and Fela stepped back with me, and we turned toward the stairs.

And that was when I saw that Devi had something in her hand.

It was a single page. Folded small. She was tucking it into the front of her satchel, quickly, furtively, the way a pickpocket tucks away a purse.

"Devi," I said. "What is that?"

She looked at me. Her face was defiant and frightened and fierce all at once.

"I didn't take the book," she said. "I only took a page. From the back. It was loose. Nobody will miss it."

"Put it back."

"No."

"Devi, put it back. We don't take anything. That was the rule."

"That was *your* rule." Her eyes were bright behind her spectacles. "I've waited six years for this, Kvothe. Six years. I'm not leaving with nothing."

"Please," Fela whispered. "Please, we have to go. The stone—"

And then, all at once, the room rang.

It was not a sound. Devi had said that, and she had been right. It was something else. A vibration, deep and terrible, rising up out of the floor and the walls and the ceiling, shaking the air, shaking my bones, shaking my teeth in my skull. It was as if the whole world had been struck like a bell.

Fela cried out. She fell to her knees, her hands clapped over her ears.

"It's telling," she gasped. "It's telling, it's telling, I asked it not to but something was taken and now it's telling—"

Far above us, in the Archives, in the University, in the whole wide sleeping world, I felt the ringing go out like a ripple on a pond.

And behind the door, very clearly now, the breathing stopped.

---

We ran.

Up the narrow stairs, past the thousands of names carved into the walls. Through the small square room, which was ringing now like a struck gong, so loud it made my eyes water. Up the great spiral stair, around and around, our footsteps echoing in the vast dark shaft.

I did not look back. I did not dare. But I could feel it, behind us, far below. A presence. A weight. Something that had been sleeping and was sleeping no longer.

It was not following us. I do not think it could. But it was awake. And it was listening.

We reached the grate. I shoved Devi through first, then Fela. They scrambled into the air shaft and began to climb down, the rusted rungs creaking under their weight.

I was about to follow when I heard the voices.

They came from above. From the top of the great stair. From the other side of the Four-plate door. Men's voices, raised in alarm, and the sound of running feet, and a sound I had never heard before and hoped never to hear again: a deep grinding rumble, like a millstone turning.

The Four-plate door was opening.

I looked down at the grate. Devi's face looked up at me from the dark of the shaft, pale and terrified.

"Come on!" she hissed. "Kvothe, come on!"

I looked up at the door. A crack of light had appeared around its edge, thin and golden. It was widening.

I looked back down at Devi.

"Go," I said.

"What?"

"Go. Both of you. Get out. Go back to your rooms and don't tell anyone you were here." I reached down and grabbed the old iron grate from the ledge where Fela had set it. "They'll need someone to find. It'll go easier if they find me."

"Kvothe, no—"

"Go!"

I pushed the grate back into its place. It did not fit properly; the rust had eaten away too much of the frame. But it would do. It would hide the shaft from anyone who did not look too closely.

Through the bars, I saw Devi's face one last time. Her eyes were full of tears.

Then she was gone, down into the dark.

I turned and began to climb the stairs toward the light.
