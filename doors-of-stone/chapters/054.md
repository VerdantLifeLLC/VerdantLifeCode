@@PART PART THREE: THE MOON'S ROAD

# The Eld

I left Imre nine days before the full moon, in the grey hour before dawn, the way I seem to leave everywhere.

Threpe gave me a horse. Not a tall grey gelding like the Maer's, but a stocky brown mare with a white blaze and a placid disposition and a remarkable indifference to everything. Her name was Pudding. Threpe apologized for the name; it had been his niece's horse, and his niece was nine. I told him it was a fine name. Pudding looked at me with deep brown eyes and chewed her bit and did not seem to care what I thought.

I rode out of Imre on the north road, with my lute on my back and Caesura at my hip and my cloak drawn close around me. Not the shaed Felurian had made. That was folded carefully in the bottom of my pack; I would need it later, in the Fae, and I did not want to be seen wearing it on the roads of the Commonwealth. Instead I wore a plain brown cloak of Threpe's, with a deep hood to hide my hair.

No one saw me go. Or if they did, they did not say so.

---

The Eld is an old forest. The oldest in the Four Corners, some say. It sprawls across the hills north and east of Severen, a great dark mass of oak and ash and beech and trees whose names no one remembers, so thick and so tangled that the roads go around it rather than through. People do not live in the Eld. They do not farm it or log it or hunt it, except around its very edges. It is too big, and too dark, and too strange.

I had been there before. I had spent the better part of a season in the Eld, the year before, hunting bandits for the Maer with a handful of mercenaries. We had found the bandits, in the end, and killed them. And then, afterward, I had wandered off alone in the night, following a sound I could not name, and I had found a glade with a pool in it and a greystone beside the pool.

And in the glade, Felurian.

It took me six days to reach the Eld, riding hard on Pudding's broad brown back. It took me two more to find the glade.

I did not remember the way. Not clearly. I had been following a song, that night, a song I had heard in my bones rather than my ears, and I had not been paying attention to where I was going. But I remembered the shape of the land. The long low ridge running north and south. The stream that cut through it, fast and cold and clear. The place where the stream widened into a pool, surrounded by old beeches with silver bark.

And I remembered the greystone. A tall grey standing stone, taller than a man, set at the edge of the pool. Smooth and old and faintly humming, the way all greystones hum.

I found it on the evening of the eighth day. I came over the top of the long ridge, leading Pudding by the bridle through the thick undergrowth, and there below me was the stream, and there was the pool, and there were the silver beeches, and there, standing tall and grey and silent at the water's edge, was the stone.

The glade was empty.

---

I made camp at the edge of the glade, under the silver beeches, and I did not sleep.

The moon rose a little after sunset. It was nearly full, fat and silver and very bright. It climbed slowly up into the sky, and its light came down through the leaves of the beeches and fell on the pool in a scatter of silver coins. It fell on the greystone, and the greystone seemed to glow.

Tomorrow night, it would be full. Tomorrow night, if I stood beside that stone and did what I had done before, I would step through into the Fae.

I sat with my back against a beech and my cold arm in my lap and Fela's stone in my good hand, and I watched the moon climb, and I thought about what Elodin had said.

*Old things. Hungry things. Things that have been waiting a very long time for a mortal with a namer's gift and the blood of the Thief.*

I thought about the Cthaeh.

I had not let myself think about it for a long time. Not since the Eolian. Not since I had watched Stanchion die and Cinder's hand close around my wrist and a shadow in the center of the room say *he is needed*. I had pushed it away every time it came into my mind, the way you push away a thought of something shameful.

But I thought about it now. I let myself. I sat under the silver beeches in the moonlight and I remembered the tree in the Fae, and the voice that came out of it. Honey poured over broken glass. The voice that had told me about Denna's patron, and what he did to her, and laughed.

It had known. It had known everything. It had known about Bredon, and the knot on Denna's wrist, and the beatings. It had known that I would go back to the mortal world and seek him out. It had known what I would find, and what I would do.

It had known I would come back to the Fae.

And it had told me what it told me, in exactly the way it told me, because it wanted me here. Right here. Sitting under a silver beech beside a greystone, on the night before the full moon, about to step through into its world.

I sat there with Fela's stone in my hand and I felt the shape of the board close around me like a fist.

---

In the morning, I sent Pudding home.

It was not easy. She did not want to go. She had grown fond of me in eight days, or at least fond of the apples I kept in my pack, and when I took off her saddle and bridle and slapped her on the rump, she only looked at me with her deep brown eyes and did not move.

"Go on," I said. "Go home. Threpe will be worried."

She did not go. She put her head down and began to crop the grass at the edge of the glade.

I sighed. I took the last apple out of my pack and held it up so she could see it. Her ears pricked up. I threw it, as hard as I could, down the slope toward the south, toward Severen and the road and the mortal world.

She went after it. She went crashing through the undergrowth, her broad brown rump bouncing, and I heard her find it and heard the crunch as she bit into it. And then, after a moment, I heard her keep going, south and west, toward home.

I stood at the edge of the glade and listened until I could not hear her anymore.

Then I went back to the greystone and sat down to wait for night.

---

I spent that day preparing. I took everything out of my pack and laid it on the grass beside the pool and looked at it.

My lute, in its case. Caesura, in its sheath. Felurian's shaed, folded carefully, the dark shadowy cloth of it shifting and rippling in the sunlight like water. Kilvin's candle. The scrap of the Dellers' cloth. The page from the Book of Watches. Fela's stone eye, and Fela's stone from the Eolian. Denna's knots: the one from the Grey Man around my neck, the one from Severen in my pocket, the letter-cord braided from her hair.

And food. Bread and cheese and dried meat and apples, enough for a span if I was careful. I knew better than to eat anything in the Fae. I had learned that lesson the first time.

I looked at it all for a long time. Then I put it all back in my pack, carefully, the way you put things away when you are not sure you will ever take them out again.

I lit Kilvin's candle one last time, sitting by the pool in the afternoon light. It burned yellow. I watched it for a long time. Then I snuffed it and put it away.

When the sun went down, I put on the shaed.

---

It is hard to describe what it feels like to wear Felurian's shaed. It is a cloak, made of shadow. Not dark cloth, but actual shadow, gathered and woven and shaped by the hands of the oldest and most beautiful of the Fae. When you wear it, you feel it moving around you, soft and cool and alive. It clings to you. It shifts with you. It hides you, if you want it to, so that you are only one more shadow among many.

It is also, I discovered that night, very warm. Warmer than it has any right to be. When I drew it around my shoulders, standing beside the pool in the gathering dusk, I felt the warmth of it sink into me, into my bones, into my cold dead arm. For the first time since the Eolian, my left hand did not feel like ice.

I stood there for a long time, feeling it. Then the moon came up over the trees.

---

It was full. Round and silver and enormous, hanging low over the eastern ridge like a great lamp. Its light came down into the glade and fell on the pool, and the pool became a mirror. It fell on the greystone, and the greystone began to sing.

I did not hear it with my ears. I heard it with something else. The same thing I had used to hear stone, and wind, and the knots in the king's hair. A long low humming note, rising up from the greystone and filling the glade. It was not a word. It was not a name. It was more like a door opening. A door made of sound.

I walked to the edge of the pool. I stood beside the greystone. I laid my good hand flat against its cold smooth surface.

I felt it humming under my palm. I felt it calling.

And I stepped forward, into the pool, into the moonlight, into the mirror of the sky.

---

I did not get wet. That is the first thing I remember. I stepped into the pool, and my foot went down, and down, and I felt the water close over it—and then it was not water. It was light. Silver light, cool and soft and bright, closing over my foot and my ankle and my knee. I took another step, and another, and the light rose up around me, and I did not sink. I walked down into it, the way you walk down a set of stairs.

The glade went away. The beeches and the greystone and the pool and the moon, all of it went away. There was only the silver light, all around me, above and below and on every side. It was very bright, and very quiet, and very still.

I kept walking.

And after a while, I don't know how long, the light began to change. It grew dimmer. Softer. It took on colors, faint at first, then stronger. Violet and grey and deep dusky blue. I felt something under my feet that was not light. Something soft and springy, like moss.

I stopped. I looked around.

I was standing in a forest. But it was not the Eld. It was not any forest in the mortal world. The trees were taller than any trees I had ever seen, so tall their tops were lost in the dusk above. Their trunks were silver and smooth, like the beeches in the glade, but they glowed faintly, the way the greystone had glowed. Their leaves were every color at once, shifting and changing as I watched, green and gold and red and violet.

And the sky above them was twilight. Not dusk, not dawn. Twilight. The endless, shadowless, sourceless light of the Fae, where there is no sun and no moon, only the sky itself, glowing softly from horizon to horizon.

I was back.

I stood there under the impossible trees for a long time, breathing. The air smelled of flowers I did not know the names of, and of rain, and of something else, something sweet and wild and faintly dangerous, like the smell of a thunderstorm on the way.

Then I pulled the shaed close around me, and I began to walk.
