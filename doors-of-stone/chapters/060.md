# Interlude: The Other Side of the Net

The innkeeper stopped. He reached for his cup of tea and found it cold, and drank it anyway.

Chronicler set down his pen. His hand ached. He flexed it, slowly, and looked across the table at Bast.

Bast was sitting in his backward chair with his arms folded across the back of it and his chin resting on his arms. He had not moved in a long time. His eyes were fixed on the innkeeper's face, and they were very bright.

"I didn't know," he said. His voice was very quiet. "That it talked to you. All that night. I didn't know."

"I didn't tell you."

"You should have."

"Why? So you could feel guilty for sleeping?" The innkeeper set down his cup. "You'd been screaming in a net for half a day, Bast. You'd nearly been shot by the Sithe. You were exhausted. You deserved to sleep."

"You didn't sleep."

"I'm used to not sleeping." The innkeeper smiled faintly. "It's one of my few real talents."

Bast looked at him for a long moment. Then he looked at Chronicler.

"Do you want to know what it was like?" he said. "From the other side? From inside the net?"

Chronicler hesitated. He glanced at the innkeeper, who shrugged slightly.

"It's his story too," the innkeeper said. "Let him tell it."

Chronicler picked up his pen. He dipped it. "Yes," he said. "I'd like to know."

---

"I was so stupid," Bast said.

He said it plainly, without any particular emotion. The way you might say *the sky is blue*, or *water is wet*.

"I was very young. Younger than I looked. Younger than I am now, by a great deal. And I had been sad for a very long time. Since my mother left. Everyone at my father's court thought I had gotten over it, because I laughed and played and chased girls and did all the things a young prince of the Telwyth Mael is supposed to do. But I hadn't gotten over it. I'd only gotten better at hiding it."

He was quiet for a moment.

"And one day I just—decided. I didn't plan it. I didn't think about it. I woke up one morning and I knew I was going to the Grove. I was going to ask the Tree what had happened to my mother. And nothing anyone said was going to stop me." He laughed softly, without humor. "My father would have locked me in a tower if he'd known. So I didn't tell him. I just went."

"Alone?" said Chronicler.

"Alone. Of course alone. Who would have come with me?" Bast shook his head. "I walked for nine days. Through the Deep Wood. Past Felurian's spring, though I didn't see her. Into the birches. And all the way, the closer I got, I could hear it. Calling. So softly. So sweetly." He shivered. "It knew I was coming. It knew before I did, probably. It was waiting."

"And the Sithe?"

"The Sithe caught me at the edge of the Grove. The very edge. I could see the tree. I could see the flowers on it, the red ones, the ones that smell so sweet. I was maybe a hundred paces away." He closed his eyes. "The net came out of nowhere. One moment I was walking, and the next I was tangled up in silver, hanging between two birches, and I couldn't move."

"And you screamed."

"I screamed." Bast opened his eyes. "Not because of the net. Because of what I heard. When the net caught me."

The common room was very quiet. The fire crackled. Outside, the night was very dark.

"What did you hear?" said Chronicler softly.

Bast looked at the innkeeper. The innkeeper looked back at him. Something passed between them.

"I've never told you," Bast said. "Have I, Reshi?"

"No."

"I've never told anyone." Bast looked down at his folded arms. "It said one thing. Just one. As the net caught me, before the Sithe came. Very soft. Right in my ear, as if it were standing beside me." He was quiet for a long time. "It said, *You will keep him in a cage of your own making. And you will call it love.*"

---

Nobody spoke.

Chronicler sat very still with his pen in his hand. He looked at Bast, and then at the innkeeper, and then back at Bast.

The innkeeper had gone very pale.

"Bast," he said quietly.

"I didn't know what it meant." Bast's voice was very steady. Too steady. "Not then. I didn't know who *him* was. I screamed because it was the most horrible thing I'd ever heard, and I didn't even know why. And then the Sithe came, and they had their arrows pointed at me, and I thought I was going to die." He looked up. "And then you stepped in front of me. And I knew."

"Bast—"

"I knew *who*. The moment you stepped in front of me. I looked at you, standing there with your back to me, with five arrows pointed at your heart, and I knew." Bast's eyes were very bright. "I've known ever since. Every day. Every single day. *You will keep him in a cage of your own making.*"

The innkeeper did not say anything. He sat very still with his hands folded on the table.

"And I did," said Bast. His voice cracked, very slightly. "Didn't I? I did exactly that. I've done it for years."

"You haven't—"

"I have." Bast stood up. His chair scraped on the floor. "This place. This inn. This town. These people. Graham and Cob and Jake and all the rest of them." He gestured around the dark common room. "I helped you build it. I helped you hide in it. I helped you forget. Every time you started to remember, every time you started to become yourself again, I—" He stopped. "I was so afraid. Of what you'd do. Of what would happen if you remembered. If you picked it back up. I thought if you stayed here, if you stayed Kote, you'd be safe."

"Bast."

"And then I realized what I'd done." Bast was shaking. "I realized that I'd built you a cage, and I'd told myself it was love. Just like it said. Just exactly like it said." He looked at the innkeeper, and his face was naked with grief. "So I tried to break it. I tried to break you out. And I—"

He stopped. He pressed his hand against his mouth.

The innkeeper was looking at him very steadily.

"And you what, Bast?" he said quietly.

Bast did not answer. He stood there with his hand pressed against his mouth, and he shook his head, over and over.

"Not yet," he said through his fingers. "Not yet. I'll tell you. I promise I'll tell you. But not yet."

He turned and went into the kitchen. The door swung shut behind him.

---

The innkeeper sat very still for a long time. Chronicler did not speak. He did not know what to say.

At last the innkeeper sighed. He picked up his cold cup of tea and looked into it.

"He's been carrying that for a long time," he said. "Longer than I knew."

"The Cthaeh's words."

"The Cthaeh's words." The innkeeper set down the cup. "That's what it does, you know. It doesn't lie. It doesn't need to. It just tells you the truth, and then it lets you carry it. And the carrying is what breaks you." He looked at the kitchen door. "He's been carrying that since the day we met. Every day. Wondering if every kind thing he did for me was the cage. Wondering if every time he protected me, he was locking the door."

"Was he?"

The innkeeper looked at Chronicler for a long moment.

"Yes," he said. "Sometimes. And sometimes no. It's not always easy to tell the difference, between a cage and a shelter." He smiled faintly. "The Cthaeh knew that. That's why it said it. It knew he'd never be able to tell."

"And what did he do? What was he going to say, just now? *I tried to break you out, and I—*"

The innkeeper was silent for a long time. He looked at the kitchen door. Then he looked at the sword above the hearth. Then, finally, he looked at his own hands, folded on the table. At the bruises on his knuckles, fading from purple to yellow.

"I think I know," he said quietly. "I think I've known since last night." He paused. "But he'll tell it when he's ready. It's his to tell."

---

After a while, Bast came back. His eyes were red, but his face was composed. He was carrying a fresh pot of tea. He set it on the table and poured three cups without speaking, and then he sat down in his backward chair again.

"Sorry," he said. "Go on, Reshi. Where were we?"

The innkeeper looked at him for a long moment. Then he reached across the table and laid his hand, very briefly, on Bast's arm.

Bast did not look up. But his shoulders loosened, very slightly.

"The Northern Gate," said the innkeeper. "We'd come to the Northern Gate. At the end of the Moon's Road. And beyond it, the mountains."

He picked up his fresh cup of tea and held it in both hands, the way you hold something warm on a cold night.

"It was snowing on the other side," said Kvothe. "I remember that. We stepped through a ring of greystones, out of the twilight, and it was snowing. Great soft flakes, falling out of a white sky. And the cold hit us like a hammer." He smiled faintly. "Bast had never seen snow before. He stood there with his mouth open, catching flakes on his tongue, and I thought he was going to cry."

"I didn't cry," said Bast.

"You cried a little."

"It was very beautiful snow."

"It was." The innkeeper looked into his tea. "It was very beautiful. And it was full of scrael."
