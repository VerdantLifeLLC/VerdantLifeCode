# A Tree in the Play

Hallow's Company put on a play at midwinter. They always did. It was the custom of that valley, Tessit told me, going back forty years: on the longest night, the three troupes wintering in the barn put aside their quarrels and their rivalries and made a single play together, for no audience but themselves.

"For the children," she said. "And the dead. Mostly the dead."

The play that year was *The Tinker and the Nine Doors*. I did not know it. It was an old Ruh piece, older than *Daeonica*, older than the Tehlin mysteries, a thing that had never been written down and was different every time it was played. A tinker comes to a house with nine doors. Behind each door is a person who wants something. The tinker has one thing in his pack for each of them, and he must decide which door to open first, because the doors open only once, and the things in his pack can be given only once.

"Who wins?" I asked Tessit.

She looked at me strangely.

"Nobody wins," she said. "It's not that kind of play."

---

They gave me a part. I did not ask for one. I was grey, that winter, and I did not want anything, and I would have been content to sit by the fire in the barn and watch the children run past and polish the same tin cup over and over until spring. But the Ruh do not let anyone sit by the fire for long. Everyone works. Everyone plays. Even the grey ones.

"You'll be the tree," said Hallow.

Hallow was the leader of the troupe that year. He was a big, bluff, bald man with a voice like a kettle drum and a laugh you could hear across the valley. He had been Tessit's husband, once, and then he had not been, and they were still fond of each other in the careful, prickly way of people who have loved each other and stopped.

"The tree," I said.

"In the courtyard. Outside the house with the nine doors." He clapped me on the shoulder. "You stand there. The whole play. With your arms out. And you don't say anything." He grinned. "Tessit says you're good at not saying anything."

I looked at him. Then I looked at Tessit, standing behind him, with her arms folded. She did not smile.

"All right," I said.

---

I stood there for three hours.

They built me a costume out of old sacking and pine boughs and strips of bark from the woodpile. They painted my face grey-brown and tied branches to my arms. A girl of about ten named Pim, who was very serious about everything, made me a crown of dried leaves and holly berries and set it on my head with great ceremony, and then stood back and looked at me with her head on one side.

"You're a very good tree," she said. "You look very sad. Trees in winter look sad."

"Thank you."

"Don't talk," she said. "Trees don't talk."

So I did not talk.

The play began at dusk. The whole company was there, and all their children, and all their dogs, and old Duchess the goat, sitting in a great half circle on hay bales around the cleared floor of the barn. There were lanterns hung from the rafters and a fire in a pit at one end, and the air smelled of pine and smoke and wet wool.

And I stood in the courtyard of the house with nine doors, with my arms out, and watched.

---

The tinker was played by an old man named Brann, who had been playing the tinker in that valley for thirty years. He was small and bent and brown as a walnut, with a voice like a creaking gate, and he came into the courtyard with his pack on his back and stopped in front of me and looked up at me, and scratched his head.

"Well," he said. "Here's a tree. Afternoon, tree."

I said nothing. The children laughed.

"Not much of a talker," said the tinker. "That's all right. Neither am I." He set down his pack at my feet. "Mind if I rest a while? It's a long road. And I've got a hard job ahead of me."

He sat down with his back against my legs. The children laughed again.

And then the first door opened.

---

It went on like that for three hours. One door after another. Behind the first door was a widow who wanted her husband back. Behind the second was a king who wanted a crown that fit. Behind the third was a child who wanted to be older, and behind the fourth an old man who wanted to be young. A soldier who wanted to forget. A scholar who wanted to remember. A girl who wanted to be loved. A boy who wanted to be left alone.

And the tinker opened his pack for each of them, and took out something, and gave it, or did not. And every time he gave something to one, it was gone, and he could not give it to another. And every time he chose, someone behind another door wept.

It was not a comedy. I had thought it would be; the first hour was full of jokes, and the children laughed until they fell off the hay bales. But the jokes grew fewer as the doors opened. And by the seventh door, nobody was laughing. Not even the children. They sat on their hay bales in the lantern light with their mouths open, watching the old man with his pack, and they did not make a sound.

I stood with my arms out. My shoulders ached. My legs ached. I did not move.

---

The eighth door was the hardest.

Behind it was a woman who did not want anything at all. She was played by Tessit. She came out of the door in a plain grey dress, with her hair loose, and she stood in the courtyard and looked at the tinker, and did not say anything.

"What do you want?" said the tinker.

"Nothing," said Tessit.

"Everyone wants something."

"I did, once." Tessit looked at the tinker with her dark, level eyes. "I wanted so much. I wanted everything. And then I lost it all, all at once, in a single night. And now I don't want anything. It's easier."

The tinker looked at her for a long time. Then he opened his pack.

"I have something for you," he said.

"I told you. I don't want anything."

"I know." The tinker reached into his pack. "That's why I have to give it to you."

He took out a small thing, cupped in his hands. He held it out. The lantern light did not show what it was. You could not see it from the hay bales. You could only see that it was small, and that the tinker held it very carefully, the way you might hold an egg, or a bird, or a coal.

Tessit looked at it. She did not take it.

"What is it?" she said.

"It's wanting," said the tinker. "Just a little. Just enough." He held it out. "You'll need it, where you're going."

"Where am I going?"

"Somewhere," said the tinker. "Anywhere. That's what it's for."

---

I do not know why that undid me. I have thought about it many times since. I was grey, that winter, as grey as I have ever been. I did not feel anything. I had not felt anything for months. I had stood in a courtyard of sacking and pine boughs for three hours and watched an old man give away everything in his pack, and I had felt nothing at all.

But when Tessit reached out, slowly, and took that small invisible thing from the tinker's hands, and held it against her chest, and closed her eyes, something happened in me.

It was not much. It was not a feeling, exactly. It was more like the memory of a feeling. Like hearing, from very far away, the last notes of a song you used to know.

And my arms, which I had held out for three hours without moving, began to shake.

---

Pim saw it. She was sitting in the front row, on a hay bale, with her serious face. She saw my arms shaking and she stood up, in the middle of the play, and pointed.

"The tree's moving!" she said.

Everyone looked at me. The tinker, and Tessit, and all the troupe, and all the children, and all the dogs. Even old Duchess the goat lifted her head.

I tried to stop. I could not. My arms shook, and the branches tied to them shook, and the dried leaves rattled, and the holly berries fell off my crown one by one and rolled across the barn floor.

Nobody said anything. There was a long, strange silence.

And then Brann, the old tinker, stood up slowly from where he had been sitting at my feet. He turned around and looked up at me. His walnut face was very still.

"Well," he said. "Look at that. The wind's come to the tree."

He reached into his pack. He took out another small invisible thing, cupped in his hands. He held it up to me.

"Here," he said. "I saved one. There's always one left, at the end. For whoever needs it."

I looked down at him. I could not speak. Trees don't speak.

"Go on," said the tinker gently. "Take it. It's only a little. Just enough."

I could not take it. My hands were tied to branches. But the old man did not seem to mind. He reached up, very slowly, and tucked the small invisible thing into a fold of the sacking at my chest, right over my heart. And he patted it once, very lightly.

"There," he said. "For later. When you're somewhere."

And then he turned back to the ninth door, and the play went on.

---

I do not remember the ninth door. I do not remember how the play ended. I remember standing in the courtyard of sacking and pine boughs with my arms shaking and my face wet under the grey-brown paint, and the children staring at me, and not being able to stop.

Afterward, when the lanterns had been put out and the children put to bed, Tessit found me sitting on a hay bale at the back of the barn, still in my costume, with the branches untied from my arms and lying on the floor beside me.

She sat down next to me. She did not say anything for a long time.

"That wasn't in the play," I said at last. "The last thing. The one he saved."

"No." Tessit looked at the dying fire. "Brann does that, sometimes. Not every year. Only when there's someone in the audience who needs it." She looked at me. "He's done it four times in thirty years. Once for a woman who'd lost her children to the fever. Once for a man who'd come back from the war without his legs. Once for me."

"When?"

"The winter after my husband died. My first husband. Before Hallow." She was quiet for a moment. "I was a tree too, that year."

I did not say anything. I sat on the hay bale with my face still wet.

"It doesn't fix anything," said Tessit. "You know that. It's only a play. It's only an old man pretending to put something in your shirt." She stood up. "But it's something to carry. Until you get where you're going."

---

Brann left the valley before the thaw. Tessit said he always did. He came and went as he pleased, she said, and no troupe could hold him, and no one knew where he wintered when he did not winter with them. He had been an old man when she was a girl. He had been an old man, the eldest of the troupe told her, when *they* were children.

I did not see him go. I only found, one morning, that the little painted cart he kept at the end of the row of wagons was gone, and the donkey with it, and the wheel ruts led away down the valley toward the road. And the sound of a small bell, very faint, far off, going away.

I did not see him again for seven years. Not until a bell rang on the road outside an inn at a crossroads, and I went to the door, and a very old man as brown as a walnut looked up at me with his bright black eyes and smiled.

He did not say he knew me. He did not have to.

---

I did carry it. I do not know what it was. There was nothing in the fold of the sacking when I took the costume off; I looked. Of course there was nothing. It was a play.

But I carried it anyway. Through the rest of that winter, and down the long road west, and into an empty inn at a crossroads. I carried it through all the grey years behind the bar, polishing the same glasses over and over. I did not feel it. I did not know it was there.

It was only a little. Just enough.

And when a scribe came up the road one autumn, with his satchel and his pens and his bloody knuckles, and sat down at a table in my common room and asked me to tell him a story, I think it was that small invisible thing, tucked over my heart by an old man in a barn, that said *yes*.
