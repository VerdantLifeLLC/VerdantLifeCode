# A Small Name

Auri came to me on the second night, on the roof of the Grey Man.

I do not know how she knew I was there. I did not know who she was. I had gone up onto the roof because the rooms were too full, and too warm, and too loud with people who remembered me and wanted me to remember them. I had climbed out the window and up the slates to the ridge, and I was sitting there with my knees drawn up, looking out at the grey rooftops of Imre and the grey river beyond them and the grey bulk of the building with no windows on the far bank.

And she was there. Sitting beside me on the ridge, with her knees drawn up too, and her bare feet very clean on the slates. I had not heard her come. I had not heard anything.

She was very small. Very thin. Her hair floated around her head like a cloud of silk, pale as a dandelion's ghost. She wore a simple dress that had once been white. There was a ribbon in her hair, very faded, the color of the sky before rain.

She did not say anything for a long time. She sat beside me on the ridge of the roof and looked out at the river, the way I was looking.

Then she said, "You're on the wrong side of the river again."

I looked at her. "Am I?"

"Yes. You always are, now." She tilted her head. "They won't let you come to the right side. Master Elodin says. Because of what you did, with the deep door." She looked at me with enormous eyes. "So I came to this side instead. Through the old drain by the water. It's very wet. I don't like it." She wrinkled her nose. "But I came."

"Why?"

She looked at me for a long moment. Her eyes were very large and very grey in the grey light.

"Because you were sad," she said simply. "I could feel it. All the way across the river. The stones told me." She tucked her feet up under her dress. "The stones always tell me when you're sad."

---

I did not know her. I want that understood. I did not know her name, or where she lived, or why she had crossed a river through a wet drain to sit on a roof with a stranger.

But I did not feel like a stranger, sitting beside her. The cord on my wrist was not humming. It was quiet. But something else was. Something I could not name. A kind of stillness, settling over me, the way stillness settles over a pond at dusk.

"I don't know who you are," I said.

"I know." She did not seem troubled by it. "That's all right. I don't mind. You didn't know who I was the first time either." She smiled, a small quick smile. "You played music on a roof, and I came to listen, and you didn't know me at all. And then you did."

"I don't remember."

"You don't have to. I remember for both of us." She looked out at the river. "I'm very good at remembering. Much better than most people. I remember things that happened a long time ago, before I came here. I just don't talk about them." She was quiet for a moment. "You never asked me to. That's one of the things I like about you."

---

We sat on the roof for a long time. The sun went down behind the hills to the west, and the sky went from grey to darker grey to black. The stars came out. The lights of Imre came on, one by one, in the windows below us.

She did not talk much. Neither did I. It was the easiest silence I had known since the cellar. Easier than with Denna, who was always watching me, waiting for me to remember. Easier than with the others, who wanted things from me I did not have to give.

This girl did not want anything. She only sat.

At last she said, "You don't have a name."

"No."

"I can see it. There's a hole where it should be." She looked at my chest, as if she could see through my shirt, through my skin, into the place where my name had been. "It's very sad. It's the saddest hole I've ever seen."

"I gave it away. To close a door."

"I know. The stones told me that too." She looked at the cord on my wrist. "It's in there. Isn't it? All tied up. Very tight. Very neat." She tilted her head. "It's very loud."

"Everyone says that."

"Everyone's right." She reached out, very carefully, and touched the cord with one finger. Just for a moment. Then she took her hand away. "It wants to come out. It's pressing against the knots. Like a moth against a window." She looked at me. "You're going to put it somewhere. Aren't you? Somewhere safe. Somewhere quiet."

"Yes. In a box. My friends are making it."

"That's good." She nodded. "That's very sensible. Names shouldn't be left lying about." She was quiet for a moment. "But then you'll have nothing. No name at all. Not even in the cord. Not even on your wrist."

"Yes."

"That's not good." She frowned. "That's not good at all. People need names. Even small ones. Otherwise they don't know who they are, and they get lost, and they wander around and around and can't find their way home." She looked at me very seriously. "I know. I was like that once. For a long time."

"What did you do?"

She looked at me for a long moment. Then she smiled.

"Someone gave me a small name," she said. "A little one. Just to wear. While my big one was sleeping." She touched the faded ribbon in her hair. "It fit very well. I've worn it ever since."

---

She stood up. She stood on the ridge of the roof, balanced as easily as a bird on a branch, and she looked down at me.

"I'll give you one," she said. "A small name. Would you like that?"

I looked up at her. At her pale face and her cloud of hair and her enormous grey eyes.

"Yes," I said.

She considered me for a long time. She tilted her head this way and that, the way she might look at a stone or a leaf or a bit of glass, trying to see its true shape. She walked slowly around me, along the ridge of the roof, looking at me from every side.

"It can't be a name for what you are," she said at last. "That's the big name. That's in the cord. If I gave you a name for what you are, it would be too close. It would call to the big one, and the big one would call back, and everyone would hear." She frowned. "It has to be a name for something else. Something smaller. Something that happened, not something that *is*."

"What happened?"

She looked at me with her enormous eyes.

"A disaster," she said simply. "You happened. To the door, and the king, and the old man, and the war. And to you." She was quiet for a moment. "You're the thing that happened. And it wasn't good, and it wasn't bad. It was just very big. And very sad. And it broke a lot of things."

She knelt down on the slates in front of me. She put her small cold hands on either side of my face and looked into my eyes.

"There's an old word," she said. "From a long way away. From before I came here. It means a thing that happens, that breaks things. A storm. A flood. A fire. It doesn't mean it's wicked. It just means it's big, and it happened, and nothing was the same after." She tilted her head. "It's a very small word. Just two sounds. It won't call anything. It's too small to call anything."

"What is it?"

She leaned forward, very close, and whispered it in my ear.

"*Kote*," she said.

---

I sat very still.

It was not a name. Not the way the cord was a name, or the thing that had been in the hole in my chest. It did not reach down inside me and catch hold of anything. It did not hum. It did not glow.

But it fit.

It fit the way a coat fits, or a pair of boots. Not a part of me. Something I could wear, over the hole, so that the hole would not show. Something I could answer to, when people called.

"Kote," I said.

She sat back on her heels and smiled at me. It was a radiant smile, sudden and bright, like the sun coming out from behind a cloud.

"There," she said. "Now you're somebody. Not your big somebody. A little one. But somebody." She tilted her head. "Do you like it?"

I thought about it.

"Yes," I said. "I like it."

"Good." She stood up. She brushed off her dress. "Wear it carefully. Don't let it get too big. If it gets too big, it'll start to be a real name, and then it'll call to the other one." She looked at me seriously. "Keep it small. Keep it quiet. Keep it just a thing that happened."

"How?"

She thought about that.

"Don't do anything big," she said at last. "Don't sing big songs. Don't call the wind. Don't fight kings or open doors or go into the Fae." She wrinkled her nose. "Do small things. Quiet things. Bake bread. Sweep floors. Polish things." She smiled. "I polish things all the time. It's very soothing. It keeps you small."

"Polish things," I said.

"Polish things." She nodded firmly. "And when your big name wakes up—and it will, someday, when it's safe—you can take off the little one and put it away. And be yourself again." She paused. "But not until then."

---

She stayed with me on the roof until very late. She did not say much more. She sat beside me with her knees drawn up and her bare feet on the slates, and she hummed, very softly, a tune I did not know.

At last she stood up again.

"I have to go," she said. "The drain gets very full when the tide comes up the river. I don't want to get my feet wet."

"Will I see you again?"

She looked at me for a long moment. Her enormous eyes were very sad.

"I don't think so," she said. "Not for a long time. Maybe not ever. You're going away. Far away. Somewhere quiet, where no one knows you." She tilted her head. "And I live here. Under the University. With the deep door. Someone has to keep an eye on it."

"The deep door?"

"The one you went to. With the book, and the names on the walls, and the Sleeper." She looked toward the far bank of the river, toward the great grey building with no windows. "It's very unhappy, since you closed the other one. It's very lonely. It keeps calling out to its brothers and sisters. The other doors. And only six of them answer now." She looked back at me. "The one you closed doesn't answer anyone. It's like a hole in a song."

I did not understand. I did not say anything.

"I'll keep it company," said Auri. "The deep door. I'll sing to it. The way you sang to the Sleeper." She smiled faintly. "Somebody should."

She turned to go. Then she stopped, and turned back, and knelt down in front of me one more time.

She reached into the pocket of her dress and took something out and pressed it into my hand.

It was a small bundle of dried flowers. Lavender. Tied with a bit of blue thread.

"For your new place," she said. "Wherever it is. So it smells like a meadow."

I looked down at the lavender in my hand.

"Thank you," I said.

"You're welcome, Kote." She leaned forward and kissed me on the forehead, very lightly, the way a mother kisses a child. "Be small. Be quiet. Polish things." She paused. "And be careful."

She stood up. She walked along the ridge of the roof to the end, where it met the next building. She stepped across the gap as easily as if it were not there.

And then she was gone, into the dark, across the rooftops of Imre, toward the river and the old drain and the Underthing on the other side.

I sat on the roof of the Grey Man with the lavender in my hand until the sun came up.

---

When I went back inside, Denna was waiting for me. She was sitting on the edge of the bed in the largest room, with her hands folded in her lap. She had not slept. I could see it in her face.

"Where were you?" she said.

"On the roof."

"All night?"

"Yes."

"Alone?"

I thought about it. "No," I said. "There was a girl. Very small. With hair like a cloud."

Denna's face changed. "Auri," she said softly. "That was Auri. You used to bring her things. Food, and presents. You never told anyone about her except me." She looked at me. "What did she say?"

I held out my hand. The one with the lavender in it.

"She gave me this," I said. "For my new place. So it smells like a meadow."

Denna looked at the lavender. Her eyes were wet.

"And she gave me a name," I said.

Denna went very still.

"A small one," I said. "Just to wear. While the big one is sleeping."

"What is it?"

I looked at her.

"Kote," I said.

Denna was quiet for a very long time. She looked at me, and her face did many things, one after another, too fast for me to follow. Grief, and something like fear, and something like relief, and something I did not have a name for.

"Kote," she said at last. "It means *disaster*. In Siaru. The old dialect. Did you know that?"

"She told me. A thing that happens, that breaks things."

"A thing that happens." Denna closed her eyes. "Yes. That's right. That's exactly right." She opened them again and looked at me. "Is that who you want to be? A disaster?"

I thought about it.

"It's who I am," I said. "For now. Until it's safe to be someone else."

Denna looked at me for a long moment. Then she stood up and came to me and put her arms around me, and held me very tight.

"Kote," she whispered into my shoulder. As if she were trying it on. As if she were trying to make herself believe it.

"Kote," I said.

And that was my name, for a very long time after.
