# Master Ash

The wall was old stone, roughly dressed, twelve feet high and topped with a coping of flat slate. It had been built to keep out thieves and to keep in privacy, and it was very good at the second and only moderately good at the first. I had spent three years on the rooftops of Tarbean. I had climbed the outside of Ambrose's rooms at the Golden Pony in the dark, with no rope, in the rain. A garden wall in Severen was not going to stop me.

I found a place where an old ivy vine had grown up the wall from the lane side, thick as my wrist, its roots cracking the mortar. I climbed it slowly, testing each handhold, making no sound. At the top I lay flat along the coping, in the shadow of an overhanging branch, and looked down into the garden.

It was beautiful. Bredon had not lied about that. It was a small garden, much smaller than the one behind the Maer's palace, but it was old in a way the palace gardens were not. The trees were ancient and gnarled, their branches twisted into strange shapes by centuries of wind. The paths were paved with mossy stone. There was a pool in the center, round and still and black, with water lilies floating on its surface. Around the edges, in the deep shadows under the trees, there were statues so old and weathered that I could not tell what they had once been meant to show.

And beside the pool, under a lantern hung from a low branch, there was a table and two chairs. A tak board sat on the table, the stones laid out in their bowls.

Bredon was sitting in one of the chairs. Denna was standing in front of him.

---

I could hear them. The garden was very quiet, and sound carried across the still water of the pool.

"—true?" Denna was saying. Her voice was shaking. "Just tell me. Is it true?"

Bredon looked up at her. His face was mild and kind and a little puzzled, the face of a grandfather whose granddaughter has come to him with a skinned knee.

"Is what true, my dear?"

"The Dellers. The farm near Aldermoor. The one you sent me to, to read the cloth." She took a breath. "They're dead. All of them. Burned in their house. And the mud was frozen, and the fire was blue, and—"

"Who told you this?"

"It doesn't matter who told me!"

"It matters a great deal." Bredon's voice was still gentle. "Sit down, Denna. You're upset. Sit, and have some wine, and tell me what you heard and who you heard it from."

"I don't want to sit. I don't want wine. I want you to tell me if it's true."

Bredon looked at her for a long moment. Then he sighed. It was a small, sad, tired sound.

"Yes," he said. "It's true. I heard the news three days ago. I'm very sorry. They were good people."

Denna stared at him. "You *knew*?"

"I knew they had died. I did not know how." He shook his head. "A fire, I was told. A terrible accident. I didn't want to tell you. I knew how it would upset you. You're so tender-hearted, my dear. It's one of the things I love most about you."

"It wasn't an accident."

"Wasn't it?"

"The fire was *blue*." Her voice cracked. "The mud was frozen. In spring. Those are the signs. The signs from the song. From *our* song. The one you've had me writing for three years." She stepped closer to him. "It was them. The Seven. You know it was them."

Bredon did not answer. He looked up at her with his mild, kind face, and he did not say anything at all.

"You sent me there," Denna whispered. "You sent me there to read the cloth. And then you sent *them*."

"No."

"Then who? Who sent them?"

"Nobody sends them, my dear. They go where they will." Bredon leaned back in his chair. "I sent you to read the cloth because it was important to the song. It contained a version of the story I had never seen before. A very old version, from before the lies. I needed to know what it said." He paused. "Others needed to know as well. Others who are not as gentle as I am. I did not tell them where to find it. But they have their ways of learning things."

"You're lying."

"I am not." His voice was very calm. "I have never lied to you, Denna. Not once in all the years I've known you. I have kept secrets. I have told you that there were things you were not ready to know. But I have never lied."

"Then tell me the truth now." She was crying. "Tell me what they are. Tell me what you are. Tell me what the song is *for*."

Bredon looked at her for a long time. Then he rose from his chair, leaning on his stick. He was taller than her. I had never noticed that before; he always seemed to stoop a little, the way old men do. But he did not stoop now. He stood straight, and he looked down at her.

"The song," he said quietly, "is for the truth. That is all it has ever been for. There is a man who was wronged, a very long time ago. His name was taken from him. His story was stolen and twisted and made into a lie, and the lie has been told so many times and for so long that it has become a prison. He cannot escape it. He cannot rest. He cannot even die." He paused. "Your song will set him free. That is what it's for."

"And the Dellers? Were they part of the lie?"

"Everyone who keeps the old story is part of the lie. Everyone who tells it, everyone who remembers it, everyone who writes it down." Bredon's voice was very gentle. "That is what makes it so strong. It lives in a thousand places. In books and songs and cloths and carvings. Every time someone tells it, it grows stronger. Every time someone forgets it, it grows weaker." He reached out and touched her cheek, very lightly. "Your song will be told a thousand times. And every time it is told, the lie will grow weaker. And one day, it will break."

Denna stood very still. She did not pull away from his hand.

"And the people who keep the old story?" she whispered. "What happens to them?"

Bredon took his hand away.

"Some stories have to end," he said, "so that others can begin."

---

I was shaking. I lay on top of the wall with my face pressed against the cold slate, and I was shaking so hard I was afraid they would hear it.

It was Bredon. Of course it was Bredon.

I had known. Some part of me had known since the steps of the Mains, since I had seen him in the snow behind Ambrose. Some part of me had known since the moment Devi put that page in my hand, with its hurried cramped writing. *He carries a staff of dark wood crowned in silver. He plays at games. He smiles.* I had known, and I had not let myself know. Because he had been kind to me. Because I had liked him. Because I had wanted, so badly, for there to be one old man in the world who looked at me and saw something worth teaching.

The Cthaeh had told me. It had told me in the Fae, in that terrible honeyed voice. *Her patron. The one she calls Master Ash.* It had told me that he beat her. It had laughed about it.

I had not believed it. Not really. I had told myself that the Cthaeh lied, that it twisted everything, that nothing it said could be trusted.

But it had not lied. It had only told me the truth in a way that would hurt me most.

And now, lying on the wall, I understood the shape of the board. Bredon had known I would come to Severen. He had known I would come to him, and play tak with him, and trust him. He had known about Denna, and the Dellers, and me. He had told me where to find her. He had known she would come here tonight, after I spoke to her. He had known I would follow.

He had known all of it. Every move. Every stone.

I was a piece on his board. I had always been.

---

"I want to stop," Denna said.

Bredon looked at her. "Stop what?"

"The song. All of it." She wrapped her arms around herself. "I don't want to write it anymore. I don't want to be part of this. I don't want anyone else to die because of me."

"No one has died because of you."

"The Dellers—"

"The Dellers died because they kept a lie. Not because of you." Bredon's voice was still gentle, but there was something underneath it now. Something hard. "You are very close, Denna. So very close. A few more verses, a few more sources, and the song will be finished. Everything we've worked for. Everything you've suffered for." He paused. "You can't stop now."

"I can. I will."

"You will not."

The words were very quiet. But they fell into the still garden like stones into the black pool, and the ripples spread out and out.

Denna lifted her chin. "Or what?"

Bredon looked at her for a long time. His face did not change. It was still mild and kind, still the face of a grandfather. But his eyes had changed. They had gone flat and cold and very, very old.

Then he reached out and took her wrist. The wrist with the knotted cord.

He did not grip it hard. I want to be clear about that. He did not twist it or wrench it or do any of the things I would have expected. He simply took it, gently, the way you might take a child's hand to lead them across a street. And he held it.

And Denna cried out.

It was not a loud sound. It was a small, choked, broken sound, like the sound a bird makes when it hits a window. Her knees buckled. She sagged against him, her face twisted in pain, and he held her up by the wrist, gently, and looked down at her with his kind grandfather's face.

"You will finish the song," he said softly. "Won't you, my dear?"

"Yes," she gasped. "Yes. Please."

"Good." He let go.

She fell to her knees on the mossy stones beside the pool. She knelt there, cradling her wrist against her chest, sobbing. The knotted cord on her wrist was glowing faintly, a dull red light, like an ember.

Bredon looked down at her for a moment. Then he turned and sat back down in his chair and picked up a white stone from the bowl on the tak board and turned it over in his fingers.

"You can come down now, Kvothe," he said. "I know you're there."

---

I did not decide to move. My body moved on its own, the way it had in the Cellarage when I ran at the Sleeper. One moment I was lying on the wall. The next I was in the garden, dropping the twelve feet to the mossy ground and rolling to my feet with Caesura already in my hand.

Denna looked up. She saw me. Her face went white with horror.

"No," she whispered. "Kvothe, no—"

I did not listen. I was across the garden in three strides. I was at the table. I had Caesura's point at Bredon's throat.

He did not move. He sat in his chair with the white stone in his fingers, and he looked up at me with his mild, kind face. The point of my sword was resting against the skin just below his jaw. A single push, and it would be over.

"Hello, Kvothe," he said. "I'm glad you came. Would you like to play?"

"Get up," I said. My voice did not sound like mine. It was very cold and very quiet. "Get away from her."

"I'm not near her. She's over there, by the pool. I'm here, at my table." He glanced at the tak board. "I've set up the stones. I thought we might have a game."

"I'm going to kill you."

"Are you?" He did not seem worried. "Well. That's certainly one way to end a game. Not a very beautiful one. But I suppose it's effective." He tilted his head, very slightly, so that the point of my sword pressed a little harder against his throat. A single bead of blood welled up on his skin, dark in the lantern light. "Go ahead. If you're going to do it."

I stood there. My hand was shaking. The point of my sword was trembling against his throat, and the bead of blood was growing, sliding down his neck and into the collar of his shirt.

I wanted to. God, I wanted to. I wanted it more than I have ever wanted anything. I wanted to push, and feel the steel slide into his throat, and watch his mild kind face go slack and empty. I wanted to kill him for the Dellers, and for Nell, and for my parents, and for every bruise he had ever left on Denna's skin.

But I did not.

Because Vashet had taught me. Because the Adem had taught me. Because I had spent months learning the Lethani, and I knew, standing there in that garden with my sword at an old man's throat, that this was not it. This was not the right action. This was not the right time. This was anger, and anger is not the Lethani. Anger is the opposite.

And because Denna was behind me, on her knees by the pool, saying *no, no, Kvothe, please, no*.

"Why?" I said. My voice cracked. "Why her? Why me?"

Bredon looked up at me. His face was still kind. But his eyes, those flat cold ancient eyes, were very steady.

"Put down the sword," he said, "and I'll tell you."

I did not move.

"Put down the sword, Kvothe. You're not going to use it. You know you're not. And you want to know. You've always wanted to know. It's your great weakness, your hunger for knowing. It's why you went down the stairs." He smiled faintly. "Put down the sword, and sit, and play with me. And I'll tell you everything you want to know."

I looked at him. At his kind face, and his cold eyes, and the thin line of blood running down his neck.

Then, slowly, I lowered my sword.

Behind me, Denna let out a long shaking breath.

Bredon smiled. He gestured to the empty chair across the table.

"Sit," he said. "Your move."
