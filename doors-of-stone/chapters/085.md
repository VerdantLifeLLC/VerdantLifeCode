# A Hanging in Renere

They hanged a man with red hair in the great square of Renere, at dawn, three days later.

There were ten thousand people there to see it. Maybe more. They packed the square from wall to wall, and filled the streets around it, and hung from the windows of every house that overlooked it. They stood on the rooftops. They climbed the statues of dead kings that lined the square's edges and clung to their stone shoulders. They had been gathering since midnight, some of them. They wanted a good view.

The gallows stood in the center of the square. It was a tall black frame of oak, very old, that had been standing in that place for longer than anyone could remember. It had hanged traitors and murderers and heretics and thieves. It had never hanged a kingkiller before. Kings of Vintas, it seemed, did not often get themselves killed.

The man with red hair was brought out of the palace at first light. He walked between four guards in grey, with his hands bound behind him and a black hood over his head. He walked slowly, a little unsteadily, the way a man walks who has been in a cell for a long time. The crowd went silent as he passed.

He climbed the steps of the gallows. The hangman, a big man in a black mask, put the noose around his neck over the hood. A priest in white stood beside him and read from a book.

And then the Penitent King came out onto a balcony of the palace, overlooking the square, and raised his hand.

And the trap fell.

---

I was not there.

I want to be very clear about that, since I have been clear about everything else. The man who hanged in the great square of Renere was not me. He had red hair, bright red, like a flame. He was about my height, and about my build. He wore my clothes, the clothes I had worn at my trial. He walked like a man who had been in a cell for a long time.

But he was not me. He was a man named Bren Colley. He had been a murderer. He had killed his wife and two of his children with a hatchet, in a farmhouse near the eastern border, and he had been condemned to hang for it a month before my trial. He had been waiting in a cell in the lower dungeons of the palace.

And four days before my trial, he had died of a fever.

---

I learned all of this later. Much later. I did not know it at the time. At the time, I was sitting in my cell in the Tower of Thorns, waiting to be hanged, and I did not care.

It was Stapes who told me. Years afterward, in a letter, written in his small precise hand. I have it still.

*It was her majesty's idea*, he wrote. *Or rather, it was the idea of a young woman of her acquaintance, a moneylender from Imre with spectacles and a very sharp tongue, who came to Renere with the army and refused to leave. She came to her majesty the night after the trial and said there must be a way. And her majesty said there was not, that the lords would never allow a pardon. And the young woman said, "Then don't pardon him. Hang him. Just not him."*

*She had heard, from a physician named Kelm, that there was a condemned man in the lower dungeons who had died of fever. His body had not yet been buried. It was being kept in the cold cellars until the proper forms could be completed.*

*She said: "Dye his hair. Put him in the Ruh's clothes. Put a hood over his head. No one will know."*

*Her majesty said: "They'll know. The man is dead. You cannot hang a dead man and make him walk to the gallows."*

*And the young woman smiled, sir, in a way I have never forgotten, and said: "Leave that to me."*

---

I do not know exactly what Devi did. She never told me. She said it was a trade secret, and that if she told me, she would have to charge me for it, and I could not afford her rates.

But I know some of it. I know that she spent two days in the cold cellars of the palace, with the dead man and her alchemical bench and a great many strange-smelling bottles. I know that when she came out, the dead man's hair was red. Bright red. The color of a flame.

And I know that on the morning of the hanging, when the four guards came to the cold cellars to fetch the prisoner, the dead man stood up.

He did not live. Devi was very clear about that, the one time she spoke of it. He was dead. He stayed dead. But she had found a way—some binding, some sympathy, something that I suspect was not taught at the University and was probably not legal anywhere in the Four Corners—to make his body move. For a little while. Enough to walk. Enough to climb the steps. Enough to stand on the trap with a noose around his neck and a hood over his face, and fall.

"It was very difficult," she said, and her face was pale when she said it. "I had to be close. In the crowd. Right at the foot of the gallows. Holding the binding the whole time. If anyone had jostled me, if I'd lost my concentration for a single heartbeat, he'd have collapsed on the steps like a puppet with its strings cut." She shuddered. "I didn't sleep for a span afterward. I kept seeing him. Walking. With his head in that hood."

"Devi," I said. "Thank you."

She looked at me. Her eyes were very serious behind her spectacles.

"Don't thank me," she said. "Just remember that you owe me. For that, and the page, and everything else." Her voice cracked slightly. "You owe me so much, Kvothe. You'll never be able to pay it back. Not in a hundred years."

"I know."

"Good." She wiped her eyes. "Good. As long as you know."

---

While the dead man walked to the gallows, I was walking somewhere else.

Down the back stair of the Tower of Thorns. The servants' stair, narrow and dark and winding, that the guards used to bring up the bread and water. I did not know it existed until that morning. I did not know anything.

I only knew that before dawn, the door of my cell had opened, and a small precise man in grey with a calm pleasant face had come in, carrying a lantern.

"Good morning, sir," he said. "If you would come with me, please. Quickly. And quietly."

I looked at him. I did not know him.

"Who are you?"

"Stapes, sir." He did not seem surprised that I did not know him. "His majesty's manservant. Formerly the Maer's. You and I have known each other some time." He held out his hand. "Please, sir. There isn't much time."

I did not move. I looked at him, standing in the doorway with his lantern.

"Where are we going?"

"Away, sir," said Stapes. "Far away. Somewhere you'll be safe."

"They're going to hang me. At dawn."

"They're going to hang someone, sir," said Stapes. His calm pleasant face did not change. "It won't be you."

---

He led me down the servants' stair. At the bottom there was a small door, very old, that opened onto a narrow passage between two walls. The passage led to another door, and another passage, and another. Stapes knew them all. He led me through the hidden ways of the palace the way he had once led me through the hidden ways of the Maer's palace in Severen, quickly and quietly and without hesitation.

At the end of the last passage there was a gate. A small iron gate, set into the outer wall of the palace, half hidden by a tangle of ivy. Stapes unlocked it with a key from his pocket and pushed it open.

Beyond it, there was a narrow lane. And in the lane, there was a cart.

It was a plain farm cart, with a canvas cover over its bed, pulled by a single brown horse. There was a man on the seat, in a battered hat, holding the reins.

And beside the cart, standing in the grey dawn light with her arms wrapped around herself, there was a woman with dark hair.

---

I stopped in the gateway. I looked at her.

She looked at me. Her face was very pale in the grey light. Her eyes were very dark.

The cord on my wrist began to hum.

"Hello," she said.

I did not know her. I did not know her name, or what she was to me, or why the sight of her face made the hole in my chest ache. I did not know anything.

But I knew the cord. And the cord knew her.

"Hello," I said.

She came across the lane toward me. She stopped in front of me, very close. She reached out and took my left hand, the one with the cord around its wrist, in both of hers.

"They let me go," she said. "Last night. The king did. He came to my rooms himself and unlocked the door and told me I was free." She looked down at the cord. "He said I should come here. He said you'd need someone. He said—" She stopped. "He said I'd know why."

"Do you?"

She looked up at me. Her eyes were wet.

"Yes," she said. "I know why."

---

Stapes cleared his throat.

"If you please, sir," he said. "Madam. The hanging is at dawn. When it's done, the whole city will be in the square. That's when you go. Out the south gate, with the crowds coming in the other way. No one will look twice at a farm cart." He glanced at the man on the seat. "Tessit knows the way."

The man on the seat touched his battered hat. "Morning," he said. "One family, under the sky."

I did not know what that meant. But something in me stirred when he said it. The way a sleeping dog stirs at a familiar voice.

"Where are we going?" I said.

"West," said Tessit. "For now. Out of Vintas. Into the Commonwealth. After that—" He shrugged. "After that, wherever you like. The roads go everywhere."

I looked at Stapes. He was standing in the gateway, with his lantern in his hand, looking at me with his calm, pleasant face.

"Thank you, Stapes," I said.

I did not know why I said it. I did not know him. But the words came out of me the way they had come out on the doorstep of the Lackless house, and in the Tower of Thorns, and in the great hall. From somewhere down at the bottom.

Stapes looked at me for a long moment. His calm pleasant face did something I had never seen it do before. It crumpled, just for a moment, like paper in a fist. Then it smoothed out again.

"You're very welcome, sir," he said. His voice was not quite steady. "It has been an honor. A very great honor." He bowed. A deep bow, the kind you give a lord. "Safe travels, sir. Wherever you go."

He stepped back through the gate. The gate closed. The key turned in the lock.

And he was gone.

---

We waited in the lane, the woman and I, until the bells began to ring.

They rang all over the city. Every church, every tower, every bell in Renere, ringing together. A great slow tolling, like the tolling for the old king's death. It went on and on.

And then, from the great square, far away, I heard the crowd.

It was a sound like the sea. Ten thousand voices, rising all at once. Not a cheer. Not a scream. Something in between. A great wordless roar, rising up over the rooftops of the city and hanging there, in the grey dawn air.

And then silence.

The woman beside me had her hand pressed over her mouth. She was shaking.

"That's it," she whispered. "That's it. It's done. They think you're dead."

I looked up at the grey sky. At the grey rooftops. At the grey bells, still ringing.

*They think you're dead.*

I did not feel anything. I did not know what I should feel. I was a grey man in a grey lane, and somewhere in a great square a dead man with red hair was swinging from a rope, and ten thousand people thought he was me.

It did not seem to matter very much.

"Get in," said Tessit, from the seat of the cart. "Both of you. In the back, under the canvas. Now, while everyone's in the square."

We got in. We lay down in the bed of the cart, under the canvas, on a pile of straw and old blankets. The woman lay beside me. She was still holding my hand.

The cart began to move.

---

We went out through the south gate of Renere an hour later, with the crowds pouring in the other way. People were coming into the city from every village and farm for miles around, to see the kingkiller's body hanging in the square. They filled the road, a great slow river of people, and the cart moved against them, slowly, patiently, like a boat against the current.

No one looked twice at a farm cart.

I lay in the straw under the canvas and listened to them pass. Thousands of voices. Talking. Laughing. Arguing. And every so often, one word, rising above the rest.

*Kingkiller.*

*They hanged the kingkiller.*

*Did you see him? Red hair. Bright red. Like fire.*

*I heard he was seven feet tall.*

*I heard he killed the king with a song.*

*I heard he was a demon.*

*I heard he sang the true story of Lanre, and the Chandrian came to kill him.*

*I heard he wasn't really dead.*

The woman beside me was crying. Silently. Her tears were running down her face and into the straw. She was holding my hand so tight it hurt.

I did not cry. I did not know how. I lay there in the dark under the canvas and listened to the voices, telling stories about a man I did not remember, with a name I did not know.

And I thought, very faintly, from somewhere far down at the bottom of the hole:

*That's the end of him, then.*

*Whoever he was.*
