# What Orla Found

Orla sent for me four days later.

I did not expect it. I had not been back to the Mill Race since the morning Denna left. I had spent those four days in the Maer's library and the Maer's gardens and the Maer's wine cellar, mostly the last, turning the shape of Bredon's game over and over in my mind and finding, every time, that I had lost. I did not want to see the room where Denna had stayed. I did not want to see Orla's face, like thunder, asking me what I had done.

But a boy came to the palace gate on the fourth morning, a skinny redheaded potboy with flour on his apron, and asked for "the musician with the red hair, what was sweet on the harp girl." And the guards, who had heard a great deal about me by then and liked very little of it, sent him up to my rooms with a look that said they hoped he was a debt collector.

"Missus Orla says come," said the boy. "Says she's found something. Says don't dawdle."

---

Orla was in the courtyard of the Mill Race when I got there, under the pear tree, sitting on the bench where Denna and I had sat, and played, and not quite said the things we wanted to say. The blossoms were gone now. The tree was green and ordinary. Orla had a basket of mending in her lap, and she did not look up when I came through the gate.

"Sit," she said.

I sat. On the other end of the bench, as far from her as I could get.

She went on mending for a while. A man's shirt, torn at the elbow. Her needle went in and out, very quick, very neat. She was a big woman, broad in the shoulders and hips, with grey hair scraped back into a knot and forearms like a baker's. Her face, when it was not like thunder, was plain and tired and not unkind.

"I had the room turned out," she said at last. "Hers. Yesterday. For the next guest. Merchant's wife from Tinuë, coming up the river." She pulled her thread tight. "Girl I had doing it found something. Under the mattress. Pushed right in, between the ropes and the ticking, where you'd never find it unless you took the whole bed apart. Which we did, because the ropes wanted tightening."

She set down her mending. She reached into the pocket of her apron and took out something and held it out to me without looking at me.

It was a cord. A short length of dark cord, braided from three strands, about as long as my forearm. And tied along it, one after another, a row of small, neat knots.

---

I took it. My hands were not steady.

"I can't read it," said Orla. "Can't read anything, much. Certainly not knots." She picked up her mending again. "But I know what it is. My grandmother was from Yll. She used to tie letters like that, for her sister in Rannish. Said it was better than ink. Said ink could lie, but a knot couldn't." She glanced at me, sidelong. "You can read it. Can't you? She taught you. I saw you, the pair of you, under this tree. Her tying and you frowning."

"Yes," I said.

"Well, then." Orla went back to her needle. "Read it. I'll not look."

---

I read it.

It took me a long time. Denna's knots were very small and very tight, and some of them I did not know. Yllish knot-script is not like letters. It is more like music. The same knot can mean different things depending on the knots around it, and the tension of the cord, and whether it is tied over or under. Denna had taught me the basics, at the Grey Man, and in this courtyard, under the pear tree. But she knew a great deal more than she had taught me.

But I could read enough.

*K*, the first knot said. The knot she used for my name. A small, neat, looping knot, like a flame.

And then:

*Forgive me.*

*He will kill you if I do not take it. He told me so. He was smiling when he said it.*

*I will keep it safe. Safer than he thinks. I know things about knots that he does not know I know.*

*Do not follow me. Please. If you follow me, he will know that you know, and he will stop being patient.*

*I am doing this so you will not have to.*

And then, at the very end, a knot I did not know. A complicated one, looped over and under itself three times, with a small twist at the end. I turned it over in my fingers for a long time, trying to read it. I could not.

---

I sat on the bench under the pear tree with the cord in my hands and did not say anything for a long time. Orla mended her shirt. The mill wheels turned in the distance, with their slow thunder. A cat walked across the courtyard, very dignified, and disappeared under the gate.

"Well?" said Orla at last. "What's it say?"

I told her. Not all of it. But enough.

Orla did not say anything for a while. She pulled her thread through, and knotted it, and bit it off with her teeth.

"I thought it'd be something like that," she said.

I looked at her.

"She came in that night looking like a ghost," said Orla. "I told you. And went out. And came back looking worse." She folded the shirt in her lap, very neatly. "I've seen that look before. On a girl's face. In a mirror, mostly." She did not look at me. "When I was twenty I was married to a man in Tinuë who kept a tavern by the docks. He was very charming. Everyone said so. Very kind. Very generous. Very clever with his hands." She smoothed the folded shirt. "And one night he told me, very kindly, what he'd do to my sister if I ever left him. Smiling. The whole time."

The mill wheels turned. The pear tree rustled.

"I left anyway," said Orla. "Three years later. In the night. Took my sister with me, and his strongbox, and never went back." She looked at me at last. Her face was very plain and very tired. "That's the look. The look of a girl who's been told what'll happen to someone she loves. By a man who smiles."

---

"Why didn't you tell me?" I said. "The morning she left. You asked me what I'd done to her."

"Because I thought it was you." Orla shrugged her broad shoulders. "The smiling man. I didn't know you. You were young, and clever, and very charming, and the Maer's pet besides. I thought, *there he is. That's the one.*" She looked at me steadily. "I was wrong. I'm sorry for it. But I'd think it again, in the same place. Better to think it and be wrong."

"Yes," I said. "I suppose it is."

She looked at the cord in my hands.

"That last knot," she said. "The one you can't read."

"Yes?"

"My grandmother used to tie that one." Orla's voice had changed. Softer. "At the end of her letters to her sister. I asked her once what it meant. She said it didn't mean anything, exactly. It wasn't a word. It was more like—" She frowned, searching for it. "Like a hand. Holding. She said you tie it at the end when there's something you can't say in words. When you need the other person to know you're holding on to them, even if you're far away." She looked at me. "She said it was the oldest knot there is. Older than letters. Older than Yll."

I looked down at the knot. Looped over and under itself three times, with a small twist at the end.

A hand. Holding.

---

I did not follow her. I want that on the record. I wanted to, more than anything. I sat in my rooms in the Maer's palace that night with the cord in my hands and a map of Vintas spread out in front of me, and I worked out every road she could have taken, and every town she could have reached, and every place she might have gone to ground. I could have found her. I am almost sure of it.

But I did not go. Because she had asked me not to. Because she had tied it into a cord, where it could not lie. *If you follow me, he will stop being patient.*

It was the first time in my life I did what Denna asked me to. It was very nearly the last.

---

"Do you still have it?" said Chronicler. "The cord?"

"No." The innkeeper looked at the fire. "I gave it back to her. Later. Much later. In Imre, the night before she left for the last time." He was quiet for a moment. "She looked at it for a long time. At the last knot. And then she said, *You kept it.* And I said, *You asked me to hold on.*"

He did not say anything else for a while. Chronicler waited.

"She tied that knot again, you know," said the innkeeper at last. "Once more. At the end." He put his hand into the pocket of his apron. Chronicler did not need to see what was in it. He had seen the innkeeper's hand go there a hundred times in three days. "She sent it to me by a tinker. A single knot, on a short length of cord made of her hair." He did not take it out. "I didn't know what it was, when he gave it to me. I only knew that when I held it, something in my chest stopped hurting."

"The same knot," said Chronicler slowly. "Looped three times. With a twist at the end."

"The same knot." The innkeeper took his hand out of his pocket. It was empty. "A hand. Holding." He looked at the scribe. "The oldest knot there is."
