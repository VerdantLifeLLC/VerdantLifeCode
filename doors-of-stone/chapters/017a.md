# The Stable

I played at the Eolian the night before the Nighting. I did not know it would be the last time I played there for a very long time. You never know which time is the last. If you did, you would play differently.

Or perhaps you would not. Perhaps you would play exactly the same, because that is the only way you know how to play. I have thought about it a great deal, and I have never decided.

---

It was a slow night. Half the students were already across the river preparing for the festival, and the other half were saving their money for it, and the Eolian was barely a third full. A fiddler played early in the evening, and a pair of sisters who sang harmony, and a very old man with a hurdy-gurdy who played the same four songs he had been playing at the Eolian for thirty years and was applauded each time as if the songs were new.

I played late. When the crowd had thinned even further and the candles on the tables had burned down to stubs. I played "Tinker Tanner" for the few who were left, and a sad Modegan song about a woman waiting at a window, and a song of my own that I had never played for anyone, a song about a girl sitting on the roof of a building with a candle in her hand. I did not say who the girl was. No one asked.

When I finished, there were perhaps a dozen people left in the great round room. They applauded, quietly, the way people applaud at the end of a long night. And then they gathered their cloaks and went out into the cold, and the Eolian was empty except for me, and Deoch at the door, and Stanchion behind the bar.

"Stay a bit," said Stanchion. "Have a drink. On the house."

I stayed.

---

He poured two glasses of something dark and sweet from a bottle he kept under the bar, a bottle I had never seen him pour for anyone. He came around the bar and sat down at a table near the stage, and I sat across from him, and Deoch came and sat with us, and for a while nobody said anything.

The Eolian was very quiet. Very dim. The candles were guttering. The stage was empty. The balconies circling above were dark. It is a strange thing, an empty hall at night, after music. It seems to hold the music still, somehow, in the air. As if the notes have not quite finished fading.

"Twenty-two years," said Stanchion at last.

I looked at him.

"Twenty-two years I've owned this place," he said. He turned his glass slowly on the table. "Twenty-two years this spring. Did I ever tell you how I got it?"

"You said it was a stable."

"It was a stable." Stanchion smiled. It was a slow smile, under his beard, and it made his big face look younger. "A livery stable. Owned by a man named Horn. He kept thirty horses in here, and a smithy out back, and he let the place go to rot. The roof leaked. The walls were cracked. There was horse dung on the floor three inches deep, and rats in the rafters as big as cats." He laughed softly. "I was nineteen. I'd just come to Imre from the Small Kingdoms with two talents in my pocket and a lute I couldn't play."

"You played?"

"Badly. Very badly. Worse than the hurdy-gurdy man." Stanchion drank from his glass. "I wanted to be a musician. More than anything. I'd heard stories about Imre, back home. About the University, and the musicians, and the halls where they played for lords and ladies. I thought I'd come here and be famous." He shook his head. "I lasted three months. Played in every tavern that would have me. Nobody wanted to hear me twice."

"What happened?"

"I ran out of money. And I was sleeping rough, in doorways, and one night it rained, and I came in here to get out of it. Horn didn't mind. He let anyone sleep in the loft if they mucked out a stall in the morning." He looked around the great round room. "I lay up there in the straw that night, listening to the rain on the roof, and I looked down at the stalls and the horses and the dung, and I thought, *this is a good room*."

Deoch laughed. "You thought a stable was a good room."

"I did." Stanchion was not embarrassed. "It was round, you see. Horn had built it round, for some reason. Round walls, round roof. And when it rained, the sound went all around you. Like being inside a drum." He was quiet for a moment. "And I thought, if you took out the stalls, and cleaned the floor, and put a stage in the middle, this would be the best room for music in the world."

---

"So you bought it," I said.

"Not then. I didn't have two pennies to rub together." Stanchion smiled. "I worked for Horn. Mucking out stalls. Shoeing horses. Two years. And every night I lay up in that loft and looked down at the room and planned. Where the stage would go. Where the bar would go. How high the balconies should be, so the sound would carry." He laughed softly. "I used to go to the Pony and the Grey Man and every other hall in Imre, and stand at the back, and listen. Not to the music. To the room. How the sound moved. Where it went dead. Where it rang."

"And then?"

"And then Horn died. Fell off a horse, drunk, and broke his neck. And his widow wanted nothing to do with the place. She wanted to sell it to a butcher who was going to tear it down and build a slaughterhouse." Stanchion's face went grim for a moment. "I went to her. I begged. I had eleven talents saved from two years of mucking stalls. It wasn't a tenth of what the butcher was offering."

"What did she say?"

Stanchion looked at me. His eyes were bright in the candlelight.

"She said, *Can you play?* And I said, *No. Not well.* And she said, *Then what do you want a music hall for?*" He was quiet for a moment. "And I said, *Because I can't play. And I want to spend the rest of my life listening to people who can.*"

The empty hall was very quiet.

"She sold it to me for eleven talents," said Stanchion. "And a promise. That I'd never turn away a musician who was hungry. That I'd always give them a meal and a place to sleep, whether they could play or not." He smiled. "I've kept that promise for twenty-two years."

---

Deoch was looking at him with an expression I had never seen on Deoch's face before. Something soft. Something almost like reverence.

"You never told me that," he said. "About the widow."

"You never asked." Stanchion drank. "Nobody ever asks. They just see the Eolian, and the stage, and the pipes, and they think it's always been here. Like the river. Like the bridge." He looked around the room. "They don't know I built every stone of that stage myself. Carried them up from the quarry on my back, two at a time. They don't know I carved every rail on those balconies. They don't know I sat up in that loft for two years, planning."

"I know now," I said.

Stanchion looked at me. Then he smiled.

"You do," he said. "You do. And you'll remember it. You've got that look. The look of someone who remembers things." He reached across the table and put his big hand over mine. "That's why I told you. So someone would remember."

---

We sat there for a long time after that, the three of us, in the dim candlelight, drinking Stanchion's dark sweet wine. Deoch told a story about a lute player from Atur who had come to the Eolian twenty years ago and tried to win his pipes by playing with his feet. Stanchion told a story about the night the old Earl of Tarsh had got so drunk in the second balcony that he had fallen into the orchestra pit and landed on a cellist. I told them a story about a troupe of Ruh who had tried to put on a play in a village in the Small Kingdoms and been chased out of town by a goat.

We laughed a great deal. It was a good night. One of the best.

And then, very late, Stanchion looked at me across the table and said, "What are you doing tomorrow night, Kvothe?"

I went very still.

"Tomorrow night?"

"The Nighting. Everyone's going across the river for the festival." He looked at me. "But you're not. I can see it in your face. You've got something planned."

I did not answer.

Stanchion looked at me for a long moment. Then he nodded, very slowly, as if I had told him something.

"Whatever it is," he said, "be careful."

"I will."

"No. You won't. You've never been careful in your life." He smiled, very faintly. "But try. For me. The Eolian needs you. Somebody's got to play here when I'm too old to listen."

"You'll never be too old to listen."

"Everyone gets too old for something." Stanchion stood up. He gathered up the glasses and the bottle. He looked down at me. "Go home, Kvothe. Get some sleep. Whatever you're doing tomorrow, you'll want your wits about you."

---

I went home. I did not sleep much.

But I remember walking back across the bridge, in the cold clear dark, with my lute on my back and Stanchion's dark wine warm in my stomach. I remember stopping at the top of the arch and looking back at Imre, at the lights in the windows, and the dark round shape of the Eolian among the other rooftops.

I remember thinking that it was a good room. The best room for music in the world.

And I remember thinking that one day, when I was very old and very famous and very tired, I would come back to the Eolian and sit at a table near the stage with Stanchion, both of us grey-haired, and listen to some young musician play. And I would tell him that I remembered. About the stable, and the widow, and the eleven talents.

I did not know, standing on the bridge that night, that Stanchion had less than a year to live.

I did not know that I would be the reason.

---

I have told you that he died on the second balcony of the Eolian, the night I sang *Tariniel*, with his beard gone white and his face gone grey, standing between Usnea and the cellar stairs with two empty buckets in his hands.

I have told you what he said to her. *Not in my place.*

I did not tell you what he said to her after that. The thing I could not hear, from the stage, over the noise of the fire and the screaming. The thing that made Usnea tilt her head, and pause, and look at him with something in her pale face that might have been surprise.

Deoch told me, later. He had been on the stairs below, close enough to hear.

"He said, *There's a girl in the cellar,*" Deoch told me. His voice was very rough. "*She's holding up my walls. You leave her be.*" He was quiet for a moment. "And then he said, *I built this place from a stable. With my own two hands. You can have me. But you can't have her, and you can't have my walls.*"

I did not say anything. I could not.

"And that thing—that woman—she looked at him," said Deoch. "For a long time. With her hand on his chest. And then she took her hand away, and he fell, and she didn't go down the stairs." He looked at me. "She didn't go down, Kvothe. She stood there, and she listened to you sing, and she didn't go down." His voice cracked. "I think he talked her out of it. I think the old bastard talked a Chandrian out of it."

---

The walls of the Eolian stood, afterward. You know that. They stood through the fire, blackened and scorched and cracked, but standing. And Deoch rebuilt the inside of it, stone by stone, the way Stanchion had built it the first time.

He told me, years later, in a letter, that when he was clearing the rubble out of the cellar, he found something carved into one of the foundation stones. Low down, near the floor, where no one would ever see it. Very old. Very worn. Carved with a knife by someone who did not know much about carving.

It was a date. Twenty-two years before. And beneath it, three words.

*A good room.*
