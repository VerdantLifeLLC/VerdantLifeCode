# The Tower of Thorns

They put me in the Tower of Thorns.

It is the oldest part of the royal palace at Renere, older than the great grey walls and the heavy battlements and the squat towers that make the palace look like a toad. It is a narrow round tower of black stone, very tall, rising out of the center of the palace like a spike. There are no windows in its lower half. In its upper half, there are only slits, too narrow for a cat to crawl through. It has been a prison for as long as there has been a kingdom of Vintas, and before that, the stories say, it was something worse.

It is called the Tower of Thorns because of what grows on it. A vine, very old, thick as a man's arm at its base, that climbs the black stone all the way to the top. It has no leaves. It has no flowers. It has only thorns, long and black and sharp as needles, so many of them that the whole tower seems to bristle like the back of an angry cat.

They say that no one has ever escaped from the Tower of Thorns. They say that the vine was planted by the first king of Vintas, and that it was grown from a seed brought out of the Fae, and that it knows when a prisoner tries to climb down, and closes around him like a fist.

I do not know if that is true. I never tried.

---

My cell was near the top. A small round room, with a stone floor and stone walls and a single slit of a window, high up, that let in a thin grey knife of light at midday and nothing at all the rest of the time. There was a pallet of straw in one corner. A bucket in another. A heavy iron door with a small barred grate in it, at head height, through which the guards could look.

They took off my chains when they put me in. Not out of kindness. There was nowhere to go.

They took the woman somewhere else. I did not know where. They took her away at the foot of the tower, and she fought them, and shouted, and tried to hold on to my hand. But there were too many of them. They pulled her away. The last thing I saw of her was her face, grey and wet, turning back to look at me as they dragged her down a corridor.

"*I'll find a way*," she shouted. "*I'll find a way, I promise, I'll—*"

A door closed, and I did not hear the rest.

---

I do not know how long I was in the Tower of Thorns. A long time. Months. The seasons turned outside my narrow window. The grey knife of light that came in at midday grew shorter, and then longer, and then shorter again. It was autumn when they put me in. It was spring, I think, when they took me out.

I do not remember most of it.

I remember the grey. Always the grey. The grey stone walls and the grey stone floor and the grey light and the grey bread they pushed through the slot at the bottom of the door once a day. The grey water in the grey cup. The grey faces of the guards, looking through the grate.

I remember sitting. For hours. For days. On the straw pallet, with my back against the wall and my knees drawn up and my arms around them. Not thinking. Not feeling. Not anything. Just sitting, in the grey, the way a stone sits at the bottom of a pond.

I remember the cord.

It was always there, on my wrist. The guards had tried to take it off, the first day. One of them had grabbed my arm and tried to cut it with his knife. The knife had slid off the cord as if it were made of iron. He had tried again. The same. He had tried to untie it. His fingers could not find the ends.

He had stared at it for a long time. Then he had let go of my arm, very quickly, and backed away, and made a sign against evil with his fingers, and left the cell. None of the guards touched it after that.

I touched it. Often. It was the only thing I did touch, in those months. I would sit on my pallet and hold my left wrist in my right hand and run my fingers over the knots. Over and over. Feeling them. Their shapes. Their loops and twists and doublings-back.

And sometimes, when I touched them, I felt something. Very faint. A voice in another room. A word I almost remembered. A face I almost knew.

It never lasted. It was like trying to hold water in your hands. But it was something.

---

I remember the dreams.

I did not dream at first. Or if I did, I did not remember. But later, after a month or two, I began to dream. And they were not grey.

They were full of color. Colors so bright they hurt. The green of new leaves in spring. The red of a fire. The blue of the sky at dusk. The gold of sunlight on a white stone city.

I dreamed of a white city on the last day of its life. I dreamed of towers and gardens and fountains full of children. I dreamed of a woman singing in a great hall.

I dreamed of a fire in the woods, and a wagon burning, and a man with black eyes crouching in front of me.

I dreamed of a girl with dark hair, sitting under a pear tree with blossoms falling around her like snow.

I dreamed of a door.

And every time I woke, the colors drained away, and the world was grey again. And I did not know what I had dreamed. Only that it had been beautiful, and that it was gone.

---

People came to see me. Not many. Not often.

The first was an old woman. Very small and very wrinkled, with bright eyes and a cat in her arms. She came to the grate in the door one afternoon and looked in at me.

"Well," she said. "You've made a fine mess of things, haven't you?"

I looked at her. I did not know her.

"Lady Tamsin," she said. "Tamsin Aerwen. I helped you get in to see the old king. Before." She peered at me through the bars. "You don't remember me."

"No."

"Hm." She looked at me for a long moment. Her bright eyes were very sharp. "They said you'd gone mad. After. That something happened to you in that cellar, and you came out with your wits scrambled." She tilted her head. "That's not it, though. Is it? That's not madness. I've seen madness. That's something else."

I did not answer. I did not know how.

"You look," said Lady Tamsin slowly, "like a man who's been emptied out. Like a jar someone's poured the wine out of." She stroked her cat. "What did they do to you, boy?"

"Nothing," I said. "I did it."

She looked at me for a long time. Then she nodded, slowly, as if I had told her something important.

"I'll come back," she said. "When I can. It's not easy. The duke doesn't like visitors." She smiled faintly. "But I'm very old, and very rich, and I know where a great many bodies are buried. Some of them literally." She turned to go. Then she stopped. "Oh. I nearly forgot. There's someone asking after you. A physician. Kelm. They let him out of the dungeons a month ago, when they couldn't find anything to charge him with." She paused. "He says to tell you he's sorry. That he couldn't do more. For the old king."

"I don't know who that is."

"No," said Lady Tamsin gently. "No, I don't suppose you do." She was quiet for a moment. "I'll tell him you said thank you anyway."

---

She did come back. Many times. Every span or two, she came to the grate in the door and talked to me. She told me things. News from the court. News from the kingdom. She did not seem to mind that I did not understand most of it, or that I rarely answered. She talked the way old women talk to a sick child, or a cat. For the comfort of talking.

I learned, from her, that the kingdom had gone to war with itself.

Duke Jakis had proclaimed himself regent, the moment he returned to Renere with his son's body. He said that there was no heir, and that someone must rule until one could be found, and that he, as the father of the murdered king, was the natural choice.

Half the lords of Vintas had agreed with him. The other half had not.

"They don't trust him," said Lady Tamsin. "Never did. Too ambitious. Too grasping. And there are rumors now. About the old man, the one who always stood behind him. About the deaths. The four heirs, and the old king, and the prince." She shook her head. "People are saying it wasn't bad luck. People are saying it was murder. And people are saying the duke knew."

"Did he?"

Lady Tamsin looked at me through the bars. It was the first question I had asked her.

"I don't know," she said. "I don't think so. Not all of it. I think the old man used him the way he used his son." She was quiet for a moment. "But it doesn't matter, does it? What matters is what people believe. And people believe the duke's hands are dirty."

"What will happen?"

"War," said Lady Tamsin. "It's already happening. The lords who won't follow the duke have gone south. To the hills. To your Maer." She smiled faintly. "He's gathering them. All the ones who hate the duke, and the ones who loved the old king, and the ones who just want to be on the winning side. He's got an army again. Not as big as the duke's. But growing."

"The Maer."

"Alveron. Your Maer. The one who sent you here." She peered at me. "You don't remember him either, do you?"

"No."

"Hm." She stroked her cat. "Well. He remembers you. He's been sending letters. To the duke, to the council, to anyone who'll listen. Demanding your release. Saying you saved his wife's life. Saying you're a hero, not a murderer." She shook her head. "Nobody listens, of course. He's a rebel now. A traitor. No one listens to traitors." She paused. "Until they win."

---

The second visitor was a physician.

He was small and grey and thin, with spectacles on the end of his nose and ink-stained fingers. He came with Lady Tamsin once, in the winter, and stood at the grate beside her, and looked in at me for a long time without saying anything.

Then he said, "May I come in?"

The guards let him. I do not know what Lady Tamsin paid them. He came into my cell and knelt beside my pallet and looked at me. He looked at my face, and my eyes, and my hands. He took my wrist—my right wrist, not the left—and held it and felt my pulse. He looked at the cord on my left wrist for a long time, without touching it.

"Remarkable," he said at last. His voice was very quiet. "Remarkable. You're healthy. Thin, but healthy. Your heart is strong. Your lungs are clear. There's nothing wrong with you at all." He looked into my eyes. "And yet there's no one home."

"I know," I said.

He looked at me for a long moment. Then he sat back on his heels.

"I studied at the University," he said. "A long time ago. I knew a man there. A master. Elodin." He was quiet for a moment. "When I was a student, he was in Haven. He'd done something to himself. Something to his name. And for three years, he looked exactly like you do now." He looked at me. "Empty. Grey. Like a house with all the lamps put out."

"Did he get better?"

"Yes. Eventually." Kelm was quiet for a moment. "He found his way back. Or found a new way. I was never sure which." He looked at the cord on my wrist. "Is that where it is? Your name?"

I looked down at the cord. At the knots, glowing very faintly in the grey light.

"Yes," I said.

Kelm let out a long breath.

"Then you're not lost," he said. "Not really. You're just—put away. Like a book on a shelf." He smiled faintly. "Someone only has to take you down and open you."

He stood up. He looked down at me for a long moment.

"I'm sorry," he said. "For the king. For all of it. I couldn't do more."

"You did enough," I said.

I did not know why I said it. I did not know who he was, or what he had done. But the words came out of me the way they had come out on the doorstep of the Lackless house. From somewhere below the grey. From somewhere down at the bottom, where the truth lives.

Kelm looked at me. His eyes, behind his spectacles, were wet.

"Thank you," he said.

---

The third visitor did not come through the door.

I was lying on my pallet one night, late, in the deep of the winter. The cell was very dark and very cold. There was frost on the inside of the walls. I could not sleep. I was lying on my back, looking up at the dark ceiling, holding my left wrist in my right hand, running my fingers over the knots.

And I heard something at the window.

A scratching. Soft and quick. Like claws on stone.

I sat up. I looked at the window, the narrow slit high in the wall, black against the black sky.

There was something in it. Something small and dark, pressed into the slit. I could see its eyes, glinting.

Deep blue.

"Reshi," said a voice.

It was very soft. Very quiet. But I heard it. And the cord on my wrist hummed.

"Reshi. It's me. It's Bast." A pause. "You don't remember me. I know. That's all right. I remember you."

I stared at the window. I did not say anything.

"I've been trying to get in for weeks," said the voice. "The vine. The thorns. They're Fae. Old Fae. They don't like me. They keep closing up whenever I try to climb." A soft laugh, very tired. "I'm covered in scratches. I look like I've been fighting cats."

"Why?" I said.

The eyes in the window blinked.

"Why what?"

"Why are you trying to get in?"

There was a long silence.

"Because you're in here," said Bast at last. "And I said I'd find you. And I did." A pause. "That's all. That's the whole reason."

I looked at the eyes in the window. At the small dark shape pressed into the slit.

"I don't know you," I said.

"I know."

"I don't know who I am."

"I know that too." The voice was very gentle. "It's all right. I know who you are. I'll remember for both of us. Until you can remember yourself."

The cord on my wrist was humming. Very softly. The way a string hums when another string nearby is plucked.

"Will you come back?" I said.

The eyes in the window blinked again.

"Every night," said Bast. "Every single night. Until they let you out."

---

He did. Every night. For the rest of that winter.

He never came into the cell. He could not; the slit was too narrow, even for a fox, even for a cat, even for whatever small dark shape he had made himself into. But he came to the window. Every night, late, when the guards were asleep. He pressed himself into the slit and looked in at me with his deep blue eyes, and he talked.

He told me stories. About the twilight, and his father's court, and the tree-palace by the dark river. About a mortal woman who had been his mother, and a shepherd, and two half-siblings he had never met. About a silver forest and a white birch wood and a net of silver silk. About a red-haired man who had stepped in front of five arrows.

He told me about the others. The ones who had gone down the cellar stair. They had all made it out, through the tunnel, into the hills. Meluan was with the Maer now, weak but alive, her hair gone white as snow. Sim had lost his left eye; the scrael's leg had cut it through. He wore a patch now, and wrote poems about it, very bad ones, that made everyone laugh. Wil had taken charge of the Maer's arcanists, what few there were, and was teaching them how to use sympathy in war. Devi was making more Surprises. Fela had gone back to the University, to Elodin, to learn more about stone.

"She says she's going to find a way," said Bast. "To help you. She says there has to be a way." A pause. "She says she wants her stone back."

I did not know what he meant. But I found that my hand had gone to my pocket, where there was a small flat stone, worn smooth. I held it. It was cold.

It said *no*. Very faintly. I could barely hear it.

But I heard it.
