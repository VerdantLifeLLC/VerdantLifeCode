# The Scribe on the Road

The rest, you know.

You were there for it. Or for the end of it. But I will tell it anyway, because I promised to tell the whole story. And because it is the last piece, the one that brings us here, to this table, to this night. And a story should always be told to its end.

---

The autumn after Shep died, things got worse.

More scrael. More of them than ever before. Not one here and one there, now, but three and four and five at a time. They came out of the woods at night and killed sheep and cattle and dogs. They killed a traveler on the Rannish road. They scratched at the doors of houses at the edge of town, and the people inside lay awake all night with their children in their arms, listening.

The people of Newarre did not know what they were. They called them spiders. They said they had come down from the mountains, because of the war, or the hard winters, or the anger of God. The priest preached sermons about sin. The men of the town took to carrying cudgels and pitchforks when they walked out at night.

I went out every night. With iron. Alone, mostly. Bast came sometimes, but more and more he did not. He stayed in the inn, in his room at the back, and I heard him talking to someone who was not there.

I did not ask him about it. I did not know how.

---

There were soldiers, too, that autumn. Deserters from the war in the north, some of them. Men who had been sent to fight the rebels and had decided they would rather not, and who were drifting south along the roads in twos and threes, stealing what they needed and taking what they wanted.

Two of them came to the Waystone near the end of autumn. You were there. You saw.

They were big men, hard-faced, with the grey of the Penitent King on their coats and swords at their hips. They came in out of the rain one night and sat at the bar and drank, and did not pay, and when I asked them to pay, they laughed at me. And when I asked again, they hit me.

I did not fight back.

You saw that. You were sitting at the table by the hearth, with your pen and your papers, and you saw me stand behind the bar and let them hit me. Once, twice, a dozen times. You saw me fall, and get up, and fall again. You saw me not raise my hands.

You wondered why. I know you did. I saw it in your face.

I will tell you.

It was not because I could not fight. My body still knew how. It had killed a king, and a dozen scrael, and it could have killed two drunk deserters without breaking a sweat.

It was because I did not want to.

That is the truth. I stood behind my bar and let them beat me because some part of me, very far down, wanted it. Wanted the pain. Wanted to be broken. Wanted something, anything, to reach down into the grey and make me feel.

And because, if I am honest—and I am being honest, tonight, for the first time in a very long time—some part of me wanted them to finish it. To end it. To give me what Haliax wanted, and could not have.

Rest.

---

They did not finish it. You know that too. Bast came out of the kitchen and saw what they were doing, and his face changed, and his eyes went black, and he did something I will not describe. And then the two soldiers were lying on the floor of the common room, and they were not moving, and Bast was standing over them breathing hard with his hands clenched.

And I was lying on the floor behind the bar with blood in my mouth, looking up at the ceiling, wondering why I was still alive.

That was two nights ago.

You were there.

---

But I am getting ahead of myself. I was telling you about the autumn. And the scrael. And how you came.

It was a span before the soldiers. A cold night, clear and bright, with a half moon riding high. I was out on the road, west of town, where a farmer had seen scrael near his barn the night before. I had a long iron bar, one of Aaron's, which he had made for me at the forge without asking why I wanted it. I had a lantern. I had my body, which knew how to fight, and my empty chest, which did not care whether I lived or died.

I heard the screaming from half a mile away.

A man's voice. High and thin and terrified. And under it, the clicking and scrabbling of scrael. Many of them.

I ran.

---

I found you at the bottom of a shallow gully beside the road, with your back against a tree and a knife in your hand and five scrael closing in on you from every side. Your horse was dead at the top of the gully. Your satchel was lying in the mud, with papers spilling out of it.

You were very frightened. I could see it in your face, in the lantern light. But you were not running. You were standing with your back against the tree and your knife held out in front of you, and your hand was shaking, but you were not running.

I remember thinking that was brave. Or stupid. Or both.

I came down into the gully with the iron bar in my hands. The scrael turned toward me. All five of them, at once. I felt them know me. I felt them turn their eyeless attention away from you and toward me.

*Child of my child*, something whispered. Very faint. Very far away.

I killed them.

It took a long time. Longer than I wanted. There were five of them, and I was tired, and my body was slower than it had been. One of them got a leg through my guard and opened my arm from elbow to wrist. Another knocked me down, and I rolled, and came up, and the iron bar came down on its shell with a sound like a bell.

When it was over, I was on my knees in the mud with the iron bar in my hands, breathing hard, bleeding. The five scrael were dead around me. And you were still standing with your back against the tree, with your knife in your hand, staring at me.

"Who are you?" you said.

"The innkeeper," I said. "From the Waystone. In Newarre."

You stared at me for a long time. At my face, in the lantern light. At my hair, which had grown out long by then, and was not dyed anymore, because I had stopped bothering years ago. Red. True red. The color of a flame.

And you knew.

I saw it in your face. The moment you knew. Your eyes went very wide, and your mouth fell open, and the knife in your hand began to shake so hard that you nearly dropped it.

"You're him," you whispered. "Aren't you? You're—"

"Kote," I said. "My name is Kote."

---

I took you back to the Waystone. I bound up my arm, and I bound up the scratches on yours, and I gave you a room and a bowl of stew and a mug of hot cider. And the next morning, when you came down the stairs with your satchel under your arm and your face pale and set, I was waiting for you behind the bar.

You asked me. You sat down at a table by the hearth and took out your pen and your papers and looked at me across the room, and you asked me.

"Are you Kvothe?"

I did not answer right away. I stood behind the bar with a cloth in my hand and looked at you. At your neat beard and your ink-stained fingers and the tired, clever, patient face of a man who has spent his life listening to other people's stories.

And I thought about an old man at a crossroads, with bright eyes like a bird's.

*Someday a man will come to your inn. He'll be a scribe. A chronicler. And he'll ask you for the true story of Kvothe. And you'll tell him.*

I had waited so long. Six years. Seven. I had stopped counting. I had stopped believing he would come. I had stopped believing in anything.

And here he was. Sitting at a table by my hearth, with ink on his fingers, asking.

"I was," I said.

---

You wanted the story. Of course you did. Everyone wants the story. You wanted to know if it was true, all of it. The draccus at Trebon and the bandits in the Eld and Felurian and the Adem and the Chandrian. The king.

I told you I would tell it. I told you it would take three days. You laughed at that. You said no story took three days. You said you had recorded the histories of kings and never needed more than two.

I did not laugh. I only said, *three days*. And you looked at me, and something in my face made you stop laughing.

And then I began.

---

I did not know, when I began, what would happen. I want you to understand that. I did not know that telling the story would bring my name back. I did not know that every word I spoke would be a piece of me, taken out of the dark and put back where it belonged. I did not know that the telling itself was a kind of naming.

Skarpi knew. I think. And Bast. And perhaps Elodin, a long time ago, in a round room at the top of the Rookery, when he told me that the best way to learn the name of a thing is to hear its whole story.

But I did not know. Not when I began.

I only knew that an old man had told me to tell it. And that I had been waiting a very long time. And that I was tired of waiting.

So I told it. From the beginning. My parents, and the troupe, and the Chandrian. Tarbean, and the University, and Denna. The Maer, and the Adem, and Felurian.

And somewhere in the middle of the first day, I felt it.

---

It was very small, at first. A flicker. A warmth. Somewhere down in the hole in my chest, where nothing had been for years. I did not know what it was. I thought it was the fire in the hearth, or the cider, or the pleasure of having someone to talk to.

But it grew. As I talked. Every word I said, it grew a little. Every memory I brought up out of the dark and laid out on the table in front of you, it grew. Every name I spoke. My mother's. My father's. Abenthy's, and Trapis's, and Skarpi's. Sim's, and Wil's, and Auri's.

Denna's.

It grew. And by the end of the first day, I knew what it was.

It was me.

---

I did not stop. I could have. I thought about it, the first night, lying in my bed at the top of the stairs with the chest at the foot of it. I thought about getting up in the morning and telling you I had changed my mind. That there was no story. That I was only Kote, the innkeeper, and you should go back to wherever you came from.

But I did not. Because I felt it. The warmth, in my chest. Growing. And for the first time in years, the world was not quite so grey.

And because I was tired. Of being small. Of being quiet. Of polishing things and pouring drinks and waiting for something that might never come.

And because Shep was dead. And Carter's horse. And a traveler on the Rannish road. And the scrael were coming, more every year, through a crack in a door that I had closed but not all the way.

And because someone had to do something.

And because, if I am honest, I wanted to know how it ended.

---

So I told it. The second day. The University, and the Maer, and the Adem. The Fae, and Felurian, and the Cthaeh. And the warmth in my chest grew and grew, until it was not a warmth anymore but a fire. Small, still. Banked, like the embers of a hearth at night. But a fire.

And the soldiers came, and I let them beat me. And I told myself it was because I wanted to feel something. And that was true. But it was also because I could feel the fire growing, and I was afraid of it. Afraid of what it meant. Afraid of what would come when it was burning bright again.

Afraid of who would come.

And now it is the third day. And I have told you almost all of it. The door, and the name, and the cord. Renere, and the trial, and the hanging. Imre, and the chest, and Denna walking away down the far side of the bridge.

And Newarre. And the Waystone. And Kote.

And you.

---

The innkeeper stopped speaking.

He sat very still at the table by the hearth, in the heatless firelight, with his hands folded in front of him and his green eyes on Chronicler's face.

"That's the story," he said. "That's all of it. From the beginning, to here." He was quiet for a moment. "To now."

Chronicler did not move. His pen was motionless in his hand. There was ink on the page in front of him, row after row of tiny crabbed cipher, all the way to the bottom and up the margin and across the top. He did not remember writing it.

"That's the end?" he said. His voice was hoarse.

The innkeeper looked at him for a long moment. Then he looked at the window, where the frost had crept all the way across the shutters now, thick and white and feathery. He looked at the fire, burning bright and giving no heat. He looked at the cup of tea in front of him, frozen solid.

"No," he said quietly. "Not quite."

He stood up.

"There's one more part," said Kvothe. "And it hasn't happened yet."
