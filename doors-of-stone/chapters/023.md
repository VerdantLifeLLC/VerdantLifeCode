# Interlude: Customers

The door of the Waystone Inn banged open, and the innkeeper stopped talking.

It was only Graham. He stood in the doorway stamping the mud from his boots, a big, slow, square-shouldered man with a carpenter's hands and a face like a well-worn saddle. Behind him came Old Cob, grey-bearded and bent, leaning on a stick of knotted ash, and Jake, and young Aaron the smith's apprentice, who ducked his head shyly as he came in, the way young men do when they are not yet sure they are welcome among older ones.

"Kote!" Graham called. "You alive in here? Your door's been shut all morning. Thought you might've been murdered in your bed."

The innkeeper rose from the table. It was a smooth motion, and as he made it, something went out of him. Chronicler watched it go. It was like watching a candle snuffed. One moment the man across the table had been someone else, someone with a voice that could fill a room and eyes that could hold you in place like a pinned butterfly. The next moment he was an innkeeper, mild and tired and a little bruised, wiping his hands on his apron.

"Not murdered," said Kote. "Just busy. I've got a guest." He nodded toward Chronicler. "Writing a book."

"A book!" Old Cob squinted at Chronicler. "What kind of book?"

"History," said Chronicler, who had been in this situation before and knew better than to say more.

"History." Old Cob seemed to consider this. "I could tell you some history. I remember when the old king was crowned. The one before the one before this one. Big to-do, it was. They roasted an ox in Baedn and gave out cider to anyone who could walk."

"That's not history," said Jake. "That's just being old."

"Same thing," said Old Cob serenely, and lowered himself onto a stool at the bar.

Kote went behind the bar and began pouring drinks without being asked. He knew what each of them wanted. Graham took dark beer. Jake took cider. Old Cob took whatever was cheapest, and asked for it in a tone that suggested he was doing the inn a favor. Aaron hesitated, looking at the bottles, and then asked for cider too, and blushed when Kote gave it to him.

"You look terrible," Graham said, peering at Kote's face. "Worse than yesterday. Those soldiers really did a number on you."

"It looks worse than it is."

"Always does," said Old Cob wisely. "Bruises. Always look worse on the second day. It's the nature of them."

Chronicler, at his table by the hearth, glanced up sharply. Kote did not react at all. He only nodded and went on pouring.

"Any news?" he asked.

"Plenty," said Jake gloomily. "None of it good."

---

The news, as it turned out, was the same as it always was, only more so.

The war was going badly. Or well; it depended on who you asked, and the people of Newarre did not know which side to ask. The Penitent King's armies had pushed the rebels back from the river at Enfast, or else the rebels had pushed the king's armies back from the hills near Tinuë; there were two stories, and Jake had heard both, and he told them one after the other with equal conviction.

The roads were worse. Carter had come back from Baedn two days ago with a story about a whole caravan found empty on the road near the old waystone at Farrel's Cut, the wagons standing in a neat line with their horses still in the traces, dead, and not a mark on them, and no sign at all of the drivers or the merchants or the guards.

"Bandits," said Graham firmly.

"Bandits take the horses," said Old Cob. "Bandits don't leave dead horses in the traces."

"Then what?"

Old Cob did not answer. He drank his beer.

And there had been more of the spiders. That was what they called them, in Newarre. Nobody knew any better name for them. Black things, hard and shiny as beetles, the size of a dinner plate, with legs like knives. They had come out of the woods last autumn and killed Carter's horse, and then there had been one in town, and then things had gone quiet for a while. But now there were stories again. A shepherd over by Rannish had lost half his flock in one night. A family near the Old Stone Road had found scratches on their door in the morning, deep gouges in the wood, as if something had been trying to get in.

"Came back up from the south, the priest says," Jake said. "Says it's a sign. Says we're being punished for our sins."

"Whose sins?" said Graham.

"Everyone's, I suppose. The priest wasn't specific."

"Never are," muttered Old Cob.

Chronicler watched the innkeeper while they talked. Kote stood behind the bar, wiping it with his white cloth in slow even circles, and nodded, and made small sounds of sympathy in the right places. He did not look up. He did not look toward the window, or the woods beyond it, or the thin grey line of smoke that still leaned against the sky.

But Chronicler noticed that the cloth had stopped moving, just for a moment, when Jake said the word *spiders*.

---

"There's something else," said Aaron.

It was the first thing he had said since he came in. Everyone turned to look at him, and he flushed red to the tips of his ears.

"I mean," he said. "It's probably nothing. It's just something I heard. On the road. When I went to Rannish last span for the iron."

"Well, go on," said Graham. "Don't leave us hanging."

Aaron looked down at his cider. "There was a man," he said. "In the inn at Rannish. A traveling man. He'd come up from the south, from Vintas, he said. And he was telling stories." He paused. "About Kvothe."

The room went very quiet.

Kote had not moved. He stood behind the bar with his cloth in his hand, and his face was mild and pleasant and interested. The face of an innkeeper hearing a story.

At the table by the hearth, Chronicler had gone very still. Beside him, Bast had stopped breathing.

"What kind of stories?" said Old Cob.

"The usual ones," said Aaron. "The draccus at Trebon. The fight at Imre. Felurian." He blushed again. "But then he told one I'd never heard. He said—" Aaron hesitated. "He said Kvothe wasn't dead."

Nobody spoke.

"He said," Aaron went on, "that the man they hanged in Renere, the one they said was Kvothe, wasn't him. He said it was some beggar they'd pulled off the street and dyed his hair red. And that the real Kvothe got away. And that he's out there somewhere, hiding." Aaron looked up. His face was flushed, but his eyes were bright. "He said Kvothe's waiting. For something. And that when it comes, he'll come back, and make everything right."

For a long moment, no one said anything at all.

Then Graham laughed. It was a big, easy, comfortable laugh, the laugh of a man who does not believe in fairy tales. "Kvothe," he said. "Waiting. Hiding under a rock somewhere, I suppose, waiting for the right moment to leap out and save us all." He shook his head. "That's the trouble with stories. People always want the hero to come back."

"Maybe he will," said Aaron stubbornly.

"And maybe pigs will fly," said Old Cob. "If he was ever alive to begin with. I heard he was a demon. Horns and all. Only looked like a man on the outside."

"I heard he was seven feet tall," said Jake.

"I heard he was an Adem," said Graham. "Or trained by them. Could kill a man with his little finger."

"I heard," said Old Cob, with the air of a man playing a trump card, "that he killed the king with a word. Just said a word, and the king fell down dead. Right in front of the whole court."

They all looked at Kote. It was a habit, in the Waystone. When there was an argument, you looked at the innkeeper. He had a way of settling things.

Kote smiled. It was a pleasant, professional, innkeeper's smile.

"I heard he had red hair," he said. "But I've heard a lot of things. Who's for another round?"

They laughed, and the tension went out of the room, and Graham slapped the bar and said he'd have another, and so would everybody else, and he'd pay. And the talk turned to other things: the price of iron, and the weather, and whether the Penitent King would raise the taxes again before the harvest.

Kote poured. He smiled. He nodded in the right places.

At the table by the hearth, Bast was staring at him with an expression of naked, burning intensity. Chronicler put a hand on his arm, gently, and after a moment Bast looked away.

---

They stayed for the better part of an hour. Kote fed them bread and cheese and cold mutton from the kitchen, and they ate and drank and talked, and when they finally left, they left in good spirits, calling their goodbyes and promising to come back that evening.

When the door closed behind them, the innkeeper stood very still behind the bar for a long moment.

Then he set down his cloth. He came back around the bar and crossed the room and sat down at the table by the hearth. He did not look at Bast. He did not look at Chronicler. He looked at the sword above the fireplace.

"Where was I?" he said.

"You'd left the University," said Chronicler quietly. "You'd crossed the bridge. Auri was on the roof with a candle."

"Yes." The innkeeper nodded slowly. "Yes." He was quiet for a moment. "The boy was right, you know. Aaron. About the man they hanged in Renere."

Chronicler's pen stopped.

"We'll get to that," said the innkeeper. "It's later." He smiled, faintly, at something only he could see. "Everything's later. That's the trouble with this story. Everything important happens at the end."

He closed his eyes. When he opened them again, the innkeeper was gone.

"I went south," said Kvothe. "To Vintas. To the Maer, and his court, and his new wife, who was my aunt and did not know it. I went to find out what the Lackless family had been keeping, all those years. And instead, I found an old man with a walking stick, waiting for me across a tak board."

He looked at Chronicler.

"Write this part carefully," he said. "It's where I started to lose."
