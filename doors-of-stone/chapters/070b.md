# Interlude: The Regulars

They came in at dusk, the way they always did. The innkeeper heard them before Chronicler did. He stopped speaking in the middle of a sentence, and turned his head toward the door, and something in his face shifted. Settled. Went quiet and mild and ordinary, the way a pond goes still after a stone has sunk.

"Reshi," said Bast softly.

"I know." The innkeeper stood up. He smoothed his apron. "It's evening. They'll want their drinks." He looked at Chronicler. "Put the pages away. Just for an hour. It's better if they don't see."

Chronicler gathered the pages. He slid them into his satchel, and wiped his pen, and set it in its case. When he looked up again, the innkeeper was behind the bar, polishing a glass. And the door was opening.

---

Graham came first. He always did. He was a big slow man with a face like a well-worn saddle, and he came in stamping the cold off his boots and rubbing his hands together.

"Kote," he said. "Evening. Cold one tonight. Frost already on the fields, and it's hardly dark." He sat down on his stool at the bar. "The usual."

The innkeeper drew him a beer. He set it on the bar. Graham looked at it, and then at him, and his big slow face creased.

"You look better," he said. "Than yesterday. Bruises are going down."

"They are."

"Hm." Graham drank. "Still don't know why you didn't let us come and deal with those two. We'd have come. Me and Jake and the boys."

"I know you would," said the innkeeper. "Thank you, Graham."

Graham looked at him a moment longer. Then he shrugged, and drank again, and turned on his stool to look around the room. His eyes fell on Chronicler, at the corner table by the hearth, and stayed there.

"Your scribe's still here," he said.

"He is."

"Three days now." Graham squinted. "What's he writing, anyway? All day, every day? Jake says he saw him through the window at noon, scribbling away like a man possessed."

"A history," said the innkeeper mildly. "Of the region."

"Of Newarre?" Graham laughed. "What's there to write about Newarre?"

"More than you'd think."

---

Jake came in next, with Carter behind him. Then Old Cob, walking slowly, leaning on his stick. Then two of the younger men from the farms on the south road, whose names Chronicler did not know. They settled into their places at the bar and around the nearest table, the way water settles into the low places of a field, each one knowing exactly where he belonged.

The innkeeper served them. He drew beer and poured cider and ladled stew into bowls. He moved behind the bar the way he always did, quietly, efficiently, with no wasted motion. Chronicler, watching him, thought that you would never know. You would never know that an hour before, this same man had been sitting at a table with his eyes blazing, telling how he had carried his aunt's daughter out of a burning camp with the Chandrian at his back.

He was just Kote. An innkeeper. Polishing glasses.

But Chronicler noticed, now, the things he had not noticed on the first night. The way the innkeeper's eyes moved, every time the door opened. The way his hand went, every so often, to the pocket of his apron, and rested there, on something small. The way he stood behind the bar with his weight balanced on the balls of his feet, very slightly, like a man who expects to have to move quickly.

He had been standing like that for seven years, Chronicler thought. And no one had ever noticed.

---

"Heard a thing today," said Carter. "From a tinker on the Rannish road."

"The same tinker?" said Jake. "The one who came through the other day?"

"No. Different one. Younger. Came up from the south." Carter drank. "Said the Penitent King's raising taxes again. For the war. Said there's rebels in the north now, as well as the east. Said there's talk of a levy. Every village to send two men."

The room went quiet.

"Two men," said Graham slowly. "From Newarre?"

"From everywhere."

"We haven't got two men to spare. We've barely got enough to bring in the harvest." Graham shook his head. "Who'd go?"

Nobody answered. Nobody looked at anyone. But Chronicler saw that several of them glanced, very quickly, toward the door. Toward the smithy across the square, where a light was burning in the forge window. Where a young man with a smith's arms and a boy's face was working late.

The innkeeper polished his glass. His face did not change.

---

"And there's something else," said Carter, lowering his voice. "The tinker said. About the Kingkiller."

"Oh, not this again," said Jake. "They hanged him. Everybody knows they hanged him."

"That's what I said. But the tinker said there's a story going round. In the south. That they hanged the wrong man." Carter leaned in. "That it was a beggar with dyed hair. And the real Kingkiller's still out there. Wandering the roads. In disguise."

"That's been going round for years," said Graham. "Every tinker from here to Tinuë tells it."

"But this tinker said something new," said Carter. "He said there's a song. A new one. Going round the taverns in the south. About the Kingkiller. And the song says he didn't kill the king for spite, or for money, or for the Maer. It says he killed him to close a door." Carter looked around at them. "A door into somewhere bad. And he gave something up, to close it. Something important. And now he's hiding, and waiting, and one day he'll come back."

The room was silent.

"What did he give up?" said one of the young men from the south road.

"The tinker didn't know. The song doesn't say. Just that it was something he couldn't live without, and he gave it up anyway." Carter shrugged. "It's a sad song, the tinker said. He sang a bit of it. I didn't like it. Gave me the shivers."

Behind the bar, the innkeeper had stopped polishing. He was standing very still, with the glass in one hand and the cloth in the other.

Nobody noticed. Nobody but Chronicler, and Bast in the kitchen doorway.

And Old Cob.

---

Old Cob had not said anything since he came in. That was unusual. Chronicler did not know Old Cob well, but on the first night, Old Cob had talked from the moment he sat down until the moment he left. He had told the story of the winter the river froze. He had told the story of the tinker who sold his father two goats sewn together. He had told the story of the Kingkiller, with its three kings and two dragons, and the part where the Kingkiller escaped from prison by turning into a crow.

On the second night, he had said almost nothing. Tonight he had told nothing at all. He sat at his place at the end of the bar with his cider in his gnarled hands, and he watched. He watched Chronicler, at the corner table, with his empty hands and his satchel full of pages. And he watched the innkeeper.

When Carter finished speaking, and the room was silent, Old Cob looked up from his cider. He looked at the innkeeper, standing very still behind the bar. For a long moment. His faded eyes were very sharp.

Then he said, into the silence, in his thin cracked old voice:

"I heard that song too. Years back. Different tinker."

Everyone turned to look at him.

"How does it go?" said Graham.

Old Cob did not answer at once. He looked at the innkeeper. The innkeeper looked back at him.

"Don't remember," said Old Cob at last. "Not the words. Just the end." He turned his cup of cider in his hands. "At the end, it says he's waiting for someone to come and ask him. To tell the whole story. And when someone does—" He stopped.

"What?" said Jake.

Old Cob looked at Chronicler. At the corner table by the hearth. At the satchel full of pages.

"Then he'll be done waiting," said Old Cob.

---

Nobody said anything for a long time. The fire crackled. Outside, the wind was rising.

Then Graham laughed, a big uneasy laugh, and slapped the bar.

"Well, that's cheerful," he said. "Cob, you're a proper ray of sunshine tonight. Kote, another round. On me. Before this old crow has us all jumping at shadows."

The innkeeper drew the round. His hands were steady. His face was mild. He set the mugs on the bar one by one, and the talk moved on, to the harvest, and the frost, and the price of barley in Rannish, and whether Carter's new horse would ever be as good as old Nelly had been.

And after a while, one by one, the regulars went home.

---

Old Cob was the last. He climbed down off his stool, slowly, with a grunt, and pulled on his coat, and wrapped his scarf around his neck. At the door, he stopped. He looked back at the innkeeper, behind the bar.

"Kote," he said.

"Cob."

The old man looked at him for a long moment. Then he looked at Chronicler, still sitting at the corner table. Then back.

"You tell it right," said Old Cob. Very quietly. "The whole thing. Don't leave any of it out." He paused. "Not even the running."

The innkeeper did not answer. But he nodded, once, very slightly.

Old Cob nodded back. And then he went out into the cold, and the door closed behind him, and they heard his stick tapping on the frozen road, going away.

---

The innkeeper stood behind the bar for a long time after that. He did not polish anything. He stood with his hands flat on the wood and looked at the door.

"He knows," said Chronicler softly.

"He's always known. A little." The innkeeper took his hands off the bar. "Everyone knows a little, in a town like this. They just don't know that they know." He came around the bar and sat down at the table again, and folded his hands. "Cob knows more than most. He's seen things. I told you."

"The Hollis farm."

"Yes." The innkeeper looked at the fire. "He ran, that night. He's been ashamed of it for twenty years. He thinks it makes him a coward." He was quiet for a moment. "He wants me to put it in. The running. So that someone knows he wasn't the only one."

"Will you?"

"I already have." The innkeeper looked at Chronicler. "Haven't I?"

Chronicler thought of the pages in his satchel. Of a boy of twelve in a forest at dusk, running from a camp full of bodies. Of a young man in a burning inn, running from a shadow made of forgetting. Of a grey man on a bridge at dawn, watching a woman with dark hair walk away, and not running after her.

"Yes," he said. "You have."

"Good." The innkeeper unfolded his hands. "Get your pages out, then. We've a long way to go tonight. And I don't think we have much time."

Chronicler got out his pages. He uncapped his ink. He dipped his pen.

Outside, the wind was rising. And on the shutters, very faint, very slow, the first thin feathers of frost were beginning to creep in from the edges.
