# Netalia

The Maer held his daughter for the first time at dawn, sitting on a flat rock at the mouth of his cave, with the grey light coming up over the hills and the smoke of the king's burning camp still rising on the horizon behind us.

I put her in his arms. He took her the way a man takes something he has never held before and is terrified of breaking. Awkwardly, carefully, with his whole body gone stiff. She woke as he took her, and opened her eyes, and looked up at him.

They were dark eyes. Not green, like mine, or grey, like his. Dark, like her mother's. Like my mother's.

The Maer looked down at her for a long time. He did not say anything. His face did not move. But his hands, holding her, began very slowly to relax.

"Netalia," I said. "Meluan named her Netalia."

The Maer closed his eyes.

"After her sister," he said.

"Yes, your grace."

He opened his eyes and looked down at the baby again. She was looking back at him with her dark solemn eyes, the way very young babies do, as if they know something they will forget as soon as they learn to speak.

"She told me once," said the Maer quietly, "that if we had a daughter, she would name her Netalia. I told her that was a terrible idea. That it would remind everyone of the scandal. That people would talk." He was silent for a moment. "She said she did not care what people said. She said her sister was the bravest person she had ever known, and she wanted her daughter to have a brave name." He looked up at me. "I did not understand, then."

"And now?"

He looked back down at the baby.

"Now," he said, "I think I do."

---

He did not ask me what had happened to Meluan. He did not need to. He had seen it in my face when I came up the valley at dawn with the baby in my arms and no one beside me. He had seen it in the Adem, walking behind me with their swords sheathed and their faces showing nothing.

He only asked one thing.

"Is she alive?"

"Yes, your grace. They need her alive. For the door."

"And you will go there."

"Yes."

"And bring her back."

I did not answer right away. I looked at him. At his hollow grey face and his bandaged arm and the tiny baby in his lap.

"I'll try," I said.

He looked at me for a long moment. Then he nodded.

"That is all anyone can do," he said.

---

We buried Tempi that afternoon.

The Adem had brought his body back from the camp. I do not know how. I did not ask. Vashet and two of the others had gone back into the dark, while the rest of us were fleeing, and they had come back an hour before dawn carrying something wrapped in a red cloak.

They buried him in a high place, on a shoulder of the hills above the Maer's valley, where the wind blew clean and cold and you could see for miles in every direction. The Adem did not use a coffin. They did not use a shroud. They laid him in the earth in his red clothes, with his hands folded on his chest and his sword laid beside him, and they covered him with stones.

Then they stood around the cairn in a ring, all eleven of them, and they did not speak.

I stood with them. I did not know what else to do. I did not know the Adem customs for death. I had only seen one Adem funeral before, in Haert, for an old woman I had barely known, and I had not understood most of what happened.

But I knew this. The Adem do not weep. Not with their faces. Not with their voices. They weep with their hands.

And standing on that hillside in the cold wind, I watched eleven Adem weep for Tempi. Their hands moved slowly, all together, in a long silent pattern. Over and over. Grief, and love, and memory, and a word I did not know. The same pattern, repeated, like a song.

I did not know the pattern. But I let my own hands move. Clumsily, badly, the way a barbarian's hands move when he tries to speak Adem. I did not know what I was saying. I only knew what I felt.

Beside me, Vashet glanced at my hands. Something moved in her face. Not an expression. Something smaller than that.

Then she reached out and took my hands in hers and moved them, gently, into the right shape. The shape of the pattern the others were making. And held them there.

We stood on the hillside until the sun went down, with our hands moving together. And I wept for Tempi the only way I could. The Adem way.

---

That night, I sat with Vashet by the Maer's fire. The others had gone to sleep. The fire was burning low. The baby was asleep in a basket beside the Maer's bed in the cave, with Mirra watching over her.

Vashet sat across the fire from me with her sword across her knees. She had not spoken since the burial.

"He taught me," I said at last. "First. Before you. He taught me the first forms of the Ketan, on the road through the Eld. He didn't know any Aturan. I didn't know any Adem. He taught me with his hands."

"I know."

"He didn't have to come here. None of you did."

"We did." Vashet looked at me across the fire. "You are ours. Shehyn said so, when she sent us. She said, *he has shamed us by being accused, and honored us by what he did at the singing-house. Go and help him. He is ours.*" Her hands moved. *Family*. "Tempi wanted to come. He asked to lead us. Shehyn said no, I would lead. He argued." Her hands moved again. *Amusement*. *Grief*. "He never argued with Shehyn. Not once in his life. But he argued about this."

I did not say anything. I could not.

"He knew," said Vashet quietly. "I think. He knew he would not come back. I saw it in him, on the road. He was very quiet. Even for Tempi." She looked into the fire. "He told me, the night before we came into your valley, that he had been thinking about the Lethani. About what it means to follow it truly. He said he thought it meant going where you are needed, even when you know the cost." Her hands were very still. "He said he thought you understood that better than any Adem he had ever known."

"I don't understand anything."

"No," said Vashet. "You do not. But you do it anyway." She looked at me. "That is the Lethani too."

---

We made our plan the next day.

It was not a complicated plan. There was not much to plan. The king's army was moving toward the Lackless house. They would be there in two days. They would go down to the door at the dark of the moon, three nights from now. And they would take Meluan with them.

And I would be there.

"Not alone," said Vashet.

"Not alone," I agreed.

But there were not many who could come. The Maer's soldiers were too few to fight the king's army, and too many to sneak past it. The refugees and the Ruh could not fight at all. And my friends—

"We're coming," said Sim.

We were sitting in the mouth of the Maer's cave, all of us. Sim and Wil and Devi and Fela. Bast. Vashet and the ten remaining Adem. The Maer, with the baby in his lap.

"Sim," I said. "You can't."

"Why not?"

"Because it's going to be very dangerous. Because the Chandrian will be there. Because there'll be things there I can't even describe." I looked at him. "Because you're a poet, Sim. You write sonnets. You argue about meter. You're not a soldier."

"Neither are you," said Sim.

I opened my mouth. Then I closed it.

"He's right," said Wil quietly. "None of us are soldiers. But we're not useless either." He held up his scarred hands. "I can't do fine sympathy anymore. But I can still do the big kind. Heat. Fire. Motion. I can still hold a binding, if it's a strong one." He looked at me. "And I'm not letting you go down there alone."

"And I've still got some Surprises," said Devi. She patted her satchel. "Well. I've got materials for some Surprises. And a lot of other things. I'm very well prepared." She smiled her spider's smile. "I'm always very well prepared."

Fela did not say anything. She sat on the rock beside me with her hands folded in her lap, and she looked at me with her dark serious eyes.

"The door is made of stone," she said at last.

"Yes."

"And it's cracked. Or it's going to be. When they open it."

"Maybe."

"Then you'll need someone who can ask the stone to hold." She looked at me. "Like at the Eolian. When everything's burning and falling down, and there's only one thing standing between you and the end. You'll need someone who can ask it to stand."

I looked at her. I looked at all of them.

"You could die," I said. "All of you. You could die down there."

"We could die up here," said Devi. "Waiting. Worrying. Wondering." She shrugged. "I'd rather die doing something."

"I wouldn't," said Sim. "I'd rather not die at all." He swallowed. "But I'd rather die doing something than live knowing I didn't."

---

The Maer was the last to speak.

He had been sitting quietly through all of it, with the baby in his lap. He had not said anything. He had only listened.

Now he looked at me.

"Kvothe," he said. "Come here."

I went and knelt beside his rock. He looked down at me with his hollow grey face.

"My wife asked you to hide our daughter," he said. "To wrap her name, the way your mother wrapped yours. So they cannot find her."

"Yes, your grace."

"Can you do that?"

I thought about it. I thought about what Selitos had told me. About my mother, wrapping her deep name in another name, like a stone wrapped in cloth.

"I don't know how," I said honestly. "Not yet. But I know someone who might."

"Who?"

I looked at Tessit and Mirra, sitting a little way off by their wagons. At Nell, in her red dress, curled up asleep against Mirra's side.

"The Ruh," I said. "My mother hid among the Ruh for twelve years. No one found her. Not the Chandrian, not the Amyr, not the old man. The Ruh are very good at hiding. They're very good at being no one." I looked back at the Maer. "If she were raised among them—if she grew up Ruh, with a Ruh name, on the roads—"

"She would be safe," said the Maer slowly.

"Safer than anywhere else."

"And she would never know who she was."

"Not until she was old enough to understand. Not until it was safe to tell her."

The Maer looked down at the baby in his lap for a long time. She was asleep. Her small face was peaceful.

"I have been Maer of Severen for twenty years," he said at last. "My father was Maer before me, and his father before him. I have spent my whole life being someone. Being important. Being *seen*." He touched the baby's cheek with one finger. "And now I am in a cave in the hills, with nothing. No city. No palace. No wife." He was quiet for a moment. "And the only thing I have left, the only thing that matters, I must give away. So that no one will ever know she was mine."

"Your grace—"

"No." He looked up at me. His face was very hard. "No. You are right. It is the only way." He looked down at the baby again. "It is the price. For everything. For the war, and the city, and my wife. For not seeing Bredon for what he was, all those years. For being so busy being someone that I did not notice the thing growing in my own house." His voice cracked, very slightly. "It is my penance."

He stood up. He carried the baby across the cave mouth to where Tessit and Mirra were sitting. They looked up at him. Mirra's round face went very still.

The Maer knelt in front of her. The Maer of Severen, knelt in the dirt in front of a Ruh woman.

"Will you take her?" he said. "Will you raise her as your own? Will you keep her safe, and hidden, and never tell anyone who she is?"

Mirra looked at him for a long time. Then she looked at the baby. Then at Tessit.

Tessit looked back at her. There was a whole conversation in that look. Years of it.

"We'll take her," said Mirra softly. She held out her arms. "One family, your grace. Under the sky."

The Maer looked at her. Then, very slowly, he placed the baby in her arms.

Mirra held her against her chest. She began to hum. A soft, low, wordless tune. A Ruh lullaby. I knew it. My mother used to sing it to me.

The Maer knelt there in the dirt and watched his daughter being held by someone else. His face did not change.

But his hands, resting on his knees, were shaking.

---

I sat with him later, by the fire, when everyone else had gone to sleep. He did not speak for a long time.

"What will you call her?" I asked at last. "Among the Ruh. She'll need a Ruh name."

The Maer looked into the fire.

"Mirra asked me that," he said. "I told her to choose. It is her daughter now." He was quiet for a moment. "She said she would call her Lark. After your mother. After the way she sang."

I did not say anything. I could not.

"Lark," said the Maer softly. "It is a good name. A small name. The kind of name no one would ever look for." He was silent for a moment. "Better than Netalia. Better than Lackless. Better than anything I could have given her."

He looked at me.

"Go to the door," he said. "Bring my wife back. And then close it. So that Lark never has to."

"I will, your grace."

He looked at me for a long time. Then he reached out and laid his hand on my shoulder.

"I believe you," he said. "God help me. I believe you."
