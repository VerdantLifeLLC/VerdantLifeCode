# A Letter to a Younger Sister

The Maer sent for me that evening, after the council, when the others had gone to their fires.

He was sitting at the mouth of his cave, where he had sat that morning when I brought him his daughter. She was asleep now, in a basket lined with an Adem cloak, at his feet. He had a small box in his lap. A traveling desk, of dark lacquered wood, with brass corners and a brass lock. The lacquer was scorched along one side, and the brass was blackened.

"One of your Adem brought this out of the camp," he said, when I knelt beside him. "The tall one. Tempi. He found it in the black tent, in the confusion, after. He thought it might be important." The Maer turned the box in his thin hands. "It was my wife's. She has had it since she was a girl. She took it everywhere."

He opened it. Inside there were papers, pens, a stick of sealing wax, a small silver knife for cutting quills. The ordinary things of a lady's writing desk. And beneath them, in a hidden compartment in the bottom, which the Maer opened by pressing on one corner of the base, a single folded letter. Old and soft and yellowed, the creases worn nearly through.

"I did not know this was here," said the Maer. "In all our marriage, short as it has been. I did not know." He held it out to me. "It is addressed to her. But I think it is meant for you."

---

I took it. My hands were not steady. I unfolded it, very carefully, in the last of the light.

It was written in a young hand. Quick and slanting and a little careless, with ink blots where the pen had been dipped too deep. The kind of hand that belongs to someone who has a great deal to say and is afraid she will not have time to say it all.

I knew that hand. I had never seen it before, and I knew it. I had seen it in a hundred small places, all my childhood, without knowing what I was seeing. On the labels of the jars in our wagon. On the margins of my father's songs, where she had made corrections. On the little slate she used to teach me my letters.

My mother's hand.

---

*Mel,* the letter began.

*I don't know if they'll let you read this. I don't know if it will even reach you. I'm giving it to a tinker, which Father would say is the same as throwing it in the river. But tinkers are the only people I trust anymore, and this one has kind eyes, and he says he's going north. So.*

*I'm sorry. That's the first thing. I'm sorry I didn't say goodbye. I'm sorry I left in the night, like a thief, without even waking you. I stood in the door of your room for a long time. You were asleep with your mouth open, the way you always sleep, and you were holding that awful stuffed rabbit, the one with one ear. And I wanted to wake you so badly. But if I had woken you, I wouldn't have been able to go. You would have cried, and I would have stayed. And I can't stay, Mel. I can't.*

*I know what they're saying. That I ran off with a trouper. That I've shamed the family. That I'm a whore and a fool and a disgrace to the name. Father will have blotted me out of the book by now. I know he will. I can see him doing it, pressing the pen into the page until the ink soaks through.*

*It's all true. I did run off with a trouper. His name is Arliden and he is the cleverest, silliest, most wonderful man in the world, and he makes me laugh until I can't breathe, and I love him. But that's not why I left.*

*I left because of the door.*

---

I stopped reading. I looked up at the Maer. He was watching me, very still, in the fading light.

"Go on," he said quietly.

---

*You know about the door. You've heard the rhyme. We all have, since we were babies. Father made sure of it, though he doesn't understand a word of it, and neither did his father. But you're too young to have heard it the way I heard it. You haven't lain awake at night and heard it humming.*

*I have. Since I was twelve. Since the summer I went exploring in the old cellars, where Father says we mustn't go, and found a stair behind the wine racks, going down. And at the bottom, a little cold room with an empty chair and a lot of old knotted cords hanging on the wall. And beyond it, a door that isn't a door.*

*Nobody told me what it was. Nobody knows anymore. I think somebody used to. I think there used to be someone who sat in that chair. But there hasn't been, not for a long time. Not since Great-Grandmother Liss, who went down to the cellars one night when Grandfather was a boy, and never came up again, whatever the family says.*

*It hums, Mel. The door. In the night. You can't hear it with your ears. You hear it with something else. And it's not just humming. It's listening. It knows I'm the eldest. It knows my name. And every night, it says my name. Very softly. Like someone calling from the bottom of a well. And every night, I want to answer.*

*I couldn't bear it anymore. I couldn't lie in that house one more night with it calling my name. I thought I would go mad. I think that's what happened to Great-Grandmother Liss. I think she answered.*

*So I left. With the one person in the world who could take me somewhere the door would never find me.*

---

*The Ruh are always moving. Did you know that? They never stay anywhere more than a few days. They never have a house, or a village, or a name that stays the same. They have a hundred names, for a hundred towns. They're like water. They don't hold still long enough to be caught.*

*Arliden says that's why the Ruh were made. To be the ones who move. So that the things that hunt names can never find them.*

*I'm going to be one of them, Mel. I'm going to change my name. Not just the one people call me. The deep one. I'm going to wrap it up in another name, the way you wrap a stone in cloth so it won't clink in your pocket. Arliden's grandmother knows how. She's very old and very strange and she chews mint leaves all day long and she says she learned it from a woman who learned it from a woman who learned it from the Ruh who came out of the burning city at the beginning of the world.*

*When she's done, I won't be Netalia anymore. Not in any way the door can hear. I'll be someone else. A trouper's wife. A singer. Someone small and ordinary and always moving.*

*And the door will stop calling. Because there'll be no one there to call.*

---

*I'm writing to tell you three things, and then I have to go, because the tinker is waiting and he keeps looking at me with his kind eyes as if he knows exactly what I'm writing, which he can't. Can he?*

*The first thing is: I love you. I've always loved you. Even when you took my ribbons and cried when I took them back. Even when you told Father about the boy from the mill. You were my little sister and I was supposed to take care of you and I'm sorry I'm leaving you there alone.*

*The second thing is: when Father dies, the house will be yours. You'll be the eldest, now that I'm gone. And the door will start calling you.*

*Don't answer it, Mel. Whatever it says. Whatever it offers. Don't ever, ever answer.*

*And the third thing is this. If you ever have a daughter—and I hope you do; I hope you have a dozen, and they're all as stubborn and as clever as you—*

*Don't teach her the rhyme.*

*Don't let them take her down to the cellar. Don't let them tell her she's the keeper. Don't let her learn her own name in that house.*

*Give her a small name, Mel. A plain one. The kind no one would ever look for. And let her grow up somewhere the door can't hear.*

*I'll try to do the same. If I ever have a child. I'll give him a name like wind in the grass. Like water. Like nothing at all. And I'll teach him to sing so loud that he never hears anything calling.*

*I love you. Burn this.*

*—Tally*

---

I did not say anything for a long time. I sat on the rock at the mouth of the Maer's cave with the letter in my hands, and the light faded, and the first stars came out over the hills. The baby in the basket stirred, and made a small sound, and was still.

*Tally*. I had never known that. My mother's name for herself, when she was a girl. Before she was Laurian. Before she was anything but a frightened eldest daughter in a cold house on a ridge, a keeper no one had taught to keep, lying awake at night listening to a door say her name.

*I'll give him a name like wind in the grass.*

Kvothe. It means *to know*, in some tongues. But in the old Ruh cant, the one my father taught me when I was very small, it means something else. It is the sound the wind makes in dry grass, at the end of summer, when the stalks rub together.

I had never known she chose it for that. I had never known she chose it at all.

---

"She did not burn it," said the Maer quietly.

"No."

"She kept it. For twenty years. In a hidden drawer, in a box she carried everywhere." He looked down at the basket at his feet. "And when we married, and I told her that if we ever had a daughter, she must not name her Netalia, it would remind everyone of the scandal—" His voice caught. "She looked at me so strangely. I did not understand. I thought she was angry."

"She wasn't angry."

"No." The Maer was silent for a long moment. "She was remembering. A letter from her sister. Asking her to give her daughter a small name. And I had just told her she could not give her daughter her sister's name." He closed his eyes. "She must have thought I understood. And then she must have realized I didn't."

---

I folded the letter, very carefully, along its worn creases. I held it out to him.

The Maer shook his head.

"It is yours," he said. "It was always yours. It was written to my wife, but it was written about you. About what your mother did, and why. About the name she gave you." He looked at me with his hollow grey eyes. "And about what she asked. For Meluan's daughter. For Lark."

"A small name," I said.

"A small name. The kind no one would ever look for. And somewhere the door cannot hear." The Maer looked down at the sleeping baby. "The Ruh. Your mother's people. She was right, and you were right. That is where Lark must go."

He reached down, very gently, and touched his daughter's cheek with one finger.

"Tomorrow," he said, "we go to the door. You, and your friends, and your Adem. And I will stay here, with my daughter, and wait." His voice was very quiet. "And if you come back—if my wife comes back—we will decide together what to do. And if you do not—"

He did not finish. He did not need to.

"Then Mirra will take her," I said. "And the Ruh will keep her. And she'll never learn the rhyme."

The Maer nodded slowly. He did not look up from the basket.

"Tally," he said, very softly, as if trying the name. "She must have been very brave."

"She was," I said. "I didn't know how brave. Until now."

---

I kept the letter. I kept it through the door and the grey and the Tower of Thorns. It went into the chest, in the end, folded small, tucked inside the lid of the humming box with no lid. I do not remember putting it there. I think Denna did. I think she read it, first. I think she must have understood it better than anyone.

When I opened the chest, it was the last thing I found. At the very bottom. Under everything else.

*I'll teach him to sing so loud that he never hears anything calling.*

She did. She taught me to sing. And for twelve years, among the Ruh, I never heard anything calling.

And then I stopped singing. For seven years. And the whole world went quiet.

And in the quiet, very faint, very far away, something began to call my name.

---

"Is that why?" said Chronicler softly. "Why you're telling it? Why now?"

The innkeeper looked at him for a long moment.

"No," he said at last. "I'm telling it because Bast begged, and Skarpi sent you, and I was tired of being small." He looked at the fire. "But I think it's why I can. Why the telling doesn't frighten me the way it should." He was quiet. "She wrote that I'd sing so loud I'd never hear anything calling. And a story is a kind of song. The oldest kind." He looked at the frost on the shutters. "So I'm singing. As loud as I can. And let him come. Let him call. I'm not listening anymore."

He picked up his cup. His hand was very steady.

"Where was I?" he said.
