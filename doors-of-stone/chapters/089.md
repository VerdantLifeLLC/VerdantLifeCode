# Across the Bridge

We came to Imre at the beginning of autumn, a year and a season after I had left it.

I did not know it. I want to be honest about that. We came over the last hill in the late afternoon, and there it was below us, a town of grey stone and brown roofs on the bank of a wide brown river, with a great stone bridge arching across the water to another town on the far side. And beyond that other town, rising above its roofs, a great grey windowless building like a block of stone that had decided to stand up in the middle of the road.

I looked at it all, and I felt nothing. It was a town. A river. A bridge. A building. That was all.

But Denna, sitting beside me in the back of the wagon, went very still. And her hand, holding mine, tightened.

"Do you remember?" she whispered.

"No."

"The Archives. The University. The Eolian." She pointed. "The bridge. You used to stop at the top of it, every time you crossed. You told me once. You'd stop and look out at the river. You said it made you feel like the world was big enough."

I looked at the bridge. At its great grey arch, curving up and over the brown water.

"I don't remember," I said.

She did not say anything. But she did not let go of my hand.

---

They were waiting for us at the foot of the bridge, on the Imre side. All of them. Someone must have sent word ahead; Tessit, perhaps, or Bast, who had gone ahead of the troupe two days before without telling anyone where he was going.

There was a thin young man with straw-colored hair and a black patch over his left eye, with a white star sewn on it. There was a dark man with scarred hands, standing very straight. There was a small fair woman with spectacles and no eyebrows, which made her look perpetually surprised. There was a girl with dark hair and a pale face, holding something small and grey in her hand.

And there was a lean man with dark restless eyes, sitting on the stone rail of the bridge with his legs dangling, eating an apple.

I did not know any of them.

The wagon stopped. I climbed down from the back. I stood in the road at the foot of the bridge and looked at them, and they looked at me.

Nobody moved.

Then the thin young man with the eye patch made a sound. A small, broken sound, like a sob that had been caught halfway. And he ran at me, and threw his arms around me, and held on.

"You're alive," he said into my shoulder. "You're alive, you're alive, you're *alive*—"

I stood very still in his arms. I did not know him. I did not know what to do.

"Sim," said the small fair woman sharply. "Sim, he doesn't—"

"I know he doesn't." The young man did not let go. His voice was muffled and wet. "I know he doesn't remember me. I don't care. He's *alive*. They hanged him and he's *alive*." He pulled back and looked at me with his one eye. It was very bright. "I wrote a poem about you, you know. When I heard. For your funeral. It was terrible. The worst thing I ever wrote." He laughed wetly. "I'm so glad I don't have to read it."

I looked at him. At his thin eager face, and his one bright eye, and the black patch with the white star.

"Sim," I said.

He went very still.

"That's your name," I said. "Sim. She said it."

His face crumpled. He put his hand over his mouth.

"Yes," he said through his fingers. "Yes. That's my name."

---

They took me to the Grey Man.

It was an inn on the western edge of Imre, tall and narrow and built of grey stone, with a sign that showed a man in a grey cloak with his face in shadow. Denna said we had stayed there once. Or she had, and I had come to see her. She had taught me something there, she said. Knots.

I did not remember.

The landlord gave us the whole top floor. Three rooms, connected, with windows that looked out over the rooftops toward the river. Someone had paid for it. A round little man in a purple coat, who came bustling up the stairs an hour after we arrived and threw his arms around me and wept, and called me *my dear boy*, and said a great many things about statues and noses that I did not understand.

His name was Threpe. He had paid for everything, it seemed. The rooms, the food, the wine. The horses that had carried my friends south to the war. The paper and ink for Sim's terrible poems.

"Anything," he said, holding my hands in his and looking up at me with wet shining eyes. "Anything you need. Anything at all. You only have to ask."

"Thank you," I said. I did not know what else to say.

He looked at me for a long moment. Then his face fell, just slightly.

"You really don't remember," he said. "Do you?"

"No."

"Not the Eolian? Not the song? Not the night you sang *Tariniel* and the whole world stopped to listen?"

"No."

Threpe was quiet for a long time. Then he squeezed my hands.

"Well," he said. "I remember. I'll remember for you. Until you can remember for yourself." He smiled, a little shakily. "That's what patrons are for."

---

Elodin came that night.

He did not come up the stairs. He came in through the window. I was sitting on the bed in the largest of the three rooms, with Denna beside me and the others scattered around on chairs and on the floor, and the window was open to the cool autumn night. And then, between one moment and the next, there was a man sitting on the windowsill, with his legs dangling inside the room, eating an apple.

Nobody had seen him climb up. Nobody ever did.

"Hello, Kvothe," he said.

I looked at him. I did not know him. But something in me went very still when I saw him. The way it had gone still when Vashet looked at me. As if some part of me knew that this was a person who could see things other people could not.

"Hello," I said.

Elodin looked at me for a long time. His dark eyes were very sharp. Then he swung his legs over the sill and dropped into the room and came across to the bed. He knelt in front of me. He put down his apple on the floor.

"May I?" he said, and held out his hand.

I did not know what he meant. But Denna did. She took my left hand, gently, and held it out to him. The one with the cord.

Elodin did not touch the cord. He held his hand above it, an inch away, the way you hold your hand above a fire to feel its heat. He closed his eyes.

The room was very quiet.

"Oh," said Elodin softly. "Oh. Oh, that's very loud."

He opened his eyes. He looked at me. His face was strange.

"Do you know what you've done?" he said.

"No."

"No. Of course you don't." He sat back on his heels. "You've done what I did. At seventeen. What put me in Haven for three years. Only you did it on purpose, and you did it all the way, and you did it *right*." He shook his head slowly. "I didn't think it could be done right. I didn't think anyone could survive it."

"I don't know if I have."

"You have. You're sitting here. You're talking. You're breathing." Elodin looked at the cord. "You're not broken. Not like I was. You're not torn in half. You're just—empty. Set aside. Put down." He looked up at Denna. "You did this?"

"I tied it," said Denna. "He pulled it out. I caught it."

Elodin looked at her for a long moment.

"Where did you learn to tie a name?" he said quietly.

Denna did not answer right away. Then she said, "From a man who used it to steal mine."

Elodin's face went very still. Then he nodded, slowly, as if she had told him something important.

"Well," he said. "You learned it well. Better than he did, I think." He looked back at the cord. "It's holding. Perfectly. Every strand. Every piece of him is in there, safe." He paused. "And it's screaming."

---

The room went very quiet.

"Screaming?" said the young man with the eye patch. Sim. "What do you mean, screaming?"

Elodin did not look at him. He was looking at the cord.

"A name is a loud thing," he said. "Especially a true one. Especially a big one. It wants to be *in* something. It wants to be what it is. Put it in a cord and tie it up tight, and it'll sit there, and it'll be safe. But it'll be loud. Like a bird in a cage, singing." He looked up at me. "Anyone who knows how to listen can hear it. From very far away."

"The Seven," said the dark man with scarred hands. Wil, someone had called him.

"The Seven. The Thief, maybe, from behind his door, if the crack is wide enough. Other things." Elodin looked at the cord. "It's been on his wrist for a year. Singing. I'm surprised no one's come for it yet."

"They thought he was dead," said the small fair woman. Devi. "They hanged him. In front of ten thousand people."

"That might have bought him some time. A little. Death is a very loud story too; it drowns out a lot of other noise." Elodin shook his head. "But not forever. Sooner or later, someone will hear it. And follow it. And find him."

"Then what do we do?" said Denna.

Elodin looked at her. Then at me. Then at the cord.

"We make it quiet," he said.

---

"How?" said Denna.

Elodin was silent for a long time. He sat on his heels on the floor in front of the bed, with his dark eyes on the cord, and he thought. We all waited.

"It needs to be put away," he said at last. "In something that holds. Something that says no. To everyone but him." He looked up. "A box, I think. Or a chest. Something with walls. Something that the name can sit inside of, and be quiet, and not be heard."

"Like the Lackless box," said Denna slowly. "The one with no lid."

"Yes. Very like that." Elodin nodded. "That box was made to hold the key. To keep it safe, and quiet, and close, for a thousand years. We need something like it. For the name."

"Can you make it?"

"Me? No." Elodin laughed. "I can't make anything. I've never made anything in my life. I break things. That's my gift." He looked around the room. At Wil, and Devi, and the girl with dark hair who had not spoken yet. "But I know people who can."

The girl with dark hair stood up.

She had been sitting on the floor in the corner, very quiet, with her hands folded in her lap. She had been holding something small and grey in one of them, the whole time. She came across the room now and stood in front of me, and held it out.

It was a stone. A small flat grey stone, about the size of a coin, worn smooth. There was a single mark scratched into it. A line, very straight, with a small curve at one end.

"You have one like this," she said. "In your pocket. I gave it to you. A long time ago. You promised to give it back."

I reached into my pocket. I did not know why. My hand found something there. Small and flat and cool. I took it out.

It was the same. The twin of the one in her hand. A small flat grey stone, with a mark scratched into it.

"Fela," said Denna softly. "Her name's Fela."

The girl with dark hair looked at me. Her eyes were very dark and very serious.

"I've been learning," she said. "At the University. With Elodin. All year. Learning stone. Learning how to ask it for things. How to make it hold." She held up her stone, beside mine. "I can make a lock. A stone lock. That says no to everyone but you."

"Everyone but me," I said. "But I'm not me. Not anymore."

Fela looked at me for a long moment.

"No," she said. "Not now. But you will be. Someday. When it's safe." She took my stone out of my hand, and held both of them together in her palm. "And when you are, the lock will know you. It'll open. Just for you."

"And until then?"

"Until then," said Fela, "it'll stay closed. Forever, if it has to." She closed her hand around the two stones. "Stone's very good at forever."
