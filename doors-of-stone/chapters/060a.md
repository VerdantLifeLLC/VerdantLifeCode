# The Gate-Warden

Where the Moon's Road leaves the grey-green hills and climbs toward the mountains, there is a gate.

It is not a gate in a wall. There is no wall. There is only the road, running up a long bare slope of silver grass toward a notch in the ridge, and at the top of the slope, standing across the road, two tall grey stones with a third laid across them. A lintel. A doorway with nothing on either side of it. You could walk around it with no trouble at all. The grass on either side was smooth and open and empty.

"Don't," said Bast, when I started to.

I stopped. "Why not?"

"Because it's a gate." He was looking at the stones with an expression I had not seen on him before. Respect, and wariness, and something close to fear. "You don't go around a gate, Reshi. Not in the twilight. That's the rudest thing there is. Worse than breaking a promise. If you go around a gate, the gate remembers. And the next gate you come to won't let you through at all."

"And if I go through?"

"Then you have to pay the warden."

I looked at the stones. There was no one there. Just the road, and the silver grass, and the two grey stones with the third across them, and the notch in the ridge beyond, and the mountains beyond that, white and enormous and very far away.

"What warden?"

Bast did not answer. He was looking at the space between the stones.

After a moment, so was I.

---

It was not there, and then it was.

I do not know how else to say it. There was no flicker, no fading in. It was simply the case, all at once, that someone was sitting in the doorway between the stones, on a three-legged stool, with a long clay pipe in one hand and a cup of something steaming in the other. And it was also the case, somehow, that they had always been sitting there, and I had simply failed to notice.

It was a small person. Smaller than Bast. Wrapped in a cloak the color of dry leaves, with the hood pushed back. Its face was old and brown and creased like a walnut shell, and its eyes were very bright and very black and very amused. I could not tell if it was a man or a woman. I am still not sure. I am not sure the question means anything, where the warden is concerned.

"Well," it said. Its voice was dry and light and pleasant, like the rustle of paper. "Two travelers. One of them mortal." It looked at me. "Very mortal. Mortal all the way through. How unusual." It drew on its pipe. "And one of them isn't."

Bast bowed. Low, with one hand over his heart. I had never seen him bow to anyone before.

"Warden," he said. "We ask passage."

"I know you do. Everyone does. Nobody sits on this road for the view." The warden looked at Bast with its bright black eyes. "I know you. Remmen's boy. The one with the mortal mother." It smiled. It had very small, very white teeth. "You've grown."

Bast went red to the tips of his ears. "Warden."

"And you're traveling with a mortal. On the Moon's Road. Toward the mountains." The warden's eyes moved to me. "With the moon's silver on his wrist and a stone in his pocket that says no." It tilted its head. "How very interesting you both are."

---

"What's the price?" I said. "For passage?"

The warden looked at me for a long moment. Then it laughed, a small dry pleased laugh.

"Straight to it," it said. "Mortals always are. You have so little time, you can't afford to waste any on manners." It set down its cup on the grass beside the stool. "The price is a riddle."

"You'll ask me a riddle?"

"No, no, no." The warden waved its pipe. "Anyone can answer a riddle. That's easy. That's nothing. No, *you'll* ask *me* a riddle. And if I can answer it, you go back the way you came. And if I can't—" It spread its small brown hands. "Then the gate is open, and you may pass."

I looked at Bast. Bast was looking at the ground.

"How many people get through?" I said.

"Oh, a few." The warden drew on its pipe. "Over the years. Not many. I've been sitting here a very long time, you understand, and I've heard a great many riddles. Almost all of them, I think." It smiled. "But there's always a chance. That's what makes it interesting."

---

I sat down in the silver grass in front of the gate. I crossed my legs. I thought.

Bast sat down beside me. He did not say anything. He knew better than to help. In the twilight, a riddle asked with help is not your riddle, and the warden would have known.

I thought of every riddle I knew. There were a great many. The Ruh are fond of riddles; we trade them around the fire on long nights on the road, and I had learned hundreds before I was ten. I had learned more at the University, from Wil and Sim and the others, drinking at Anker's. I had learned some very strange ones from Elodin, which did not have answers, or had answers that were other riddles, or had answers that made you feel as if the floor had tilted underneath you.

The warden would know them all. I was sure of that. It had been sitting on this road since before the Ruh were on the roads, since before there was a University. It had heard every riddle there was.

Every riddle that had been asked.

---

"Well?" said the warden, after a while. "I'm in no hurry. But you are."

I looked up at it.

"What's the name of the thing in my pocket?" I said.

The warden blinked. It looked at me. Then it looked at my pocket.

"That's not a riddle," it said. "That's a question."

"It's a riddle if you can't answer it."

The warden looked at me for a long moment with its bright black eyes. Then it laughed again, delighted.

"Oh, very good," it said. "Very Ruh. All right. I'll allow it." It leaned forward on its stool. "The thing in your pocket is a stone. It was cut from the foundation of a building in Imre. A tavern. A place where music lived. It burned, and the stone did not." Its eyes narrowed. "And a woman held it, once, with her whole heart, and asked it to stand. And it stood. And now it says *no*." It sat back, satisfied. "The name of the thing in your pocket is *No*."

I shook my head.

"That's what it says," I said. "Not what it's called."

The warden's eyes went very still.

---

"Its name," I said, "is whatever Fela called it. When she held it in the burning Eolian and asked it to stand. She spoke to it. She must have called it something. And it answered." I looked at the warden. "I don't know what she called it. She never told me. I don't think she knows herself; I think it just came out of her, in the fire. But the stone knows. And she knows, somewhere deep, where the truth lives." I spread my hands. "And I don't think you do."

The warden did not say anything for a long time.

It looked at my pocket. It looked at me. Its bright black eyes narrowed, and narrowed, and I could feel it reaching. Not with its hands. With something else. Something older and subtler, the way Elodin reached for the name of the wind.

And then, very slowly, it began to smile.

"No," it said. "I don't."

---

It stood up from its stool. It was even smaller standing than sitting; its head came barely to my chest. It knocked out its pipe against the side of the left-hand stone.

"That's the first time in a very long while," it said, "that anyone's asked me a riddle whose answer was a name I didn't know." It looked at me with its head on one side. "Do you know why that almost never happens?"

"No."

"Because mortals don't think of the things in their pockets as having names." The warden tucked its pipe away in its cloak. "Not real names. Only what they say, or what they're for. A key is a key. A coin is a coin. A stone is a stone." It looked at me. "But you do. Somewhere in you, you know that everything has a name. Even a little stone from a burned-down tavern." It smiled. "Especially a little stone from a burned-down tavern."

"Is that good?"

"It's dangerous," said the warden. "It's very dangerous. Most mortals can't bear to think like that. It makes the world too big. Too full." It picked up its stool and its cup. "But it's also how you get through gates."

It stepped aside. The space between the two grey stones was empty. Beyond it, the road ran on up the slope toward the notch in the ridge, silver in the twilight.

"Pass," said the warden. "Both of you. Remmen's boy too; he's with you, and I'm feeling generous." It looked at Bast. "Look after him, little one. He's going to need it. I can smell the mountains on him already. And something older than the mountains."

Bast bowed again. Very low.

---

We passed through the gate. I felt it as I went, a small cool tingle across my skin, like walking through a curtain of mist. And then we were on the other side, and the road was climbing, and the notch in the ridge was ahead of us.

I looked back. The gate stood where it had stood, two grey stones and a lintel across the road. There was no one sitting in it. There was no stool, and no cup, and no smell of pipe smoke on the air.

"Where did it go?" I said.

"Nowhere," said Bast. "It's still there. It's always there. You just can't see it once you've paid." He was quiet for a moment. "Reshi. That was very clever."

"Thank you."

"No. I mean it was *very* clever. Too clever." He looked at me sidelong. "Do you know what you did?"

"I asked it a riddle it couldn't answer."

"You asked it the name of something," said Bast. "Something you carry. Something small. And it couldn't find it." He was quiet for a long moment. "Reshi. That means the stone has a name so deep, so hidden, so wrapped up inside itself, that even a gate-warden of the old twilight can't reach it."

I stopped walking.

"Fela," I said slowly.

"Yes." Bast looked at me. "Whatever she did to that stone, in the fire, she did it very, very well." He was quiet. "I'd want to know that. If I were you. If I ever needed something that nobody could open."

I did not answer. I stood on the road in the silver twilight with the stone in my pocket, and I put my hand over it, and I felt it there. Small and cool and stubborn. Saying *no*.

I would remember that, later. In a workshop in the Fishery, in the middle of the night, with Kilvin's roah planks laid out on the bench and Fela sitting across from me with two stones in her hands.

*If I ever needed something that nobody could open.*

---

We climbed on. The notch in the ridge came nearer. And beyond it, rising white and enormous against the twilight sky, the Stormwal waited.

Behind us, on the empty road, I thought I heard—very faint, very dry—someone laughing.
