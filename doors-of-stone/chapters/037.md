# Flight

There is a moment, in any fight, when the world goes very slow.

I have felt it many times. In the alleys of Tarbean, when a man with a knife stepped out of the shadows. In the Eld, when the bandits came at us through the trees. In Ademre, on the practice grounds, when Vashet's sword came whistling toward my face and I had one heartbeat to decide which way to move.

It is not that time stops. It is that you begin to see it. Every grain of it. Every drop. The world does not slow down so much as open up, like a flower, and you can see every petal of it, every vein in every leaf.

I felt it now, kneeling beside the dead king's bed with a handful of grey hair in my lap and a dozen guards in the doorway.

I saw Ambrose's smile. I saw the lantern light gleaming on the guards' drawn swords. I saw the captain at the front, a big red-bearded man with a scar through one eyebrow, taking a step forward into the room. I saw Kelm, frozen by the window with his hand over his mouth. I saw the night nurse, backing slowly toward the dressing room door.

I saw the fire, roaring in the great hearth. A dozen candles, burning in their sconces around the room. And the door to the dressing room, standing half open behind the nurse, leading back to the servants' passages.

I let the grey hair fall from my hands. I rose to my feet.

"I didn't kill him," I said.

"Of course you didn't," said Ambrose. "You just happened to be kneeling at his bedside in the middle of the night, when no one was supposed to be here, with his hair in your hands, when he died." He laughed. "Take him."

The guards came forward.

---

I did not draw my sword. That was the first thing I decided, in that slow bright moment. There were twelve of them, and they were the king's guards, and they had done nothing wrong except follow the orders of the wrong man. I did not want to kill them.

So instead, I reached for the fire.

I had not done sympathy in anger for a long time. Not since Ambrose, in the Commons, the day I called the wind. I had been careful since then, very careful, the way you are careful with a dog that has bitten you once. But I did it now. I did it without thinking.

I split my mind. One part of me held the link, the binding between the great fire in the hearth and the dozen candles around the room. The other part of me reached for the heat.

And I pulled.

Every candle in the room flared at once. Not a little. A great deal. Each flame shot up two feet high, white and roaring, like a torch dipped in oil. The room went bright as noon. Brighter. So bright that the guards cried out and threw their arms over their eyes.

And then, all at once, I let go. And every candle went out.

The fire in the hearth went out too. I had pulled too much from it; it had nothing left. The room plunged into darkness so sudden and so total that it was like being struck blind.

I was already moving.

---

I had the whole room in my head. I had seen it in that slow bright moment, every chair and table and curtain, every step between me and the dressing room door. I crossed the floor in five strides, in the pitch dark, while the guards shouted and stumbled and crashed into each other behind me. I found the door by memory. I went through it.

The nurse was there, in the dark of the dressing room. I felt her hand on my arm.

"This way," she hissed. "Quick."

She pulled me through the dark, through the hanging robes, to the narrow door at the back that led into the servants' passages. She pushed me through it.

"Left," she said. "Then down. Then right at the bottom. It'll take you to the laundry. The laundry door opens on the kitchen yard." She squeezed my arm. "God go with you, lad."

"Come with me."

"Me? I'm too old to run." She laughed, a short hard sound. "Besides, they'll need someone to tell them which way you went. And I'll tell them wrong." She pushed me again. "Go!"

I went.

---

Left. Then down. The servants' stairs were narrow and steep and utterly dark. I went down them by feel, my hand trailing along the rough stone wall, my feet finding each step by instinct. Behind me, far above, I could hear shouting. Doors slamming. Running feet.

At the bottom, I turned right. A long narrow corridor, smelling of soap and wet linen. I ran along it. At the end, there was a door, and beyond the door a great low room full of steam and copper tubs and lines of washing hanging from the ceiling like ghosts.

The laundry. There was a door at the far end, with a crack of moonlight showing beneath it. I ran for it.

It opened before I reached it.

My guard was standing there. One of the two the Maer had sent with me. His name was Harl. He was a big quiet man with a broken nose and a grizzled beard, and in all the days we had ridden together he had never said more than ten words to me at a time.

He had his sword drawn. He looked past me, into the laundry, and his face was grim.

"Heard the shouting," he said. "Figured you'd come out this way. Corrin's with the horses. Round the back of the kitchen yard." He jerked his head. "Go on."

"Come with me."

"No. They'll be right behind you. Someone's got to hold the door." He stepped past me into the laundry and turned to face the corridor. "Go on, sir. The Maer'd have my hide if I let you get caught."

"Harl—"

"*Go.*"

I went. I ran out into the kitchen yard, into the cold moonlight, and I did not look back. I heard the shouting behind me grow louder. I heard the clash of steel. I heard a man cry out.

I did not know, then, whose voice it was. I told myself it was one of the palace guards. I told myself that Harl was a soldier, a veteran of a dozen campaigns, and that he could hold a narrow door against a dozen men for as long as it took.

I found out later that he held it for nearly a quarter of an hour. Long enough for me to reach the horses. Long enough for Corrin and me to ride out through the kitchen gate and into the dark streets of Renere. Long enough for us to lose ourselves in the maze of alleys and lanes around the palace hill.

He killed three of them before they brought him down.

I never learned if he had a family. I never learned where he was from, or why he had become a soldier, or whether he liked music. I knew him for eleven days, and in all that time he said perhaps a hundred words to me. And then he died holding a door so I could run.

I have thought about him often, over the years. I have thought about all the people who died so I could run. There are more of them than I like to count.

---

Corrin and I rode through the dark streets until the horses were blown. Then we left them in an alley and went on foot. Corrin knew Renere; he had served in the city guard as a young man, before he went to work for the Maer. He led me through a part of the city I had never seen, down by the rivers, where the streets were narrow and crooked and the houses leaned together overhead so that you could barely see the sky.

"Lady Tamsin," I said, as we ran. "We need to get to Lady Tamsin."

"She's in the palace. We can't get back in."

"Then Kelm. Or Petre Ashford."

"Kelm'll be arrested by now. He was in the room." Corrin shook his head. "And Ashford's rooms are in the treasury. That's in the palace too." He looked at me. "There's nobody, sir. We're on our own."

"Then we need to get out of the city."

"The gates'll be shut. They'll have shut them the moment the king died." He was quiet for a moment, thinking. "But there's the rivers. Three of them. They meet down at the Weir, south of the old market. Boats go out at dawn, carrying goods down to the coast. If we can get on one—"

"Can we?"

"Maybe. If we can pay."

I touched the purse at my belt. The Maer's purse, still heavy with silver.

"We can pay," I said.

---

We hid until dawn in a boathouse by the Weir, among coils of rope and stacks of oars and the smell of river mud and tar. Neither of us slept. We sat in the dark and listened to the bells of Renere ringing across the city, one after another, a great slow tolling that went on and on. The death knell. The king was dead.

And beneath the bells, closer, the sound of men searching. Tramping boots on cobblestones. Voices calling. Doors being pounded on. Once, very close, a group of guards went past the boathouse door, and I heard one of them say *the Ruh* and another say *red hair, can't miss him*, and I put my hand on Caesura's hilt and held my breath until they had gone.

When the sky began to grey, Corrin went out. He was gone a long time. When he came back, his face was grim.

"There's a barge," he said. "Going downriver to Sevvan and then out to the coast. Carrying wool. The bargemaster will take us, for a price." He paused. "A big price."

"Pay it."

"There's more." Corrin hesitated. "They're saying things, sir. In the market. Everyone's talking about it." He looked at me. "They're saying the Maer's Ruh killed the king. With witchcraft. They're saying you sang him to death. That you bewitched the prince's horse and then went to the king's bed in the night and pulled his hair out by the roots and sang a song over him until his heart stopped."

I did not say anything.

"They're saying the Maer sent you," Corrin went on quietly. "To clear the way for himself. They're saying the Maer wants the throne." He looked at me. "Duke Jakis has already called for the Maer's arrest. For treason."

I closed my eyes.

This was it, then. The shape of the board. I had been so busy looking at the knots in the king's hair that I had not seen the pattern Bredon was building all around me. He had not needed to kill me. He had not needed to stop me. He had only needed me to come to Renere and do exactly what I did. Go to the king's bedside. Try to save him. Be found there, with his hair in my hands, when he died.

And now I was the Ruh who had killed the king. And the Maer, who had sent me, was a traitor. And Duke Jakis, whose son was fourth in line for the throne, was the only man in Vintas who could be trusted.

It was beautiful. It was the most beautiful move I had ever seen.

And I had played my part in it perfectly.

---

We got on the barge at dawn. The bargemaster was a sour, narrow-faced woman named Brisk who took the Maer's silver without counting it and told us to get down in the hold among the wool bales and not come out until she said so. We got down in the hold. It was dark and hot and smelled of sheep. We lay among the bales and listened to the river slapping against the hull and the bargemaster shouting orders and the long slow creak of the sweeps as the barge pushed off from the Weir and began to move downstream.

We did not come out until noon. By then, Renere was far behind us.

I climbed up on deck and stood at the stern and looked back. The river was wide and brown and slow, winding between low green hills. Far behind us, on the horizon, I could just see a smudge of grey that might have been the city. Or might have been cloud.

Corrin came and stood beside me. He did not say anything for a long time.

"What now, sir?" he said at last.

I thought about it. I thought about the Maer, in Severen, with Duke Jakis calling for his arrest. I thought about Meluan, heavy with child, in her green room. I thought about Harl, dead in the laundry door. I thought about Kelm, and the night nurse, and Lady Tamsin, and everyone else in Renere who had helped me and who would now pay for it.

I thought about Denna, somewhere out there, with a box and a knot and a man who could stop her heart.

And I thought about the king, lying in his great red bed, gripping my wrist with his clawed hand. *Ambrose is of the line. A drop is enough. For a key.*

"You go back to Severen," I said. "To the Maer. Tell him everything. Tell him what happened in Renere, and what the king said to me before he died. Tell him about Ambrose. Tell him to prepare."

"Prepare for what?"

"War," I said. "It's coming. Duke Jakis will put his son on the throne. The old duke and the twins will die, one way or another. And when Ambrose is king, he'll come for the Maer. For Severen. For Meluan." I looked at him. "Tell the Maer to protect her. Whatever else happens. Tell him to keep her safe."

"And you?"

I looked out over the brown river, at the hills rolling away to the south. Somewhere beyond them, a long way off, was the sea. And beyond the sea, far to the west, was Tarbean.

"I need to find someone," I said. "An old man. A storyteller. He knows something I need to know."

"Where?"

"Tarbean."

Corrin stared at me. "Tarbean? That's the other side of the world."

"Not quite. But nearly." I smiled faintly. "I've been there before. I know my way around."

He looked at me for a long moment. Then he shook his head, slowly, the way the Maer did when I said something he did not understand.

"You're a strange one, sir," he said.

"So I'm told."

---

Corrin left the barge at Sevvan, three days downriver, to ride back north to Severen. I stayed on until the coast. At the mouth of the river there was a small, dirty port town called Hallow's End, and there I bought passage on a merchant ship bound west along the coast, for Tarbean.

I stood at the rail as the ship pulled out of the harbor. The wind was fresh, and the sails bellied out, and the land fell away behind us, green and brown and hazy in the afternoon light.

I lit Kilvin's candle that night, in my tiny cabin below decks. It burned yellow for a long time. And then, very late, just as I was about to snuff it, it flickered blue. Only for a moment. A single heartbeat.

Somewhere, far away, something had happened. Something cold.

I sat on my narrow bunk in the dark, with the ship rocking beneath me and the candle burning yellow again, and I wondered where. And to whom.

I found out, much later, that it was the night the old duke died. The one who had not left his bed in three years. He died peacefully, his physicians said, in his sleep. They found frost on his pillow in the morning.

The twins died a span later. A fever, the physicians said. It went through the nursery and took them both in a single night.

And on the first day of summer, in the great cathedral at Renere, with the bells ringing and the crowds cheering and the banners of the Jakis house flying from every tower, Ambrose Jakis was crowned King of Vintas.

I was in Tarbean when I heard. I was sitting in a tavern in Waterside, with a mug of cheap beer in front of me and a storyteller's chair empty by the fire.

I remember that I laughed. I do not remember why.
