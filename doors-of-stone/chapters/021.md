# On the Horns

They called it being put on the horns.

It is an old phrase, older than the University, and nobody knows where it comes from. Some say it refers to the horns of a bull, which toss a man into the air and gore him when he lands. Others say it refers to the horns that were blown in the old days to summon the masters to judgment. Still others say it comes from the Aturan phrase *cornu*, which means a dilemma, a choice between two equally terrible things.

I have been on the horns three times in my life. The first time was for using malfeasance against Ambrose. The second was for conduct unbecoming a member of the Arcanum. Both times I was punished. Both times I survived.

The third time was the last.

---

The hearing was held in the Masters' Hall, three days after the Nighting. It was a long room with a high vaulted ceiling and tall windows of old glass that turned the grey winter light into something pale and wavering, like light seen through water. The nine masters sat at a long table on a raised dais. The Chancellor sat in the center, in a high-backed chair. The rest of the room was empty except for a few benches along the walls, where students and members of the Arcanum could sit to watch.

The benches were full. Word had spread. Word always spreads, at the University.

I stood in the center of the floor, alone, in front of the masters. I had dressed carefully that morning, in the best clothes I owned. I had combed my hair and polished my boots. I had thought about wearing Caesura, and then decided against it. It was still in Lorren's office, in any case.

The charge was read out by the Chancellor's clerk, a thin nervous man with ink-stained fingers. He read it in a high, carrying voice that echoed off the vaulted ceiling.

"Kvothe, son of Arliden, Re'lar of the Arcanum, stands accused of trespass beyond the Four-plate door, contrary to the oldest law of the Archives. He stands accused of descending the forbidden stair, and of entering the Chamber of Watches, and of reading the Book of Watches, which no living member of the University may read. He stands accused of waking the Sentinel. How does he answer?"

A murmur went through the benches. Most of them, I think, had not known about the Chamber of Watches or the Book of Watches or the Sentinel. They had not known there was anything beneath the Four-plate door at all. They were learning now, and I could hear the hunger in their whispers.

"I answer that it's true," I said.

The murmur grew louder. Herma raised his hand, and it died away.

"You do not deny the charge?" he said.

"No, Chancellor. I went down the stairs. I entered the chamber. I read the book." I paused. "I did not wake anything. Not on purpose. But I understand that something woke, and that I was there when it did."

Herma looked at me for a long time. Then he turned to the masters.

"Does anyone wish to speak for the student?" he said.

There was a long silence.

Then Kilvin stood up.

---

He was a big man, Kilvin, and when he stood, he seemed to fill the whole dais. He looked down at me with his dark shaggy brows drawn together, and then he looked at the other masters.

"I will speak," he said.

Herma nodded. "Master Artificer."

"This student," said Kilvin, "is the most gifted artificer I have seen in twenty years. He has made the arrow-catch, which has saved lives. He has made improvements to the sympathy lamp, which are now used in the Fishery every day. He has made the heat-funnel, which works better than my own." He paused. "He is careless. He is proud. He does not listen. But he is not wicked. He did not go down those stairs to do harm."

"Why did he go down, then?" said Hemme. He was sitting at the end of the table, his fat face twisted in an expression of satisfaction that he did not bother to hide. "If not to do harm?"

"Because he wanted to know," said Kilvin. "Because he is curious. Because he is a student, and students are curious, and that is what the University is for." He looked at Hemme with contempt. "Or have you forgotten, Master Rhetorician? Have you been sitting in your chair so long that you have forgotten what it is to want to know a thing?"

Hemme flushed red. "I have not forgotten the law."

"Nor have I. But the law is not the only thing." Kilvin turned back to Herma. "I ask for leniency. Suspension, perhaps. A term. Two terms. But not expulsion. It would be a waste."

He sat down.

Herma looked at the other masters. "Does anyone else wish to speak?"

Arwyl stood. He was a small man, old and bent, with a kindly face and a quiet voice. "I will add my voice to Kilvin's," he said. "The boy has been a good student in the Medica. He has a steady hand and a quick mind. He saved a woman's life last term, when she came in with a wound no one else could stanch. I would not like to lose him."

He sat down.

Then, to my great surprise, Mandrag stood. The Master Alchemist was a tall, spare man with stained fingers and a sour disposition, and I had never been sure he liked me.

"I will say only this," he said. "The boy is a nuisance. But he is a clever nuisance. And clever nuisances have a way of becoming great men, if they are not crushed too early." He sat down.

Nobody else stood.

Herma looked at Lorren. "Master Archivist. You have been silent."

Lorren sat very still in his chair. His long pale hands were folded on the table in front of him. He did not look at me.

"The law is clear," he said. "Anyone who descends the forbidden stair is to be expelled. There is no provision for leniency. There never has been."

"And yet you have heard three masters ask for it."

"I have heard them." Lorren looked up at last. His eyes met mine, and they were very cold. "But they do not know what I know. They do not know what lies at the bottom of those stairs. They do not know what woke, three nights ago, and turned its head toward this boy." He paused. "If they knew, they would not ask for leniency. They would ask for him to be gone. Far away. As quickly as possible."

A silence fell over the hall. The students on the benches had stopped whispering. They were staring at Lorren, and then at me, and I could see the fear beginning to creep into their faces.

"Master Namer," said Herma quietly. "Do you agree?"

Elodin was sitting at the far end of the table, lounging in his chair with his feet up on the edge, as he always did. But he was not lounging now. He sat very still, with his hands folded and his eyes fixed on me.

"I agree," he said.

My heart dropped into my stomach.

"Not because he deserves it," Elodin went on. "He doesn't. He's been foolish, but not wicked. And he's been brave, in his way. Braver than any of us have been, in a very long time." He looked at the other masters. "But Lorren is right. Something woke. And it knows him now. It knows his name. And as long as he's here, sitting on top of it, it will be listening for him. It will be *calling* him." He looked back at me. "The best thing for him, and for all of us, is for him to be somewhere else. Somewhere far away. Where it can't hear him."

He paused.

"I'm sorry, Kvothe," he said. "I truly am."

---

The vote was taken. It was not close.

Kilvin voted against expulsion. Arwyl voted against. Mandrag voted against.

Lorren voted for. Elodin voted for. Hemme voted for, with an expression of such smug satisfaction that I wanted to climb up onto the dais and break his nose. Brandeur voted for. Herma voted for, with a heavy sigh.

That was five. Five was enough. The ninth master, Master Linguist, did not vote at all; the chair had been empty since Herma had become Chancellor, and they had not yet found anyone to fill it.

"Kvothe, son of Arliden," said Herma. His voice was heavy. "By a vote of the masters, you are hereby expelled from the University. Your name will be struck from the rolls of the Arcanum. You will surrender your gram and any other artifacts made in the Fishery that may be of danger to others. You will leave the grounds of the University within three days, and you will not return." He paused. "Do you understand?"

"I understand."

"Do you have anything to say?"

I looked at them. At the nine masters sitting on their dais, in their robes, beneath the high vaulted ceiling with its pale wavering light. At Kilvin, who looked angry. At Arwyl, who looked sad. At Lorren, who looked at nothing. At Elodin, who looked at me.

I thought about all the things I could say. I thought about arguing, and pleading, and shouting. I thought about telling them about the Chandrian, and my parents, and the Book of Watches, and the line of Loeclos. I thought about telling them that they were making a mistake, that I was the only one who knew, that I was the only one who could do anything about it.

And then I thought about the Adem. About Vashet, and her hard hands and her harder eyes, teaching me to be still. About the Lethani. About knowing when to act, and when not to.

"No, Chancellor," I said. "I have nothing to say."

Herma looked at me for a long moment. Something moved in his face. It might have been respect.

"Then this hearing is concluded," he said.

---

I walked out of the Masters' Hall into the grey afternoon light. The courtyard was full of students, milling about, talking in low voices. They fell silent as I came out. They stared at me. Some of them looked away.

Sim was there. And Wil. They were standing at the bottom of the steps, waiting for me. Sim's face was white and stricken. Wil's face was carefully blank, which with Wil meant he was angrier than I had ever seen him.

"Is it true?" said Sim. "They expelled you?"

"It's true."

"For going down some stairs?" His voice cracked. "That's insane. That's completely insane. Kvothe, you have to appeal. You have to—"

"There's no appeal, Sim."

"There's always an appeal!"

"Not for this." I put my hand on his shoulder. "It's all right. It really is."

"It's not all right!" He was almost crying now. "It's not all right at all! You're the best student in the whole University, and they're throwing you out because you went down some *stairs*!"

"It wasn't just stairs," said Wil quietly.

Sim turned to look at him. Wil was looking at me, and his dark eyes were very steady.

"Was it?" he said.

I looked back at him. I thought about lying. I thought about it very seriously. But Wil had been my friend for a long time, and he deserved better.

"No," I said. "It wasn't just stairs."

Wil nodded slowly. He did not ask any more questions. That was one of the things I loved about Wil. He knew when to stop asking.

"Where will you go?" he said.

"I don't know yet. Vintas, probably. To the Maer."

"When?"

"They gave me three days."

Wil nodded again. Then he did something I had never seen him do before. He reached out and put his arms around me and held me tight, the way you would hold a brother.

"Three days," he said into my shoulder. "Then we'd better make them count."
