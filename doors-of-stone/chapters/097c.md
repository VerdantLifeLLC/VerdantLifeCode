# What Old Cob Saw

Old Cob told stories. Everyone in Newarre knew that, and most of them had stopped listening years ago. He told the same dozen stories over and over, with the same pauses in the same places and the same jokes at the end, and if you had grown up in Newarre, you could recite most of them along with him, which some of the younger men did, under their breath, to annoy him.

He told the story of the winter the river froze so hard they drove a cart across it. He told the story of the traveling tinker who sold his father a cow that turned out to be two goats sewn together. He told the story of Taborlin the Great and the story of Oren Velciter and the story of the Kingkiller, which he had heard from a traveler, and which grew longer and more elaborate every time he told it. By my fourth year at the Waystone, the Kingkiller had killed three kings, two dragons, and a bishop, and had escaped from prison by turning into a crow.

I did not correct him. I polished the glasses.

But there was one story Old Cob did not tell. I did not know that for a long time. I only learned it by accident, on a night in my fourth winter, when the snow was very deep and the common room was empty except for the two of us.

---

He had stayed late. The others had gone home early, because of the snow, but Cob lived alone in a cottage at the end of the lane, and he said he did not fancy sitting in the dark there listening to the wind. So he stayed at the bar with his mug of cider, and talked, and I polished, and listened, the way I always did.

He talked about his father. About the farm on the east road, before the drought, before the bank took it. About his wife, who had died of the winter fever twenty years ago. About his daughter, who had married a cooper in Rannish and never came to visit.

And then, somewhere in the middle of it, he stopped talking. He sat looking into his mug for a long time. The wind was very loud outside.

"Kote," he said. "You ever seen something you couldn't explain?"

I set down the glass I was polishing.

"Once or twice," I said.

---

Old Cob nodded slowly. He did not look up from his mug.

"I was a carter," he said. "Most of my life. Before my knees went. Worked for a man in Hallowfell, running goods up and down the north road. Wool and salt and barrels. Up to the mountains and back." He turned the mug in his gnarled hands. "It was good work. I liked it. I liked the road."

"Hallowfell," I said. My voice was very steady.

"You know it?"

"I've heard of it."

"Little place. Nothing much there. A mill and an inn and a few farms." Cob was quiet. "And one night, coming back late from the mountains, I passed a farm on the road outside town. A big farm. The Hollis place. Everyone knew the Hollises; they were the richest family for twenty miles. Six children. A grandmother. Three hired men." He paused. "And the farm was burning."

The fire in the hearth cracked. Neither of us looked at it.

---

"I stopped the cart," said Old Cob. "Of course I did. I jumped down and ran toward the house, shouting. Thought I'd help. Get people out. Throw water." He shook his head slowly. "But there wasn't any shouting. That's what I noticed first. A fire like that, you'd expect screaming. Dogs barking. Cows lowing in the barn. There wasn't any of it. It was quiet. Dead quiet. Just the fire."

He took a long drink of cider. His hand was shaking a little.

"And the fire was the wrong color," he said.

I did not say anything. I could not.

"Blue," said Old Cob. "The flames. All over the house. Tall and blue, like a spirit lamp, like the flame on a pudding at midwinter. And there wasn't any heat from it. I got right up close, right up to the door, and I couldn't feel any heat at all." He was quiet for a moment. "It was cold, Kote. Standing next to that fire. Cold as a winter night."

---

"What did you do?" I said.

"I looked in the door." Old Cob's voice was very low. "It was open. Standing wide open. And I looked in."

He did not say anything for a long time. The wind howled around the inn. Snow hissed against the shutters.

"They were all at the table," he said at last. "All of them. The Hollises. The grandmother and the six children and the mister and missus. Sitting at the big table in the kitchen, like they were sitting down to supper. With the plates set out, and the bread, and the candles." He swallowed. "And they were dead. All of them. Just sitting there. Dead. With their eyes open."

I gripped the edge of the bar.

"And there was someone else," said Old Cob. "At the head of the table. In the mister's chair."

---

"I couldn't see him right," said Cob. "I've tried, over the years. To remember. But every time I try, it slides away, like trying to look at something in the corner of your eye. There was a man. Sitting in the chair at the head of the table. With his back to me, mostly. And there was something wrong with the light around him. It was darker than it should have been. Like there was a shadow sitting on him."

He was quiet for a long moment.

"And there was another one," he said. "Standing by the hearth. A tall one. Pale. With his hair all white, like frost. And he turned around. And he looked at me."

My heart was hammering. I could hear it in my ears.

"He had black eyes," said Old Cob. "All black. No white in them at all. Like holes. Like a well at night." He shuddered. "And he smiled at me. And he said something. I don't remember what. Something about the harvest. Something about how it was a poor harvest, this year." He shook his head. "And then I ran."

---

"I ran back to the cart," said Cob. "I don't remember climbing up. I don't remember driving. I just remember whipping the horses, harder than I ever did before or since, and the road going by in the dark, and the blue light behind me. Getting smaller. And then gone." He drank. "I drove all night. I didn't stop in Hallowfell. I drove straight through, and on down the south road, and I didn't stop till noon the next day, in a town I'd never been to. And I got down off the cart, and I sat down in the road, and I couldn't stand up again for an hour."

"Did you tell anyone?"

"Who'd believe me?" Old Cob laughed, a short dry sound. "An old carter, half-drunk on road wine, talking about blue fire and a man with eyes like wells. They'd have locked me up." He shook his head. "I heard, later, that the Hollis farm burned down that night. With everyone in it. The constable said it was a lightning strike. There wasn't any lightning. It was a clear night. But that's what he said." He looked at his mug. "And the neighbors said the ashes were cold by morning. Stone cold. Like they'd been lying there for a year."

---

I did not say anything for a long time. I stood behind the bar with my hands flat on the wood.

I was thinking of a ledger. Kilvin's ledger. Twenty years of careful notes, in his square, heavy hand. The date and the time and the weather and the phase of the moon. And one entry in particular, which I had found and marked, years before, sitting in his office in the Fishery with a blue flame burning on the desk in front of me.

*Three days, eleven years ago. The candle burned blue.*

And a constable's report, in a dusty box in the Stacks. *A farmstead near Hallowfell. Strange lights in the sky. The ashes cold to the touch by morning.*

"Cob," I said. My voice sounded very far away. "How long ago was this?"

He thought about it.

"Must be—twenty years, near enough," he said. "I was just shy of fifty. Bess was still living. She took the fever the winter after." He looked at me. "Why?"

I did not answer. I was doing arithmetic in my head. Eleven years before I found the ledger. And eight more since then, give or take a season. Near enough.

Near enough.

---

"You never told anyone?" I said at last. "All these years?"

"Not a soul." Old Cob shook his head. "Not even Bess, God rest her. Not my daughter. Not anyone." He looked at me. "You're the first, Kote. I don't know why I'm telling you now. The snow, maybe. The wind." He was quiet. "Or maybe because you're the only man I know who wouldn't laugh."

"No," I said. "I wouldn't laugh."

"I tell all those other stories," said Cob. "Taborlin and the Kingkiller and the two goats sewn together. I tell them over and over. Everyone's sick of them. I know they are." He looked into his mug. "But I tell them because they're not that one. Do you understand? As long as I'm telling those, I'm not telling that one. And as long as I'm not telling it, I don't have to remember it."

I understood. Better than he could possibly have known.

"And the Chandrian?" I said carefully. "Do you tell stories about them? Ever?"

Old Cob looked at me very sharply.

"No," he said. "Never." He was quiet for a long moment. "I heard the rhyme, when I was a boy. Everyone did. *When the hearth fire turns to blue.* But I never thought—" He stopped. "I never thought it was real. Not until that night."

He finished his cider. He set down the mug.

"You won't tell anyone," he said. "Will you?"

"No."

"Good." He climbed off his stool, slowly, with a grunt, the way old men do. He pulled on his coat and his scarf. At the door he stopped, and looked back at me.

"Kote," he said. "The man with the frost hair. The black eyes." His face was very pale. "You looked like you knew him. When I was telling it. You looked like you'd seen him too."

I did not answer. I stood behind the bar with a glass in my hand.

Old Cob looked at me for a long moment. Then he nodded, slowly, as if I had answered after all.

"Well," he said. "Good night, then."

And he went out into the snow.

---

He never mentioned it again. The next night he was back at the bar, telling the story of the river that froze so hard they drove a cart across it, and Jake and Carter were rolling their eyes, and Graham was complaining about his barn roof. And I was polishing glasses. Everything was the same as it always was.

But sometimes, after that, when Old Cob told the story of the Kingkiller, with its three kings and two dragons and escape by crow, he would glance at me, very quick, sidelong, in the middle of it. And there was something in his eyes that had not been there before.

Not suspicion. Not quite.

Something more like kinship.

---

"He saw Cinder," said Chronicler. His pen had stopped moving. "Old Cob. Twenty years ago. And he never told anyone."

"No."

"And then you came, and he told you."

"Yes." The innkeeper was quiet for a moment. "And when you came, and I began telling this story, I think Cob knew. The first night. When he saw you sitting at the corner table with your pens." He looked at the window. "He came in the next evening and sat at the bar and didn't tell a single story. Not one. Just sat there and drank his cider and watched you." He paused. "I think he was waiting to hear if I would tell his."

"Will you tell him? When it's done? That you told it?"

The innkeeper did not answer for a long time.

"No," he said at last. "He'll know. He'll read it someday, maybe, in some bookshop in some town, with a man with frost-colored hair on the page. And he'll know." He smiled faintly. "He'll know he wasn't the only one who ran."
