# Interlude: Princesses

The innkeeper stopped speaking. For a long time, nobody said anything.

Chronicler's pen had stopped moving. He sat with it poised above the page, a single drop of ink trembling at the tip of the nib. He was looking at the innkeeper with an expression of mingled wonder and something that was almost dismay.

"You told me," he said slowly. "Just now. You told me who she is."

The innkeeper looked at him. His green eyes were very calm.

"Did I?"

"The crest. The tower and three stars. The royal house of Modeg. You said her name."

"I said a princess of Modeg had disappeared. I said her name was Ariel. I said I saw a banner in a gallery." The innkeeper's voice was mild. "I never said Auri was Ariel. I promised I'd never say it, and I haven't." He smiled faintly. "You can draw your own conclusions. That's what scribes are for."

Chronicler stared at him. Then, slowly, he looked down at the page in front of him. At the tiny, crabbed cipher that recorded every word the innkeeper had spoken.

He put down his pen.

"I won't write it," he said.

The innkeeper raised an eyebrow.

"The ring. The crest. The banner." Chronicler's voice was quiet but firm. "I'll leave it out. I'll write that you found a signet ring among her things, and that you promised never to learn whose it was, and that you kept that promise. That's all."

"That's not the true story."

"It's true enough." Chronicler looked up. "It's the truth she'd want told."

The innkeeper looked at him for a long moment. Something moved in his face, something warm and surprised.

"You're an interesting man, Devan Lochees," he said. "I think I underestimated you."

"Most people do." Chronicler picked up his pen again. He looked at the page. Then, carefully, he drew a line through a section of text. "I've found it makes them talk more."

Bast laughed. It was a sudden, delighted sound, and it seemed to surprise even him. He clapped his hand over his mouth, but his eyes were dancing.

"Oh, I like him," he said through his fingers. "Reshi, I like him. Can we keep him?"

"He's not a stray cat, Bast."

"No, but he could be. I'd feed him. I'd take him for walks." Bast grinned. "I'd teach him tricks."

Chronicler gave him a look of great dignity and did not dignify that with an answer.

---

"The line," said Chronicler, when Bast had subsided. "About princesses. The one in the stories." He hesitated. "People say you stole princesses back from sleeping barrow kings. I always assumed it was one of those things that grew in the telling. A rescue of some lord's daughter from bandits, turned into a fairy tale."

"Most of the stories about me are like that," said the innkeeper. "Something small that grew."

"But this one wasn't."

"No." The innkeeper looked down at his hands. "This one was smaller than the stories. And larger." He was quiet for a moment. "It was one princess, not many. And one barrow king, who was not wicked, only lonely. And I did not fight him. I sang him to sleep."

"That's not how the stories tell it."

"The stories never tell it right. That's the trouble with stories." The innkeeper smiled, a little sadly. "In the stories, there's a great battle. I fight my way through a horde of undead warriors with a flaming sword and carry the princess out on my shoulder while the barrow collapses behind me. There's usually a dragon, for some reason. People like dragons."

"There was a draccus in Trebon," said Chronicler.

"And people have never stopped talking about it." The innkeeper shook his head. "No. What really happened was quieter than that. It was a lullaby, sung in the dark to a tired old man who had been waiting too long for something that might never come." He paused. "I think about that sometimes. More than I'd like."

"Why?"

The innkeeper looked around the common room of the Waystone. At the empty tables, the dark wood of the bar, the bottles on their shelves. At the sword hanging over the hearth.

"Because," he said quietly, "I know how he felt."

---

Bast had stopped smiling.

He sat in his backward chair with his arms folded across its back and his chin resting on his arms, and he looked at the innkeeper with an expression that Chronicler could not read. It was not sympathy, exactly. It was something older and more complicated than that. Something with teeth.

"Reshi," he said softly. "You never told me about the Sleeper."

"I've never told anyone about the Sleeper."

"But you should have told *me*." There was an edge to Bast's voice. "You know what that means. What he said to you. *Key*. You know what that means."

"I know what it means now. I didn't then."

"But you know now." Bast leaned forward. "And he told you. He *warned* you. *Do not come to the door*. And you—"

"Bast."

The word was very quiet. It was not a command, exactly. But Bast stopped. He sat back in his chair. His jaw was tight and his eyes were bright, but he did not say anything more.

The innkeeper looked at him for a long moment. Then he sighed.

"Yes," he said. "He warned me. And yes, I went anyway. You know that. You were there, at the end." He turned his cup a quarter turn on the table. "But we're not at the end yet. We're not even at the middle. And if you keep interrupting, we'll never get there at all."

Bast's mouth twisted. For a moment he looked as if he wanted to say something else. Then he looked away, out the window, at the thin grey line of smoke still leaning above the trees.

"Fine," he said. "Go on."

The innkeeper looked at Chronicler. "Where was I?"

Chronicler glanced at his notes. "Auri was well again. You'd promised not to learn who she was."

"Yes." The innkeeper nodded slowly. "Yes. And the term went on. And for a while, a few spans, things were quiet. I went to class. I worked in the Fishery. I played at the Eolian. I worked on my father's song." He smiled faintly. "It was the last quiet time I ever had. I didn't know that, of course. You never know when the last quiet time is. If you did, you'd savor it more."

He was silent for a moment.

"And then," he said, "Devi came to me with a plan."

---

Outside the window of the Waystone, the sun had passed its height and begun its long slow slide toward evening. The shadows of the trees stretched out across the fields like fingers. A few farmers were working in the distance, bent over their rows, their shapes small and dark against the pale earth.

At the edge of the wood, where the smoke rose, something moved.

It was very small. Very quick. A flicker of darkness at the edge of the trees, there and gone again, like a bird darting between branches. No one in the fields looked up. No one noticed.

Bast noticed. He was sitting very still at the table, his eyes on the window, his face blank. He watched the place where the darkness had flickered for a long time.

Then he looked back at the innkeeper, who was talking now about Devi's rooms above the butcher's shop, and a map spread out on a table, and a plan that was very clever and very foolish in equal measure.

Bast listened. But his hand, resting on the table, had curled into a fist. And beneath the table, where no one could see, his other hand had slipped into the pocket of his coat, and closed around something small and hard and cold.
