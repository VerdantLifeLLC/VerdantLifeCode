# Severen

I left Nell with Hallow's Company.

It was not an easy decision, and it was not mine alone. On the morning after the fire, the headman of Aldermoor came out to the Deller farm with half the village behind him, and they stood in the yard and looked at what was left, and some of the women wept and some of the men swore and the priest said prayers over the frozen mud. They asked me what had happened. I told them there had been a fire. It was true, as far as it went.

They asked about the girl. I told them she was with us. The headman, a heavy grey man named Tobin with a farmer's hands and a farmer's patience, came back to the camp with me and looked at Nell sleeping in Mirra's lap and was quiet for a long time.

"There's no kin," he said at last. "Deller's people are all dead or gone to Atur. His wife's people were from somewhere in the east. Nobody knows where." He looked at Mirra. "She could go to the church. The priest'd take her."

Mirra did not say anything. She only held Nell a little tighter.

"Or," said Tobin slowly, "she could go with you. If you'd have her."

Tessit and Mirra looked at each other. There was a whole conversation in that look, years of it, the kind of conversation that only happens between people who have been married a very long time.

"We'd have her," said Tessit.

And so Nell of the Deller farm became Nell of Hallow's Company, Edema Ruh, one family under the sky. When I left them two days later at the crossroads outside Rannish, she was sitting on the seat of the lead wagon beside Mirra, wrapped in a blanket, holding the reins of the old grey horse while Mirra showed her how. She did not smile. She did not wave. But she watched me go, the way Auri had, and I felt her eyes on my back for a long way down the road.

I kept the scrap of knotted cloth. Mirra had tried to give it back to Nell, and Nell had shaken her head and pushed it into my hands.

"You keep it," she had said. "You'll know what to do."

I did not know what to do. But I kept it.

---

It took me twelve more days to reach Severen.

I will not tell you much about that journey. It was long and hard and mostly uneventful. I crossed the border into Vintas at a place called Tiller's Ford, where a bored soldier looked at my letter from the Maer and waved me through. I walked through the rolling green hills of northern Vintas, past vineyards and orchards and great stone houses set back from the road behind high walls. I slept in inns and haylofts and once, memorably, in a church, where the priest caught me in the morning and tried to convert me to a sect of Tehlinism I had never heard of, which apparently involved a great deal of fasting and a total abstinence from music. I declined.

I lit Kilvin's candle every night. It burned yellow every night. I did not know whether that made me feel better or worse.

And every night, before I slept, I took out the scrap of knotted cloth and looked at it.

I could read some of it now. More than I had been able to on the first night. I had spent hours with it, turning it over in my hands, puzzling out the knots one by one the way Denna had taught me. It was slow work. Many of the knots were ones I had never seen before, and some of them were so complex that I could not follow them at all. But I could make out pieces.

*Keeping*. That one was everywhere, tied over and over into the fabric. *Door*. *Name*. *Line*. A knot that I thought meant *stone*, though I was not sure. And one, near the center of the scrap, that I recognized with a jolt of cold certainty. It was the same knot I had seen in the blue book in the Archives, beside the names of the Lackless family. The same knot I had seen in the heart of Kilvin's candle.

I did not know what it meant. But I knew, now, that it meant something. It was a mark. A signature. A sign that said *this belongs to the keeping*.

The Deller family had been keepers. Minor ones, perhaps. Distant cousins of some old line. But they had kept a cloth that recorded something about the doors, and they had kept it for generations, and they had made everyone who looked at it write their name in a book.

And then Denna had come. And then the Chandrian.

I did not know how to feel about that. So I did not feel anything. I put the cloth away and blew out the candle and lay in the dark and did not sleep.

---

Severen is a city built on a cliff. I have told you about it before, I think. The high city sits on top of the cliff, with its great houses and its gardens and its wide clean streets. The low city sprawls at the bottom, crowded and dirty and loud. And in between, connecting them, there is a single road cut into the face of the cliff, a road so steep and narrow that carts have to be hauled up it by teams of oxen.

The Maer's palace sits at the very top of the high city, at the very edge of the cliff. From its windows, you can look down on the whole of Severen spread out below you like a map, and beyond it the great flat plain of the river valley, stretching away green and gold to the horizon.

I climbed the cliff road on foot, as I had the first time I came to Severen, more than a year before. It was hard going. By the time I reached the top, my legs were burning and my shirt was soaked with sweat and my breath was rasping in my throat. I stopped at the top of the road and leaned against the wall and looked back down the way I had come, and I remembered the first time I had stood there. How young I had been. How foolish. How sure of myself.

I did not feel young now. I did not feel sure of anything.

---

The Maer received me in his study, a long room lined with books and maps, with tall windows looking out over the cliff. He was sitting at his desk when I was shown in, and he rose to greet me with a smile.

He looked well. Much better than when I had first met him, when Caudicus had been poisoning him slowly and he had been thin and grey and trembling. He had filled out. His color was good. His eyes were bright and sharp and clear. He moved like a man who had many years ahead of him and intended to use every one.

"Kvothe," he said warmly. "Welcome back to Severen. I did not expect to see you so soon."

"Nor did I, your grace."

"Sit, sit." He waved me to a chair and sat down himself. "Stapes tells me you walked up the cliff road. You should have sent word. I would have sent a carriage."

"I wanted to walk."

He raised an eyebrow. "Did you? How very Ruh of you." He smiled to take the sting out of it. "Well. You are here now. Tell me why."

I had thought a great deal about how to answer that question. I had rehearsed it on the road, a dozen different ways. I had thought about lying. I had thought about telling him the whole truth. In the end, I told him something in between.

"I was expelled from the University," I said.

The Maer's face did not change. But his eyes sharpened.

"Expelled," he said. "For what?"

"For going somewhere I was forbidden to go. In the Archives."

"And what did you find there, in this forbidden place?"

I hesitated. The Maer watched me with his sharp clear eyes, and I knew that he was reading my face the way I had learned to read faces in his court. He was very good at it. Better than me.

"Old things," I said at last. "Old records. About families. About old lines." I paused. "About the Lackless family, your grace."

Something moved in the Maer's face. It was very small, very quickly controlled. But I saw it.

"My wife's family," he said.

"Yes."

He was quiet for a long moment. Then he rose from his desk and went to the window and stood looking out over the cliff, his hands clasped behind his back.

"You know," he said, "that my wife's family is very old. Older than mine. Older than the crown of Vintas itself. They were lords in these lands before there was a Vintas, before the Aturan Empire, before anyone kept records." He paused. "And you know, I think, that they have their secrets. Every old family does. But the Lackless family has more than most."

"I know about the box, your grace."

He turned from the window and looked at me. "The box."

"The one Meluan showed me. The one with no lid and no lock."

He was silent for a long time.

"She has not shown it to anyone since," he said at last. "Not even me. She keeps it locked away in her rooms, and she will not speak of it." He came back to his desk and sat down. "I asked her once what was in it. She told me that no one knew. That no one had ever known. That it had been passed down in her family for longer than anyone could remember, and that it was never to be opened." He looked at me. "Why do you ask about it?"

"Because I think I know what's in it."

The Maer leaned forward. "Tell me."

I took a deep breath. "A key," I said. "A key of stone."

---

I told him some of it. Not all. I did not tell him about the Chandrian, or my parents, or the Book of Watches. I did not tell him about the Sleeper, or the voice behind the door. I did not tell him that his wife was my aunt.

But I told him about the line of Loeclos. About the old family that had kept a door, and the key that opened it. About the rhyme the children sang, and how it might be more than a rhyme. I told him that I thought there were people who wanted that key, and that they might be dangerous.

He listened without interrupting. When I was done, he sat back in his chair and steepled his fingers and looked at me over them for a long time.

"This is a very strange story," he said.

"I know, your grace."

"If anyone else had told it to me, I would have had them thrown out of my study." He paused. "But you are not anyone else. You saved my life. You found the bandits in the Eld. You helped me win my wife." He smiled faintly. "And you have a way of being right about strange things, even when they seem absurd."

"Thank you, your grace."

"Do not thank me yet." His smile faded. "I will tell you something now. In confidence. You will not repeat it."

"Of course."

He leaned forward. "Two months ago, someone tried to enter my wife's rooms at night. They were very skilled. They passed three sets of guards and two locked doors without anyone seeing them. They were discovered only because my wife could not sleep, and was sitting up in the dark, and heard the latch of her bedroom door turn." He paused. "She screamed. The guards came. The intruder was gone. Nothing was taken."

"What were they looking for?"

"My wife believes they were looking for the box." The Maer's face was grim. "She has not let it out of her sight since. She keeps it with her day and night. She sleeps with it under her pillow." He shook his head. "It is not good for her. She is with child, Kvothe. Six months gone. She should be resting, not lying awake every night with a box under her pillow, waiting for someone to come for it."

I was quiet for a moment. Then I said, "Do you know who it was?"

"No. But I have my suspicions." He looked at me. "You have heard, I suppose, about the deaths in the royal family."

"I've heard some."

"Four now. Four in two years. All of them in the line of succession. All of them accidents, or illnesses, or misfortunes." His voice was flat. "And the king himself is ill. Very ill. His physicians say it is a wasting sickness, and that he may not see the summer."

"Poison?"

"Perhaps. Perhaps not. I have had my own people look into it, and they have found nothing." He was silent for a moment. "But I have noticed something. Each of those deaths has moved one man closer to the throne. One man, and one family."

"Jakis," I said.

The Maer looked at me sharply. "You know."

"I know Ambrose. His son. We were at the University together."

"Ah." Something like amusement flickered in his eyes. "Yes. I believe I remember hearing about that. Something about a broken arm, and a fire, and a great deal of shouting." He waved a hand. "Baron Jakis is a powerful man. Very powerful. He has friends in every corner of the court. And his son is now fifth in line for the throne."

"Fifth?"

"Fifth, as of a month ago. Another cousin died. A fall from a balcony, at a party in Renere. Quite drunk, by all accounts." The Maer's voice was dry. "Everyone was very sad."

I sat back in my chair. Fifth. When I had last seen Ambrose, in the snow on the steps of the Mains, he had been seventh. In the months since, two more people had died.

"Your grace," I said. "I don't think Baron Jakis is doing this. Not alone."

"Oh?"

"I think someone is helping him. Someone very patient. Someone very old." I hesitated. "Someone who wants more than the throne."

"What more is there than the throne?"

I did not answer. I thought about the box under Meluan's pillow. About the key of stone. About the door beneath the Lackless house, that could be opened only by the line.

The Maer watched me. He did not press. He was a patient man himself, in his way.

"You will stay here," he said at last. "In the palace. I will have Stapes prepare rooms for you. You will be my guest, and my advisor, and my eyes and ears in matters I do not understand." He paused. "And you will speak to my wife."

"Your grace?"

"About the box. About her family. About whatever it is you know that you have not told me." He smiled faintly. "Do not look so surprised. I know when a man is holding something back. I have been dealing with courtiers my whole life." His smile faded. "She is frightened, Kvothe. More frightened than I have ever seen her. And she will not tell me why. Perhaps she will tell you."

---

Stapes showed me to my rooms. They were the same rooms I had stayed in before, a set of three connecting chambers in the eastern wing of the palace, with tall windows that looked out over the gardens. They were cleaner than I remembered. Someone had put fresh flowers on the table and fresh linen on the bed.

Stapes stood in the doorway after he had shown me in, his hands folded, his face as calm and pleasant as ever. He was the Maer's manservant, a small precise man who ran the palace with the efficiency of a general running a campaign. I had not liked him when I first met him. I had come to respect him a great deal.

"Is there anything else you require, sir?" he asked.

"No. Thank you, Stapes."

He did not leave. He stood there for a moment longer, looking at me with his calm, pleasant face.

"If I may say so, sir," he said, "it is very good to have you back. His grace has been—" He hesitated, choosing his words with care. "His grace has been troubled, of late. And her ladyship even more so. I think your presence will be a comfort to them both."

"I hope so, Stapes."

He nodded. Then he paused again, as if he had one more thing to say and was not sure whether he should say it.

"Sir," he said. "There is a gentleman at court who has been asking after you. He asked the moment you were announced, and he asked me to tell you that he would be pleased to see you at your earliest convenience." A faint smile touched his lips. "He said you would know him by his stick."

My heart gave a strange little lurch.

"Bredon," I said.

"Yes, sir. He has rooms in the western wing. He said that he would be in the small garden tomorrow afternoon, with a board, if you cared to join him." Stapes bowed. "Good night, sir."

He left. I stood alone in my rooms, looking at the door he had closed behind him.

Bredon. Of course. Bredon was always at court. He had been at court when I first came to Severen, and he would be at court long after I left. He was one of those people who seemed to be part of a place, the way a statue in a square is part of it, or an old tree.

I thought about seeing him again, and I felt something warm and glad rise up in me. Bredon had been kind to me when I was new at court and did not know how anything worked. He had taught me tak, and patience, and the art of losing gracefully. He had been something like a friend. Something like a teacher. Something, if I am honest, like a grandfather, though I had never had one and did not know what that was supposed to feel like.

I thought about seeing him on the steps of the Mains, in the snow, standing behind Ambrose.

The warmth in me flickered. Just a little.

I told myself it meant nothing. I told myself he had explained it perfectly well: an old acquaintance of Baron Jakis, escorting the son home. I told myself that Bredon knew everyone.

I went to bed. I lay in the dark, in the soft clean bed with the fresh linen, and I listened to the silence of the palace, and I did not sleep for a long time.

And when I did sleep, I dreamed of a tak board. Black stones and white, laid out in patterns on a grid of lines. I was playing, and I was losing, and I did not know why. Every move I made seemed good. Every move seemed to strengthen my position. And yet, stone by stone, move by move, I was being surrounded.

Across the board, I could not see my opponent's face. Only his hands, old and spotted and gentle, placing the stones one by one. And beside the board, leaning against the table, a walking stick of dark wood, with a head of gleaming silver.
