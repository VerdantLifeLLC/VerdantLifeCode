# Chapter Four: The Tell-tale

The Fishery smelled the way it always had: of hot metal and coal smoke, of quenching oil and scorched leather, of sweat and effort and the particular sharp tang that clings to any place where men and women make things with their hands. I walked through the great doors the next morning and breathed it in, and I felt something in my shoulders unknot that I had not known was knotted.

Kilvin was in his office. He was always in his office, as far as I could tell. I sometimes wondered if he slept there, curled up on the floor beneath his desk like a great bear in a cave. When I knocked on the open door, he looked up from the thing he was working on, a tangle of copper wire and glass that looked like a bird's nest made by a very angry bird, and his face split into a broad grin beneath his beard.

"Kvothe!" he rumbled. "Good. You are here. Come, come. Sit."

I sat. Kilvin set aside his tangle of wire with great care and turned his whole body to face me. Kilvin never did anything by halves. When he looked at you, he looked at you with the whole of himself.

"You have been in Vintas," he said.

"Yes, Master Kilvin."

"You have made things there?"

I hesitated. "A few things. Small things. Mostly I was busy with other matters."

He frowned. "Busy. Everyone is always busy. But hands that do not make things forget how." He held up his own hands, broad and scarred and blackened with a lifetime of work. "These hands have not forgotten in forty years. Because every day, I make something. Even if it is only a small thing. Even if it is only a nail." He lowered his hands. "You understand?"

"I understand."

"Good." He leaned forward. "Now. I have a project. I think it will interest you."

He reached beneath his desk and pulled out a small wooden box. He set it on the desk between us and opened the lid. Inside, nestled in straw, was a candle.

It was a plain candle, as far as I could tell. White wax, a little yellowed with age, about the length of my hand. The wick was black and curled. There was nothing remarkable about it at all.

"This," Kilvin said, "is a very old candle."

"How old?"

"Nobody knows. It was found in the Archives, in a sealed box, with no label. Lorren gave it to me, many years ago, to see if I could discover anything about it." He took the candle out of the box and held it up to the light. "It is not an ordinary candle. Watch."

He set it on the desk. He took a match from his pocket, struck it, and held it to the wick.

The wick caught. The flame rose, small and steady and yellow.

And then, slowly, the flame turned blue.

I stared at it. I could not help it. My heart had started to pound, and my mouth had gone dry, and every part of me wanted to leap up out of the chair and run. I did not. But it was a near thing.

Blue flame. The sign of the Chandrian.

"What is it?" I said. My voice sounded strange in my own ears.

"I do not know." Kilvin watched the flame with a scholar's interest. "This is the question. Most flames are yellow because of the impurities in the fuel. Sodium, mostly. A pure flame is blue. But this wax is not pure. I have tested it. It is ordinary tallow and beeswax, with some impurities. It should burn yellow." He tapped the desk beside the candle. "And yet."

"And yet it burns blue."

"Not always." Kilvin leaned closer to the flame. "Sometimes it burns yellow. For days, weeks. And then, for no reason I can find, it will turn blue. And then yellow again." He shook his head. "I have tested it for twenty years. I have recorded every time it changes color. I cannot find a pattern."

"May I see your records?"

He looked at me. Something in my voice must have caught his attention, because his eyes narrowed slightly. "Why?"

I hesitated. How much could I tell him? Kilvin was a practical man. He believed in things he could measure and test and hold in his hands. He did not believe in fairy stories.

But he had also kept this candle for twenty years, testing and retesting it, trying to understand why it did something no candle should do. That was not the behavior of a man who dismissed mysteries.

"I've heard stories," I said carefully, "about blue flame. Old stories. They say that when a flame burns blue, it means something is near. Something dangerous."

"What kind of something?"

"The Chandrian."

Kilvin was silent for a moment. Then he laughed, a deep rumbling sound. "Children's stories," he said. "Bogeymen to frighten little ones into bed."

"Maybe," I said. "But I'd still like to see your records."

He looked at me for a long moment. His eyes were very dark, and very shrewd, and I remembered, suddenly, that Kilvin was not a stupid man. He was a practical man, and a careful one, but he had not become the Master Artificer by dismissing things he did not understand.

"Very well," he said at last. "I will show you."

---

The records filled three large ledgers. Kilvin brought them out of a cabinet in the corner of his office and set them on the desk, and I spent the rest of the morning poring over them while he went back to his tangle of wire.

He had been meticulous. Every time the candle changed color, he had recorded the date, the time, the weather, the phase of the moon, the temperature in the room, and a dozen other details. He had noted who was in the Fishery at the time and what they were working on. He had noted the condition of the candle itself, how much it had burned down, whether the wick needed trimming. He had even, in a few places, noted his own mood.

At first, I could see no pattern either. The changes seemed random. Blue for an hour on a cold winter evening, then yellow for a month. Blue for three days in the height of summer, then yellow for a year. There was nothing to connect them.

But then I started looking at the dates.

I had spent a great deal of time in the Archives. I had read a great many histories, and a great many records, and a great many old accounts of strange happenings. And as I looked at the dates in Kilvin's ledgers, some of them began to seem familiar.

Here: a span of three days, eleven years ago, when the candle had burned blue. I knew that span. It was when a farmstead near Hallowfell had burned to the ground with the whole family inside. Nobody had ever discovered what had caused the fire. The local constable's report, which I had found in a dusty box in the Stacks, said that the neighbors had seen strange lights in the sky that night, and that the ashes had been cold to the touch by morning.

Here: a single evening, seven years ago. I knew that one too. It was when a wedding party in the hills north of Anilin had been slaughtered. Every guest, every servant, every musician. The bride and groom had been found holding hands, their faces pale and peaceful, as if they had simply gone to sleep.

Here: a span of four days, six years ago.

I stopped reading. My hands had begun to shake.

Six years ago. Late summer. I knew that date. I knew it the way you know the shape of your own scars.

It was the day my troupe had died.

---

"Master Kilvin," I said. My voice was very steady. I was proud of that, later. "Where was this candle six years ago? In late summer?"

Kilvin looked up from his work. "Here," he said. "In this office. It has always been here."

"Always? It's never been moved?"

"Never." He frowned. "Why?"

I did not answer. I was thinking.

The candle had burned blue on the day my troupe died. But my troupe had died hundreds of miles from here, in a wood near the border of the Commonwealth. The candle had been here, in this office, in the Fishery, the whole time.

So the candle was not showing when the Chandrian were near *it*. It was showing when the Chandrian were near *anyone*. When they were active, when they were abroad in the world.

Or at least, when they were doing something.

I looked back at the ledgers. At the dates, scattered across twenty years like stars in a dark sky. Some of them I recognized. Many of them I did not. But I was willing to bet that if I went into the Archives and searched through the records for each of those dates, I would find something. A fire. A massacre. A family found dead in their beds with frost on their eyelashes in the middle of summer.

"Master Kilvin," I said. "I think I know what this candle is."

---

I told him. I told him about my troupe, and about the Chandrian, and about the signs. I did not tell him everything. I did not tell him about the conversation I had heard in the firelight, or about the man with black eyes who had asked me whose parents had been singing the wrong songs. But I told him enough.

Kilvin listened without interrupting. When I finished, he was silent for a long time.

"You believe this," he said at last. It was not a question.

"I've seen them, Master Kilvin. With my own eyes."

"And you think this candle shows when they are near."

"Not near. Active. When they're doing something." I tapped the ledger. "I can prove it. Give me a span in the Archives, and I'll find a record for every one of these dates."

Kilvin looked at the candle, still burning blue on his desk. Then he reached out and snuffed it with his fingers. The smoke curled up, grey and thin.

"If this is true," he said slowly, "then this is a very dangerous thing."

"Yes."

"And a very useful one."

"Yes."

He looked at me. "What would you do with it?"

I had been thinking about that since the moment the flame turned blue. "I'd study it," I said. "I'd find out how it works. Why it works. And then I'd make more of them."

"More?"

"A candle that burns blue when the Chandrian are near. A lamp. A ring. A stone you could carry in your pocket." I leaned forward. "If I could make something like this, something small and portable, something that could warn people, then maybe nobody else would have to—"

I stopped. Kilvin was looking at me with an expression I had never seen on his face before. It took me a moment to recognize it. It was compassion.

"Maybe nobody else would have to lose their family," he said quietly.

I did not answer. I could not.

Kilvin nodded slowly. He picked up the candle and put it back in its box and closed the lid. Then he pushed the box across the desk toward me.

"Take it," he said. "Study it. Find out how it works. And if you can make more—" He paused. "If you can make more, I will help you."

I stared at him. "Master Kilvin—"

"I have kept this candle for twenty years," he said. "I have tested it and measured it and recorded it. And in all that time, I have never understood it. Perhaps you will." He smiled faintly. "And if you do not, at least it will be in the hands of someone who cares."

---

I took the box back to my workbench in the Fishery and set it down and sat staring at it for a long time.

My workbench was just as I had left it. My tools were still there, in their places. The half-finished projects I had abandoned when I left for Vintas were still there too, covered in a fine layer of dust. Someone had swept around them, but no one had touched them. That was the unwritten law of the Fishery: a man's bench was his own, and you did not touch another man's work.

I opened the box. I took out the candle. I held it in my hands.

It was an ordinary candle. Tallow and beeswax, a little yellowed with age. I could feel the faint oily smoothness of it against my fingers. It smelled faintly of honey and of old dust.

But there was something else. Something beneath the surface. It was hard to describe. The best I can say is that it felt the way a word feels when it is on the tip of your tongue. Something almost there. Something just out of reach.

I closed my eyes and let my mind slip into the Heart of Stone.

It is a difficult thing to explain, the Heart of Stone. It is a way of thinking, a way of holding the mind apart from itself, so that you can see clearly without being swayed by fear or hope or anger. It is the first thing any sympathist learns, and the last thing he ever masters. I had learned it young, from a man named Abenthy who had traveled with my troupe when I was a boy. I had used it every day since.

In the Heart of Stone, I looked at the candle.

And I saw something.

It was very faint. The barest trace of a binding, woven into the wax itself. Not a sympathetic binding, not like anything I had ever learned. It was older than that. Stranger. It was like looking at a piece of cloth and suddenly seeing that it was not woven at all but knotted, every thread tied to every other thread in a pattern so complex that it made my eyes ache.

I opened my eyes. My head was pounding.

I looked at the candle for a long time. Then I put it back in its box and closed the lid and sat there with my hands flat on the bench, breathing slowly, waiting for the pounding in my head to fade.

Knots. Not sympathy. Knots.

I did not know what that meant. Not yet. But I knew, the way you sometimes know things without knowing how, that it meant something important.

---

"You look like someone stepped on your grave."

I looked up. Fela was standing beside my bench, her arms folded, her head tilted to one side. She was wearing a leather apron over her clothes and there was a smudge of soot on her cheek. Her dark hair was pulled back in a braid.

"Fela," I said. "Hello."

"Hello yourself." She smiled. "Sim told me you were back. I didn't believe him until I saw you walk in this morning. You've been sitting there staring at that box for an hour."

"Have I?"

"You have." She came closer and leaned against the edge of my bench. "What's in it?"

I hesitated. Then I opened the box and showed her.

She looked at the candle for a long moment. Then she looked at me. "It's a candle."

"It is."

"You've been staring at a candle for an hour."

"It's a very interesting candle."

She laughed. "Of course it is." She reached out and touched it with one finger, lightly, the way you might touch a sleeping animal. Then she drew her hand back, frowning. "That's strange."

"What?"

"It feels—" She hesitated. "It feels like stone. Doesn't it? Not like wax. Like stone."

I stared at her. Fela had been studying naming with Elodin for as long as I had. Longer, in fact. And she had done something I had never managed: she had found a name. The name of stone. I had heard it from Sim, who had heard it from her, who had been reluctant to talk about it. She had called the name once, by accident, the way I had called the wind. She had split a stone in half with a word.

"What do you mean?" I asked.

She shook her head, still frowning. "I don't know. It's just a feeling." She looked at the candle again. "There's something in it. Something that doesn't belong. It feels like the way a stone feels when there's something hidden inside it. A fossil, or a crystal, or a crack." She looked up at me. "Where did you get it?"

"Kilvin. It was found in the Archives."

"The Archives." She nodded slowly. "That makes sense. The Archives are full of old things." She was quiet for a moment. Then she said, "Kvothe. Can I tell you something strange?"

"Of course."

"Sometimes, when I'm in the Archives, I feel like the stone is listening." She laughed, embarrassed. "That sounds mad, I know. But it's true. The whole building is made of stone, and sometimes when I'm there, especially down in the lower levels, I feel like it's listening to me. Like it's waiting for something." She shook her head. "Elodin says that's what happens when you learn a name. You start to hear things you couldn't hear before. And it doesn't always make you happy."

I thought about Auri, and the doors that were sleeping. I thought about my dream, and the breathing on the other side of the stone.

"What do you think it's waiting for?" I asked.

Fela looked at me. Her eyes were dark and serious.

"I don't know," she said. "But I don't think it's something good."

---

That night, alone in my room under the eaves, I lit the candle.

I do not know why I did it. It was foolish. If the candle burned blue, it would mean the Chandrian were abroad somewhere in the world, doing something terrible, and there would be nothing I could do about it. And if it burned yellow, it would mean nothing at all.

But I lit it anyway. I set it on the windowsill and struck a match and held it to the wick, and I watched the flame catch and grow and steady.

It burned yellow. A warm, ordinary, homely yellow, the color of every candle I had ever seen.

I sat on the edge of my bed and watched it for a long time. I watched it until the moon rose over the rooftops and the stars came out and the sounds of the University faded into the deep quiet of the night.

It stayed yellow.

At last, when my eyes were growing heavy and the candle had burned down a finger's width, I leaned forward to snuff it.

And just before my fingers touched the wick, the flame flickered. It was only for a moment. A single heartbeat. But in that heartbeat, I saw it turn.

Not blue. Not quite. It turned a pale, cold, silvery color, like the light of the moon on snow. And then it was yellow again.

I sat very still. My heart was pounding.

I had never seen that color before. Kilvin had never recorded it in his ledgers. I did not know what it meant.

But I knew, the way you sometimes know things, that something, somewhere, had just woken up.
