# What Mirra Remembered

Mirra told stories while she cooked. That was the way of it, in Hallow's Company. Tessit told stories on the road, walking beside the lead horse, to pass the miles. Brin told stories at night, after the fiddle was put away, stories so long and so wandering that half the troupe was asleep before he reached the middle. Illa told stories to the children, very short and very sharp, with teeth in them.

But Mirra told stories while she cooked. She stood at the great iron pot over the fire, stirring, and she talked, and anyone who wanted to listen could sit on the upturned buckets and crates around the fire and listen.

I sat and listened. Every night, that summer, on the road west. I did not know why. I did not know Mirra, or the troupe, or the stories. But something in me wanted to be near the fire, and the pot, and the voice.

And one night, a little past midsummer, somewhere in the hills near the border of the Commonwealth, she told a story about my mother.

---

"She couldn't cook," said Mirra. She was stirring a stew of rabbit and wild onions and something she had found growing by the road that she said was good for the blood. "Your mother. Laurian. Did I tell you that?"

"Yes," I said. "You said she couldn't boil water."

"She couldn't. It was a scandal. Every Ruh woman can cook. It's the first thing you learn, before you learn to sing." Mirra shook her head, smiling. "But she'd never learned. She'd grown up in a great house, with servants. Nobody ever let her near a kitchen." She tasted the stew. "The first time she tried, she set a wagon on fire."

Across the fire, Nell giggled. Lark, asleep in her lap, stirred and was still.

"She set a wagon on fire?" said Nell.

"The back of one. The canvas." Mirra added a pinch of salt. "We'd camped with Arliden's troupe, that summer, outside Hallowfell. The summer she was new. She was trying to impress everyone. Arliden's mother was still alive then, old Beth, and she was very fierce, and she didn't approve of Arliden marrying a girl from a great house who couldn't cook. So Laurian decided to make supper. For the whole troupe. By herself." Mirra laughed softly. "She put the pot too close to the wagon and forgot about it and went off to practice a song, and by the time anyone noticed, the canvas was on fire."

"What happened?" said Nell.

"Arliden put it out. With his cloak. Burned his hands doing it." Mirra's smile softened. "And then he came and found her, where she was practicing, and she was crying, because she thought old Beth would make him send her away. And he knelt down in front of her with his burned hands and said, *I don't care if you can cook. I didn't marry you to cook.*"

"What did he marry her for?" said Nell.

Mirra looked across the fire at me. Her round face was very gentle.

"He said, *I married you because when you sing, I can hear the whole world singing with you.*"

---

I did not say anything. I sat on my upturned bucket with my hands on my knees and looked at the fire.

I did not remember. I did not remember my father's hands, or my mother's voice, or old Beth, or a wagon on fire outside Hallowfell. I did not remember anything.

But something in me was listening. Very hard. The way you listen for footsteps in an empty house.

"Tell me another," I said.

Mirra looked at me for a long moment. Then she smiled, and went back to stirring.

---

She told me about the first time my mother sang for the troupe. Outside Hallowfell, the same summer, at the midsummer fair. How she stood up on the back of Arliden's wagon in a borrowed dress and sang an old ballad about a woman waiting at a window, and how the whole fair went quiet. The jugglers stopped juggling. The peddlers stopped shouting. The drunk men stopped fighting. And when she finished, there was a silence so deep you could hear the river running, a mile off.

She told me about the time my mother fell out of a tree. At twenty-two, grown and married, with a child on the way. She had climbed it to get a better view of a hawk, and a branch had broken, and she had landed in a thornbush, and she had laughed so hard Arliden thought she had hit her head.

She told me about the night my mother told the troupe that she was with child. How Arliden had stood up in the firelight and let out a whoop so loud it frightened the horses, and then picked her up and spun her around and fallen over backward into a creek.

"And then you came," said Mirra. "In the autumn. In a wagon, on the road outside Anilin, in the rain. With the whole troupe standing outside in the wet, waiting." She smiled. "I was there. We'd met up with Arliden's troupe again, that year. I held your mother's hand."

I looked at her.

"You were there," I said. "When I was born."

"I was there." Mirra's eyes were wet. "You came out screaming. Loudest baby I ever heard. Your mother laughed and said, *Oh, he's going to be a singer.* And your father said, *He's going to be trouble.*" She laughed through her tears. "They were both right."

---

The fire had burned low. Nell was asleep, curled against Mirra's side with Lark in her arms. Across the camp, Brin was telling one of his endless stories to Kellen and Wat, and they were both nodding off.

Mirra sat down on an upturned crate beside me. She did not say anything for a while.

"There's one more," she said at last. Her voice was quiet. "One I haven't told anyone. Not even Tessit." She looked at the fire. "Your mother asked me not to. But I think—" She hesitated. "I think you should have it. Now. When you've got nothing else."

I waited.

"It was the last time I saw her," said Mirra. "The summer before. A year before they—" She stopped. "Before. We'd met up with Arliden's troupe on the road near the Eld. We camped together for a span. And one night, very late, your mother came and found me by the fire, the way you're sitting now. And she sat down beside me. And she didn't say anything for a long time."

The fire crackled.

"And then she said, *Mirra, if anything happens to us, will you look after him?*" Mirra's voice cracked. "I said, *Of course. Nothing's going to happen. Don't be silly.* And she looked at me with those eyes of hers, those green eyes, the same as yours, and she said, *Promise me.*"

Mirra wiped her eyes with the back of her hand.

"I promised," she said. "And then something happened. And I didn't know. We were in the south that year. We didn't hear until the next summer, and by then you were gone. Nobody knew where. Everyone said you were dead." She looked at me. "I didn't look after you. I broke my promise."

"You didn't know."

"I should have looked. I should have gone looking." She pressed her hand against her mouth. "I looked a little. Asked around. And then I let it go. Because it was easier to think you were dead than to think you were out there somewhere, alone, and I hadn't found you."

---

I sat very still on my upturned bucket. I did not know what to say. I did not know anything.

But I remembered something. Very faint. Very far down. A city of stone and smoke and rain. Rooftops, and gutters, and a boy of twelve curled up in a doorway with his arms around a broken lute. And a voice, somewhere, a long way off, singing a Ruh lullaby in a tavern.

It was not Mirra's voice. It was not anyone's I knew. But it had been a Ruh voice, and a Ruh song, and I had lain in that doorway and listened, and I had not been quite so cold.

"You're looking after me now," I said.

Mirra looked at me.

"You cut my hair," I said. "And dyed it. And fed me. And told me stories." I looked at the fire. "You're looking after me now."

Mirra did not say anything for a long time. Then she reached out and put her round warm hand on the back of my neck, the way you touch a child, and left it there.

"Yes," she said. "Yes, I am." Her voice was thick. "Better late than never. That's what old Beth used to say. She was a terrible woman. But she was right about that."

---

We sat by the fire until it burned out. Mirra's hand on my neck. Lark and Nell asleep. Brin's voice, far off, still telling his story to no one.

I did not remember my mother. I want that understood. I did not remember her face, or her voice, or her hands. I did not remember a wagon on fire, or a woman on the back of a wagon singing, or a laugh from a thornbush.

But I had them now. In Mirra's words. Set down in me like stones in a riverbed. Not my memories. Hers. But true.

And when, years later, I told this story to a scribe at a table by a hearth, and my name came back to me piece by piece, those stones were there. Waiting. And my own memories settled around them, the way water settles around stones.

I have never been able to tell, since, which memories of my mother are mine and which are Mirra's.

I have decided that it does not matter.
