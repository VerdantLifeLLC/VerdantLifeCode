# Myr Tariniel

We came over the high pass at noon on the second day, and I saw the white city.

I had been imagining it for years. Ever since I was twelve years old, sitting on the floor of the Half-Mast in Tarbean, listening to Skarpi tell the story of Lanre. I had imagined it the way you imagine any place in a story: bright and clean and beautiful, a city of white towers rising against a blue sky. I had sung it into being in the Eolian. I had made hundreds of people see it with me.

I had not imagined this.

---

The pass opened out onto a great bowl in the mountains. A valley, high and wide and bare, surrounded on every side by peaks so tall and so white they seemed to hold up the sky. The floor of the valley was flat and smooth and covered in snow, dazzling white under the clear blue sky.

And in the center of the valley, there was a city.

Or there had been. Once. A very long time ago.

It was white. That much the stories had right. White stone, all of it, so white that it hurt the eyes to look at it in the sunlight. But it was not towers. It was not gardens and fountains and great halls. It was ruins. Great tumbled heaps of white stone, scattered across the valley floor like the bones of some enormous creature. Broken walls, standing alone, with nothing on either side of them. Fallen columns, lying in the snow like felled trees. Arches with nothing above them. Stairs that led up into empty air.

It was huge. Far bigger than I had imagined. The ruins stretched across the whole valley floor, a mile or more in every direction. There must have been a hundred thousand people living there once. More. It must have been the greatest city in the world.

And it was dead. Utterly, completely dead. Nothing moved there. Nothing lived. The snow lay deep and smooth and unbroken over everything, and the wind moaned softly through the broken arches, and the sun glared down on the white stone, and there was no other sound at all.

I stood at the top of the pass and looked at it, and I could not speak.

Beside me, Bast stood very still. His face was pale. His deep blue eyes were very wide.

"Is that it?" he whispered. "The white city? From your song?"

"Yes."

"It's—" He stopped. He did not seem to know what it was.

"Yes," I said. "It is."

---

We went down into the valley. The silver string led us, floating ahead of us over the snow, pointing toward the center of the ruins. We followed it.

It was a long walk. The snow was deep, and the ruins were vast, and we had to pick our way around great heaps of fallen stone and through narrow gaps between broken walls. The wind was softer here, in the valley, sheltered by the peaks. But it was still very cold. And the silence was very deep.

I did not speak. Neither did Bast. It did not seem right, somehow, to speak in that place. It was like speaking in a graveyard. Or a church.

We passed things as we walked. Things I will never forget.

A fountain. Or what had been a fountain. A great round basin of white stone, half buried in snow, with a broken column rising from its center. There were carvings on the rim of the basin, worn almost smooth by the wind and the years. Children. Carved children, holding hands, dancing around the edge of the fountain in a ring.

I stopped beside it. I brushed the snow off the rim with my good hand and looked at the carved children for a long time.

*I was one of the children playing in the fountains*, Skarpi had said. *I was the only one who lived.*

I wondered if this was the one. I wondered if he had hidden here, under a slab of white marble, listening to his city burn.

I did not know. I would never know. There were so many fountains.

---

A great hall. Its walls still stood, most of them, though its roof was long gone. Its floor was a smooth expanse of snow, and at the far end, there was a raised platform of white stone. A stage. There were steps leading up to it, worn in the center by the passage of many feet.

*She sang in the great hall of the city*, Skarpi had said. *And people came from all over the world to hear her.*

I climbed the steps. I stood on the stage and looked out over the snow-covered floor, the broken walls, the empty sky. I tried to imagine it full of people. Full of music. Full of a woman's voice, rising like a bird.

I could not. It was too empty. Too cold. Too dead.

Bast stood at the foot of the steps, looking up at me. He did not say anything. But after a while, he held out his hand.

I came down the steps and took it, and we walked on.

---

The silver string led us to the center of the ruins. To a great open space, a plaza, surrounded on every side by broken walls and fallen columns. The snow here was smooth and unbroken, except for a single line of footprints leading from one side of the plaza to the other.

Footprints. In the snow. Fresh ones.

I stopped. I stared at them.

They were a man's footprints. Large and deep, made by heavy boots. They came from the east side of the plaza, where a great arch still stood, and they crossed the smooth snow in a straight line, and they led to the west side, where there was a building.

It was the only building in the whole city that still stood whole. A small round tower of white stone, not very tall, with a domed roof and a single door. There was smoke coming out of a chimney in the roof. A thin grey thread of it, rising straight up into the clear blue sky.

And in the doorway, there was a man.

---

He was very tall. Taller than any man I had ever seen, taller even than the Sithe. He stood in the doorway of the round tower with one hand resting on the frame, and he looked across the plaza at us.

He was old. That was the first thing I noticed, even from so far away. Not old like Bredon, weathered and worn. Old like the mountains. Old like the stone of the ruined city around him. His hair was white, and long, and fell over his shoulders like snow. His beard was white too, long and full, reaching almost to his waist. His face was lined and hard and very still.

He wore a long robe of grey wool, plain and heavy, belted with a cord. His feet were bare, in the snow.

And he had one eye.

The other was a stone.

I could see it from across the plaza, glinting in the sunlight. A smooth round stone, grey and polished, set into the socket where his left eye should have been. It caught the light and threw it back, like a mirror.

The silver string on my wrist went slack. It fell to the snow at my feet, and lay there, and did not rise again.

We had arrived.

---

The man in the doorway did not move. He stood there with his hand on the frame, looking at us. His one good eye was grey, like the stone. It was very steady.

Then he spoke.

His voice was deep and slow and very quiet. It carried across the plaza without effort, the way a voice carries across still water.

"Singer," he said. "You have come a long way."

I could not answer. My throat had closed up. I stood in the snow at the edge of the plaza, with Bast beside me and the silver string lying dead at my feet, and I stared at him.

"Come," he said. "Come in out of the cold. There is a fire, and tea. And a great deal to talk about."

He turned and went back into the tower. The door stayed open behind him.

Bast was clutching my arm. His fingers were digging into me so hard it hurt.

"Reshi," he whispered. "Reshi, is that—"

"Yes," I said.

My voice sounded very far away.

"That's Selitos."
