# Interlude: The Waterfall

Bast went out to the waystone while the innkeeper was upstairs.

He did not tell anyone he was going. He slipped out the kitchen door and across the yard, past the well and the stable, and out onto the road. It was late afternoon on the third day, and the light was thin and gold and slanting. The square of Newarre was empty. Everyone was at home, or in the fields, or at Shep's widow's house, where there was always someone sitting with her now.

The waystone stood at the corner of the crossroads, where it had always stood. Tall and grey and weathered, taller than a man, leaning very slightly to the east. There was lichen on its north face, and a crack running down the south side, and at its foot, half buried in the grass, the remains of an old stone bench where travelers used to rest.

Bast stopped in front of it. And the noise hit him.

---

It was always like this. Every time. Seven years, and he had never gotten used to it.

Mortals did not hear it. The innkeeper had told him that, the first year, when Bast had come back from the crossroads white-faced and shaking. *It's just a stone, Bast. An old greystone. There are hundreds of them all over the Four Corners.* And it was true; mortals walked past the waystone every day, and leaned on it, and let their children climb on it, and never heard a thing.

But Bast was not mortal. And to Bast, standing in front of the waystone was like standing at the foot of a great waterfall. A roar. A thunder. A vast, endless, crashing sound that filled his ears and his head and his whole body, drowning out everything else. The wind, and the birds, and the rustle of the grass. His own heartbeat. His own thoughts.

His own name.

That was the thing. That was what the waystone did. It drowned out names. All of them. Anything that tried to speak its name near the stone was lost in the roar, the way a single voice is lost in a thunderstorm. That was why the innkeeper had chosen this place, seven years ago. Or why Skarpi had chosen it for him. Because here, beside the waterfall, the thing in the chest upstairs could scream as loud as it liked, and no one would hear.

Bast stood in the roar and closed his eyes.

---

He had come here a great many times, over the years. More than the innkeeper knew. He came when he could not bear the inn any longer. When the silence of it pressed on him like a hand on his chest. When the innkeeper had gone a whole day without saying a single thing that was not *pass the salt* or *the roof is leaking* or *yes*.

He came and stood in front of the waystone and let it drown him. It hurt. It always hurt. But it was a clean kind of hurt, like cold water, and afterward, when he walked away and the roar faded behind him, the world always seemed a little quieter. A little more bearable.

And sometimes, standing in the roar, he talked.

He did not know if the stone heard him. He did not think it could. It was a stone. It did not have ears. But Bast had grown up in the twilight, where everything had ears, where the trees listened and the rivers remembered and even the wind kept secrets. And he could not quite believe that anything as loud as the waystone could be entirely deaf.

So he talked. And the stone drowned out every word. And that was the point.

---

"I did something terrible," Bast said now, to the stone.

The roar swallowed it. He could not hear his own voice. He went on anyway.

"Three days ago. Four. I don't know anymore; the days are all running together. I found two men on the road. Soldiers. Deserters. And I brought them to the inn. I lied to them. I told them he had gold." He swallowed. "I wanted them to hurt him. I wanted him to fight back. I thought if he fought back, he'd remember. I thought if he got angry enough—"

He stopped. The roar went on.

"He didn't fight back," said Bast. "He just stood there. And they hit him, and hit him, and he didn't do anything. He just stood there with his hands at his sides and let them." His voice cracked. The stone did not care. "And I couldn't bear it. And I killed them. Both of them. In the common room. With my hands." He looked down at his hands. They were shaking. "And then I carried them out into the woods and buried them. And I came back and cleaned the floor. And I haven't told him. I haven't told anyone."

The waystone roared.

"And now there's a scribe," said Bast. "And the innkeeper is telling him everything. Everything. And every word he says, his name gets louder. I can hear it. Even with the chest shut. Even with the waystone. It's getting louder. It's coming back." He was crying now. He did not wipe his face. "And that's what I wanted. That's what I've wanted for seven years. That's why I did all of it. The soldiers and the scribe and all of it."

He pressed his hands against the cold grey stone.

"So why am I so frightened?"

---

The waystone did not answer. It never did. It went on roaring, the way it had roared since before there was a Newarre, since before there was a road, since before anyone could remember. It roared, and Bast stood with his hands pressed against it and his forehead pressed against it and wept.

He did not hear the footsteps behind him. He could not hear anything, in the roar.

But he felt the hand on his shoulder.

---

He spun around. His hands came up. His teeth were bared. For a moment, just a moment, his glamour slipped, and something else looked out of his face. Something old and wild and dangerous, from the deep twilight, with eyes like the sea at night.

It was Chronicler.

The scribe stood very still. He did not back away. He had seen Bast's true face before, on the first night, on the road outside Newarre, and he had been terrified. He was terrified now. Bast could see it. But he did not back away.

Bast let the glamour close again. Slowly. His hands came down. He stepped away from the stone, and the roar faded, and he could hear again. The wind, and the birds, and his own ragged breathing.

"What are you doing here?" he said. His voice was hoarse.

"He sent me," said Chronicler. "He said you'd be here. He said you come here, sometimes, when things are hard." He hesitated. "He said to tell you supper's ready."

Bast stared at him.

"He knows?" he said. "That I come here?"

"He said he's always known."

---

They stood in the road for a long moment, in the thin gold light. Bast looked at the scribe. At his thin face, and his careful eyes, and the bruises on his knuckles from the pen.

"How much did you hear?" he said.

"Nothing." Chronicler looked at the waystone. "I couldn't. It's very loud. Isn't it?" He hesitated. "I didn't know that. I thought it was just a stone. But when I came close, when I put my hand on your shoulder—" He frowned. "I felt it. Just for a moment. Like standing under a bridge when a cart goes over."

"You're a namer," said Bast slowly. "A little. Aren't you? More than you let on."

Chronicler did not answer. That was answer enough.

Bast looked at him for a long time. Then he looked back at the waystone. Grey and tall and weathered, leaning slightly east, with the lichen on its north face.

"I'm going to tell him," Bast said. "Tonight. Or tomorrow. Before it's over." He did not look at Chronicler. "Something I did. Something terrible. I've been carrying it, and I can't carry it anymore." He was quiet. "Will you be there? When I tell him?"

"If you want me to be."

"I don't know if I want you to be." Bast laughed shakily. "But I think I need someone to be. Someone who isn't him. Someone who'll write it down." He looked at Chronicler. "So it's real. So it's said. So I can't take it back."

Chronicler was quiet for a moment.

"I'll be there," he said.

---

They walked back to the Waystone together, across the empty square, in the fading light. At the door of the inn, Bast stopped.

"Scribe," he said.

"Yes?"

"He said he's always known. That I come to the stone." Bast's voice was very quiet. "Do you think he knows the other thing too? The terrible thing?"

Chronicler looked at him. Then he looked through the window of the inn, at the innkeeper, moving behind the bar, setting out bowls for supper. Red hair, and green eyes, and a face that was calmer, now, on the third day, than it had been on the first.

"I think," said Chronicler carefully, "that he knows a great many things now. That he didn't know three days ago."

Bast looked at him for a long moment. Then he nodded, very slightly, and opened the door, and went in.

Behind them, at the corner of the crossroads, the waystone roared. The way it had always roared. The way it would roar long after all of them were gone.

It had heard every word. Of course it had.

It was a stone. Stones are very good at keeping things.
