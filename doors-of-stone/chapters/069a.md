# Interlude: The Candle

"Do you still have it?" said Chronicler. "The candle? Kilvin's?"

The innkeeper looked up. He had just stopped speaking, at the end of the ridge above the burning camp, with Meluan walking into the shadow and a baby in his arms, and he had been sitting very still for a long time, looking at his hands.

"Yes," he said.

"Here?"

"Upstairs." The innkeeper was quiet for a moment. "In a drawer. I haven't lit it in years."

Chronicler hesitated. "Would you? Now?"

The innkeeper looked at him for a long moment. Then at Bast. Bast had gone very still in his backward chair.

"Reshi," Bast said softly. "Maybe not."

"Why not?"

"Because of the smoke," said Bast. "This morning. Over the woods." He looked at the window. "You saw it. How it leaned."

The innkeeper looked at the window too. At the thin grey line of smoke above the trees at the edge of the fields, still there in the late afternoon light, still leaning.

"Yes," he said quietly. "I saw it."

He stood up. He went to the stairs. He went up.

---

He was gone a long time. Chronicler heard him moving about overhead, in the room at the top of the stairs. A drawer opening. A long silence. A drawer closing.

When he came down, he was carrying a small wooden box. Plain and old, with a sliding lid. He set it on the table between them and sat down and looked at it.

"Kilvin gave it to me," he said. "In his office in the Fishery, with his great scarred hands. He'd kept it for twenty years." He slid back the lid. "I've kept it for nearly half as long again."

Inside, nested in old straw, there was a candle. Plain white tallow, yellowed with age, about the length of a hand. The wick was black and curled. Kilvin's ledger was folded underneath it, the pages soft as cloth from handling.

The innkeeper took it out. He set it upright in a pewter dish from the bar. He took a match from his apron pocket.

He looked at Chronicler.

"You understand," he said. "What it means. If it burns blue."

"Yes."

"It won't mean they're coming. It only means they're doing something. Somewhere. Anywhere in the world."

"I understand."

The innkeeper struck the match. He held it to the wick.

---

The flame caught. Small and bright and yellow. It steadied. It rose.

They sat and watched it. All three of them. Nobody spoke.

It burned yellow. A warm, ordinary, homely yellow. The color of every candle Chronicler had ever seen.

He let out a breath he had not known he was holding.

And then the flame bent.

Not flickered. Bent. As if a draft had caught it, though there was no draft, though the door was closed and the shutters were closed and the air in the common room was perfectly still. It bent sideways, toward the window. Toward the woods beyond the fields. Toward the leaning smoke.

And it stayed bent. Leaning. Like the smoke.

And very slowly, from the tip downward, it began to turn blue.

---

Nobody moved. Nobody breathed.

The blue crept down the flame the way frost creeps across a window. Slowly. Steadily. From the tip, down, and down, until the whole small flame was blue. A deep, cold, winter-dusk blue. The blue of a bruise.

And it was leaning toward the woods.

"Oh," said Bast. Very softly. His face was white. "Oh, Reshi."

The innkeeper did not say anything. He sat very still and looked at the blue flame, leaning toward the window, and his face showed nothing at all.

Then he reached out and pinched it out between his finger and thumb.

The smoke curled up, thin and grey. It did not rise straight. It leaned. Toward the window. Toward the woods.

---

"Not somewhere," said Chronicler. His voice did not sound like his own. "Not anywhere in the world. There." He looked at the window. "It was pointing."

"Yes."

"Kilvin's candle doesn't point. You said. It shows when they're doing something, anywhere—"

"It's never pointed before." The innkeeper was looking at the smoke from the snuffed wick, drifting toward the shutters. "Not in all the years I've had it. Not in all the years Kilvin had it." He was quiet for a long moment. "It points now because he's close. So close it can feel him. The way a compass feels the north."

"Who?"

The innkeeper looked at him.

"You know who," he said.

Chronicler did know. He had been writing about him for two and a half days. A man with black eyes and frost-colored hair, who crouched in front of a boy in the firelight among the bodies of his family and smiled.

"He's out there," said Chronicler. "In the woods. Right now."

"He's been out there since this morning. Maybe longer." The innkeeper put the candle back in its box, very carefully, and slid the lid closed. "Since the first day. Since I started telling."

"And you kept telling."

"Yes."

"Why?"

The innkeeper looked at him for a long moment. His green eyes were very calm.

"Because I'm not finished," he said.

---

He put the box on the shelf behind the bar, beside the bottles. He came back and sat down. He picked up his cup of cider, and looked at it, and set it down again.

Bast had not moved. He was sitting in his backward chair with his arms folded across its back, and he was staring at the innkeeper with an expression Chronicler could not read.

"Reshi," he said. "We could go. Now. Tonight. Out the back, into the dark. He'd never find you. He never found you before."

"He never looked before," said the innkeeper. "Not really. He didn't know there was anything to look for." He looked at the window. "He knows now."

"Then we run faster."

"Bast." The innkeeper's voice was very gentle. "I've been running for seven years. Sitting very still behind a bar, in a town no one's heard of, beside a stone that drowns out names. That's a kind of running." He looked at his hands. "I'm tired of it."

Bast stared at him. His deep blue eyes were very bright.

"Then what do we do?"

The innkeeper was quiet for a long time. Then he looked at Chronicler, and at the pen lying on the table beside the stack of pages, and he smiled. A small, crooked, rueful smile.

"We finish the story," he said.

He picked up his cup. He drank. He set it down.

"I carried my aunt's daughter back to the Maer at dawn," said Kvothe. "And he held her for the first time, sitting on a rock at the mouth of his cave. And she opened her eyes and looked at him." He paused. "They were dark eyes. Like her mother's. Like my mother's."

Chronicler picked up his pen.

Outside, beyond the shutters, beyond the fields, at the edge of the woods, the smoke leaned. And the light began, very slowly, to fail.
