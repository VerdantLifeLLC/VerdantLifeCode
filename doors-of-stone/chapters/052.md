# Vashet

She came on the sixth day, at dawn, and she came through the window.

I was asleep. I had finally begun to sleep again, a little, in the nights since Elodin's visit. Not well, and not long. But enough. I was asleep in the narrow bed in Merra's house, with my cold arm resting on a pillow and the knot around my neck, and I was dreaming of a white city.

I woke because the air changed.

I cannot explain it better than that. One moment I was asleep, and the next I was awake, with my heart pounding and every nerve in my body singing. Someone was in the room. Someone who moved without sound, without shadow, without disturbing the air. Someone who had been trained to move that way, the way I had been trained, on a hillside in Ademre with a sword at my throat.

I did not move. I opened my eyes a crack and looked.

She was standing at the foot of my bed. Small and slim, in the blood-red clothes of the Adem mercenaries, close-fitting and bound with a hundred cords. Her dark hair was cut short. Her face was plain and calm and unremarkable. There was a sword on her back.

She was looking at me with an expression I knew very well. It was the expression she had worn a hundred times on the practice grounds at Haert, just before she knocked me flat.

"You are awake," said Vashet. "Do not pretend. You were never good at pretending."

I sat up. "Vashet."

"Kvothe." She did not smile. The Adem do not smile, not with their faces. But her hands moved, very slightly, at her sides. *Relief*, they said. *And anger. And something else.* "You are alive."

"Barely."

"Barely is enough. Barely is always enough." She looked at my arm on its pillow. Her hands went still. "What is that?"

"A long story."

"Tell it."

---

I told it. All of it. The Archives and the door and the expulsion. Severen and Bredon and Meluan. Renere and the king's hair and Ambrose's crown. Tarbean and Skarpi. The Eolian, and the song, and the fire, and what came to stop it.

She listened without interrupting. She stood at the foot of my bed with her hands at her sides, and she did not move, and she did not speak. But her hands spoke. They spoke all the way through. Small movements, subtle and swift, the way the Adem speak when they are not trying to speak. *Surprise. Anger. Grief. Fear.*

When I told her about Cinder's hand on my wrist, her fingers curled into fists.

When I told her about Stanchion, they went very still.

When I finished, she was silent for a long time.

"We heard," she said at last. "In Haert. We heard that the Maer's Ruh had killed the king of Vintas. That he had sung him to death. That he was a demon, and a murderer, and a kingkiller." Her hands moved. *Disgust.* "Shehyn called the school together. She said that one of ours had shamed us. That the sword Caesura had been given to a barbarian who killed kings from behind curtains with songs." She looked at me. "She sent me to take it back."

I went cold.

"Take it back?"

"Caesura belongs to the school. It was lent to you. On condition." Vashet's voice was flat. "The condition was that you would use it within the Lethani. A sword that has killed a king by treachery is not within the Lethani. It is shamed. It must come home."

"I didn't kill the king."

"So you say."

"I tried to *save* him."

"So you say." She looked at me. "I heard your story. I know how you tell stories. You are very good at making yourself sound right."

I stared at her. I did not know what to say.

Then her hands moved. *Joke*, they said. And then, *I believe you.*

---

I laughed. It came out of me before I could stop it, a startled cracked laugh, and it hurt my chest. Vashet watched me laugh, and her face did not change, but her hands said *satisfaction*.

"You were always easy to fool," she said. "Even at the end. Even when you had learned the Ketan and the Lethani and could almost fight like a person." She sat down on the edge of the bed. "Of course I believe you. I knew you. I taught you. You are foolish and proud and you talk far too much and you have no sense of how to be quiet. But you are not a coward. And killing a man in his sickbed with a song is a coward's work." She looked at my arm. "And that. That is not a coward's wound."

"Will you tell Shehyn?"

"I will tell Shehyn what I have seen. She will decide." Vashet was quiet for a moment. "But I do not think she will want the sword back. Not after this."

"Why not?"

Vashet looked at me for a long moment. Her hands were very still.

"Because you stood on a stage," she said, "and sang the truth, while the thing that killed your family walked toward you. And you did not stop. Not when they came. Not when your friend died. Not when Cinder took your arm." She paused. "That is the Lethani, Kvothe. That is the whole of it. To do the right thing, at the right time, for the right reason, and to keep doing it no matter what it costs."

I did not say anything. I could not.

"Shehyn will understand," said Vashet. "When I tell her. She is very wise, for an old woman." Her hands moved. *Joke.* "Do not tell her I said that."

---

She stayed. She did not ask if she could; she simply did. She took up a place in the corner of my room, sitting on the floor with her back against the wall and her sword across her knees, and she did not leave. When I slept, she watched. When I woke, she was there. When Sim or Wil or Devi came to visit, she looked at them with her calm Adem eyes and they became very polite and very quiet and left sooner than they had intended.

"Is she going to stay forever?" Sim whispered to me, on the second day.

"I don't know."

"She's terrifying."

"She's supposed to be. She's Adem."

"She looked at me," Sim said, "and I felt like she was deciding which of my bones to break first."

"That's just how she looks at people."

"That's not reassuring, Kvothe."

---

On the third day, Vashet made me get out of bed.

"You are weak," she said. "You have been lying here for more than a span, feeling sorry for yourself. Your body has forgotten how to move. Get up."

"My arm—"

"Your arm is one arm. You have two. And two legs. And a back, and a belly, and a head, though you do not use the last one very often." She stood over me. "Get up. We will do the Ketan."

"Now?"

"Now."

So I got up. And in the small cramped room at the top of Merra's house, with the window open and the sounds of Imre drifting in, I did the Ketan.

It was terrible. I had not done it in months, not since before Renere. My body was stiff and weak and slow. My left arm hung useless, throwing off my balance, making every movement awkward and wrong. I stumbled through the first few forms like a drunk, and fell twice, and nearly put my foot through the wall.

Vashet watched without speaking. When I finished the first sequence, she said, "Again."

I did it again. It was slightly less terrible.

"Again."

I did it again.

We did it all morning. Over and over, the same forms, the same sequences, until my legs were shaking and my shirt was soaked with sweat and my breath was rasping in my throat. Vashet did not let me stop. She did not let me rest. She stood in the corner with her arms folded and said *again*, and I did it again.

And somewhere in the middle of the morning, something changed.

I stopped fighting my arm. I stopped trying to move it the way I used to, trying to make it do what it could no longer do. I let it hang. I let it be what it was. And I found, slowly, that my body could move around it. That the forms could shift and adapt, the way water shifts around a stone in a stream. That there was a Ketan for one arm, as well as for two.

It was not the same. It would never be the same. But it was something.

When I finished the last sequence, I stood in the center of the room with my chest heaving and my cold arm hanging at my side, and Vashet looked at me for a long time.

Her hands moved. *Better.*

---

That night, she told me about the Lethani.

We were sitting on the floor of my room, in the dark, with the window open and the stars showing through it. I was too tired to sleep. She was too Adem to need much sleep. We sat there together in the quiet, and after a long time, she spoke.

"You asked me once," she said, "what the Lethani is. On the hillside at Haert. You wanted me to explain it."

"You wouldn't."

"No. Because it cannot be explained. Only lived." She was quiet for a moment. "But I will tell you a story. About Shehyn. About a thing she did, long ago, when she was young."

"All right."

"When Shehyn was young, younger than you are now, there was a man in Haert who did a terrible thing. I will not tell you what. It was very terrible. He hurt many people. Children. And when he was caught, he was brought before the school, and the school had to decide what to do with him."

"What did they decide?"

"They argued. For many days. Some said he should be killed. Some said he should be cast out. Some said he should be made to work, to pay for what he had done. They could not agree." Vashet paused. "And then Shehyn stood up. She was very young, and no one expected her to speak. But she stood up, and she walked to where the man was sitting, and she looked at him for a long time. And then she drew her sword and killed him."

I stared at her in the dark.

"The school was very angry," said Vashet. "They said she had acted without the Lethani. That she had not waited for the school to decide. That she had killed in anger, or pride, or haste." She was quiet for a moment. "Shehyn said nothing. She stood before them and let them be angry. And when they were done, she said one thing."

"What?"

"She said, 'He was going to do it again. I saw it in his eyes. And while you argued, he would have had time.'" Vashet's voice was very quiet. "And then she laid down her sword, and said that the school could do with her as they liked."

"What did they do?"

"They did nothing. They looked at her for a long time, and then they picked up her sword and gave it back to her." Vashet was silent for a moment. "Three days later, they found that the man had hidden a knife in his cell. And a list of names. Children's names."

The room was very quiet.

"Why are you telling me this?" I asked.

Vashet looked at me in the dark.

"Because you are going to face a choice," she said. "Soon. I can see it on you, the way I could see the Ketan in you before you learned it. A choice like Shehyn's. Where you must act, and there is no time to wait, and everyone will say afterward that you were wrong." She paused. "And you will not know if you were right. Not for a long time. Maybe not ever."

"How will I know what to do?"

"You will not know. You will only choose." Her hands moved in the dark; I could not see them, but I felt the air shift. "That is the Lethani, Kvothe. Not knowing. Choosing. And bearing what comes after."

---

She left on the ninth day. She stood at the window at dawn, as she had come, and looked at me.

"I will go back to Haert," she said. "I will tell Shehyn what I have seen. And then I will come back." Her hands moved. *Promise.* "You will need me. Before the end. I can feel it."

"How will you find me?"

"I found you this time." She did not smile. But her hands said *amusement*. "You are not hard to find, Kvothe. You are the loudest quiet person in the world."

She turned to the window. Then she stopped and looked back.

"The sword," she said. "Caesura. You still have it?"

"Yes."

"Good. Keep it. Use it well." She paused. "And if you ever use it for something that is not the Lethani—if you ever use it for anger, or pride, or revenge—then I will come and take it back. And I will not be gentle."

"I understand."

She looked at me for a long moment. Then her hands moved, very slightly, in a sign I had never seen her make before. It took me a long time to understand it, after she had gone.

It meant *family*.

Then she was gone, out the window and down into the grey dawn streets of Imre, and I was alone.
