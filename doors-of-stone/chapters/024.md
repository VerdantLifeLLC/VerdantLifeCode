@@PART PART TWO: THE BEAUTIFUL GAME

# The Southern Road

There is a particular kind of loneliness that belongs to roads. It is not the loneliness of a room, where the walls press in and remind you that no one is there. It is not the loneliness of a crowd, where everyone is near and no one is close. It is a moving loneliness. It walks beside you. It keeps pace with you, mile after mile, and when you stop to rest at the side of the road, it sits down next to you and waits for you to get up again.

I had known that loneliness before. I had known it as a boy of twelve, walking out of the forest where my family had died, with nothing but a broken lute and the clothes on my back. I had known it on the long road to Tarbean, and on the longer road out of Tarbean, three years later, with a stolen purse and a head full of dreams.

But I had not known it for a long time. And I had forgotten how heavy it could be.

---

I went south along the Great Stone Road, the old Aturan highway that runs from Imre down through the heart of the Commonwealth toward the border of Vintas. It was early spring, and the road was a river of mud. Carts sank to their axles. Horses slipped and stumbled. Travelers walked along the verges where the grass held the soil together, their boots caked with clay up to the knee.

I walked with them. I could have bought a horse; I had the Maer's money still, most of it. But I did not want a horse. I wanted to walk. I wanted to feel every mile under my feet, the way you want to feel a wound, pressing on it to make sure it is still there.

I had been expelled. I kept saying it to myself as I walked, trying to make it real. *I have been expelled from the University.* I had worked for three years to get there, and three more to stay, and I had lost it in a single night. I had lost my place in the Arcanum. I had lost the Archives, with all their books and all their secrets. I had lost my bench in the Fishery and my room at Anker's and my twice-a-span at the Eolian. I had lost Sim and Wil and Fela, and Devi, and Auri. I had lost Elodin, who had never quite been my teacher and was now never going to be.

I had lost it all by going down a set of stairs.

And the worst of it, the part that sat on my chest at night like a stone and would not let me sleep, was that I did not regret it. Not really. Not at the bottom, where the truth lives. I had gone down those stairs and I had learned something I could not have learned any other way. I had learned what I was.

*The line of Loeclos. The Thief's own blood.*

I did not know what to do with that. It was too large. It was like being told that the sky was a lid, or that the ground beneath your feet was the back of a sleeping beast. It did not fit anywhere in my head. And so I walked, and I carried it, and I did not look at it too closely.

---

On the fourth day out of Imre, I fell in with a troupe.

I heard them before I saw them. It was late afternoon, and the sun was slanting low through the trees, and from somewhere up ahead came the sound of a fiddle. Not a good fiddle. It was sawing away at "Tinker Tanner" with more enthusiasm than skill, and every third note was flat. But it was a fiddle, and it was being played on the road, and underneath it I could hear the creak of wagon wheels and the jingle of harness and a woman's voice laughing.

My heart turned over in my chest. I began to walk faster.

They were Edema Ruh. I knew it before I came around the bend, knew it from the rhythm of the fiddle and the pitch of the laughter, the way you know your own mother's voice in a crowded room. And when I came around the bend and saw them, three wagons painted in bright colors gone dull with the dust of the road, I knew it for certain.

A man was walking beside the lead wagon, leading the horse. He was tall and lean and brown as a nut, with a beard going grey and a battered hat pulled low over his eyes. He heard my footsteps and turned, and his hand went to the knife at his belt, the way any Ruh's hand does when a stranger comes up behind him on the road.

Then he saw my face. And my hair. And the lute case on my back.

His hand came away from the knife.

"One family," I said.

His eyes widened. Then a slow smile spread across his face.

"Under the sky," he said. "One family. Well met, brother. Where are you bound?"

"South. Vintas."

"So are we, as far as Rannish. Walk with us?"

"I'd be glad to."

---

Their troupe was called Hallow's Company, after the old man who had founded it forty years before. He was dead now, and his son led the troupe: the lean brown man with the grey beard, whose name was Tessit. His wife was Mirra, a round cheerful woman with a laugh like a bell, and she was the one I had heard laughing. There were nine of them in all. Tessit and Mirra and their three children, two of them grown. An old man named Brin who played the fiddle, badly, and knew it, and did not care. A pair of brothers, Kellen and Wat, who did tumbling and knife-work. And a young woman named Illa, with dark eyes and a sharp tongue, who sang.

They took me in without question. That is the way of the Ruh. A Ruh on the road is never a stranger to another Ruh. We share our fires and our food and our songs, and we ask nothing in return but the same.

That night, around their fire, I played for them.

I had not played for Ruh in a very long time. Not since I was a boy. I had played in taverns and in the Eolian and in the halls of the Maer, for lords and ladies and merchants and students. But I had not played for my own people. For people who knew what music was, down in their bones. For people who had grown up with it the way other people grow up with bread and water.

It was different. I cannot explain how. The best I can say is that when I played for the Eolian, I was showing them something. When I played for Hallow's Company, around their fire, with the stars coming out overhead and the smell of woodsmoke and horses in the air, I was not showing them anything. I was just playing. The way you breathe. The way you speak to a friend.

I played "Tinker Tanner," and Brin joined in on his fiddle, and his flat notes did not matter at all. I played "Jenny Nettle," and the children danced. I played "The Lay of Sir Savien Traliard," and Illa sang the Aloine part, and her voice was rough and sweet as honey from a wild hive, and when we finished, Mirra was crying and laughing at the same time and Tessit was looking at me with an expression I did not understand.

"Where did you learn to play like that?" he said.

"My father," I said. "And the road."

"Who was your father?"

I hesitated. It had been a long time since I had said it aloud.

"Arliden," I said. "Of the Edema Ruh."

The fire crackled. Nobody spoke. Tessit looked at me for a long time, and then he looked at Mirra, and something passed between them.

"Arliden's troupe," said Mirra softly. "Oh, child."

"You knew them?"

"Everyone knew them." Tessit's voice was quiet. "Arliden was the best. Everyone said so. The best troupe on the roads. The best songs. The best plays." He shook his head. "And then one year they just didn't come to Hallowfell for the midsummer fair. Nobody knew why. And then the next year there was talk. Somebody had found wagons in the woods, burned. Nothing left. Nobody knew who'd done it."

"I know who did it," I said.

Tessit looked at me. The fire crackled between us.

"Do you," he said. It was not a question.

I did not answer. After a moment, Tessit nodded slowly.

"You're his boy, then," he said. "The little one. We heard there was one. Red hair, like his mother. We heard he was dead."

"I was, for a while."

Mirra came around the fire and put her arms around me. She smelled of woodsmoke and lavender and bread, and she held me the way my mother had held me, a long time ago, and I did not know what to do. I sat there stiffly in her arms, with my lute in my lap, and my eyes stung, and I could not speak.

"Your mother," Mirra said into my hair. "Laurian. She was the loveliest thing. Voice like a lark. And the way she carried herself, like a queen." She pulled back and looked at me, her eyes wet. "Everyone knew she wasn't born to the road. Everyone could see it. But nobody ever asked. She was Arliden's, and she was Ruh, and that was that."

"Did she ever talk about where she came from?"

Mirra shook her head. "Never. Not once, not to anyone. It was the one thing she wouldn't talk about." She hesitated. "But I remember once, at a fair, somebody sang that old rhyme. The one about Lady Lackless. And your mother went white as milk and walked away and didn't come back to the fire all night." She shook her head. "I always wondered about that."

I did not say anything. I did not have to.

---

I traveled with Hallow's Company for nine days. They were among the best days of that year.

We played in villages along the road, in inn yards and on village greens. We put on a play in a market square in Cheshire, a comedy about a tinker who outwits a greedy lord, and I played the tinker, and the crowd laughed so hard that an old woman fell off her bench and had to be helped up. We camped in fields and woods, beside streams, under the stars. We ate stew and bread and whatever the villages gave us, and we sang late into the night.

I taught Illa three new songs. She taught me a knife trick that Kellen and Wat had taught her, and I cut myself practicing it and she laughed at me until she cried. Brin showed me how to rosin a fiddle bow properly, which I already knew, but I let him show me anyway. The children followed me everywhere and asked me to tell them stories, and I told them about Felurian, leaving out the parts that were not for children, and they did not believe a word of it.

For nine days, I was Edema Ruh again. Just Ruh. Not a student, not an arcanist, not a line or a key or the last of anything. Just a red-haired boy with a lute, walking a road with his people.

It could not last. Nothing like that ever does.

---

On the ninth night, we camped near a village called Aldermoor, a day's walk north of Rannish. It was a small place, a few dozen houses clustered around a crossroads, with a mill and a smithy and a little stone church. We had played there that afternoon, and the villagers had been kind, and had given us a whole ham and a cask of cider, and we were feasting on both around the fire.

I had gone a little way from the camp to be alone. I did that sometimes, in the evenings. I would take Kilvin's candle out of its box and light it and sit with it for a while, the way I did every night, watching it burn.

It burned yellow. I watched it for a while, and let my mind drift, and thought about nothing in particular.

And then, without warning, it turned blue.

---

I did not move. I sat there in the dark beside the candle, and I watched it burn blue, and I felt my heart stop.

It was not the pale flicker I had seen before, the brief shiver of color that came and went. It was a deep, steady, unwavering blue, the blue of a winter sky at dusk, the blue of a bruise. It burned blue, and it did not stop.

Somewhere in the world, the Chandrian were doing something.

I stood up. I did not know what I was going to do. I did not know where they were or what they were doing or how I could possibly stop them. I only knew that I had to move. I had to do something.

And then I smelled the smoke.

It was faint at first. Just a thread of it, on the wind, coming from the south. It could have been a cookfire, or a bonfire, or a farmer burning brush. But it was not. I knew it was not. There was something in it, something beneath the ordinary smell of woodsmoke, that set my teeth on edge. Something sour and wrong, like the smell of rot, like the smell of rust.

I snatched up the candle and ran back to the camp.

Tessit was on his feet before I reached the fire. He had smelled it too. So had Mirra, and Kellen, and Wat. They stood around the fire with their faces turned to the south, and their eyes were wide.

"Fire," said Tessit. "South of here. Toward the Deller farm."

"How far?"

"Two miles. Maybe three." He looked at me. "What's that?"

He was looking at the candle in my hand, still burning blue.

I did not answer. I did not know how.

"Stay here," I said. "All of you. Stay here, stay together, keep the fire high. Don't go toward the smoke."

"Kvothe—"

"Please." I looked at him. "Tessit. Please. I know what this is. Stay here."

He looked at me for a long moment. Then he looked at the candle. And something in his face changed. Something old and deep and Ruh.

"Go," he said quietly. "We'll stay. Come back to us."

---

I ran.

I ran south through the dark, across fields and through hedges, toward the smoke. I had Caesura at my hip and the candle in my hand, its blue flame streaming in the wind of my running but never going out. I did not know what I would do when I got there. I did not care.

The smoke grew thicker as I ran. Soon I could see the glow of the fire, orange against the black sky. And then I came over the crest of a low hill and saw it.

It was a farmhouse. Or it had been. A long low building of stone and timber, with a thatched roof and a barn beside it and a yard full of carts and tools. It was burning now. The roof was a sheet of flame, roaring and crackling, sending sparks spiraling up into the sky. The barn was burning too. The whole yard was lit up orange and red, bright as day.

And the flames were blue.

Not all of them. Most of the fire was ordinary, orange and yellow and red. But here and there, in the heart of it, there were tongues of blue. Cold blue, winter blue, the blue of the candle in my hand.

I stopped at the edge of the yard. I stood there with my chest heaving and my sword in my hand, and I looked.

There were bodies in the yard.

I will not describe them. I have described such things before, and I do not want to do it again. I will say only that there were five of them, and that they were lying in the mud in front of the burning house, and that some of them were very small.

And I will say that the mud around them was frozen. In the middle of spring. On a night warm enough that I had been sitting outside without a cloak. The mud around the bodies was frozen hard as stone, and there was frost on their eyelashes.

---

I searched the yard. I searched the barn, as much as I could before the heat drove me back. I searched the fields around the farm, calling out, my voice raw and cracked. I found no one. No survivors. No Chandrian. Nothing but the burning house and the frozen mud and the five small shapes lying in the firelight.

The candle in my hand burned yellow again. I did not notice when it changed.

I stood in the yard as the fire burned down, and I did not know what to do. I had come too late. I had always come too late. I had come too late at eleven years old, running back through the woods to find my family dead. And I had come too late now.

It was nearly dawn when I heard the sound.

It was very faint. A small sound, like a kitten mewing. It came from the well at the edge of the yard, a round stone well with a wooden cover and a bucket on a rope.

I went to the well. I pulled off the cover and looked down.

There was a girl in the bucket.

She was very small. Six, maybe, or seven. She was curled up in the bucket at the end of the rope, halfway down the well, in the dark. Her face was white and streaked with soot and tears. Her eyes were enormous. She was clutching something against her chest.

"Hello," I said softly. "It's all right. I'm not one of them. I'm here to help."

She stared up at me. She did not speak.

"I'm going to pull you up," I said. "Is that all right?"

She did not answer. But after a long moment, she nodded.

I pulled her up. Slowly, carefully, hand over hand on the rope. When the bucket came level with the rim of the well, I reached down and lifted her out and held her against my chest. She was cold and shaking and very light. She did not cry. She was past crying.

"What's your name?" I asked.

She looked up at me. Her lips moved. At first no sound came out. Then, very quietly, she said, "Nell."

"Nell. That's a good name. I'm Kvothe."

She did not say anything. She was looking over my shoulder, at the burned house, at the shapes in the mud.

"Don't look," I said. "Don't look at that. Look at me."

She looked at me.

"Nell," I said. "Can you tell me what happened?"

She was quiet for a long time. Then she held out the thing she had been clutching against her chest.

It was a scrap of cloth. Old and frayed and stained with soot. It had been cut, or torn, from something larger. There were knots on it. Dozens of them, tied into the threads of the cloth, in patterns that made my eyes ache.

"Mama's cloth," Nell whispered. "The old cloth. From Great-Gran. Mama said we had to keep it safe. Always and always." Her face crumpled. "They wanted it. They burned it. I got a piece. I hid."

I took the scrap of cloth. It was warm from her hands. The knots were Yllish. I could read a few of them. *Keeping*. *Door*. *Name*.

"Who wanted it, Nell?" I asked. "Who came?"

She looked at me with her enormous eyes.

"First the pretty lady came," she said. "Yesterday. She was nice. She had dark hair and a nice voice and she asked if she could see Mama's cloth. She said she was writing a song. She looked at it for a long time and wrote things down, and she gave Mama a silver for letting her look, and then she went away." Nell's voice cracked. "And then tonight the others came. The cold ones."

I stood very still in the grey dawn light, with the girl in my arms and the scrap of knotted cloth in my hand, and the world seemed to tilt beneath my feet.

*She had dark hair and a nice voice. She said she was writing a song.*

"The pretty lady," I said. My voice sounded very far away. "Did she say her name?"

Nell shook her head. Then she stopped. She frowned, as if trying to remember.

"She wrote it," she said. "In the book. So Mama would know who'd looked." She pointed toward the burned house. "It was in the big book. With all the names of everyone who'd ever looked at the cloth. Mama made everyone write their name."

I looked at the house. It was nothing but ash and blackened stone now, smoking in the dawn. Whatever book had been in it was gone.

"Do you remember what she wrote?" I asked.

Nell thought about it. Then she nodded.

"It was a funny name," she said. "It started with a D. Mama said it was a pretty name. Like a flower."

She looked up at me.

"Dinael," she said.

---

I carried Nell back to Hallow's Company. Mirra took her from my arms without a word and wrapped her in a blanket and held her by the fire, rocking her, humming softly. Nell fell asleep almost at once.

I sat down beside the fire. Tessit brought me a cup of something hot and strong and put it in my hands and sat down next to me. He did not ask any questions. He only sat there, a solid, silent presence at my side, while the sun came up over the fields.

I held the scrap of knotted cloth in my lap and looked at it.

Dinael. Denna had used that name once, years ago. Dinael, Dianne, Dinnah, Dyanae. She changed her names the way other women changed their dresses. I had always thought it was a kind of armor. A way of keeping herself safe, of never letting anyone hold her too tightly.

She had been here. Yesterday. She had come to this farm and asked to see an old cloth covered in Yllish knots. She had looked at it for a long time and written things down. She had paid for the privilege and gone away.

And then the Chandrian had come, and burned the cloth, and killed everyone who had kept it.

I did not believe she had known. I could not believe it. Not Denna. Not the girl who had cried over a dead child at Trebon, who had sung "The Lay of Sir Savien" with me at the Eolian with tears running down her face.

But she had been here. And she had been sent here. By someone who wanted to know what was written on that cloth. By someone who had then sent the Chandrian to make sure no one else would ever know.

By her patron.

I sat there by the fire as the sun came up, and I felt something settle into me that I had never felt before. It was not anger. Anger is hot, and this was cold. It was not fear. Fear makes you want to run, and this made me want to be very still.

It was purpose. Cold and clear and hard as stone.

I would find him. Denna's patron. Master Ash, or whatever he called himself. I would find him, and I would learn who he was, and what he wanted, and why he had sent a girl who loved songs to a farmhouse where a family kept an old cloth, knowing that she would be followed by death.

And then I would stop him.

I did not know how. I did not know when. But I knew, sitting there in the dawn with the smell of smoke still in my hair and the scrap of cloth in my hands, that I would.

It was a promise. And unlike most of the promises I have made in my life, I kept it.

God help me. I kept it.
