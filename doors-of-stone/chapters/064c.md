# Lyra's House

On our last morning in the white city, Selitos took me to see where Lyra had lived.

He did not tell me where we were going. He came out of the tower in the grey light before dawn, wrapped in a heavy cloak of white fur, with his staff in his hand, and he looked at me where I sat by the banked fire with Bast asleep beside me.

"Walk with me, singer," he said.

I walked with him.

---

We went down through the ruins. Past the great plaza with its broken columns and its drifted snow. Past the fountain with the carved children on its rim. Down a long white street so choked with rubble that we had to climb over it in places, Selitos moving slowly and carefully, leaning on his staff, and me behind him with my good hand out, ready to catch him if he fell.

He did not fall. He had walked these streets for a very long time.

At the bottom of the street, where the city ran up against the side of the mountain, there was a house. Or what was left of one. A small house, by the standards of that city. Two stories, of the same white stone as everything else, with a garden wall around it and a gate that had once been iron and was now a lacework of rust. The roof was gone. The upper story was gone, mostly. But the lower walls still stood, and the doorway, and one window, facing east.

Selitos stopped at the gate. He did not go in. He stood with both hands on his staff, and he looked at the house for a long time.

"Here," he said.

---

I did not ask whose house it was. I knew. I had known since we turned into that street. There was something about it, even in ruin. A feeling. The way a room feels after music has been played in it.

"Lyra," I said.

"Lyra." Selitos's voice was very quiet. "And Lanre. When he was home. Which was not often." He looked at the doorway. "She kept this house for eleven years while he was at war. She kept it clean, and the garden weeded, and a lamp in that window every night. So he could see it from the pass. So he would know the way home."

"Did he?"

"Always." Selitos was quiet for a moment. "He told me once that he could see it from the top of the pass, on clear nights. A single light, very small, at the bottom of the city. He said it was the brightest thing in the world."

The wind moved over the snow. Somewhere far above us, among the peaks, a bird was calling. A thin, high, lonely sound.

"May I go in?" I said.

Selitos looked at me with his one good eye. The stone eye, beside it, was very still.

"She would have liked you," he said. "Go in."

---

I went in. Through the rusted gate, across the garden, where nothing grew now but snow and a few dry grey stalks of something that might once have been lavender. Through the doorway, which had no door.

It was a small room. The walls were white and bare. There was snow on the floor, drifted into the corners, and the sky above was grey and open where the roof had been. There was no furniture. There was nothing at all. Only the walls, and the snow, and the single window facing east.

And beneath the window, a shelf. Carved into the stone of the wall itself. A small shelf, about the length of my forearm.

There was something on it.

---

I brushed the snow away with my good hand. Underneath, the shelf was clean and white. And set into the stone, as if it had grown there, was a lamp.

It was a very simple lamp. A shallow bowl of white stone, with a lip for the wick and a small handle on one side. There was no oil in it. There had been no oil in it for a very long time. But there was a mark on the stone around the lip, very faint, the kind of smoke-stain that only comes from years and years of burning. Every night. In the same place.

I stood and looked at it for a long time.

"She lit it the night he came home for the last time," said Selitos, behind me. He had come in so quietly that I had not heard him. He was standing in the doorway, leaning on his staff. "The night before the battle at Drossen Tor. He came home to her for one night. They sat here, in this room, with the lamp burning in the window. And in the morning, he went to the battle. And she went with him."

"And she died."

"And she died." Selitos did not move. "And he called her back. With her name. The way she had once called him back, when he fell." His voice was very flat. "You know that part. Skarpi told you."

"Yes."

"And then she died again. For good. And he came home. Here." Selitos looked at the lamp. "He came home to this house, and he sat in this room, and he lit this lamp. Every night. For a year. For no one." He was quiet for a long moment. "And then he did what he did. And the city burned."

---

I did not say anything. I stood in the small white room with the snow on the floor and the lamp on the shelf, and I thought about a man sitting here, alone, lighting a lamp every night for a woman who was never coming home.

I thought about a bar at a crossroads, and a man behind it, polishing glasses. I did not know, then, that I was thinking about that. I had not been there yet. But I think some part of me knew. Some part of me looked at that lamp and saw, very far away, a small grey inn with a sword over the bar, and a man waiting there for something that might never come.

"Why did you bring me here?" I said.

Selitos did not answer at once.

"Because you are going to the door," he said at last. "The last door. The one under the house of your mother's family. And when you get there, you will have to give up something. Something very dear. Perhaps everything." He looked at me with his one good eye. "And I wanted you to see what happens. When a man loses what he loves most, and cannot let it go."

"Lanre."

"Lanre." Selitos looked at the lamp. "He was the best of us, singer. Did Skarpi tell you that? The bravest and the kindest and the most loved. Better than me. Better than Aleph. Better than any of us." His voice was very quiet. "And he broke. Not because he was weak. Because he loved her so much that he could not bear the world without her in it. So he decided the world should not be."

---

"I don't want to break," I said.

"No one does." Selitos turned from the lamp and looked at me. "But everyone who loves, breaks. A little. That is the price of it. The question is not whether you break. The question is what you do with the pieces." He was quiet for a moment. "Lanre gathered his pieces and made them into a weapon. He made them into Haliax. A shadow that cannot die and cannot forget and cannot stop grieving. That was his choice."

"What should I do? With mine?"

Selitos looked at me for a long time.

"I do not know," he said. "I have been asking myself that for a very long time. About my own pieces." He touched the stone in his eye socket, very lightly. "I made mine into a watch. A long, cold watch, alone at the top of the world. Waiting for a door to open. Listening for a song." He almost smiled. "It is not a very good choice either. But it is better than Lanre's."

"Is there a good one?"

"Perhaps." Selitos looked back at the lamp on the shelf. "Lyra's."

---

"She lit a lamp," he said. "Every night. For eleven years. For a man who was far away, who might not come home. Not because she was sure he would. Because she hoped." He was quiet. "She did not know what would happen. She did not know if the war would end, or if he would live, or if the world would go on. She only knew that there should be a light in the window. In case."

He reached out and touched the lamp, very gently, with one long finger.

"That is what I would tell you to do," he said. "With your pieces. When you break. Not make a weapon. Not make a watch." He took his hand away. "Make a light. A small one. In a window. And keep it burning. Not because you are sure. Because you hope."

---

We walked back up through the ruins in silence. The sun was coming up over the peaks, and the white city was turning gold and rose in the dawn, the way it must have looked every morning for a thousand years before it burned.

At the top of the long white street, I looked back. The small house at the bottom was in shadow still, against the side of the mountain. I could not see the window from there.

But I thought, for a moment, that I saw a light in it. Very small. Very faint. The kind of light you see at the edge of your eye, and then, when you turn to look, is gone.

I did not say anything. Neither did Selitos.

But when we came to the tower, and Bast came out yawning to meet us, Selitos stopped at the door and looked back down the street the way we had come. And his one good eye was wet.

"She is still lighting it," he said. So softly that only I heard. "After all this time. For him. In case."

And he went inside, and did not come out again until we left.

---

"Is that why?" said Chronicler. "The inn? Seven years behind a bar, waiting?"

The innkeeper was quiet for a long time. He looked around the common room. At the bar, and the bottles, and the sword on the wall. At the window, with its shutters closed against the dark, and the frost creeping in at the edges.

"I didn't know it," he said at last. "Not then. I didn't know anything, then. I didn't choose to keep a light burning. I just kept an inn." He looked at the window. "But I think Selitos knew. I think he saw it. On that last morning. What I was going to be." He was quiet. "A man in a small house at the bottom of the world, keeping a lamp in the window. Not because he was sure. Because he hoped."

"Hoped for what?"

The innkeeper looked at him. At the scribe, and the pen, and the pages stacked three days high.

"For someone to come," he said. "And see the light. And knock."
