# A Winter with the Ruh

We spent that winter with Hallow's Company, in a valley in the western hills of the Commonwealth, where the Ruh had a winter camp they had used for forty years.

It was a good place. A long narrow valley between two wooded ridges, with a stream running down the middle and a great old barn at the head of it, half ruined, that the troupe had patched up over the years with salvaged timber and painted canvas. They drew the wagons up around it in a half circle and hobbled the horses in the meadow and cut firewood from the ridges and settled in to wait for spring.

There were other Ruh there too. Two other troupes, smaller than Hallow's, who had been wintering in the valley with them for years. Twenty-odd people, all told, and a great many children, and a great many dogs, and one very old goat named Duchess who belonged to no one and went wherever she pleased.

They took me in. They did not ask who I was. Tessit told them I was a cousin, a trouper who had been hurt in the war and lost his wits, and they nodded and did not ask anything else. That is the way of the Ruh. One family, under the sky.

---

I worked.

That was the first thing I learned that winter, as Kote. How to work. Not the way I had worked before—I did not remember how I had worked before—but the way a man with no name works. With his hands. With his back. Without thinking about it.

I cut wood. Every day, from dawn until noon, up on the ridges with an axe and a saw. I did not know how, at first. I was clumsy and slow and I cut myself twice. But my hands learned. They were good hands; they had been trained, once, to do fine and careful things, and they remembered some of it even when I did not. By midwinter I could fell a tree and limb it and cut it into lengths faster than any man in the camp.

I hauled water. From the stream to the barn, two buckets at a time, twenty trips a day.

I mended things. Harness and wagon wheels and the patched canvas walls of the barn. The Ruh were always mending things, and there were never enough hands to do it, and mine were good ones.

I cooked. Mirra taught me. She stood beside me at the great iron pot over the fire, with her round face very grave, and showed me how to cut onions without crying and how to skin a rabbit and how much salt to use and when. I learned. I learned the way my hands had learned to cut wood: slowly at first, then all at once.

"You've a knack for it," said Mirra, the first time I made the stew alone and everyone ate it and no one complained. She looked at me with her head on one side. "Your mother never did. She could sing like a lark, but she couldn't boil water without burning it." She smiled. "Your father did all the cooking in your troupe. He was very good."

"I don't remember."

"I know." She patted my arm. "But your hands do."

---

I polished things.

I do not know why. No one told me to. But every evening, when the work was done and the others were gathered around the great fire in the barn, singing and telling stories and mending their costumes for spring, I would find something that needed polishing. A copper pot. A harness buckle. A lantern. Kellen's knives, which he used for juggling and which were always dull from use.

I would sit a little apart from the others, with a rag and a pot of grease or oil or ash, and I would polish. Slowly. Carefully. In small even circles. Until the thing gleamed.

It was soothing. I did not know why. It kept me small. It kept me quiet. It filled up the hours between work and sleep, when otherwise I would only have sat and looked at the fire and felt the hole in my chest.

Someone had told me to do it. A girl with hair like a cloud, on a roof in the dark. I did not remember her name. But I remembered her voice. *Polish things. It keeps you small.*

---

Bast did not like it.

"You're not a servant," he said one night, sitting beside me while I polished Mirra's big copper stewpot. "You don't have to do that."

"I like doing it."

"You don't like anything. You don't *feel* anything." His voice was sharp. "You're just doing it because someone told you to."

I looked up at him. He was sitting on an upturned bucket, with his elbows on his knees and his chin in his hands, glaring at the stewpot. His deep blue eyes were very bright in the firelight.

"Maybe," I said. "But it's something to do."

"There are better things to do."

"Like what?"

He opened his mouth. Then he closed it again. He did not seem to know what to say.

"You used to play," he said at last. "The lute. Every night. Wherever we were. By the fire, or on a roof, or in a tavern. You used to play until your fingers bled." He looked at me. "You used to sing."

"I know. Everyone tells me."

"But you don't remember."

"No."

Bast looked at me for a long time. Then he looked away. His jaw was tight.

"I hate this," he said quietly. "I hate this so much. Watching you. Polishing pots. Cutting wood. Being *small*." He swallowed. "You were never small, Reshi. Never. Not once. You were the biggest thing I ever saw. Bigger than my father's court. Bigger than the Sithe. Bigger than the mountains." His voice cracked. "And now you're polishing a stewpot."

I did not say anything. I did not know what to say.

After a while, Bast got up and went away. I heard him outside the barn, in the dark, making a sound I did not recognize. It might have been weeping.

I went back to polishing the stewpot.

---

Lark learned to walk that winter.

She was a year old by then, or near it. A small, solemn, dark-eyed child, with a fuzz of dark hair that was beginning to curl. She did not cry much anymore. She watched everything with her dark eyes, very gravely, the way very old people watch things.

She liked me. I did not know why. Nell said she did not like anyone else, except Mirra and Nell herself. But whenever I sat down, she would crawl over to me and pull herself up by my trouser leg and stand there, swaying, holding on, looking up at me.

And one evening, in the barn by the fire, she let go.

She stood there by herself, swaying, with her arms out for balance. She looked at me. I looked at her. Everyone in the barn went quiet.

She took a step. Toward me. Then another. Then she fell forward, into my lap, and grabbed my shirt with both hands, and looked up at me, and laughed.

It was the first time I had heard her laugh. It was a small, bright, startled sound, like a bird taking flight.

Everyone in the barn laughed with her. Mirra was crying. Nell was clapping. Tessit was grinning under his battered hat.

I looked down at the child in my lap, laughing up at me with her dark eyes shining.

And something happened. Very faint. Very far down. At the bottom of the hole.

It was not a feeling. Not exactly. It was more like the memory of a feeling. The shape of it, without the substance. Like the ghost of a smile, or the echo of a song.

But it was warm.

---

I tried the lute again, once, near the end of winter.

It was not my lute. My lute was in the chest, in the back of Tessit's wagon, under a canvas cover, locked away. This was Brin's old lute, the one he had played before he switched to fiddle. It was battered and cracked and missing a string, and the pegs would not hold their tuning.

But it was a lute. And one night, when everyone else had gone to bed and I was alone in the barn with the fire, I picked it up.

I tuned it as best I could. I played a chord.

It was nothing. Grey. The same as before. A painting of a fire on a wall.

I played another. And another. I played a song. I did not know which song; my hands chose it. A slow one, a sad one, with a melody that rose and fell like wind in trees.

It was nothing. It was grey.

But I played it to the end. And when I finished, I looked up, and Lark was standing in the doorway of the barn, in her nightdress, with her thumb in her mouth. She had woken up and come looking for the sound.

She was looking at me with her dark solemn eyes.

"Again," she said.

It was one of the first words she ever said. Mirra told me later. *Again*.

I looked at her. I looked down at the battered lute in my lap.

I played it again.

---

We left in the spring. When the snow was gone from the ridges and the stream was running high and brown with meltwater and the troupe was painting their wagons and mending their costumes for the season on the roads.

Tessit tried to make me stay.

"You've a place here," he said. We were standing by the wagons in the grey dawn, with the horses hitched and the troupe gathering. "With us. With the Ruh. You're family. You always will be." He looked at me. "Stay. Travel with us. Play, if you want. Or don't. Cook, and cut wood, and polish pots. It doesn't matter. Just stay."

"I can't."

"Why not?"

I looked at the wagon where Lark was sitting, on Mirra's lap, with her thumb in her mouth. Watching me with her dark solemn eyes.

"Because of her," I said. "Because of what she is. And what I am." I did not know how I knew it. But I knew it, the way I knew how to cut wood. "If they come looking for me—the ones who want what's in the chest—they'll find her too. And then everything will have been for nothing."

Tessit was quiet for a long time.

"Your mother said something like that once," he said at last. "To Arliden. When you were very small. She said, *we have to keep moving, because if they find me, they'll find him too*." He shook his head. "She never said who *they* were."

"Did he listen?"

"He listened. They kept moving. They never stopped. Not for twelve years." Tessit looked at me. "And in the end, they found her anyway."

"I know."

"Then why—"

"Because I'm not going to keep moving," I said. "I'm going to stop. Somewhere quiet. Somewhere small. Somewhere no one would ever look for anyone." I paused. "And I'm going to stay there. And be small. And wait."

"Wait for what?"

I did not answer. I did not know.

---

Nell cried when I left. She held on to my leg and would not let go, and Mirra had to come and pry her fingers loose one at a time. She was nine now, or near it, and she was trying very hard to be grown up about it, and failing.

"You pulled me out of the well," she sobbed. "You're not allowed to leave. You pulled me out of the well."

I knelt down in front of her. I did not know what to say. But I remembered something. Very faint. From somewhere far down.

"You'll see me again," I said.

She looked at me with wet enormous eyes. "Promise?"

"I promise."

I did not know if it was true. I did not know anything. But she stopped crying.

Lark did not cry. She sat on Mirra's hip and looked at me with her dark solemn eyes, the way she always did. When I went to say goodbye to her, she reached out one small hand and touched my face. Very lightly. The way someone else had touched it, on a bridge, at dawn.

"Again," she said.

I did not understand. Mirra did. Her eyes were wet.

"She wants you to come back," she said softly. "And play for her again."

I looked at the child. At her dark solemn eyes. At her small hand on my face.

"Someday," I said.

---

Bast and I walked out of the valley that morning with the cart and the chest and a mule named Patience, who had been a parting gift from Tessit and who had, as far as anyone could tell, none at all.

At the top of the ridge, I stopped and looked back.

The troupe was pulling out of the valley in the other direction. Three painted wagons in a line, bright against the green of spring. Brin was on the seat of the first one, sawing at his fiddle. I could hear it, faintly, every third note flat. Illa was singing.

And on the tailboard of the last wagon, a small girl in a red dress was sitting with a smaller girl on her lap. They were both looking back up at the ridge. At me.

The smaller girl raised her hand.

I raised mine.

Then the wagons went around a bend in the valley, and they were gone.

"Reshi," said Bast quietly, beside me. "We should go."

"Yes."

I did not move. I stood on the ridge and looked at the empty valley for a long time.

It was grey. Everything was grey.

But I stood there anyway.
