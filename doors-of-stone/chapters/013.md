# Stone

For a span after that, I did not go down into the Underthing at all.

I told myself it was because I was busy. It was the end of term, and there were examinations to prepare for, and a great deal of work to finish in the Fishery. Kilvin had set me to making a new kind of heat-funnel, and I was struggling with it. The Eolian wanted me to play three nights a span instead of two, because the winter crowds were large and restless and hungry for music. And I was working on my father's song every night, late, by the light of Kilvin's candle, filling page after page with fragments and half-verses and melodies that did not quite fit together.

But that was not the real reason. The real reason was that I was afraid.

I have been afraid many times in my life. I was afraid on the streets of Tarbean, when I was a child and alone and every shadow might hide a man with a knife. I was afraid in the Eld, hunting bandits with a handful of mercenaries. I was afraid in the Fae, in the arms of Felurian, when I knew that a single wrong word would mean my death or my madness.

But I had never been afraid like this. It was not the fear of death. It was not the fear of pain. It was something older and deeper and harder to name. It was the fear of being known.

The Sleeper had spoken my name. Not the name I was called by, not *Kvothe*, the name my parents gave me. My true name. The name that was the shape of me, the thing that made me what I was. I had not recognized the word, but I had known it the way you know your own face in a mirror.

And if he knew my name, he had power over me. That was the first thing Elodin had ever taught me about naming, and the most important. To know a thing's name is to have mastery of it. To know a man's name is to hold him in the palm of your hand.

I did not know who the Sleeper was. I did not know what he wanted. But I knew that he had my name. And I was afraid.

---

It was Elodin who found me, in the end. He found me the way he always found people, which is to say without appearing to look.

I was sitting in the round room at the top of the Rookery, the shy room that would not let me find it again after that term. I had come there to listen to the stone, but I was not listening. I was staring at the wall, thinking about the Cellarage, and I did not hear him come in.

"You look," said Elodin, "like a man who has seen a ghost."

I jumped. He was standing in the doorway, leaning against the frame with his arms folded, watching me with his dark unreadable eyes.

"Not a ghost," I said. "Something else."

"Ah." He came into the room and sat down on the floor across from me, cross-legged, like a child. He picked up Elodin's stone and turned it over in his hands. "Tell me."

I hesitated. I had not told anyone about the Cellarage. Not Sim or Wil, not Devi, not Fela. It was Auri's secret, and I had promised long ago never to tell anyone about Auri.

But Elodin already knew about Auri. He had known about her before I did. And he was the Master Namer. If anyone could tell me what I had heard, it was him.

So I told him. I told him about the Cellarage, and the treasures, and the bed of black stone. I told him about the Sleeper, breathing. I told him about the word he had spoken, and how I had known it was my name.

Elodin listened without interrupting. His face did not change. When I was done, he sat for a long time turning the stone over in his hands.

"Well," he said at last. "That's inconvenient."

I stared at him. "Inconvenient?"

"Very." He set the stone down on the floor between us. "You should not have gone there. Neither should she. But she's been going there for years, and it's never noticed her, because she's very small and very quiet and very careful. You are none of those things."

"You know about the Cellarage?"

"Of course I know about the Cellarage. I've been down there. Once. A long time ago." He looked at the stone. "I didn't go back."

"Who is he? The Sleeper?"

Elodin was silent for a long time. When he spoke, his voice was quieter than I had ever heard it.

"I don't know his name," he said. "Nobody does. That's the point of him. But I know what he is." He looked up at me. "He's a guard."

"A guard? Guarding what?"

"What do you think?" Elodin gestured around us, at the walls of the round room, at the Rookery, at the whole University spread out beneath us. "Why do you think there's a University here, of all places? Why do you think the Archives were built on this spot, on this particular stretch of riverbank, when there are a hundred better places a day's ride in any direction?"

I did not answer. I did not know.

"Because something was already here," Elodin said. "Something very old. Something that needed to be watched. And the people who built the Archives, whoever they were, knew that. So they built it here, on top of it, like a lid on a jar. And they put a guard at the bottom, to make sure the lid stayed on." He picked up the stone again. "And then they went away, and people forgot, and the University grew up around the Archives like moss on a rock. And now nobody remembers what's down there, or why."

"What is down there?"

Elodin looked at me. His eyes were very dark.

"A door," he said.

---

I did not ask him what kind of door. I did not have to. I had dreamed of it.

"The Four-plate door," I said slowly. "It's not the real door. Is it? It's just the top of the stair."

Elodin raised an eyebrow. "Who told you that?"

"A friend."

"Your friend is well informed." He turned the stone over in his hands. "The Four-plate door is a seal. One of several. It keeps people from going down. There are other seals, deeper. And at the bottom—" He stopped.

"At the bottom?"

"At the bottom, there's the thing the seals are protecting. Or protecting against. I've never been sure which." He set the stone down. "And beside it, sleeping, there's the Sleeper. Who has been down there for a very long time, keeping watch. Dreaming. Waiting for the day when someone comes to open the door."

"And then what? He wakes up?"

"And then he wakes up." Elodin smiled faintly. "And then, I imagine, things become very interesting for whoever is standing in front of the door."

We sat in silence for a while. Outside the window, the snow was falling again, soft and white.

"He spoke my name," I said at last. "Why? Why would he know my name?"

Elodin looked at me for a long moment. There was something in his face I could not read. Something that might have been pity.

"I don't know," he said. "But I can guess. Can't you?"

I thought about it. I thought about the blue book in the Archives, and the black mark where my mother's name should have been. I thought about the line of writing on the last page. *The door holds while the line holds.*

"Because of my family," I said slowly. "Because of who my mother was."

Elodin did not say anything. But he did not look surprised.

"You knew," I said. "About my mother. You knew."

"I suspected." He shrugged. "You have a look about you. Something in the eyes, something in the bones. I've seen it before, in old portraits. In the faces of people who carried a certain name." He picked up the stone again and held it out to me. "Which is why I wanted you to learn stone. Of all the names in the world, that's the one you'll need most."

I took the stone. It was cold in my hand. Colder than it should have been.

"Why?"

"Because," Elodin said, "the door is made of stone. And someday, whether you want to or not, you're going to be standing in front of it. And when you do, you'd better know how to ask it to stay closed."

---

He left after that. He stood up and walked out of the round room without another word, the way he always did, and left me alone with the stone.

I sat there for a long time, holding it. Feeling its weight. Its coldness. Its patience.

I did not try to hear it. I did not reach for it. I was too tired, and too frightened, and too full of thoughts I did not want to think. I simply sat there, holding it, the way you might hold the hand of a friend at a funeral. Not asking anything. Just being there.

And after a while, I don't know how long, I realized that I could hear it.

It was very faint. Fainter than a whisper. Fainter than the sound of my own blood in my ears. It was not a word, not exactly. It was more like a feeling. A long, slow, patient feeling, stretching back further than I could imagine and forward further than I could follow.

It was *no*. Just as Fela had said. The most patient no in the world.

No, I will not move. No, I will not change. No, I will not break.

I sat there with the stone in my hands and listened to it, and I felt something I had not felt in a very long time. It was peace. A deep, quiet, bottomless peace, like the peace at the bottom of the sea. The peace of something that has been itself for so long that nothing can ever make it anything else.

I closed my eyes. I let the stone's no wash over me, through me, into me. I let it settle into my bones.

And then, very quietly, I heard something else.

It was beneath the stone's voice. Far beneath. So deep that I could barely feel it, the way you can barely feel the rumble of a cart on a road a mile away. It was not saying no. It was saying something else. Something I could not make out.

But it was not patient. And it was not peaceful.

It was hungry.

---

I opened my eyes. The round room was dark. The snow had stopped falling outside the window, and the sky was clear and black and full of stars. I had been sitting there for hours.

I looked down at the stone in my hands. It was just a stone. Grey and rough and ordinary.

But I could still hear it, faintly, at the edge of my mind. Its long slow no. And beneath it, fainter still, that other thing. That hungry thing.

I stood up. My legs were stiff and my back ached. I put the stone in my pocket and went down the stairs of the Rookery and out into the cold clear night.

The stars were very bright. The snow crunched under my feet. The University was silent and dark around me, its great grey buildings looming against the sky.

And I could hear them. All of them. Every stone in every wall, every paving stone in every courtyard, every foundation stone buried deep in the earth. They were all saying no. Softly, patiently, endlessly. A great slow chorus of no, rising up from the bones of the world.

Fela had said it was the most beautiful thing she had ever heard. She was right.

But beneath it, so faint I could almost convince myself I was imagining it, I could hear that other voice. The one that was not saying no. The one that was hungry.

It was coming from the Archives. From deep beneath the Archives. From somewhere far below the Four-plate door, far below the Cellarage and the sleeping king, far below anything I had ever seen.

It was coming from behind the door.

And I knew, with a certainty that I could not explain, that it was listening to me too.
