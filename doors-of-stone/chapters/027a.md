# The Maer's Table

There is a particular kind of war that is fought across dinner tables. It has no swords and no armies and no banners. It has spoons, and salt cellars, and the exact order in which people are seated. It has compliments that are insults and insults that are compliments. It has silences that last exactly one heartbeat too long.

I had learned to fight in that war the year before, at the Maer's court, with Bredon's help. I had not been very good at it. I had been young and proud and far too quick with my tongue, and I had made a great many enemies who did not need to be enemies. But I had learned. And by the time I left Severen for Ademre, I could hold my own at a dinner table against most of the courtiers in Vintas.

I had not expected to need that skill again so soon.

---

The Maer's dinner, on my third night back in Severen, was a small one. Only forty guests. The Maer at the head of the long table, with Meluan at his right hand. The Maer's cousin, a thin sour man named Lord Varren, at his left. And then, in careful descending order down both sides of the table, the lesser lords and ladies of the court, arranged by rank and favor and the subtle calculus of who had offended whom most recently.

I was seated two-thirds of the way down, on the left. Below the salt, but not far below it. It was a carefully calculated position. It said, to everyone who knew how to read such things: *This young man is the Maer's guest, and he has done the Maer great service, but he is a Ruh, and a commoner, and he is not one of us.*

I did not mind. I had been seated below the salt before.

What I minded was the woman across from me.

---

Her name was Lady Ennet. She was perhaps forty, handsome in a sharp-edged way, with dark hair piled high and a necklace of pearls that must have cost more than most villages. She was the wife of a minor lord from the eastern marches, and she had, I learned later, a younger sister who had run away with a troupe of players twenty years before and never been heard from again.

She hated the Ruh. She hated them the way some people hate spiders, with a deep instinctive loathing that had nothing to do with reason. And she had been seated directly across from a Ruh, at the Maer's own table, and she did not intend to let the evening pass without making her feelings known.

She began with the soup.

"I understand," she said, to the lady on her left, in a voice pitched to carry exactly as far as me and no farther, "that the Maer has taken to collecting performers. Like a child collecting pretty stones."

The lady on her left tittered.

I did not look up from my soup. I had learned, at the Maer's court, that the first rule of dinner-table warfare is never to acknowledge the first shot. It shows you are wounded.

"I suppose it's charming," Lady Ennet went on. "In its way. A little Ruh, performing at dinner. Like a trained monkey." She sipped her wine. "One does worry about the silver, of course."

The lady on her left tittered again. Several people nearby had gone quiet, the way people go quiet when they sense blood in the water.

I finished my soup. I set down my spoon. I looked across the table at Lady Ennet and smiled.

"My lady is very kind to worry," I said. "But she needn't. I've been in the Maer's house for three days now, and I've counted every piece of silver on this table twice." I paused. "There's a spoon missing from your setting. I assumed you'd taken it for safekeeping."

---

It was not a very good riposte. I have made better. But it landed.

Lady Ennet looked down at her place setting. There were three spoons beside her plate. There should have been four. The fourth was lying on the floor beneath her chair, where she had knocked it with her elbow during the first course, and a servant had not yet noticed to retrieve it.

The people nearby laughed. Not loudly. Not cruelly. Just the small surprised laugh of people who have seen a clever thing.

Lady Ennet's face went very red. Then very white.

"How dare you," she said.

"My lady?"

"How dare you imply—"

"I implied nothing, my lady." I smiled again. "I only said you'd taken it for safekeeping. Which was very wise of you, with a Ruh at the table." I picked up my wine. "One does worry about the silver."

---

I thought that would be the end of it. It usually was, at the Maer's court. A shot, a counter-shot, a laugh, and then everyone moved on to the fish course and pretended nothing had happened.

It was not the end of it.

Lady Ennet did not speak to me again through the fish. Or the fowl. Or the roast. She talked to the lady on her left, and the lord on her right, and she did not so much as glance across the table at me. I thought she had given up.

Then the sweet course came. And she set down her fork and looked across the table at me, and her face was calm and cold and terrible.

"You're Ruh," she said. Not loudly. But the people nearby heard it, and went quiet.

"I am."

"Where from?"

"The road, my lady. The Ruh are from the road."

"Your family. Your troupe. Which one?"

I hesitated. It was the barest hesitation. But she saw it.

"Arliden's troupe," I said.

Lady Ennet went very still.

"Arliden," she said slowly. "The trouper. The one who made songs."

"Yes."

"He had a wife." Her voice was very quiet now. "A singer. Laurian."

I did not answer. I did not have to. She was looking at my face, at my hair, at my eyes. And something was happening in her own face. Something I did not understand.

"My sister," said Lady Ennet, "ran away with a troupe. Twenty years ago. The summer she was sixteen." Her voice had gone flat. "She sent a letter, once. Only once. From a town in the Commonwealth. She said she was happy. She said she was singing. She said she'd met a woman in the troupe who was teaching her, a woman with the most beautiful voice she'd ever heard." Lady Ennet looked at me. "The woman's name was Laurian."

---

The table had gone utterly silent. Not just the people near us. The whole table. Forty lords and ladies, with their forks halfway to their mouths, staring.

At the head of the table, the Maer had set down his wine glass. Beside him, Meluan had gone white as milk.

"What happened to her?" I said. My voice was very quiet. "Your sister."

Lady Ennet looked at me for a long moment.

"I don't know," she said. "She never wrote again. I waited. For years. I sent letters to every town in the Commonwealth where the Ruh were known to play. No one knew where she'd gone." Her voice cracked, very slightly. "And then, a few years ago, I heard a story. From a traveler. About a troupe that had been found in a forest, burned. Everyone dead." She looked at me. "Arliden's troupe."

I did not move.

"Was she there?" said Lady Ennet. "My sister? Her name was Karis. She'd be thirty-six now. Dark hair, blue eyes. She had a little scar on her chin, from falling out of a tree when she was seven." Her voice was shaking now. "Was she there? When they died?"

I sat very still at the Maer's table, with forty faces turned toward me, and I opened the doors of sleep, just a crack, and I looked.

I saw the troupe. The wagons. The fire. The faces around the fire, singing. My father, and my mother, and Trip, and Shandi, and Teren, and old Abenthy before he left us.

And a young woman with dark hair and blue eyes and a small scar on her chin. Sitting beside my mother, learning a harmony. Laughing when she got it wrong.

Karis. We had called her Kari. She had joined us the summer I was eight. She had taught me to whistle through my fingers.

She had been there. At the end. I had seen her. Lying in the grass beside the burning wagon, with her dark hair spread out around her head and her blue eyes open, looking at the stars.

---

I closed the doors of sleep.

I looked at Lady Ennet across the table. At her sharp handsome face, and her pearls, and her eyes, which were blue. I had not noticed, before. They were very blue.

"Yes," I said quietly. "She was there."

Lady Ennet made a sound. A small broken sound, like the sound a bird makes when it hits a window.

"She taught me to whistle," I said. "Through my fingers. She was very good at it. Better than anyone. She could whistle so loud it made the horses jump." I paused. "She was happy, my lady. She was so happy. She laughed all the time. She was learning to sing, and she was getting good, and my mother said she'd be the best voice in the troupe someday."

Lady Ennet was weeping. Silently. At the Maer's table, in front of forty lords and ladies, with her hand pressed against her mouth.

"She talked about you," I said. I did not know, until I said it, that it was true. But it was. It came up out of the dark with the rest. "She said she had an older sister who was very beautiful and very proud and very angry with her. She said she was going to write to her, someday, when she was famous. So her sister would know she'd done the right thing."

Lady Ennet stood up. She stood up so suddenly that her chair went over behind her with a crash. She stood at the Maer's table with her face wet and her hand over her mouth, and she looked at me.

Then she turned and walked out of the dining hall. Very straight. Very fast. Without a word to anyone.

---

No one said anything for a long time.

Then, at the head of the table, the Maer picked up his wine glass. He looked down the long table, at all the frozen faces, and he said, in a perfectly ordinary voice:

"The pudding is very good tonight. My compliments to the kitchen."

And slowly, uncertainly, the table began to eat again. And talk again. And the war of the dinner table resumed, as it always did, as if nothing had happened.

But I did not eat. I sat at my place, two-thirds of the way down the table, with my hands in my lap and my spoon untouched, and I looked at the empty chair across from me.

---

She found me later that night, in the gardens.

I was sitting on a bench under an old oak, in the dark, not thinking about anything. I heard footsteps on the gravel path and looked up, and she was standing there. She had changed out of her dinner gown into something plainer. She had taken off the pearls. Her face was pale and her eyes were red.

"May I sit?" she said.

"Of course, my lady."

She sat on the bench beside me. She did not say anything for a long time.

"I was cruel to you," she said at last. "At dinner. About the silver. About the monkey." She looked down at her hands. "I've been cruel to every Ruh I've met, for twenty years. Because of her. Because she left." She was quiet for a moment. "I told myself they'd stolen her. That they'd turned her head with songs and pretty words and taken her away. I told myself it was their fault."

"It wasn't anyone's fault, my lady. She wanted to go."

"I know." Lady Ennet's voice was very small. "I know. I think I always knew." She looked at me. "You said she was happy."

"She was."

"Truly?"

"Truly." I looked at her. "My lady, I was a child. I didn't understand much. But I understood that. She was one of the happiest people I've ever known."

Lady Ennet closed her eyes. Tears slid down her cheeks. She did not wipe them away.

"Thank you," she whispered.

---

We sat together on the bench under the oak for a long time. She asked me questions about Kari, and I answered them as best I could. What songs she sang. What she laughed at. Whether she had ever fallen in love. I did not remember all of it. I had been so young. But I remembered some. And I told her what I remembered.

At last she stood up. She looked down at me in the dark.

"You're Laurian's son," she said. "Aren't you? Not just one of the troupe. Her son."

"Yes, my lady."

She looked at me for a long moment. Something moved in her face.

"The Maer's wife," she said slowly. "Meluan. Her sister ran away with a Ruh too. Everyone at court knows the story. Netalia Lackless." She was quiet. "I saw her face, at dinner, when you said your mother's name."

I did not say anything.

Lady Ennet looked at me for a long time. Then she nodded, once, very slightly. As if she had decided something.

"I won't say anything," she said. "To anyone. I owe you that. For Kari." She paused. "But be careful, boy. This court has ears everywhere. And some of them belong to people who would be very interested to know who your mother was."

"Anyone in particular?"

Lady Ennet was quiet for a moment.

"The old man," she said. "The tak player. Bredon." She looked at me. "He was sitting at the far end of the table tonight. Did you see him?"

I had not. I had been too busy with Lady Ennet, and the silver, and Kari.

"He was watching you," said Lady Ennet. "The whole time. Especially when you said Laurian's name." She drew her shawl around her shoulders. "He smiled."

She turned and walked away down the gravel path, into the dark.

I sat on the bench under the oak for a very long time after she had gone. And I did not go to play tak with Bredon the next day, as I had planned.

I told myself I was tired.
