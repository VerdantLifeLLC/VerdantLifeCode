# PROLOGUE: A Silence Waiting

Dawn came slowly to the Waystone Inn, and when it came it found a silence of three parts.

The first part was the silence of a house that has not yet decided to wake. The fire in the great stone hearth had burned down to a bed of red and grey, and the grey was winning. The chairs sat upside down on the tables like sleeping animals with their legs in the air. Nothing creaked. Nothing settled. The kettle hanging on its iron arm did not so much as tick as it cooled. If there had been a cat, it would have been asleep, and if there had been a dog, it would have been dreaming, and neither of them would have made a sound. But there was no cat and no dog, and so the silence of the house was a plain one, clean and uncomplicated, like a bowl that has been washed and set upside down to dry.

The second part was a waiting silence. It came in under the door and through the chinks in the shutters along with the thin grey light. It was the silence of a town that has learned to hold its breath. In the months past, the people of Newarre had learned many new silences. There was the silence of a road where no carts came. There was the silence of a field left unharvested because the man who worked it had gone off to fight for a king he had never seen. There was the silence that fell when a stranger walked down the only street, and the particular hard silence of a mother who has heard something moving in the dark beyond the edge of the lamplight and knows better than to go and look. All these silences had crept into Newarre and settled there, and on this grey morning they gathered close around the inn as if they were waiting for something. As if they knew.

The third silence was not an easy thing to notice. If you had walked into the Waystone that morning, you might have stood for an hour among the upturned chairs and never heard it at all. But it was there. It lived in the long bar of dark wood and in the bottles behind it. It lived in the blank space on the wall above the hearth, where a sword hung on two pegs beneath a plaque with a single word burned into it. It lived most of all in the innkeeper, who stood behind the bar with a white cloth in his hand, polishing a stretch of mahogany that did not need polishing.

He had red hair. Not ginger, not auburn, not the color of copper or of rust or of the leaves in autumn. Red, true red, the color of a flame that has been burning for a long time and means to burn for a long while yet. It was the only thing about him that seemed to mean anything at all.

The rest of him was bruised. There was a darkness along his jaw like a smudge of ink, and one of his eyes was swollen, and he moved his left arm with the careful economy of a man who knows exactly where the pain begins and has no wish to go looking for its edges. He did not look like a man who had been in a fight so much as a man who had been caught out in weather. Something had happened to him, and he had let it.

That was the third silence. It was the silence of a man who is waiting, and has been waiting for a long time, and has grown so used to waiting that he has forgotten what it was he wanted. It was the patient, cut-flower silence of a man who is waiting to die.

He polished the bar. Outside, a bird tried a few notes and thought better of it.

---

Somewhere above him, a board creaked.

The innkeeper's hands stopped. It was a small thing. A man walking past the bar would not have seen it. But for the space of a breath the cloth lay still on the wood, and the innkeeper's head tilted ever so slightly toward the ceiling, the way a musician's head will tilt at the first note of a tune he knows too well.

Then the moment passed, and the cloth began to move again in its slow, patient circles.

The stairs creaked. A young man came down them, dark-haired and fine-boned, moving with a graceful, slouching ease that made the stairs seem like a courtesy he was paying to the world rather than a thing he needed. He paused on the bottom step and looked across the room at the innkeeper, and his face did something complicated.

"Reshi," he said.

"Bast." The innkeeper did not look up. "You're awake early."

"I never went to sleep." Bast crossed the room and stood on the other side of the bar. He looked at the bruise along the innkeeper's jaw. He looked for a long time. "It's worse."

"Bruises are always worse on the second day. It's the nature of the thing. You know that."

"I know that you're a terrible liar when you're tired."

"Then it's good I'm not lying." The innkeeper folded the cloth into a neat square and set it on the bar. For the first time, he looked up, and his eyes were a flat and quiet green, like a pond at the edge of a wood. "It's the third day, Bast."

Bast's face went through several shapes before settling on something careful. "Yes."

"Is he awake?"

"Not yet. I checked. He sleeps like a scribe. On his back, with his hands folded, as if someone might come and read him." Bast hesitated. "Reshi. About the story."

"Go and put the kettle on."

"Reshi—"

"The kettle, Bast." The innkeeper's voice was mild. It was always mild. It had the texture of old linen, soft from a thousand washings. "We'll want tea. And porridge. And there's bread from yesterday that can be toasted if you watch it, which you won't, so I'll watch it. Go on."

For a moment it seemed Bast would argue. Something moved behind his eyes, something older and colder than a young man ought to carry. Then it went away again, and he was only a dark-haired boy with an unhappy mouth, and he went into the kitchen.

The innkeeper stood alone in the common room. He looked at the sword above the hearth. It hung there as it always did, grey and plain and unremarkable, the sort of blade a traveler might hang up to pay a debt. The plaque beneath it had been made by a man named Graham, who was good with wood and had been proud of the work. A single word was burned into it in black letters.

FOLLY.

The innkeeper looked at it the way a man looks at a letter he has read so many times that the paper has gone soft at the folds.

Then he began to take the chairs down from the tables, one by one, and set them on their feet. He did it quietly. He did it well. There was a rhythm to it, down and turn and set, down and turn and set, and anyone watching would have said that here was a man who knew his trade and took some pleasure in it.

Anyone watching would have been wrong.

---

Upstairs, in the best room the Waystone had to offer, a man named Devan Lochees opened his eyes and lay very still, listening to the sound of chairs being set upon the floor below.

Most people called him Chronicler. He had been called that for so long that he sometimes forgot it was not his name. He was a man past the first flush of youth, with a neat beard and the kind of face that suggests its owner has been told a great many lies and written most of them down anyway. His right hand ached in a dull, familiar way. There was ink ground into the side of his middle finger so deep that no amount of scrubbing would ever take it out. He had spent two long days listening and writing, and his hand knew it even if the rest of him had slept.

He lay there and thought about the story.

He had come to Newarre looking for a legend. He had found an innkeeper. He had been told, in a quiet voice across a quiet table, that the innkeeper would tell the whole of it, the true story, but that it would take three days. Chronicler had laughed at that. He had never taken more than two days to record any story, and those were long ones, the kind where the teller wandered off into his grandfather's childhood and had to be led back by the elbow.

Now it was the third day, and they were not close to done.

He thought about a young man in a red cloak standing before a door without a handle. He thought about a girl with dark hair who never stayed. He thought about a thing in a tree that could see every path the world might take and chose, every time, the one that ended worst.

He thought about the look on the innkeeper's face last night, after the soldiers, as he sat with a wet cloth to his jaw and stared into nothing. It had not been the look of a man in pain. It had been the look of a man who had been expecting pain for a long time, and was faintly disappointed that it had not been more.

Chronicler sat up. He reached for the leather satchel on the chair beside the bed and drew out a sheaf of paper, a fresh bottle of ink, and a pen he had cut the night before. He checked the nib. He checked it again. It was a habit, like a soldier checking his straps before a march.

Then he sat for a moment with the pen in his hand and did not move.

He had spent most of his life writing down true things. He was good at it. Better than good. He could take a man's rambling half-remembered account of a battle and render it down to its bones and set those bones on the page in such a way that anyone reading them would know what had happened, and why, and how it had felt. He had a cipher of his own devising that let him write faster than any man could speak. He had trained his memory until it was a sharp and orderly thing, a room with shelves where every word had its place.

And yet this morning he had the strangest feeling. It was the feeling a man gets when he has been carrying something heavy for a long time without noticing, and then someone asks him what it is, and he looks down and discovers that it is not his.

The story he was writing was not only a story. He could not have said how he knew that. But he knew it the way you know, walking into a room, that someone has been crying there.

Chronicler put the pen back in its case. He dressed. He went to the window and opened the shutter and looked out at the grey morning and the grey road and the scattering of grey houses that made up the town of Newarre.

Far off, above the trees at the edge of the fields, a thin line of smoke rose into the sky. It did not rise straight, the way smoke from a chimney rises on a still morning. It bent. It leaned, as if something were pulling at it.

Chronicler watched it for a long time. Then he closed the shutter and went downstairs.

---

The innkeeper looked up as he came into the common room and smiled. It was a good smile. Warm, practiced, professional. It was the smile of an innkeeper greeting a guest.

"Good morning," he said. "There's tea, and there will be porridge soon if Bast doesn't burn it."

"I heard that," said Bast's voice from the kitchen.

"You were meant to." The innkeeper gestured to a table near the hearth, where two chairs had already been set down and a third was waiting upside down on the tabletop, legs in the air, as if it could not decide whether to join them. "Sit. Eat. Then we'll begin."

Chronicler sat. He put his satchel on the table and did not open it. After a moment the innkeeper brought over a pot of tea and two cups and sat down across from him. He poured. His hands were steady. The bruise on his jaw had gone the color of a plum in the night, and the swelling around his eye had closed it halfway, but his hands were perfectly steady.

"You're hurt," Chronicler said.

"I've been hurt before." The innkeeper set the pot down. "And I suspect I will be again before the day is done. Drink your tea."

Chronicler drank his tea. It was very good tea, and he said so.

"It's the water," said the innkeeper. "There's a spring behind the stable. It comes up cold even in summer. Old Cob says it's the coldest water in three counties, and while Old Cob says a great many things, I've never caught him lying about water." He turned his cup a quarter turn on the table and looked into it. "It's the third day."

"Yes."

"You've been wondering how I'll fit it all in."

Chronicler hesitated, then nodded. "Honestly? Yes. I've recorded a great many stories. I know how long a thing takes to tell. And from where we stopped last night—" He spread his hands. "There's a great deal left. The Maer. The University. The girl. The king. I don't see how—"

"You don't see how I can come to the end of it in a day." The innkeeper smiled again, but this smile was a different animal altogether. It was small and crooked and a little sad, and it did not belong to any innkeeper. "Neither do I. But I said three days, and I meant it. A thing that is said three times becomes true, and I have said it more than three times now." He lifted his cup. "Besides. Some stories are longer than the time it takes to tell them. And some are shorter. You'll see."

Bast came out of the kitchen carrying two bowls of porridge with the air of a man carrying the crown jewels of a small but proud nation. He set them down with a flourish. The porridge was not burnt. It was, in fact, perfect, and Bast looked so pleased with himself that Chronicler almost laughed.

The innkeeper looked down at his bowl for a long moment. Then he looked up at Bast.

"Thank you, Bast," he said.

Something in the way he said it made Bast go still. The young man's eyes, which were a very deep blue, went bright for a moment. Then he sniffed and turned away and said, "It's only porridge, Reshi," and went back into the kitchen, and they heard him banging pots there, rather more loudly than was strictly necessary.

They ate. Outside, the morning wore on. A wagon passed on the road, the first in days, its wheels creaking. Someone shouted a greeting and was answered. The silences of Newarre drew back a little, the way they always did in the daylight, and waited.

When the bowls were empty, the innkeeper pushed his away and folded his hands on the table.

"Get out your pen," he said.

Chronicler got out his pen.

The innkeeper looked at him for a long moment. There was something in his green eyes, something moving under the surface like a fish under ice. Then it was gone.

"I have told you," he said quietly, "how I came to the University, and what I did there, and how I left it for a time. I have told you about the Maer, and the Adem, and Felurian. I have told you a great deal about myself that I would rather not have said aloud." He paused. "Today I will tell you how I lost everything I had worked for. I will tell you how I came to be called Kingkiller. I will tell you about the doors of stone."

He looked across the room, at the sword hanging over the hearth.

"And I will tell you how I came to be here," he said. "Which is, I think, the part you've been waiting for."

Chronicler dipped his pen.

"Where did we stop?" asked the innkeeper, but he did not wait for an answer. He was already somewhere else, somewhere far away and long ago. His voice changed as he began to speak. It grew deeper, and warmer, and somehow younger, and the Waystone Inn seemed to lean in close around the table to listen.

"I came back to the University," said Kvothe, "with more money than I had ever held in my life. And I want you to understand how dangerous that was."
