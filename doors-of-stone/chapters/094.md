# The Storyteller at the Crossroads

We traveled through the Commonwealth for most of that spring and summer, Bast and I, with the cart and the chest and the mule named Patience.

I do not remember much of it. Roads. Towns. Inns and haylofts and camps by the roadside. Rain, and then sun, and then rain again. Bast talked, most of the time. He told me stories, and pointed out birds, and argued with Patience, who did not like him and made it very clear. I listened. I did not say much.

We did not know where we were going. That was the trouble. Bast had said an old storyteller would help us find a place. But we had not seen any old storyteller. We had seen a great many roads, and a great many towns, and none of them had felt right.

"How will we know?" I asked Bast once, when we had stopped for the night in a field outside a town called Hallowfell, and I was polishing the harness buckles by the light of our small fire.

"Know what?"

"When we've found it. The place."

Bast was quiet for a long time. He was lying on his back in the grass, looking up at the stars.

"I don't know," he said at last. "He said we'd know. He said it would be quiet. And small. And old." He turned his head and looked at me. "And he said there'd be a stone."

"A stone?"

"That's all he said. A stone." Bast looked back up at the stars. "He likes to be mysterious. It's very annoying."

---

We found him at a crossroads, at the end of summer.

It was a nothing sort of crossroads, in the middle of a nothing sort of country. Rolling fields of wheat and barley, gold and ripening in the late summer sun. A few farmhouses scattered here and there, with thin smoke rising from their chimneys. A line of woods on the horizon, dark and green. Two roads, crossing each other at right angles, both of them dusty and rutted and empty.

And at the corner where they crossed, there was an inn.

It was not much of an inn. A low stone building with a slate roof, and a stable, and a yard with a well in it. There was no sign. There was no name. There was only a lantern hanging over the door, unlit, and a bench beside the door where a man could sit and watch the roads.

There was a man sitting on it.

He was very old. Tall and thin, with a long white beard and hair the color of old snow. His face was lined and weathered, the face of a man who has spent his life outdoors. His clothes were ragged. And his eyes, when he looked up at the sound of our cart on the road, were very bright. Bright and dark at the same time, like the eyes of a bird.

"Well," he said, as we came up the road toward him. His voice was deep and rich and warm. "Well, well. Look who's finally come."

I stopped the cart. I looked at him. I did not know him.

But something in me went very still. The way it had gone still for Vashet, and for Elodin, and for the girl with cloud hair on the roof. As if some part of me recognized, even without a name, that this was someone who saw things.

Bast had gone very still too. He was sitting on the seat of the cart beside me, staring at the old man with wide eyes.

"You," he said. "You're—"

"Skarpi," said the old man. He smiled. His teeth were very white. "Yes. Hello, little prince. I told you I'd find you." He looked at me. "Hello, Kvothe."

"Kote," said Bast quickly. "He's Kote now."

"Is he?" Skarpi looked at me for a long moment with his bright bird's eyes. "Hm. Yes. I see. The little one's given you a little name." He nodded slowly. "That's clever. Very clever. She always was clever, that one." He stood up from the bench, unfolding himself like a heron rising from a pond. "Come in. Come in out of the sun. There's cider, and bread, and a great deal to talk about."

---

The inn was empty. No landlord, no serving girl, no other guests. Only a long low common room with a scatter of rough tables and benches, and a great stone hearth at one end with a fire burning in it, and a bar at the other end with a few bottles on a shelf behind it.

Skarpi went behind the bar as if he owned it. He poured three mugs of cider from a cask and brought them to a table by the hearth and set them down.

"Sit," he said. "Drink."

We sat. We drank. The cider was cold and sharp and very good.

Skarpi sat across from me and looked at me for a long time without speaking. His bright eyes moved over my face. My short brown-stained hair. My hands, resting on the table. My left wrist, with the thin pale line around it where the cord had been.

"You did it," he said at last. "You actually did it. You closed the door."

"I don't remember."

"No. Of course you don't." He sighed. "That's the price. I told you it would be. Or Selitos did. Or someone." He shook his head. "I'm getting old. I forget who told who what."

He was quiet for a moment.

"He heard it, you know," he said. "Selitos. In his tower. When you pulled your name out at the door. He heard it the way he hears everything, through that stone eye of his." Skarpi looked at me. "He sent word to me. By way of a raven, of all things. Very dramatic. The note said only one thing." He smiled faintly. "It said, *He found another way. Not the one I hoped for. But a way.*"

I did not say anything. I did not know what to say.

"And then I heard the other song," said Skarpi. "The girl's. The one she sang in the duke's hall, in Renere. The true one." His bright eyes were very soft. "That was well done. Very well done. I didn't think she had it in her. I was wrong."

"She's gone," I said. "Far away. I don't know where."

"I know." Skarpi nodded. "I know where. I won't tell you." He looked at me. "It's better that way."

---

"You said you'd help us find a place," said Bast. He had been quiet all this time, holding his mug of cider in both hands and staring at the old man. "Somewhere quiet. Somewhere safe."

"I did say that." Skarpi nodded. "And I will. But first, I need to know something." He looked at me. "The chest. The one with your name in it. Where is it?"

"In the cart."

"Outside? In the yard? In the sun?" Skarpi raised his eyebrows. "Well. That explains why it's so loud."

"Loud?"

"Very loud. I've been hearing it for a month. Getting closer. Coming down the road." He shook his head. "Your friends did good work. The roah, the locks, the girl's knot. It's very quiet. Much quieter than the cord was, on your wrist." He tapped the table with one long finger. "But it's still a name. A big one. The biggest one in the world, right now, maybe. And even locked up in a box of roah wood, it hums." He looked at me. "Anyone who knows how to listen can hear it. If they're close enough."

"How close?"

"Closer than you'd like." Skarpi leaned back. "It needs somewhere else. Somewhere that'll help it be quiet. Somewhere that drowns it out."

"Like where?"

Skarpi looked at me for a long moment. Then he smiled.

"Like a waystone," he said.

---

He told us about them, sitting by the fire in the empty inn, with the late summer sun slanting through the windows.

"You've seen greystones," he said. "Standing stones, in rings or alone, on hilltops and in fields. The old ones. Everyone has. They're all over the Four Corners."

"Doors," said Bast. "To the twilight. To my world."

"Some of them. Not all." Skarpi nodded. "Some of them are doors. Some are markers, for the old roads, the ones that ran between here and there before the war. And some of them—a very few—are something else." He leaned forward. "They're anchors. Things the namers put down, long ago, at the end of the war, to hold the world together. Big stones. Old stones. Stones that say no so loudly and so long that everything around them gets a little quieter."

"Quieter?"

"Names. Voices. Magic. Everything." Skarpi spread his hands. "Stand next to one of those stones and try to call the wind. You'll find it very hard. Try to do sympathy. Harder still. Try to hear a name." He shook his head. "You can barely hear your own thoughts. It's like standing next to a waterfall."

"And the chest—"

"Put the chest near one of those stones," said Skarpi, "and it'll be drowned out. The stone's *no* will be so loud that no one will hear the name humming underneath it. Not the Seven. Not the Thief. Not anyone."

I sat very still.

"There's one," I said. "Somewhere. Isn't there? That's why you're here."

Skarpi smiled.

"There's one," he said. "Not far from here. Two days' travel, north and east. In a little town at a crossroads, much like this one. A town so small and so quiet that no one's ever heard of it." He paused. "The stone's been there since before the town. Since before the roads. The people there don't know what it is. They call it the waystone, because it marks where the old road used to run. They walk past it every day and never think about it."

"And the town?"

"Is called Newarre," said Skarpi. "And there's an inn there, right beside the waystone, that's been empty for three years. The old innkeeper died. His widow went to live with her daughter in Baedn. The inn's for sale." He smiled. "Very cheap. No one wants it. It's too quiet. Too small. Too far from anything."

He looked at me with his bright bird's eyes.

"It's perfect," he said.

---

We stayed at the empty crossroads inn that night. Skarpi slept in a chair by the fire. Bast slept on a bench. I did not sleep. I sat at the table by the window and looked out at the dark road, and at the cart in the yard with the chest under its canvas cover.

I could not hear it humming. I did not know how to listen. But Bast could. He had told me, many times, on the road. He said it was like a song in another room, very faint, very sad. He said he heard it every night, when he tried to sleep.

Sometime after midnight, Skarpi woke. He came and sat across from me at the table. He did not say anything for a long time.

"You're wondering why I'm helping you," he said at last.

"Yes."

"Because you're the last Singer," said Skarpi. "Or you were. You sang the true song, at the Eolian. The whole of it. With the child in it." He was quiet for a moment. "That made you one of mine. Whether you remember it or not."

"I don't sing anymore."

"I know." Skarpi looked at me. "But you will. Someday." He leaned forward, and his bright eyes were very serious. "Listen to me, Kote. I'm going to tell you something. And I want you to remember it. Even if you don't remember anything else."

"All right."

"Someday," said Skarpi, "a man will come to your inn. Not soon. Years from now. Many years, maybe. He'll come down the road on a horse, or on foot, and he'll be running from something. And he'll come into your inn, and he'll sit at your bar, and he'll ask you for a story."

I looked at him.

"He'll be a scribe," said Skarpi. "A chronicler. A writer-down of true things. He'll have ink on his fingers and a satchel full of paper and a cipher of his own devising. And he'll ask you for the true story of Kvothe." He paused. "And you'll tell him."

"I don't remember the story."

"You will," said Skarpi. "When he comes. When he asks. It'll come back to you. A piece at a time." He smiled faintly. "Stories are names too. Did you know that? The oldest kind. You tell a man's whole story, from the beginning, and you're speaking his name. One word at a time."

I stared at him.

"Tell it to him," said Skarpi quietly. "All of it. The true story. Take as long as you need. And when you're done—"

"What?"

Skarpi looked at me for a long moment. His bright eyes were very old, and very tired, and very kind.

"When you're done," he said, "you'll know what to do."

---

He was gone in the morning. The way he always is. We woke, Bast and I, and the chair by the fire was empty, and the fire had burned down to ashes, and there was no sign he had ever been there at all.

But on the table, beside my empty cider mug, there was a purse. A heavy one. And a letter, folded small and sealed with plain wax.

I opened the purse. It was full of gold. More than enough to buy an inn. More than enough to buy three inns.

I opened the letter. It was written in a small precise hand I did not know.

*Sir,* it said. *His Majesty asks me to send you this, by way of an old friend of yours who says he can find you. His Majesty says it is not a gift. He says it is wages owed, for services rendered, which were never paid. He says it is the least he can do. He says it is far from the most.*

*Her Majesty sends her love. The child is well. She is walking now, and singing, and she has your mother's eyes.*

*I remain, sir, as ever, your servant.*

*Stapes.*

I read it three times. Then I folded it up very carefully and put it inside my shirt, next to the thread with the small knot on it.

Bast was watching me.

"What does it say?" he asked.

I looked at him.

"It says we're going to Newarre," I said.
