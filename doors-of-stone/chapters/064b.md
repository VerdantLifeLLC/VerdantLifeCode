# The Children in the Fountain

I have told you that Bast wandered the ruins of Myr Tariniel, those three days we stayed with Selitos, and came back each evening with his face very still and would not say what he had seen. I have told you what Selitos said to him, on the second evening. *Yes. They are still there. Some of them. The ones who would not leave.*

I did not ask, then. But on the third evening, Bast came back from the ruins and stood in the doorway of the round tower and looked at me across the room, where I sat among the books.

"Come," he said. "Please. There's something you should see."

---

He led me through the white city in the last of the light. The sun was going down behind the peaks, and the white stone had gone gold, and then rose, and the shadows of the broken walls stretched out long and blue across the snow. It was very quiet. It was always very quiet, in Myr Tariniel. But this evening the quiet had a different quality. It was not empty. It was listening.

Bast did not speak. He walked ahead of me through the ruins, his hooves making small crisp sounds in the snow, and he did not look back.

He led me to the fountain.

The one I had stopped at, the first day. A great round basin of white stone, half buried in snow, with a broken column rising from its center. With carvings on its rim, worn almost smooth by the wind and the years. Children, holding hands, dancing in a ring.

Bast stopped a little way from it. He stood very still.

"Watch," he whispered. "When the sun goes down. Just watch."

---

The sun went down.

The gold went out of the white stone. The rose went out of the snow. The sky above the peaks went from blue to violet to the deep dark blue that comes just before night. The first stars came out, very bright and very far away.

And the children came.

I do not know how else to say it. One moment the fountain was empty. A ruin, half buried in snow, with a broken column and a ring of worn carvings. And the next moment, there were children.

They were very faint. Fainter than the pale guests at the Lord of Hollow Hours' table. Like reflections in dark water. Like breath on a cold window. But they were there. A dozen of them. More. Playing in the fountain.

They were splashing. There was no water in the basin, only snow. But they were splashing, the way children splash in a fountain on a hot day, kicking and laughing and throwing handfuls of nothing at each other. They were chasing each other around the broken column. They were climbing on the rim, and falling off, and climbing on again. One very small girl was sitting on the edge with her feet dangling, singing.

I could not hear her. There was no sound. Only the wind, and the snow, and the great silence of the dead city. But I could see her mouth moving, and I could see the shape of the song on her face, and I knew it was a happy song.

I stood beside Bast in the blue dusk and watched them play.

---

"They come every night," said Bast. His voice was very quiet. "When the sun goes down. I watched them the first night, and the second. They play for an hour, maybe. And then—"

He stopped.

"And then what?"

Bast did not answer. He was looking at the children. His face was very still in the dusk.

I looked too.

The children had stopped playing.

All at once. The splashing stopped. The chasing stopped. The very small girl on the rim of the fountain stopped singing. They stood very still in the empty basin, all of them, and they turned their heads. All together. Toward the east. Toward the great arch on the eastern side of the plaza, where Selitos stood each morning to watch us go out.

And their faces changed.

I will not tell you what I saw on those faces. I cannot. It is the kind of thing that there are no words for, and if there were, I would not want to use them. I will tell you only that they were children, and that they had been playing, and that they had turned their heads toward the east at the sound of something coming. Something they had never heard before. Something that was the end of everything.

And they began to run.

Not away. That was the terrible thing. They did not run away. There was nowhere to run. They ran to each other. The older ones grabbed the younger ones. The very small girl on the rim of the fountain was snatched up by a boy of nine or ten, a thin dark-haired boy, and he held her against his chest and ran with her toward the far side of the fountain, toward a fallen slab of white marble that leaned against the broken column.

He pushed her under it. He pushed her into the gap between the slab and the stone floor of the basin, and he crawled in after her, and he pulled her close, and he put his hand over her mouth.

And then the fire came.

---

I did not see the fire. There was no fire. Only the blue dusk and the snow and the silent ruins. But the children saw it. I saw them see it. I saw their faces lit orange and red by a light that was not there. I saw them fall.

Not all at once. One at a time. Some of them reaching for each other. Some of them with their eyes closed. Some of them with their mouths open, calling out a name I could not hear.

And then they were gone. The fountain was empty. The snow lay smooth and unbroken in the basin. The first stars shone on the white stone.

Only the slab of marble remained, leaning against the broken column. And in the gap beneath it, for one moment more, very faint, a thin dark-haired boy with his arms around a very small girl, with his hand over her mouth, and his eyes wide open in the dark, staring out at the burning city.

Then they were gone too.

---

I stood beside Bast in the dusk for a long time. Neither of us spoke.

"Every night," said Bast at last. His voice was rough. "Every night, for ten thousand years. They play. And then it comes. And they die." He wiped his face with the back of his hand. "I asked Selitos. He said they don't know. They don't know they're dead. They only know the last hour. The playing, and then the fire. Over and over." He looked at the empty fountain. "He said he's tried to lay them to rest. Every way he knows. Names and songs and prayers. They won't go." Bast was quiet for a moment. "He said they're waiting for something. He doesn't know what."

I looked at the slab of white marble, leaning against the broken column.

"The boy," I said. "Under the slab. With the girl."

"Yes."

"That wasn't Skarpi."

Bast looked at me.

"Skarpi was alone," I said slowly. "He told me. He was hiding under a slab of marble, alone, and Selitos dug him out. He said he was the only one who lived." I looked at the fountain. "But that boy wasn't alone. He had the girl."

Bast did not say anything.

"That was his sister," I said. "It must have been. The girl. Skarpi had a sister. And he put her under the slab, and he held her, and he put his hand over her mouth so she wouldn't scream." My voice was very quiet. "And she died anyway. In the smoke, or the heat. With his hand over her mouth. And he lived."

The dusk was very still. The first stars were very bright.

"He never told me," I said. "He told me he was alone."

"Maybe he was," said Bast softly. "After."

---

I went back the next evening. Alone. I did not tell Bast. I did not tell Selitos. I went through the white city in the last of the light, with my lute on my back, and I sat down on the rim of the fountain, beside the place where the very small girl had sat with her feet dangling, singing.

And when the sun went down, and the children came, I played.

I did not play *Tariniel*. That was the song of their dying. They had heard it every night for ten thousand years; they did not need to hear it again.

I played the song the very small girl had been singing. I did not know it. I could not hear it. But I had watched her mouth, the night before, and I had seen the shape of the song on her face. A happy song. A children's song. The kind of song that every child in every age of the world knows, about nothing at all, about sunshine and water and running.

I played it the way I had played the stone's no to the Sleeper. Not to be heard. For them. So that they would have something, this once, besides the fire.

And the children stopped playing, and turned, and looked at me.

---

Not toward the east. Toward me. A dozen faint faces, like reflections in dark water, turned toward the red-haired stranger sitting on the rim of their fountain with a lute.

The very small girl came and sat beside me. Very close. I could not feel her. There was nothing to feel. But I saw her sit, and dangle her feet, and look up at me with her faint face, and open her mouth.

And sing.

I could not hear her. But I played what her mouth was shaping. Note for note. Word for word, though I did not know the words. And she sang, and I played, and the other children came and sat around the rim of the fountain and listened.

And the hour came when the fire should have come.

And it did not come.

They sat and listened. They did not turn toward the east. They did not run. They sat on the rim of their fountain in the blue dusk and listened to the very small girl sing, and to a stranger play along. And the hour passed. And the stars came out, all of them, bright and cold and very far away.

And one by one, the children stood up, and walked away into the ruins, and did not come back.

---

The very small girl was the last. She sat beside me on the rim of the fountain until the song was finished. And then she looked up at me, one more time, with her faint face.

She smiled.

And then she stood up and walked across the empty basin to the slab of white marble leaning against the broken column. And she knelt down and looked into the gap beneath it, the way a child looks for something she has lost. And then she crawled in. And was gone.

I sat on the rim of the fountain in the dark for a long time.

---

Selitos was standing at the door of the round tower when I came back. He did not say anything. He looked at me with his one good eye and his stone eye, glinting in the starlight.

"They're gone," I said.

"I know." His voice was very quiet. "I felt them go." He was silent for a long moment. "Ten thousand years."

"They were waiting," I said. "You said they were waiting for something. You didn't know what."

"And do you?"

I looked back across the dark ruins, toward the fountain.

"For someone to play them something else," I said. "Something besides the fire."

Selitos looked at me for a long time. Then he did something I had never seen him do. He reached out with his long hand and touched my face. Very lightly. The way you touch something you are afraid will break.

"Singer," he said.

That was all. He turned and went back into the tower.

---

I told Skarpi, years later, at a crossroads, when I did not remember any of it. I told him about the fountain, and the children, and the very small girl under the slab.

I did not remember telling him. Bast told me, afterward. He said Skarpi sat very still on his bench outside the empty inn and did not say anything for a long time. And then he put his long thin hands over his face and wept.

And then he said a name. A small name. A girl's name, very old, in a language no one speaks anymore.

Bast would not tell me what it was. He said it was not his to tell.

He said it was the name of a very small girl who liked to sing.
