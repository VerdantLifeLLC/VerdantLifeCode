# Kingkiller

I did not take the stone.

I turned my back on the door, which is the hardest thing I have ever done, harder than keeping my voice steady while Stanchion died, harder than walking away from Tempi's cairn. I turned my back on the door while it breathed on my neck and whispered my name, and I looked at Bredon, sitting at his little table with a black stone held out between two fingers.

"No," I said.

Bredon lowered his hand. He did not seem disappointed. He seemed interested.

"No?" he said.

"I'm not playing your game anymore."

"Everyone is playing. Always. I told you that." He set the black stone down on the edge of the board, carefully, the way you set down something you will want again later. "But very well. If you won't play, I'll simply tell you the position. It's quite simple, really." He folded his hands on the head of his stick. "Behind you is a door. In your head is its name. Around the young king's neck is its key. On the floor beside him is a woman of the line." He tilted his head toward Denna, still sitting on the floor with her harp. "And on that girl's wrist is a cord that I can tighten whenever I like."

"I know the position."

"Then you know how it ends." He smiled. "You speak the name. The door opens. I let the girl go, and your aunt, and all your little friends up on the ledge. You'll have saved everyone. You'll be a hero." He paused. "Isn't that what you've always wanted?"

"And the world?"

"The world will be different." Bredon shrugged. "Better, perhaps. Worse, perhaps. Who can say? But different. And my master will have his rest, after ten thousand years." His voice softened. "Is that so terrible, Kvothe? To give an old man his rest?"

"You're not asking for his rest. You're asking for the Thief."

"I'm asking for a door to be opened. What comes through it is not my concern." He picked up the white stone again and turned it over. "You're very young. You think everything has to be decided. That there must be a right answer and a wrong one. But the world is not a tak board. Most moves are neither right nor wrong. They are only moves." He looked at me. "Speak the name."

"No."

Bredon sighed.

"Very well," he said. "Then we'll do it the slow way."

He looked at Ambrose.

---

I will tell you what Ambrose did, because I have promised to tell the truth, and because it matters. What I did next only makes sense if you know what he did first.

He had been standing very still in front of the door, a few paces to my left, with the box on its chain around his neck and his hands clenched at his sides. He had not spoken since I came down the stair. He had not looked at me. He had been looking at the door, with a strange expression on his white face. Hungry. Afraid. Awed. The expression of a boy at the edge of a cliff, looking down.

When Bredon looked at him, he flinched. Then his face hardened.

He turned to Meluan, kneeling on the floor beside him. He took her by the arm and hauled her to her feet. She cried out. She was very weak. She could barely stand.

"Ambrose," I said. "Don't."

He did not look at me. He dragged Meluan forward, toward the door. With his other hand, he pulled a knife from his belt. A small jeweled knife, the kind a nobleman carries to cut his meat at table.

He cut her palm. Quickly, carelessly, the way you might cut a rope. She gasped. Blood welled up, very dark in the silver light.

Then he lifted the box from his chest, still on its chain, and pressed it against her bleeding palm, and pressed her palm against the door.

---

The door screamed.

It was not a sound. Or not only a sound. It was a shock that went through the whole chamber, through the stone of the floor and the walls and the domed ceiling, through my feet and my legs and my chest and my teeth. It was like being inside a great bell at the moment it is struck.

The soldiers in their ring cried out. Several of them dropped their torches. One of them fell to his knees and clapped his hands over his ears.

And the crack in the door opened.

Not all the way. Not even very far. But the thin silver line down the center of the door widened, suddenly, violently, to the width of a hand. And through it poured light. Silver light, cold and bright and blinding. And wind. A wind so cold it burned, howling out of the gap in the door, whipping my hair and cloak and tearing at my clothes.

And beneath the wind, a voice. Very deep. Very old. No longer whispering.

*YES*, it said.

Meluan was screaming.

---

Her hand was stuck to the door. I could see it. Her palm, pressed flat against the stone beside the crack, with the box pressed against the back of it and Ambrose's hand pressing the box. The blood from her cut palm was running down the face of the door in dark lines. And the door was drinking it.

I could see that too. The blood did not drip to the floor. It ran across the stone and sank into it, the way water sinks into dry sand. And where it sank, the stone glowed. And the crack grew wider.

And Meluan was fading.

Her face was going grey. The color was draining out of it, the way color drains from a leaf in autumn, the way it had drained from Stanchion's face when Usnea laid her hand on his chest. Her dark hair was going white at the temples. Her hand on the door was thinning, the skin going papery and translucent over the bones.

The door was not drinking her blood. It was drinking her. Her life. Her years. Her name. The part of her that was the line.

"*Ambrose!*" I shouted. "*Stop! You're killing her!*"

He turned to look at me. His face was lit silver by the light pouring out of the crack. His eyes were wide and bright and wild.

"It's working," he said. His voice was high and strange. "Do you see? It's *working*. He said it would. He said the blood would wake it, and the key would turn it, and a king could command it." He laughed. "And I'm a king. I'm a *king*. And it's listening to me!"

"It's not listening to you. It's eating her."

"She's *nothing*!" His face twisted. "She's just a woman. Just a vessel. Just blood. He said so. He said the door wants the line, and she's the line, so give it to her." He turned back to the door. "And when it's open, I'll be more than a king. He promised. The one behind it will make me more than a king. He'll make me—"

He stopped. He looked down at Meluan, sagging against the door with her grey face and her white hair and her thin papery hand pressed flat to the stone.

"It's too slow," he said. "It's taking too long." He raised the jeweled knife. "He said if it was too slow, I should give it all of her. All at once."

He pressed the blade against her throat.

---

I have thought about that moment more than any other moment of my life.

I have taken it out and turned it over and looked at it from every side, the way you look at a stone you have carried in your pocket for so long that you know every edge and hollow of it by touch. I have wondered whether there was another way. Whether I could have called the wind and knocked the knife from his hand. Whether I could have spoken his name and bound him. Whether I could have talked to him, the way I had talked to him in the burning camp, and made him see.

Maybe I could have. I do not know. I will never know.

But I know that there was no time. The knife was at her throat. His hand was already moving. And I knew, the way Shehyn had known, looking into the eyes of a man with a list of children's names hidden in his cell, that he was going to do it. That he would do it before I could finish a single word.

*You will face a choice*, Vashet had said, sitting on the floor of my room in the dark. *Where you must act, and there is no time to wait, and everyone will say afterward that you were wrong.*

I drew Caesura.

I did not decide to. I want that understood. I did not stand there and weigh it and choose. My hand went to the hilt, and the sword came out of its sheath with the sound it always made, like a breath let out, and I was moving. The way the Adem move. The way Vashet taught me. Without anger. Without hesitation. Without thought.

*Tree Falling. Water Flows Around. Lightning in the Hand.*

Three steps. The last one long.

The blade went into Ambrose Jakis just below his left arm, where the fine black velvet of his coat stretched over his ribs. It went in cleanly and deeply, the way Adem swords go in, and it found his heart.

---

He did not scream. He did not cry out. He made a small sound, a sort of hiccup of surprise, the sound a child makes when it is startled.

The jeweled knife fell from his hand. It clattered on the stone floor.

He turned his head and looked at me. His face was very close to mine. His eyes were wide. There was no anger in them. No hatred. Only confusion. The confusion of someone who has been told a story all his life, and has just discovered that it was not true.

"Oh," he said.

He looked down at the sword in his side. He looked back up at me.

"He said," said Ambrose Jakis, King of Vintas. "He said I was special."

Then his knees gave way, and he slid off the blade, and fell.

---

He lay on the stone floor in front of the door, in the silver light, on his back. His silver circlet had fallen off and rolled away. The box was still on its chain around his neck, lying on his chest, rising and falling as he breathed. Once. Twice.

Then it stopped rising.

I stood over him with Caesura in my hand. The blade was red to the hilt. It was dripping on the stone.

Beside me, Meluan had slid down the face of the door and was lying in a heap at its foot. Her hand had come away from the stone when Ambrose fell. It was grey and thin and bloodless. Her hair was white. But she was breathing. I could see her chest moving.

She was alive.

And the chamber was silent.

---

I looked up.

Every face in the chamber was turned toward me. The soldiers, in their ring, with their fallen torches guttering on the stone. The Seven, standing very still. Cinder, with his black eyes wide, and something on his face I had never seen there before. Denna, on her knees beside her harp, with her hand pressed against her mouth.

And Bredon. At his little table. With a white stone halfway to the board.

He was looking at me. His face was not mild anymore. Not kind. Not interested. For the first time since I had known him, it was simply still. As if a wind had blown across it and wiped it clean.

Then, slowly, he set the white stone down on the board.

"Well," he said quietly. "*That* was not the move I expected."

---

Somewhere at the edge of the ring, a soldier spoke. A young man, barely more than a boy, with a torch in his shaking hand. His voice was high and thin and cracked with terror.

"He killed the king," he said. "He killed the *king*."

And someone else said it. And someone else. It went around the ring of soldiers like fire through dry grass. *He killed the king. The Ruh killed the king. The Ruh. The kingkiller.*

*Kingkiller.*

I stood in the silver light with my red sword in my hand and my enemy dead at my feet and my aunt breathing in a heap against the door, and I heard them say it. The word. The name. The one that would follow me for the rest of my life, into every tavern and every town and every story. The one I would never be able to put down.

*Kingkiller.*

I have been called many things. Kvothe the Bloodless. Kvothe the Arcane. The Ruh. Maedre. Reshi. I have been called hero and liar and madman and demon.

But that is the one that stuck. That is the one they remember.

And the worst of it, the thing I have never told anyone until now, is this: it is the only one that is entirely true.

---

The soldiers moved first.

Not toward me. Away. They broke from their ring and fled toward the stair, dropping torches, shoving each other, stumbling. Most of them. Not all. A dozen or so stood their ground, gripping their swords, their faces white and furious.

"*Take him!*" someone shouted. An officer, by his voice. "*Take the kingkiller! Kill him!*"

They came at me.

And from the ledge above, with a sound like a single breath, the Adem came down.

---

I did not see all of what happened next. I was in the middle of it, and the middle of a fight is the worst place to see anything. I saw pieces. Flashes. The way you see things in a lightning storm.

I saw Vashet drop from the ledge like a stooping hawk and land among the soldiers, her sword already moving.

I saw Dagon and his men come down the narrow stair, three steps at a time, shouting the Maer's name.

I saw Devi at the top of the stair, with a clay pot in each hand, throwing them one after the other into the fleeing soldiers, and the white fire and thunder of the Surprises going off, and men screaming.

I saw Wil, halfway down the stair, with his scarred hands raised and his face grey with effort, and a guttering torch on the floor suddenly flaring up into a pillar of flame between a soldier and Sim.

I saw Sim, pale as milk, with a sword he did not know how to use held out in front of him, standing over Meluan's body at the foot of the door.

I saw Fela on the ledge, on her knees, with her palms pressed flat against the stone and her lips moving.

And I saw Bast.

He was beside me. I had not seen him come down the stair. But he was there, at my shoulder, with the iron knife in his hand and his face twisted with pain. He did not look at me. He was looking at the door.

"*Reshi*," he said. His voice was a whisper. "*Reshi, look.*"

I looked.

The door was still open. The crack still gaped, a hand's width wide, pouring out silver light and freezing wind.

And something was coming through it.
