# Folly

Her name was Vashet. Denna told me that, afterward, when I asked. She was Adem, from a place called Haert, in the mountains to the north. She had been my teacher. She had taught me to fight, and to be still, and something called the Lethani, which Denna did not understand and could not explain.

I did not remember any of it. But my hands remembered her. And so, it seemed, did my body. When she stood beside the wagon and looked up at me, I found that I had gone very still, the way a student goes still when his teacher walks into the room. I found that I was breathing slowly and evenly, in a rhythm I did not know I knew.

"Get down," said Vashet. "Bring the sword."

I got down. I did not know what sword she meant. But Denna reached into the back of the wagon and brought out a long bundle wrapped in oiled cloth, and put it in my hands, and I knew.

It was heavy. Heavier than it looked. I unwrapped the oiled cloth, and there it was. A long straight blade, grey and plain, with a simple hilt wrapped in leather. There was no ornament on it. No jewel, no engraving, no gilt. It was the plainest sword I had ever seen.

And it was beautiful. Even in the grey. Even with nothing inside me to see it with. I held it in my hands and looked at it, and I knew that it was beautiful, the way you know that a mountain is high even when you cannot see the top.

"Caesura," said Vashet.

---

We walked a little way from the wagons, she and I, to a flat place beside the river where the grass grew short and green. The troupe watched us go. Denna watched us go. No one followed.

Vashet stopped in the middle of the flat place. She turned to face me.

"Draw it," she said.

I drew it. It came out of its sheath with a sound like a breath let out. I held it in front of me, the way my hands told me to hold it.

Vashet looked at it for a long time. At the blade, grey and clean. I had cleaned it, I suppose, at some point. Or someone had. There was no blood on it now.

"It killed a king," she said.

"Yes."

"In a cellar. In the dark. Before a door."

"Yes."

"Do you remember?"

"I remember the sword. I remember my hands on it." I looked down at the blade. "I don't remember anything else."

Vashet was silent for a long moment.

"Shehyn sent me," she said at last. "After the battle. After the trial. She sent word from Haert. She said I must come to you, wherever you were, and speak to you about the sword." Her hands moved, very slightly, at her sides. "She said I must decide."

"Decide what?"

"Whether to take it back."

---

I did not say anything. I held the sword and looked at her.

"I told you once," said Vashet, "a long time ago, in a small room in Imre, that if you ever used this sword for something that was not the Lethani—for anger, or pride, or revenge—I would come and take it back. And I would not be gentle." She looked at me. "Do you remember that?"

"No."

"No." Her hands moved. *Grief*, I think. I did not know the sign, but my body knew it, somewhere. "No, I suppose you do not."

She was quiet for a long time. The river ran past beside us, brown and slow. The wind moved in the grass.

"I was in the chamber," she said. "Beneath the Lackless house. I saw what happened. I saw the young king with the knife. I saw you draw. I saw you strike." Her voice was very flat. "I have thought about it every day since. Every day. On the march to Renere. In the battle on the Field of Three Rivers. In the long nights afterward, waiting for your trial." She looked at me. "I have asked myself, over and over. Was it the Lethani?"

"Was it?"

Vashet did not answer right away.

"I do not know," she said at last. "That is the truth. I do not know. I have been Adem all my life, and I have studied the Lethani since I was a child, and I do not know." Her hands moved. *Confusion.* *Grief.* *Something else.* "You acted without anger. I saw that. Your face was calm. Your body was calm. You moved the way I taught you, cleanly, without waste. It was the most perfect strike I have ever seen." She paused. "And you killed a king. And a war came of it. And many people died. And the world is different now, and not better."

"So it wasn't the Lethani."

"I did not say that." Her voice was sharp. "Listen. You never listen. That was always your problem." She took a breath. "I said I do not know. And that is the point. That is what I have learned, after all these months of thinking. The Lethani is not a rule. It is not a law. It does not tell you what is right. It only tells you to choose, and to bear what comes after." She looked at me. "You chose. And you have borne it. You gave your name to bear it. More than any Adem I have ever known would have given."

I did not say anything.

"But the sword," said Vashet quietly, "is another matter."

---

She held out her hand.

"Give it to me," she said.

I gave it to her. I did not hesitate. I did not know why I should.

She took it. She held it up in front of her and looked at it for a long time. At the grey blade, and the plain hilt, and the leather wrapping. Her face showed nothing.

"This is Caesura," she said. "It is very old. It was made in Haert, before any of us were born. It has been carried by eleven Adem before you, and it has killed many men, and every one of those deaths was within the Lethani." She was quiet for a moment. "Or so the school believed."

She turned the sword in her hands. The light slid along the blade.

"A sword has a name," she said. "Among the Adem. A true name. Given to it by the one who first carries it, and kept by all who carry it after. The name is the sword's history. Its Lethani. Everything it has done, and why." She looked at me. "When a sword is used outside the Lethani, the name breaks. The sword is no longer what it was. It must be brought home and melted down and made into something else."

"Is that what you're going to do?"

Vashet was silent for a long time.

"No," she said at last.

I looked at her.

"I cannot take it home," she said. "Shehyn has said so. The school has said so. A sword that has killed a king—a king of the barbarians, in a cellar, before a door that should never have been opened—that sword cannot come back to Haert. It is too heavy. Its history is too heavy. It would bring that door with it, into the school, into the mountains, into the Lethani itself." She looked down at the sword in her hands. "So it cannot come home. And it cannot be melted down, because it cannot come home to be melted. And it cannot keep its name, because the name is broken."

"Then what happens to it?"

Vashet looked at me for a long moment. Then she held it out to me, hilt first.

"It stays with you," she said. "It is yours now. Not the school's. Not Caesura. Yours." Her hands moved, very slightly. "It has no name. You must give it one."

---

I looked at the sword. At the hilt, held out toward me. At the grey blade behind it.

"I can't name things," I said. "I have no name. I can't—"

"This is not that kind of naming." Vashet's voice was very quiet. "This is not the naming of the old stories, the namers and the shapers and the wind. This is a smaller thing. An Adem thing. You look at the sword. You look at what it has done. You say what it is." She paused. "Even a man with no name can do that."

I looked at the sword for a long time.

I did not remember what it had done. I did not remember the cellar, or the door, or the king. I did not remember the burning camp where a quiet man in red had died so I could run. I did not remember the Eld, or the bandits, or the long road to Ademre.

But I held out my hand and took the hilt. And my hand remembered. All of it. Not the events. The weight. The weight of everything this sword had done, while I held it. The weight of a king falling. The weight of a war.

The weight of a foolish young man who had thought he could open doors and close them again, and not pay for it.

"Folly," I said.

Vashet went very still.

"Folly," I said again. My voice was flat. Toneless. But the word came out of me from very far down, from the bottom of the hole, from the place where the truth lives. "That's what it is. That's what it's done. That's what I did with it."

Vashet looked at me for a long, long time.

Then her hands moved. A sign I did not know. My body did not know it either. I have learned it since. It is a very rare sign among the Adem. It is used only once in a person's life, if at all.

It means *I have seen the Lethani in you*.

"Folly," said Vashet. "Yes. That is its name." She stepped back. "Keep it. Carry it. Hang it on your wall, if you like, where you can see it every day. And remember."

"I don't remember anything."

"Then let it remember for you." She looked at the sword in my hand. "That is what names are for."

---

She stayed with us that night. She sat by the fire with the troupe, very straight and very still, and she did not speak. The children of Hallow's Company stared at her. Nell brought Lark to show her, and Vashet looked at the baby for a long time with her calm Adem face, and then did something I have never seen an Adem do, before or since.

She smiled. With her face. Just for a moment. Just a little.

Nell was so startled she nearly dropped the baby.

---

In the morning, Vashet left. She stood by the road at dawn, in her red clothes, with her own sword on her back, and she looked at me for a long time.

"I will not see you again," she said. "I think. Shehyn is old. I will be needed in Haert. And you are going somewhere far away, where no one will find you." Her hands moved. *Farewell*. *Grief*. *Family*. "But I will remember you. I promised Cinder that. I will promise you too."

"I don't remember you."

"I know." She looked at me. "But your hands do. And your feet. And your breath. And the way you stand." She was quiet for a moment. "That is enough. For now."

She turned to go. Then she stopped and looked back.

"Kvothe," she said. "When your name comes back. And it will. I have seen names come back. When it does—" She hesitated. It was the only time I ever saw Vashet hesitate. "When it does, you will want to take that sword down from your wall and do something foolish with it. You will want to go looking for Cinder, or the old man, or the door. You will want to put everything right."

"Should I?"

She looked at me for a long moment.

"You should choose," she said. "And bear what comes after."

Then she was gone, walking north along the road, small and straight and red against the grey morning. She did not look back.

I stood by the road with Folly in my hand and watched her until she was out of sight.

---

Denna found me there, a little while later. She came and stood beside me and looked north along the empty road.

"What was that about?" she said. "With the sword?"

I looked down at the grey blade in my hand.

"She told me its name," I said.

"What is it?"

"Folly."

Denna was quiet for a long time. Then she laughed, very softly. It was not a happy laugh. But it was not an unhappy one either.

"That's right," she said. "That's exactly right." She took my hand. My left hand, with the cord. "Come on. Mirra's made breakfast. And Tessit says we're going to Imre."

"Why Imre?"

Denna looked down at the cord on my wrist. At the knots, glowing very faintly in the grey morning light.

"Because this can't stay on your wrist forever," she said. "It's too close. Too loud. Something will hear it, eventually. The door, or the Seven, or something else." She looked up at me. "It needs to go somewhere safe. Somewhere that says no. And I only know one place with the right people to make that."

"What people?"

She smiled. It was a small, sad, fond smile.

"Your friends," she said. "The ones you don't remember."
