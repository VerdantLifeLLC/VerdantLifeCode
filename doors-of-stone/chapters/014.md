# The Sleeping King

Auri disappeared on the first night of the new term.

I did not know it at first. I had been busy all day with admissions, standing in line in the cold and then standing before the masters in Hollows while they asked me their questions. It went well enough. Elodin did not subtract anything this time, but he did not add anything either, and Kilvin argued on my behalf for a lower tuition because of the work I had done on his heat-funnel. I came out with a sum I could pay easily from the Maer's purse, and I paid it, and then I went to the Eolian to celebrate with Sim and Wil.

It was very late when I finally went down to the grate behind the Mains. I had a small gift for Auri in my pocket, a honey cake wrapped in paper, and I whistled the three rising notes as I always did.

Nobody answered.

I waited. I whistled again. The shaft below me was dark and silent.

That was not unusual. Auri did not always come when I called. Sometimes she was busy, or asleep, or simply not in the mood for company. I had learned not to take it personally. I left the honey cake on the edge of the grate, where she would find it, and went home to bed.

The next night, the honey cake was still there.

---

I went down.

I had not been into the Underthing since the night Elodin had talked to me about the door. I had been afraid to. But I was more afraid of what might have happened to Auri, and so I climbed down through the grate with my sympathy lamp in one hand and Elodin's stone heavy in my pocket, and I went looking for her.

I looked in Mantle. Her little bed of blankets was there, neatly made, and her small collection of treasures was arranged in its careful patterns on the shelf. But she was not there.

I looked in the Twelve. In Vaults. In the Crumbling, where the moonlight came down. In Tumbrel, where the little grey moths fluttered up around me as I passed. In Hush, where I whispered her name and heard it come back to me, clear as a bell, from the empty air.

She was not anywhere.

I stood in the center of the Twelve with my lamp in my hand and my heart pounding, and I tried to think. Where would she go? Where could she be? She knew the Underthing better than anyone alive. She could have been anywhere, in any of a thousand rooms and passages I had never seen.

But I knew. I think I had known from the moment I saw the honey cake lying untouched on the grate.

The stone in my pocket was heavy. Heavier than it had ever been. And when I listened, very carefully, I could hear it saying its long slow no. And beneath it, fainter, that other thing. The hungry thing.

But there was something else now, too. Something between them. A third voice, very deep and very slow, like the grinding of stone on stone.

It was coming from the Cellarage.

And it was awake.

---

I went down the steep narrow slope that Auri had called too loud. I understood now what she had meant. The closer I came to the bottom, the louder it grew. Not a sound, exactly. A pressure. A weight on the air, on my ears, on the inside of my skull. It was like standing at the edge of a great waterfall, where the roar is so loud you cannot hear anything else, cannot think anything else.

The old door at the bottom was standing open. Beyond it, the Cellarage was full of light.

Not the red light of my lamp. A pale, cold, silver light, like moonlight on snow. It came from everywhere and nowhere, from the air itself, and it cast no shadows. In that light, the heaped treasures glittered and gleamed as if they were new. The rusted weapons shone. The rotted tapestries glowed with colors I had never seen before, deep crimsons and royal blues and golds that seemed to burn.

And on the dais at the far end of the room, the black bed was empty.

The Sleeper was standing beside it.

He was taller than I had thought. Much taller. He stood nearly seven feet, gaunt and grey, in his long rich robe with its embroidery of tarnished gold. His crown gleamed on his brow. His eyes were open.

They were grey, those eyes. Grey as stone, grey as ash, grey as the sky before snow. And they were looking at me.

In his arms, cradled against his chest like a sleeping child, he held Auri.

---

I did not move. I could not. His gaze held me the way a pin holds a butterfly. I stood in the doorway with my lamp in my hand and my mouth dry as dust, and I could not so much as blink.

The Sleeper looked at me for a long time. Then he spoke.

His voice was very slow. Each word came out of him like a stone being pulled from the earth. It was not a language I knew. It was older than Tema, older than Siaru, older than any language I had ever heard. But I understood it. I understood it the way you understand a dream.

"*Key,*" he said.

I did not answer. I could not.

"*You came back,*" he said. "*I felt you. I felt you listening. You heard the stone. You heard what is beneath the stone.*" He paused, and the pause stretched out for so long that I thought he had fallen asleep again. Then: "*You should not have listened.*"

"Give her back," I said. My voice cracked. "Please. She's done nothing to you."

The Sleeper looked down at Auri, cradled in his arms. Her face was pale and peaceful. Her eyes were closed. She looked, in that silver light, like a figure carved from ivory.

"*She is of a royal line,*" he said slowly. "*I smelled it on her. I have smelled it on her for years, as she crept about my halls. A princess. A daughter of kings.*" He looked back at me. "*I am a king. I have been alone a very long time. A king should not be alone in his barrow. A king should have a companion, to sleep beside him until the end of days.*"

Something cold went through me. "She's not yours."

"*She is now.*" He turned, slowly, toward the black bed. "*She will sleep beside me. She will dream my dreams. And together we will keep watch, as I was set to keep watch, until the door is opened and the world ends.*"

"No!"

I did not decide to move. My body moved on its own. I was across the room before I knew it, running between the heaped treasures, my lamp swinging wildly in my hand.

The Sleeper turned his head and looked at me.

And the stone of the floor rose up and caught my feet.

---

It was like being grabbed by a hand. The stone flowed up around my ankles like water, then hardened again, holding me fast. I fell forward and caught myself on my hands, and the stone flowed up around my wrists too, and held them.

I was pinned. Kneeling on the floor of the Cellarage, my hands and feet held fast in stone, my lamp lying on its side beside me, its red light spilling across the floor.

The Sleeper looked down at me. There was no anger in his face. There was nothing in his face at all. It was a face of stone, grey and still and patient.

"*You are the key,*" he said. "*I cannot harm you. That is not my purpose. But I cannot let you pass.*" He turned back toward the bed, Auri still cradled in his arms. "*Go back to the world above. Forget this place. Forget her. And never come down again.*"

I struggled against the stone. It did not move. It held me the way the earth holds a buried seed, patiently, completely.

And as I struggled, I heard it.

The stone that held me was saying no. Its long slow patient no. No, I will not let go. No, I will not move. No, I will not change.

I stopped struggling.

I closed my eyes. I let my mind go still, the way I had in the round room. I did not reach for the stone. I did not try to make it do anything. I simply listened to it, the way you listen to a friend.

*No,* it said. *No. No. No.*

And beneath that, fainter, I could hear the Sleeper. His voice was stone too, but it was a different kind of stone. It was old and tired and very, very lonely. It had been saying no for longer than I could imagine. No to the door. No to the thing behind it. No to everyone and everything that came down the long slope into the dark.

It was so tired.

I opened my mouth. I did not know what I was going to say. I had no plan, no idea, nothing but the stone's voice in my ears and the Sleeper's loneliness in my heart.

I began to sing.

---

It was not a song I knew. It was not a song anyone knew. It came up out of me the way the name of the wind had come up out of me once, long ago, in a moment of cold, clear fury. But this was not fury. This was something else. Something quieter.

It was a lullaby.

I sang the stone's no, the long slow patient no that had been saying itself since the beginning of the world. I sang it softly, gently, the way my mother had sung to me when I was very small and could not sleep. I sang it the way you would sing to a child who has been awake too long, who is too tired to know how tired he is.

*No,* I sang. *No, there is nothing you must do. No, there is nowhere you must go. No, there is nothing to watch for, nothing to wait for. Rest. Rest. The stone holds. The stone will always hold. Rest.*

The Sleeper stopped.

He was standing beside the black bed, with Auri in his arms. He had been about to lay her down on it. But he stopped, and he stood very still, and he listened.

I kept singing. I sang the stone's no, and I sang the Sleeper's loneliness, and I sang the long slow dark of the Cellarage, the patient silence of the deep places of the earth. I sang of rest. Of sleep. Of the peace at the bottom of the sea.

The stone around my wrists began to soften.

The Sleeper turned to look at me. His grey eyes were heavy. His gaunt face was slack.

"*What is that song?*" he said. His voice was slower than ever.

"It's yours," I said, and kept singing.

He swayed on his feet. His eyes began to close.

"*I am so tired,*" he said.

"I know," I sang. "Rest. The stone will hold. You can rest now."

The stone released my wrists. My ankles. I rose slowly to my feet, still singing, still softly, and I walked toward him across the silver-lit floor of the Cellarage.

He did not stop me. He stood beside the black bed with his eyes half closed and his head bowed. When I reached him, I held out my arms, and he looked at me for a long moment with his heavy grey eyes.

Then, slowly, he placed Auri in my arms.

She was very light. Lighter than she should have been. She was cold, and her skin was pale as ivory, but she was breathing. I held her against my chest and kept singing, and the Sleeper turned away from me and lay down on the black bed and folded his hands across his chest and closed his eyes.

The silver light began to fade.

I kept singing. I sang until the light was gone and the Cellarage was dark again except for the red glow of my lamp, lying on its side on the floor. I sang until the Sleeper's breathing slowed, and slowed, and grew so faint I could barely see his chest rise. I sang until the terrible pressure in the air was gone, and the Cellarage was just a room again, a cold dark room full of forgotten things.

Then I stopped.

The silence was enormous.

I stood there with Auri in my arms and listened. The Sleeper breathed. Very slowly. Very deeply. The breath of a man in the deepest of sleeps.

And just before I turned to go, I heard him speak one more time. It was barely a whisper. Barely a word.

"*Key,*" he said. "*Do not come to the door. Whatever they tell you. Whatever they offer. Do not come to the door.*"

Then he was silent, and he did not speak again.

---

I carried Auri up out of the Cellarage and up the steep narrow slope and all the way back to Mantle. She did not wake. I laid her on her little bed of blankets and covered her up and sat beside her through the long hours of the night, holding her hand.

She woke at dawn. Or what passed for dawn in the Underthing: a faint greying of the darkness at the top of the old chimney, the barest hint of light.

She opened her eyes and looked at me. For a long moment she did not seem to know me. Then her face cleared.

"Kvothe," she whispered.

"I'm here."

"I was dreaming." Her voice was very small. "I was dreaming I was a queen. In a great hall, with a great king. And we were very old, and very tired, and we were waiting for something." She shivered. "It was a sad dream."

"It's over now."

"Is it?" She looked at me with her enormous eyes. "Did you bring me back?"

"Yes."

"From the Sleeper?"

"Yes."

She was quiet for a long time. Then she said, very softly, "How?"

"I sang to him."

"What did you sing?"

I thought about it. "I sang him the stone," I said. "I sang him to sleep."

She looked at me for a long moment. Then she smiled, a small, tired, radiant smile.

"That was very clever," she said. "And very kind. Most people wouldn't have been kind to him."

"He took you."

"He was lonely." She closed her eyes. "Lonely people do foolish things."

She fell asleep again after that. I sat beside her until the grey light at the top of the chimney had turned to gold. Then I tucked the blanket around her shoulders, and left the honey cake beside her pillow, and climbed back up into the world.

It was a bright, cold morning. The snow sparkled in the sunlight. Students were hurrying across the courtyard on their way to class, laughing and talking, their breath steaming in the air.

I stood among them and felt like a ghost. Like a man who has come back from somewhere very far away, and does not quite remember how to be in the world anymore.

*Do not come to the door*, the Sleeper had said. *Whatever they tell you. Whatever they offer.*

I did not know who *they* were. I did not know what they might tell me, or what they might offer.

But I knew, standing there in the bright cold morning, that I would find out.
