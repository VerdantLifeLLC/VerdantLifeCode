# Deoch

Deoch came to find me on the sixth night of the nine I spent finishing *Tariniel*. He did not come to Devi's rooms. He did not know about them. He came to the roof.

I was sitting on the ridge of Devi's roof, above the butcher's shop, with my lute and my papers and a candle stub in a jar. It was very late. The streets of Imre were empty and dark below me. I was working on the verse about Lyra, the one I had found in the Countess Laverne's green book, and it would not come right.

And then there was a scraping sound on the slates behind me, and a grunt, and a curse, and Deoch's handsome face appeared over the edge of the roof, red with effort.

"God's teeth," he said. "How do you do this? Every night? You're mad."

"Probably."

He hauled himself up onto the slates and sat down beside me on the ridge, breathing hard. He was a big man, Deoch, broad-shouldered and easy-moving, and he was not made for rooftops. He looked down at the street far below and went a little pale.

"How did you find me?"

"I asked Threpe. Threpe asked Devi. Devi told Threpe to tell me that if I fell off her roof and died she'd charge my estate for the cleanup." He grinned weakly. "She's a charming woman."

"She is, actually."

"I know. That's what frightens me." He looked at me. "Can I talk to you? About something?"

I set down my lute.

---

He did not say anything for a long time. He sat on the ridge of the roof with his big hands clasped between his knees and looked out at the dark rooftops of Imre.

"Denna," he said at last.

I waited.

"You know I knew her before you," he said. "Before anyone, almost. When she first came to Imre. She was fifteen, maybe sixteen. She sang for coppers outside the Eolian. Stanchion wouldn't let her in; she was too young, and she didn't have any pipes." He smiled faintly. "I used to bring her out a cup of cider and a heel of bread. Between sets. She'd sit on the step and eat it, and we'd talk."

"You loved her."

"Everyone loves her." Deoch did not look at me. "That's the trouble with Denna. Everyone loves her, and no one can keep her. She comes and she goes, and every time she comes back, you think, *this time*. And every time, she goes again." He was quiet for a moment. "I stopped hoping, after a year or so. I thought I'd stopped. I thought I'd got over it."

"And?"

He laughed softly. "And then she came to the Eolian tonight."

I went very still.

"Not in," said Deoch. "Not to sing. She came to the back door, late, after closing. Where I used to bring her cider." He looked at me. "She was wearing a dark cloak with a hood. She wouldn't come in. She stood in the alley and she asked me if you were in Imre."

"What did you tell her?"

"I told her yes." Deoch looked at his hands. "I told her you were going to sing a song at the Eolian at the dark of the moon. She already knew. She'd seen the posters." He was quiet for a long moment. "She asked me not to tell you she'd come. Then she asked me to tell you."

"Which was it?"

"Both." Deoch smiled crookedly. "That's Denna. She always means both."

---

I did not say anything for a long time. I sat on the ridge of the roof and looked out at the dark rooftops, and I thought about Denna in an alley behind the Eolian, in a hooded cloak, asking about me.

*Don't tell him. Tell him.*

"Was she all right?" I said at last.

Deoch thought about it.

"No," he said. "I don't think so. She looked thin. Tired. There was something on her wrist; she kept touching it, the whole time we talked, like it hurt." He looked at me. "And she was frightened, Kvothe. I've seen Denna a lot of ways, in three years. Sad and angry and drunk and silly. I've never seen her frightened. Not like that."

"What did she say?"

"Not much. She asked about you. And then she said—" Deoch hesitated. "She said, *He's going to do something brave and stupid, isn't he?* And I said yes, probably, that's what you do. And she laughed, the way people laugh when they want to cry. And she said, *Then I have to be there. I have to. Even if—*"

He stopped.

"Even if what?"

"She didn't finish." Deoch looked at me. "She just touched the thing on her wrist again, and went away up the alley, and didn't look back."

---

We sat on the roof for a long time after that. Neither of us said anything. Below us, a cat crossed the street, very slowly, and disappeared into an alley. Far off, a watchman called the hour.

"Why tell me?" I said at last.

Deoch did not answer right away.

"Because she's in trouble," he said. "Real trouble. The kind I can't help with. The kind that frightens her." He looked at me. "And you're the only one I've ever seen her look at like she looks at you."

"How does she look at me?"

Deoch was quiet for a long moment. Then he smiled. It was a sad smile, and a fond one, and it made him look older.

"Like you're a door," he said. "And she's standing outside it in the rain. And she wants to come in more than anything in the world. And she's afraid that if she does, she'll never be able to leave."

I did not say anything. I could not.

"I never got that look," said Deoch. "Not once. Three years. I got fond looks, and grateful looks, and *you're so sweet, Deoch* looks." He laughed softly. "I got a lot of cider drunk. But I never got that look." He stood up, carefully, balancing on the ridge with his arms out. "So I thought you should know she's here. And that she's frightened. And that she's going to be at the Eolian, the night you sing, whatever happens."

"Thank you, Deoch."

"Don't thank me." He looked down at me. "Just keep her safe. If you can. Whatever it is. Whatever's hurting her wrist." His voice was very quiet. "Someone should. And it was never going to be me."

---

He climbed down off the roof, very slowly, cursing the whole way. I heard him land in the alley below with a thump and a groan, and then his footsteps, going away.

I sat on the ridge of Devi's roof until the candle in the jar burned out. And then I picked up my lute and my papers, in the dark, and I went back to the verse about Lyra.

It came right, that night. All at once. The way things sometimes do, after you have stopped trying.

*Then I will always sing to you. So that if you ever go back to that quiet, you will hear me, and come home.*

I sang it, three nights later, in the Eolian, with the candles burning blue. I sang it looking at no one. Not at the balconies, not at the crowd, not at the shadow in the center of the room.

But I knew she was there. On the first balcony, beside a white-haired man with a stick. With her hand pressed against her wrist.

And I think she knew who it was for.
