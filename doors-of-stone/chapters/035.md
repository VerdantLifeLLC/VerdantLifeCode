# The Yew Hedge

I spent the rest of that afternoon in my rooms at the inn the Maer had arranged for me, a grand old place called the Seven Swans, and I did not play my lute and I did not eat and I did not sit still.

I paced. Back and forth across the thick carpet, from the window to the door and back again, with my hands clasped behind my back the way the Maer clasped his. My two guards sat in the outer room and listened to me pace and did not say anything. They were very good at not saying anything.

I was thinking about the knots.

I had told myself, walking back from the palace, that I was wrong. That I had imagined it. That one knot looks very like another, and a doubled loop is only a doubled loop, and there must be a hundred people in the world who tie Yllish knots with that same small neat precision.

But I did not believe it. I had spent days with Denna, in the Grey Man, watching her hands. I knew the way she tied a knot the way I knew the way she held a harp, or tilted her head when she listened, or said my name. I knew it the way you know a friend's handwriting on a letter before you have read a single word.

She had tied those knots. The ones in the king's hair. The *ending* and the *slowly* and the name.

I did not know how. I did not know when. I did not know if she had known what she was doing. But her hands had tied them.

And I thought I knew why she had not known. Because I remembered something she had said, sitting with me in the Grey Man, her fingers moving through a cord of knots. *He's been teaching me to read them. So I can write the song properly.*

Teaching. Reading. Writing.

What had he given her to practice on? What names had he set her to tying, over and over, in the long quiet evenings, until her hands knew the shapes as well as her voice knew the notes? Had he told her they were names from the old stories? Heroes and kings from the Creation War, to be woven into the fabric of her song?

And then taken the cords she had tied, and carried them somewhere, and put them where they would do their work?

I stopped pacing. I stood in the middle of the room and pressed my hands against my eyes, hard, until I saw colors.

*Some stories have to end*, he had said, *so that others can begin.*

---

At sunset, Lady Tamsin sent for me.

She received me in her apartments in the old wing of the palace, a warren of small crowded rooms full of cats and cushions and portraits of people who had been dead for a hundred years. She was sitting by the fire in a chair much too large for her, with a cat in her lap and a glass of something dark and sweet in her hand. She did not offer me any.

"You saw the king this morning," she said.

"Yes, my lady."

"And you spoke to the prince."

I hesitated. "Yes."

"What did he say to you?"

I did not answer.

Lady Tamsin looked at me with her bright old eyes. Then she smiled. It was not a pleasant smile.

"Very good," she said. "Very discreet. The Maer chose well." She sipped her drink. "I'll tell you what I know, then, since you won't tell me what you know. The prince has been asking questions. Quiet ones. About his father's illness, and about the deaths of the heirs. He has been asking them for some time, and he has been asking the wrong people." She stroked the cat. "This afternoon, he asked one of them too many."

I went cold. "What do you mean?"

"I mean that Prince Andrian has gone riding." Lady Tamsin's voice was very flat. "He went out an hour ago, alone, on his favorite horse. He told his grooms he wanted to clear his head. He rode out of the city toward the hills." She paused. "He has not come back."

"It's only been an hour."

"It's been an hour and a half. And the sun has set. And Andrian is afraid of the dark; he has been since he was a child. He never rides after sunset. Never." She looked at me. "Someone should look for him. Someone who is not the royal guard, because the royal guard answers to the captain of the guard, and the captain of the guard is Baron Jakis's cousin."

I was already on my feet.

"Which way did he go?"

"The north road. Toward the Hollow Hills." Lady Tamsin set down her glass. "Take a lantern. And your sword. And for God's sake, boy, be careful."

---

I found him a little before midnight.

I had ridden out of the city at a gallop, with my two guards pounding along behind me, their lanterns swinging wildly on their poles. We took the north road into the hills. It was a clear night, cold and still, with a thin moon riding high and the stars very bright. The road wound up through the low green hills, past farms and orchards, and then higher, into rougher country where the hills were steep and rocky and cut through with narrow ravines.

We found his horse first. It was standing at the side of the road near the top of a steep rise, its reins trailing, its saddle empty. It was trembling. When I rode up beside it, it shied away and rolled its eyes, and I saw that its flanks were streaked with sweat and that there was foam at its mouth.

I dismounted and caught its bridle and calmed it as best I could. And as I held the bridle, I felt something under my fingers. A roughness on the leather, just behind the bit.

I held my lantern close and looked.

It was a knot. A tiny knot, tied in a thread so fine and so dark it was almost invisible, wound around the leather of the bridle. I could barely see it. But I could feel it. The faint tightening of the air. The sense of something reaching out and catching hold.

I did not need to read it. I knew what it said.

---

We found Andrian at the bottom of the ravine beside the road.

He was lying on his back among the rocks, forty feet below. His arms were flung out to either side. His face was turned up toward the stars. His eyes were open.

His neck was broken.

My guards climbed down to him with ropes and lanterns. I did not go with them. I stood at the edge of the ravine and looked down at the small still shape at the bottom, lit by the swinging lanterns, and I did not move.

It looked like an accident. It looked exactly like an accident. A young man, riding alone at night, on a road he did not know well. His horse shied at something in the dark, a fox or an owl or a shadow. He was thrown. He fell. He died.

It happens. Everyone would say so. It happens every day.

I held the bridle of his horse in my hand and felt the knot beneath my fingers, and I thought about a young man with a weak chin and frightened eyes, standing by the fire in his father's bedchamber. *Please. He's my father.*

I thought about the door in the east wall of the palace garden, behind the yew hedge. Unlocked, now, and waiting for a visitor who would never come, to meet a prince who would never be there.

And I thought about the knot. Small and neat and precise, with a particular way of doubling back on the loops.

I closed my eyes.

---

We brought him back to the city on his own horse, wrapped in my cloak. We came in through the north gate an hour after midnight, and the guards on the gate took one look at what we were carrying and began to shout, and in a quarter of an hour the whole city was awake.

By dawn, everyone knew. The prince was dead. Thrown from his horse on the north road, in the dark. A terrible accident. A tragedy.

By noon, everyone knew something else. The king was dying, and the prince was dead, and the next in line for the throne was an old uncle of the king's, a duke of eighty years who lived in a castle on the coast and had not left his bed in three years. And after him, a pair of young cousins, twin boys of six. And after them—

Ambrose Jakis.

Fourth in line. And the three ahead of him were a dying old man and two small children.

I did not go back to Lady Tamsin's rooms that day. I did not go to see Kelm, or Petre Ashford, or anyone else. I sat in my rooms at the Seven Swans with the bridle of Andrian's horse in my lap, and I cut the knot out of the leather with the point of my knife, very carefully, and held it up to the light.

It was so small. Smaller than my smallest fingernail. A few threads of dark silk, looped and twisted around each other.

I could read it now, in the daylight, holding it close to my eye. *Fear*. That was the first knot. *Dark*. The second. *Fall*.

And woven through it, so fine I could barely see it, a name. The shape of a young man with a weak chin and frightened eyes.

I sat there for a long time, holding it. Then I took out the little knot Denna had left me, the one that said *forgive*, and I held them side by side.

The loops doubled back on themselves in exactly the same way.

---

That evening, I had a visitor.

He came to the Seven Swans at sunset, with a train of servants and guards behind him, and he did not knock. He simply walked into my rooms as if they were his own, and my two guards stood up and put their hands on their swords, and he looked at them with an expression of such utter contempt that they sat down again.

He was taller than I remembered. Or perhaps he only seemed taller. He was dressed in black, from head to foot, black velvet and black silk and black leather, the colors of mourning. But the black was cut so finely and fit so well that it did not look like mourning. It looked like a crown.

"Hello, Ruh," said Ambrose Jakis.

I did not stand. I sat in my chair by the window with the bridle in my lap and looked at him.

"Ambrose," I said.

"Lord Ambrose, now. My father was made a duke last span, did you hear? Duke of Hallowmere. So I'm a lord in my own right." He smiled. "And very soon, perhaps, something more."

"Fourth in line."

"Fourth in line." He savored the words. "An old man who can't get out of bed. And two little boys." He shook his head sadly. "It's a tragedy, really. So much death in one family. Everyone is saying so."

"Everyone," I said, "is right."

He looked at me. His smile did not falter. But something in his eyes changed. Something sharpened.

"I heard you found my cousin," he said. "On the north road. I heard you brought him home." He came a little closer. "That was kind of you. Very kind. I'll have to thank you properly, sometime."

"There's no need."

"Oh, but there is. There is." He looked down at the bridle in my lap. At the small hole in the leather where the knot had been. Something moved in his face, very quickly, and was gone. "I also heard you visited the king. Played for him. Looked at his hair." He tilted his head. "That was very bold of you. A Ruh, in the king's bedchamber. People are talking."

"People always talk."

"They do. They do." He leaned down, close, the way he had on the steps of the Mains, in the snow. I could smell the wine on his breath, and the heavy sweet perfume. "Let me tell you what they're saying, Ruh. They're saying that a Ruh came to Renere, sent by the Maer of Severen, who everyone knows has ambitions of his own. They're saying that this Ruh went to the king's bedchamber and sat at his bedside and played strange songs, and that the king got worse afterward. They're saying that this Ruh spoke to the prince alone, and that an hour later, the prince rode out to his death." He smiled. "They're saying that Ruh are thieves and liars and poisoners, and that the Maer should be very careful about the company he keeps."

I did not say anything. I looked at him.

"Leave Renere," Ambrose said softly. "Tonight. Go back to Severen, to your Maer, and tell him that the court is no place for his pet musician. And if you're wise, go further than that. Go back to your University." He paused. "Oh, wait. I forgot. You can't."

He straightened up. He smiled down at me.

"Leave," he said, "or I'll make sure that everyone in Renere knows exactly what you are. And then we'll see how long you last."

He turned and walked out. His servants and his guards trailed after him. The door closed.

I sat in my chair by the window and looked at the door for a long time.

Then I looked down at the bridle in my lap, and the two small knots in my hand. The one that had killed a prince, and the one that said *forgive*.

I did not leave Renere that night.

It was the first of many mistakes I made in that city. But it was not the worst.
