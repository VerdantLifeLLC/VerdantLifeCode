# The King in the Tower

The king came to see me four nights before my trial.

I did not know he was the king. I want to be clear about that. I did not know anything, in the Tower of Thorns. I did not know that the war was over, or that the Maer had won, or that he had been offered the crown three times and taken it on the third. I did not know that he had put on grey, and a single white thread, and called himself the Penitent King. I did not know that there was a Penitent King.

I only knew that late one night, long after the guards had changed, the iron door of my cell opened, and two men came in with a lamp.

---

The first was tall and grey and very straight, with his hands folded in front of him. He set the lamp on the floor just inside the door and stood beside it, and did not speak, and did not look at me. He looked at the wall above my head. I learned later that his name was Stapes, and that he had not been inside a prison cell in thirty-one years of service, and that he did not look at me because he could not bear to.

The second was thin and pale and hollow-eyed, in a plain grey coat with a single white thread running down the breast. He was not old. But he moved like an old man. Slowly, carefully, as if every part of him hurt. He stood in the middle of my cell and looked at me for a long time.

I looked back. I was sitting on my pallet with my back against the wall. I did not get up. I did not know that I should.

"Kvothe," he said.

I did not answer. I did not know, then, that it was my name. I only knew that people said it, sometimes, when they looked at me.

---

He sat down on the floor. That is what I remember most clearly, of that night. The king of Vintas, in his grey coat, sat down on the stone floor of a cell in the Tower of Thorns, among the straw and the dust and the mouse droppings, with his back against the opposite wall. So that his face was level with mine.

Stapes made a small sound. The king did not look at him.

"Leave us," he said. "Please."

Stapes hesitated. Then he bowed, very stiffly, and stepped outside the door. He did not close it. I could see his shadow on the stones of the passage, very straight, not moving.

The king looked at me.

"You do not remember me," he said.

"No."

"No." He was quiet for a moment. "Tamsin told me. And the queen. And the Fae boy, the one who comes to the window. They all told me. But I wanted to see for myself." He looked at my face. "I wanted to see what I had done."

---

I did not understand. I sat on my pallet with my hands in my lap and looked at him.

"I sent you to Renere," he said. "The first time. To find out what was killing the old king. You found it. You found the knots in his hair, and the woman who tied them, and the man who taught her. And for that, you were accused of murder. And you fled." He looked at his hands. "And I let you. I let you flee. I did not speak for you. It was not convenient."

The lamp on the floor flickered. Stapes's shadow did not move.

"And then you came back," said the king. "To me. In the hills. When I had lost my city and my wife and everything I had. You came back with the Adem and the Ruh and your friends from the University, and you walked into the king's camp, and you brought me my daughter. And then you went to the door, and you brought me my wife." His voice was very quiet. "And you killed a king. For me. For all of us. To close a door that I did not even know was there."

He looked at me.

"And now I am going to try you," he said. "For that. For the murder of a king. In four days. In the great hall, before all the lords of Vintas. And I am going to find you guilty. And I am going to sentence you to hang."

---

I did not say anything. I did not feel anything. I looked at the thin pale man sitting on the floor of my cell, and I waited for whatever came next.

"You should hate me," said the king. "You should be screaming. You should be cursing my name." He was watching my face. "I think I came here hoping you would. I think I wanted you to. So that I would have something to set against it. So that I could say, *he hates me, and he is right to, and I will carry it.*"

He was quiet for a long time.

"But you do not hate me," he said. "You cannot. You do not remember enough to hate anyone." His voice broke, very slightly. "And that is worse. That is so much worse."

---

"Why?" I said.

It was the only question I had. It was not a very good one. But the king looked at me as if I had asked something important.

"Why must I try you?" he said.

I did not know if that was what I had meant. But I nodded.

The king closed his eyes.

"Because the lords would not follow me otherwise," he said. "Because I took the crown on one condition, and that condition was that there would be justice. For everyone. For the lords who fought against me, and the ones who fought for me. For the peasants whose fields were burned and the merchants whose shops were looted. For the old king, poisoned in his bed. For the young king, killed in a cellar." He opened his eyes. "If I pardon the man who killed a king, because he was my friend, because he did it for me, then there is no justice. There is only a new king, doing what kings have always done. Protecting his own." He looked at me. "And the war begins again. And more cities burn."

"All right," I said.

The king stared at me.

"All right," he repeated. "You say *all right.*" He laughed. It was a terrible sound, dry and cracked, with no humor in it at all. "I tell you I am going to hang you, for saving my wife and my daughter and my kingdom, and you say *all right.*"

"Yes."

He put his face in his hands.

---

He stayed a long time. He did not say much more. He sat on the floor of my cell with his face in his hands, and his shoulders shook, a little, and then were still. Outside the door, Stapes's shadow did not move.

After a while, he took his hands away from his face. His eyes were red. He reached into his grey coat and took out something small and set it on the floor between us.

It was a tak stone. White. Smooth and round and worn, the way river stones are worn.

"This was in your pocket," he said. "When they brought you out of the Lackless house. The guards took it, with your other things. I had it brought to me." He looked at it. "I do not know where you got it. But I know the man who plays white, in the small garden in Severen. I know how he opens. The corner. Always the corner." He was quiet. "I think you must have taken it from his board. On the night you learned what he was."

I looked at the stone. It meant nothing to me.

"Keep it," said the king. "It is yours. Whatever it means." He stood up, slowly, stiffly, like an old man. "I cannot give you back your name. I cannot give you back your freedom, or your friends, or your life. I cannot give you anything but a trial and a rope." He looked down at me. "But I can give you this. A stone you took from the most dangerous man in the world. To remind you, if you ever remember anything, that you beat him."

He picked up the lamp.

"I am sorry," he said. "I know it means nothing. I know you cannot hear it. I am saying it anyway. So that it has been said."

And he went out, and Stapes closed the door behind him, and I heard the lock turn, and their footsteps going away down the long black spiral of the stair.

---

I kept the stone. I held it in my hand for the rest of that night, and the next day, and the day after that. I did not know why. I did not know what it was. But my fingers kept closing around it, the way they kept closing around the knots on my wrist.

It was in my hand when they came for me, on the morning of my trial. It was in my hand when I stood in the great hall, before all the lords of Vintas, and the Penitent King asked me if I had anything to say, and I said *no*.

And it was in my hand, three days after that, when they took me out of the Tower of Thorns in the dark before dawn, by a way I did not know, while a dead man with red hair walked up the steps of the gallows in my place.

---

"Do you still have it?" said Chronicler.

The innkeeper reached into his apron pocket. He took out a small white stone, smooth and round and worn, and set it on the table between them, beside the little wooden wheel and the knot.

"It was in the chest," he said. "Denna put it there. With the lute." He looked at it. "I didn't remember what it was, when I took it out. I thought it was just a stone. And then I held it." He turned it over with one finger. "And I was in the garden with the black pool. In Severen. On the night he told me what he was. With the board between us and the old man smiling at me across it."

"Bredon."

"Bredon." The innkeeper was quiet for a moment. "He opened in the corner, the way he always did. And we played while he talked. And when he had finished telling me what he was, and what he wanted, he stood up and took his stick." He turned the stone over with one finger. "And I reached across the board and took his first stone. The corner stone. I don't know why. I think I wanted to take *something* from him. And he saw me do it."

"What did he do?"

"He smiled," said the innkeeper. "And said, *Keep it, my boy. You'll want something to remember the game by.* And walked away down the garden path." He put the stone back in his pocket. "I didn't see him again until the door."

He picked up his cup.

"The Maer knew," he said. "When he gave it back to me. He knew what it meant. He'd played Bredon himself, for eleven years. He'd lost every game." He was quiet. "He wanted me to remember that I'd won one. Even if it cost me everything. Even if it was the only one." He looked at the fire. "It was the kindest thing anyone did for me, in the Tower. And I didn't even know it was kindness."
