# A Mouse in the Grey

There was a mouse in my cell in the Tower of Thorns.

I did not notice it for a long time. I did not notice much, in the grey. I sat on my pallet with my back against the wall and watched the thin knife of light from the window slit move across the floor at midday, and then I watched the dark, and then I watched the light again. The days went by like that. I did not count them.

But one day, when the guards brought my bread, I did not eat it at once. I do not remember why. Perhaps I forgot. I set it down on the floor beside the pallet and went back to watching the light. And when I looked down again, some time later, there was a mouse sitting beside the bread, eating it.

It was a very small mouse. Brown, with a white patch on its chest and a torn left ear. It was sitting up on its hind legs with a crumb held in its front paws, nibbling very fast. When it saw me looking at it, it froze. Its black eyes were very bright.

I did not move. I did not feel anything about it. I looked at the mouse and the mouse looked at me.

After a while, it went back to eating.

---

It came back the next day. And the day after. It came out of a crack in the stones at the base of the wall, near the door, at the same time every day, just after the guards brought my bread. It sat beside my pallet and waited.

I began breaking off a piece of the bread for it. I do not know why. I did not decide to. My hands did it, the way my hands did most things, in the grey. They tore off a corner of the loaf and set it on the floor beside the pallet, and then they gave me the rest.

The mouse ate its corner. Then it cleaned its whiskers, very carefully, with both paws. Then it went back into its crack.

That was all. Every day. For a long time.

---

I have thought about that mouse a great deal, since. I have wondered why it chose my cell, out of all the cells in the Tower of Thorns. I have wondered how it got so high, up that long black spiral of stone, with the thorn-vine on the outside and the guards on the stairs. I have wondered what it saw, when it looked at me with its bright black eyes.

I think it saw something harmless. That is the only explanation I have ever been able to find. Mice are very good at knowing what is dangerous. They have to be. And the man who had been Kvothe, who had called the wind and killed a king and sung the Chandrian into a burning room, was the most dangerous thing in that tower. The guards knew it. They never came into my cell alone. They never turned their backs on me.

But the mouse knew better. The mouse looked at me and saw what I was, then. A grey shape on a straw pallet, that broke off bread. Nothing more. Nothing to be afraid of.

It was right. That was the terrible thing. It was right.

---

The man in the cell below knew about the mouse. He tapped about it, through the stones. I did not understand all of his tapping, then. He was teaching me the code, slowly, night by night, and I learned it the way a very small child learns words, without knowing I was learning. Some of what he tapped in those months I only understood years later, remembering the patterns. He had been keeping a kind of diary, tapping it into the wall. A record. And some of it was about me.

*The man above has a mouse,* he tapped, one day. *I hear it. I hear him feed it. He does not speak to it.*

And later: *The man above still does not speak. But he feeds the mouse every day. That is something.*

And later still: *A man who feeds a mouse is not gone. Not all the way.*

---

In the autumn, the mouse did not come.

I did not notice at first. The guards brought my bread, and my hands broke off a corner and set it on the floor, and then they gave me the rest. And I ate it, and watched the light.

But the corner was still there the next day. Untouched. And the day after that.

I looked at it. A small piece of bread, going stale on the stone floor beside my pallet. I did not know why I was looking at it. I did not feel anything. But I found that I could not stop.

On the third day, I got up.

It was the first time I had got up from my pallet in—I do not know how long. Weeks. Longer. My legs were weak and stiff and they did not want to hold me. But I got up, and I crossed the cell, slowly, holding on to the wall, and I knelt down beside the crack in the stones at the base of the wall near the door.

And I looked in.

---

It was very dark in the crack. I could not see anything. I put my face close to the stones, and I listened.

Nothing. No scratching. No small quick breathing.

I do not know how long I knelt there. A long time. Long enough for the knife of light from the window to move all the way across the floor and up the opposite wall and vanish. Long enough for the cell to go dark.

And then, in the dark, very faintly, from the cell below, the tapping began.

I understood only a little of it. Not then. But I remembered the pattern, the way I remembered everything from that time, without knowing that I did. And years later, sitting in an inn at a crossroads, I worked out the rest.

*I am sorry about your mouse,* the man below had tapped. *The cold took it. There was one in my cell too, once. They do not live long.*

A pause.

*You got up. I heard you. You got up for the mouse.*

A long pause.

*You are still in there.*

---

I went back to my pallet. I lay down. I did not feel anything.

But the next day, when the guards brought my bread, my hands broke off a corner and set it on the floor beside the pallet. The way they always had.

And the day after that. And the day after that. Every day, until they took me out of the Tower of Thorns to be tried and hanged. A corner of bread, set on the stone floor, for a mouse that was not coming back.

I do not know why I did it. The guards thought I was mad. Perhaps I was.

But I think, now, that it was the same thing that made my fingers move on the lute in a farmhouse in the rain, and made me get up from behind the bar when the scrael came, and made me say yes to a scribe with bloody knuckles. The part of me that the grey could not reach. The part that the cord had not taken, because it was too small to be a name.

The part of me that fed the mouse.

---

"You never told me that," said Bast.

He was sitting very still. His face was strange.

"I didn't remember it," said the innkeeper. "Not until now." He looked at his hands. "It's odd. The things that come back. I thought the big things would come first. The fire. The door. Denna. And they do. But the small things come too. A mouse with a torn ear. A crust of bread." He was quiet for a moment. "I think the small things were holding the big ones up. The whole time. Like the stones at the bottom of a wall."

Chronicler did not say anything. He wrote.

And when he looked up again, the innkeeper had broken off a corner of the bread on the plate beside him, without seeming to notice he was doing it. And had set it on the table, at the edge, near the wall.

Bast looked at it for a long moment. Then he looked away, very quickly, and rubbed his eyes with the heel of his hand.

Nobody said anything about it. The bread stayed where it was, at the edge of the table, near the wall, for the rest of the afternoon.
