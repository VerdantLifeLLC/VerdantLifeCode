# Seasons

The years went by the Waystone the way the carts went by on the road. Slowly, one after another, each one a little different and all of them the same.

I will not tell you all of them. There is not much to tell. That was the point of them. They were small years. Quiet years. The kind of years that do not make stories.

But I will tell you a little. So that you understand. So that you know what it was like, to be Kote. To be small.

---

In the first spring, Jake's wife had her baby. A boy. They named him Tam, after Jake's father, and they brought him to the inn when he was a month old to show him off. Everyone crowded around to look at him. Old Cob said he had Jake's nose, which was not a compliment. Shep said he had his mother's temper, which was. Graham held him in his big carpenter's hands and looked at him for a long time and said nothing at all, and his eyes were wet.

They asked me if I wanted to hold him. I did not know why. I was only the innkeeper.

I held him. He was very small and very warm and he smelled of milk. He looked up at me with blue eyes that had not yet decided what color they would be.

And something happened. Very faint. Very far down. The ghost of a feeling. The shape of warmth.

I gave him back quickly.

---

In the first summer, a bard came through.

He was a young man, not much older than I had been when I went to the University, though I did not know that. He had a lute on his back and a bright red cloak and a great deal of confidence. He came into the Waystone on a hot evening and asked if he could play for his supper.

I said yes. The regulars were bored, and it was a long time since they had heard any music that was not Shep singing off-key at the end of the night.

He played well. Not brilliantly. But well. He played "Tinker Tanner" and "Jenny Nettle" and "The Lady and the Lord and the Bath." He played a sad song about a sailor who never came home. The regulars clapped and stamped and bought him drinks.

And then, near the end of the night, he said, "I've got a new one. Very new. Just learned it, in Tinuë, from a man who'd learned it in Vintas." He tuned his lute. "It's banned there, they say. In Vintas. The Penitent King banned it. You can get your tongue cut out for singing it." He grinned. "But we're not in Vintas, are we?"

"What's it called?" said Graham.

"*Tariniel*," said the bard. "It's about the white city. From the old stories."

I was behind the bar, polishing a glass. I did not know the name. It meant nothing to me.

But my hands stopped.

The bard began to play.

---

I will not tell you what it was like. I cannot. I do not remember it clearly. I remember only pieces.

I remember the first notes of the lute, rising up into the smoky air of the common room. I remember the bard's voice, clear and young and a little too sure of itself. I remember the words. A white city, on the last day of its life. Towers rising against a blue sky. Children playing in fountains.

I remember that the glass in my hand broke.

I did not mean to break it. I did not know I was holding it so tightly. But it broke, in my hand, and the pieces fell on the bar with a small bright sound, and there was blood on my palm.

No one noticed. They were all listening to the bard.

I stood behind the bar with blood running down my wrist, and I listened too. I did not want to. Every part of me wanted to leave. To go into the kitchen, or up the stairs, or out into the yard. Anywhere. Anywhere but here, where the song was.

But I could not move. I stood there. And I listened.

And the song went into me. Through the hole in my chest where my name had been. Down and down, into the dark, into the place where nothing was. And it did not find anything there. It went down and down and found nothing, and came back up empty.

But it went down. That was the thing. It went down, and it looked, and it did not find. And the looking hurt more than anything had hurt since the cellar.

---

When the bard finished, the common room was silent. Rena the widow was weeping quietly into her wine. Graham was staring at the floor. Old Cob, for once in his life, had nothing to say.

"Well," said the bard, a little uncertainly. "That's—that's the one. *Tariniel*. What did you think?"

Nobody answered.

I came out from behind the bar. I did not decide to. My feet moved on their own. I walked across the common room to where the bard was sitting, on a stool by the hearth, with his lute in his lap. I stood in front of him.

He looked up at me. His face was young and bright and a little frightened.

"You played it wrong," I said.

My voice was flat. Toneless. But it carried across the silent room.

The bard stared at me. "What?"

"The third verse. The part about the fountains. You played it too fast. It should be slower. Much slower. Like a child playing. Not like a march." I did not know how I knew it. The words came out of me from somewhere very far down. "And the last line of the song. You sang it like an ending. It's not an ending. It's a beginning. The boy in the fountain is asking to be a singer. He's not finishing anything. He's starting."

The bard looked at me for a long moment. His face was very pale.

"How do you know that?" he said.

I did not answer. I did not know. I stood in front of him with blood dripping from my hand onto the floor, and I did not know anything at all.

Then I turned around and went into the kitchen and closed the door.

---

Bast found me there an hour later, after the regulars had gone home and the bard had gone up to bed. I was sitting on the floor of the kitchen with my back against the wall and my bloody hand in my lap.

He knelt beside me. He did not say anything. He took my hand and looked at the cut on my palm. It was deep. There were still small pieces of glass in it.

He picked them out, one by one, very carefully. He washed the cut with water from the pump. He bound it with a clean cloth.

Then he sat down beside me on the floor, with his back against the wall too.

"You heard it," he said at last. Very quietly.

"Yes."

"Did you remember?"

"No." I looked down at my bound hand. "Nothing. It went down and down and there was nothing there."

Bast was quiet for a long time.

"But you knew," he said. "About the verse. The fountains. The last line." He turned his head and looked at me. "Reshi, you *knew*. That's your song. You wrote it. And you knew how it should go."

"I don't remember writing it."

"But you knew." His voice cracked. "Some part of you knew. Some part of you is still in there."

I did not say anything.

Bast looked at me for a long time. Then he put his head on my shoulder, the way a child does, and closed his eyes.

"I'm going to make sure no one ever sings it here again," he said. "Ever. I'm going to make sure no bard ever comes through that door with that song in his mouth." His voice was fierce. "I won't let it hurt you."

"It didn't hurt."

"It did. I saw your face." He was quiet for a moment. "It hurt worse than the cellar."

I did not answer. Because he was right.

---

In the second year, the Penitent King raised the taxes.

The war was over in Vintas, but there was another war now. In the Commonwealth. Rebels in the north, who said the Penitent King had no right to the throne of Vintas and even less right to meddle in the affairs of the Commonwealth, and who had taken up arms against his allies there. The Penitent King sent soldiers to help his allies. The soldiers needed paying. The taxes went up.

The tax collector came through Newarre in the autumn, with a guard of six soldiers in grey. He sat at a table in the Waystone with a ledger and a strongbox, and the people of the town came in one by one and paid what they owed. Some of them paid easily. Some of them paid with their faces tight and their hands shaking. Some of them could not pay at all, and the tax collector wrote their names in his ledger, and the soldiers wrote something else.

Jake could not pay. He had a new baby, and a bad harvest, and a sick cow. He stood in front of the tax collector's table with his hat in his hands and his face white, and he said he would pay next season, he swore it, he would find the money somehow.

The tax collector wrote his name in the ledger.

I watched from behind the bar. I did not feel anything. I did not know Jake well; he was only a regular, a young man who drank cider and talked about his farm.

But when the tax collector had gone, and Jake was sitting alone at the end of the bar with his head in his hands, I went and stood in front of him.

"How much?" I said.

He looked up at me. His eyes were red.

I paid it. From Skarpi's purse, which was still more than half full. I paid it to the tax collector's clerk the next morning, before they left town, and I told the clerk to strike Jake's name from the ledger.

Jake came to see me that night. He stood in front of the bar and did not know what to say.

"Why?" he said at last.

I thought about it. I did not know.

"Because you have a baby," I said. "And I held him."

Jake looked at me for a long time. Then he nodded, slowly, as if I had said something important.

He did not tell anyone. But the next spring, when his cow calved, he brought me the first of the milk. And the spring after that, a ham. And every year after that, something. He never said anything. He only left it on the bar, and nodded, and went to sit with the others.

---

In the third year, Bast fell in love.

Or he said he did. Bast fell in love a great deal. With the miller's daughter, and the smith's niece, and a widow in Rannish, and a girl who came through once with a troupe of players and was gone the next morning. He fell in love the way other people catch colds: often, and briefly, and with a great deal of drama.

But this one was different. Her name was Emberlee, and she was the daughter of a farmer on the south road, and she was the most beautiful girl in three counties. Everyone said so. Bast said so, loudly and often.

He courted her all that summer. He brought her flowers and sang her songs and walked with her in the evenings along the edge of the fields. She laughed at him, and teased him, and let him kiss her once behind the mill.

And then, at the end of the summer, she married a young man from Baedn with a good farm and a steady temper, and moved away.

Bast did not come out of his room for three days.

When he finally did, I was in the kitchen, making bread. He came and sat at the table and watched me knead the dough. He did not say anything for a long time.

"Reshi," he said at last. "Does it ever stop hurting?"

I looked at him. At his fine-boned face, pale and drawn. At his deep blue eyes, red-rimmed from weeping.

I thought about it. I did not know. I did not remember ever being hurt like that. I did not remember loving anyone.

But I remembered a bridge. At dawn. A woman with dark hair, walking away down the far side, very straight, with her head high. Not looking back.

I remembered that I had stood at the top of the bridge for a long time afterward. And that I had not felt anything. And that I had known I should.

"I don't know," I said. "I've never been able to feel it long enough to find out."

Bast looked at me for a long moment. Then his face crumpled, and he put his head down on the table, and wept.

I kept kneading the bread. I did not know what else to do. But after a while, I reached out with one floury hand and put it on the back of his head. And left it there.

---

The years went by.

Old Cob told the same stories. Graham built a new barn. Carter's horse grew old and slow. Shep laughed at his own jokes. Aaron grew tall enough to see over the bar, and then tall enough to lift the beer casks, and then tall enough to go to the smithy every morning and come back every evening with soot on his face and burns on his hands, grinning.

Tam, Jake's boy, learned to walk, and then to run, and then to climb the fence behind the Waystone and steal apples from the tree in the yard. I let him. I left the lowest branches unpicked, on purpose, so he would have something to steal.

The Penitent King raised the taxes again. And again. The war in the north went on. Soldiers came through Newarre on the road, sometimes, marching north. Some of the young men went with them. Not all of them came back.

And I polished the bar. And poured the drinks. And made the bread and the stew. And listened, and nodded, and said the right thing.

And waited.

It was a small life. A quiet one. The kind of life that does not make stories.

It was exactly what a girl with cloud hair had told me to live.

And it was killing me. Very slowly. So slowly I did not notice. The way the twilight kills mortals who stay too long.

But I did not know that. Not then.

Bast knew. I think he always knew.

But he did not say anything. Not for a long time.
