# Plans

"It's the Nighting," Devi said. "Of course it's the Nighting."

She had a map spread out across her desk, pinned flat at the corners with a teacup, an inkwell, a small brass scale, and her cat, who had lain down on the northeast corner and refused to be moved. The map was very old and very large and drawn on something that was not quite paper and not quite parchment. It showed the Archives. Not the Archives as I knew it, with its Stacks and Tomes and long dim reading rooms, but something older. A plan of the foundations. Of the rooms beneath.

"The Nighting?" said Fela.

She was sitting beside me on Devi's narrow bed, her hands folded in her lap, looking at the map with an expression of polite horror. I had not wanted to bring her. I had argued against it for most of a span. But Devi had insisted, and in the end, Fela had insisted too.

"You need someone who can hear stone," she had said, quietly, when I tried to talk her out of it. "You can barely hear it. I can hear it well. If there's a door down there, and it's made of stone, you'll need me."

"If we're caught, they'll expel you."

"I know."

"You've worked so hard. You're almost a Re'lar. You could be a master someday."

"I know." She had looked at me with her dark, serious eyes. "But I've been hearing that thing under the stone for two years now, Kvothe. The hungry thing. I hear it every night. I want to know what it is." She had smiled faintly. "And I don't want you going down there alone. You'll do something stupid."

I had not been able to argue with that.

"The Nighting," Devi repeated, tapping the map. "The festival. It's the one night of the year when the Archives are empty. The scrivs all go to the feast in Imre, and Lorren locks the doors at sundown and goes to his rooms and doesn't come out till morning." She looked up at us. "It's the only night when nobody will be anywhere near the Four-plate door."

"And you know this how?" I asked.

"Because I tried to do this before." She smiled thinly. "On the Nighting. Six years ago. I got caught because I didn't know about the second seal." She tapped a place on the map, deep beneath the Archives, where a small square room was marked with a sigil I did not recognize. "I made it past the Four-plate door. I got into the stairwell. I got down three flights. And then I hit this, and it went off like a bell, and Lorren was there inside of a quarter-hour."

"You made it past the Four-plate door?" said Fela.

"Not through it. Around it." Devi traced a line on the map with one finger. It ran from the edge of the page, where a series of small dashes indicated what I recognized as the Underthing, up through the foundations and into a narrow shaft that ran alongside the great stairwell behind the Four-plate door. "There's an old air shaft. It was sealed off hundreds of years ago, but the seal's cracked. You can get into it from the Underthing, and it runs straight up to a grate in the side of the stairwell, below the door."

I stared at the map. "How did you find that?"

"I looked." She shrugged. "I told you. Nobody asks about the things right under their noses."

"And the second seal?"

"That's the problem." Devi sat back in her chair. "I don't know what it is. It's not sympathy. Not any kind I've ever seen. It's not alchemy either. It's just—there. A room at the bottom of the third flight. Empty, as far as I could tell. Stone walls, stone floor, stone ceiling. And the moment I stepped into it, it *rang*." She shook her head. "Not a sound. Something else. I felt it in my teeth. And somewhere, Lorren felt it too."

Fela and I looked at each other.

"Stone," Fela said quietly.

"What?"

"It's stone. The seal. It's made of stone. Named stone." Fela looked at the map, at the little square room. "Someone asked the stone to listen for anyone coming down those stairs. And when you came, it told."

Devi stared at her. "That's not possible."

"It's possible," I said. "If someone knew the name of stone. If they knew it very, very well." I looked at Fela. "Could you ask it not to tell?"

She was quiet for a long time.

"Maybe," she said at last. "If I could hear it. If it would listen." She bit her lip. "I've never asked stone for anything. I've only ever heard it. Once, I broke it, but I didn't mean to. I don't know if I can make it do something on purpose."

"Can you try?"

She looked at me. Her face was pale, but her eyes were steady.

"Yes," she said. "I can try."

---

We spent the next two spans preparing.

It was a strange time. On the surface, everything was ordinary. I went to class and worked in the Fishery and played at the Eolian. I ate with Sim and Wil and told them nothing. I worked on my father's song late into the night, and sometimes, when the words would not come, I lit Kilvin's candle and sat watching it burn yellow until my eyes grew heavy.

But underneath, everything was different. Every day after classes, I went down into the Underthing and found my way to the place Devi had marked on her map, where the old air shaft came down through the stone. It took me three days to find it. It was hidden behind a fall of rubble in a part of the tunnels even Auri rarely visited, and the entrance was so narrow I had to take off my cloak and shirt to squeeze through.

On the other side, the shaft went up. Straight up, into darkness. The walls were rough stone, cold and damp, and there were iron rungs set into them at intervals, so rusted that some of them crumbled in my hand when I tested them.

I climbed it. It took me the better part of an hour, going slowly, testing every rung, bracing myself against the walls when the rungs gave out. At the top, I found the grate. It was old iron, rusted almost through, set into the side of a wide stone stairwell that spiraled down into darkness.

I put my face to the grate and looked through.

The stairwell was enormous. Wider than any stair I had ever seen, wide enough to march an army down twenty abreast. The steps were worn in the center, hollowed out by the passage of countless feet over countless years. Far above me, I could see the underside of a great slab of stone, gray and smooth, with four circles of dull copper set into it.

The Four-plate door. From the other side.

Far below me, the stairs spiraled down and down, into a darkness so deep it seemed to have weight.

I stayed there for a long time, looking down. I did not open the grate. I did not go through. But I listened.

And from far below, faint as a whisper, I heard the stone saying no. And beneath it, hungry, that other thing.

---

Fela practiced too.

She did not tell me much about it. She was private about her naming, the way some people are private about prayer. But I knew she went down into the Underthing with me sometimes, to the places where the stone was old and deep, and sat for hours with her hands pressed flat against the walls. I knew she came back pale and shaking, and that she slept badly, and that she would sometimes stop in the middle of a sentence and stare off into nothing, listening to something I could not hear.

Once, I found her in the Twelve, sitting in the center of the great round room with her eyes closed and her palms flat on the floor. Her lips were moving. She was speaking, very softly, in a language I did not know.

I stood in the doorway and did not interrupt. And after a long time, I heard something.

It was the stone. It was answering her.

Not with words. With a kind of hum, a deep low vibration that I felt in the soles of my feet and the pit of my stomach. It rose up out of the floor and filled the room, and the dust on the walls trembled with it, and the air grew thick and heavy.

Fela opened her eyes. She looked up at me. Her face was white as bone, and there were tears running down her cheeks.

"It listened," she whispered. "Kvothe, it *listened*."

---

Devi prepared in her own way. She spent those two spans in her rooms above the butcher's shop, surrounded by her books and her papers and her alchemical bench, working on something she would not tell me about. When I came to see her, the air was thick with strange smells, and there were burns on her fingers, and her eyes were bright and feverish behind her spectacles.

"Insurance," she said, when I asked. "In case the stone doesn't listen."

"What kind of insurance?"

She smiled her spider's smile. "The kind that makes a great deal of noise and a great deal of smoke and gives us a great deal of time to run."

I did not ask any more questions after that.

---

The night before the Nighting, I went to see Auri.

I found her in Mantle, arranging her treasures on their shelf. She was well again, or nearly. She hummed as she worked, and she smiled when she saw me, and she accepted the small bag of dried cherries I had brought her with great solemnity and pronounced them "joyful."

I sat with her for a while and watched her work. She was moving her treasures around, trying them in different places, standing back to look at them with her head tilted to one side. It was a slow, careful, patient process. She would move a single pebble half an inch to the left, look at it for a long time, and then move it back.

"Auri," I said at last. "I'm going somewhere tomorrow night. Somewhere deep."

She stopped moving. She did not turn around.

"The deep door," she said. It was not a question.

"Yes."

She was quiet for a long time. Then she set down the pebble she was holding, very carefully, and turned to look at me.

"The Sleeper told you not to," she said.

"I know."

"He told you not to, and you're going anyway."

"I'm not going to open it. I just want to see it. I need to know what's behind it."

She looked at me with her enormous eyes. There was no reproach in them. There was only sadness, deep and quiet and very old.

"You can't see a door without wanting to open it," she said softly. "That's what doors are for."

I did not have an answer to that.

She came and sat down beside me. She took my hand in both of hers and held it, the way she had held her ring. Her fingers were very cold.

"I'll light a candle for you," she said. "In Hush. Where the Underthing keeps its secrets. And I'll ask the stone to be kind to you." She squeezed my hand. "It doesn't always listen. But sometimes it does."

"Thank you, Auri."

She looked at me for a long moment. Then she leaned forward and kissed me on the forehead, very lightly, the way a mother kisses a child.

"Be careful, Kvothe," she said. "Be careful and be polite and don't take anything that isn't yours." She paused. "And if the door speaks to you, don't answer. Whatever it says. Don't answer."

"What would it say?"

She let go of my hand. She stood up and went back to her shelf and picked up the pebble she had been moving.

"It would say your name," she said, without turning around. "And it would ask you to come in."
