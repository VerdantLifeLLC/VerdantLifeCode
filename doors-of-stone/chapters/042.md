# Interlude: The Tinker

A bell was ringing on the road outside.

It was a small bell, bright and tinny, and it rang in a steady jingling rhythm, the way a bell rings when it hangs from the harness of a slow-moving donkey. It came closer. And with it, under it, a voice. An old man's voice, cracked and cheerful, singing a song that every child in the four corners of civilization knows.

The innkeeper stopped speaking. For a long moment, he did not move. Then he turned his head and looked toward the door, and something went across his face that Chronicler had not seen there before.

It was fear.

It was only there for a moment. Then it was gone, smoothed away, and the innkeeper was an innkeeper again. He rose from the table and wiped his hands on his apron.

"A tinker," he said. "We haven't had a tinker through in months."

"Reshi," said Bast. He had come out of the kitchen at the sound of the bell, a wooden spoon still in his hand, and he was standing very still in the doorway. "Reshi, don't."

"Don't what, Bast?"

"You know what."

The innkeeper looked at him. His face was mild. "It's a tinker," he said. "The town will want pots mended and needles sold. I'll want salt, and some thread, and news from the road." He smiled faintly. "It's only a tinker."

"There's no such thing," said Bast, "as only a tinker."

The innkeeper did not answer that. He went to the door and opened it.

---

The tinker was very old. That was the first thing Chronicler noticed about him, coming to stand beside the innkeeper in the doorway. He was old and bent and brown as a walnut, with a white fringe of hair around a bald brown scalp and a face so wrinkled that it seemed to be made entirely of smiles. He wore a coat that had once been many colors and was now mostly the color of dust. He walked beside a small grey donkey, which was so laden with packs and bundles and dangling pots and pans that it looked like a walking tinker's shop all on its own.

He stopped in the road in front of the Waystone and looked up at the sign. Then he looked at the innkeeper, standing in the doorway. His eyes were very bright, and very black, and very merry.

"Well, well," he said. "An inn. And an innkeeper. And a guest, with ink on his fingers." His eyes flicked to Chronicler, and then past him, into the dim common room, where Bast stood in the kitchen doorway with his spoon. "And another one, too. My, my. It's a regular crowd."

"Welcome to the Waystone," said the innkeeper. "Will you be wanting a room?"

"A room? No, no. Tinkers don't take rooms. We sleep under the sky, like the Ruh, like the birds." The old man chuckled. "But I'd take a drink, if one was offered. And a bit of bread. And a place to spread out my wares, so the good people of this town can come and see what I've brought them." He looked around at the quiet street, the shuttered houses, the empty square. "If there are any good people in this town. It seems very quiet."

"It's a quiet town."

"Hm. Yes. I can see that." The tinker looked at the innkeeper for a long moment. "Quiet towns are the ones with the most to say. You just have to know how to listen."

---

He spread out his wares on a blanket in the inn yard, and the people of Newarre came.

They came slowly at first, the way people do in a town that has grown wary of strangers. A woman with a cracked kettle. A man with a knife that needed sharpening. A pair of children who stood at the edge of the yard and stared at the donkey with enormous eyes until the tinker called them over and gave them each a sugared almond from a paper twist in his pocket. Then more came, and more, until it seemed that half the town was crowded into the inn yard, haggling and laughing and holding up pots and ribbons and bright glass beads to the afternoon sun.

The tinker sang as he worked. He sang "Tinker Tanner," of course, but other songs too. Old songs, strange songs, songs in languages Chronicler did not recognize. He mended pots and sharpened knives and sold needles and thread and salt and spices and little bottles of something that he swore would cure anything from gout to heartbreak. He told jokes, very bad ones, and laughed at them himself until he wheezed. He was, Chronicler thought, watching from the door of the inn, the most cheerful man he had ever seen.

And he never once took his bright black eyes off the innkeeper.

---

The innkeeper stayed inside. He stood behind the bar and polished glasses that did not need polishing, and he did not go out into the yard. Chronicler sat at his table by the hearth, with his pen and his papers, and watched him.

Bast had come to sit across from Chronicler. He was holding his wooden spoon in both hands, twisting it, and his face was pale.

"What is it?" Chronicler asked quietly. "About the tinker?"

Bast did not answer at first. He watched the innkeeper behind the bar. Then he said, very low, "Do you know the saying? About tinkers?"

"A tinker's debt is always paid," said Chronicler. "Everyone knows that."

"Do you know what it means?"

Chronicler hesitated. "That tinkers are honest? That if you do a tinker a kindness, he'll repay it?"

"That's what people think it means." Bast twisted the spoon. "It means more than that. Tinkers aren't just peddlers. Some of them are. Most of them. But some of them are something else." He looked out the window at the old man in the yard, laughing with a knot of children around him. "Some of them walk the roads because the roads need walking. Because there are debts in the world that need to be paid, and someone has to carry them. And when a tinker comes to your door, it's because something is owed. By you, or to you." He looked back at the innkeeper. "And he's come to collect."

"Collect what?"

Bast did not answer.

---

It was near sunset when the tinker came in.

The crowd in the yard had dwindled away. The last of the townsfolk had gone home with their mended pots and their bright new ribbons. The donkey was dozing in the shade of the stable. The tinker packed up his wares, slowly, humming to himself, and then he walked into the common room of the Waystone Inn and went straight to the bar.

"A drink," he said. "If you please. I've been talking all afternoon, and my throat's as dry as a Tehlin's sermon."

The innkeeper poured him a mug of cider. The tinker drank it off in one long swallow and set the mug down on the bar and wiped his mouth with the back of his hand.

"Ah," he said. "That's good cider. Very good. Better than you'd expect, in a town like this." He looked at the innkeeper. "You make it yourself?"

"I buy it from a farmer up the road."

"Hm. Well, he makes good cider." The tinker set a coin on the bar. The innkeeper pushed it back.

"On the house," he said.

The tinker looked at the coin, and then at the innkeeper. His bright black eyes were very merry.

"Now that," he said, "is a kindness. And a tinker never forgets a kindness." He picked up the coin and turned it over in his fingers. Then he held it out. "I'll give you something for it."

"There's no need."

"There's always a need." The tinker smiled. "Hold out your hand."

The innkeeper hesitated. Then, slowly, he held out his hand.

The tinker did not put the coin in it. Instead, he reached into the pocket of his dusty coat and took out something small and dark and pressed it into the innkeeper's palm, and closed the innkeeper's fingers over it with both his own brown wrinkled hands.

"There," he said. "That's for you. You'll need it."

The innkeeper looked down at his closed hand. He did not open it.

"What is it?" he said. His voice was very quiet.

"Something you lost," said the tinker. "A long time ago. On the road." He let go of the innkeeper's hand. "I found it, and I've been carrying it ever since, waiting to find you. It's been a very long walk." He smiled. "But a tinker's debt is always paid."

"I don't owe you anything."

"No. You don't." The tinker picked up his empty mug and looked into it. "But someone owed you. A long time ago. And they asked me to carry it for them, until the time was right." He set the mug down. "The time is right."

"Who?"

The tinker looked at him for a long moment with his bright black eyes. Then he reached across the bar and patted the innkeeper's cheek, very gently, the way an old man pats a child.

"You'll know," he said. "When you open your hand."

He turned and walked out of the Waystone Inn. At the door, he paused and looked back, and his eyes found Chronicler, sitting at the table by the hearth with his pen in his hand.

"Write carefully, scribe," he said. "Every word. Every one." His eyes crinkled. "Some stories are very heavy. But they're heavier still if you drop them halfway."

Then he was gone. They heard the jingle of the donkey's bell in the yard, and the old man's cracked voice singing "Tinker Tanner," fading away down the road, until it was gone.

---

The innkeeper stood behind the bar with his hand closed. He did not open it. He stood there for a long time, very still, looking at nothing.

"Reshi," said Bast softly. He had risen from the table and come to stand at the end of the bar. "Reshi. Open it."

The innkeeper did not move.

"Please."

Slowly, the innkeeper opened his hand.

Chronicler, from the table by the hearth, could not see what was in it. He saw only the innkeeper's face, looking down at his open palm. He saw it change. He saw it go very white, and very still, and then crumple, just for a moment, like a paper lantern when the candle inside it goes out.

Then the innkeeper closed his hand again. He put it in the pocket of his apron. He picked up his white cloth and began to polish the bar.

"Supper," he said. His voice was perfectly steady. "The regulars will be here soon. Bast, is the stew ready?"

Bast stared at him. "Reshi—"

"Is the stew ready, Bast?"

Bast looked at him for a long moment. Then he looked away.

"Nearly," he said.

"Then go and finish it. And don't forget the thyme."

Bast went into the kitchen. The innkeeper polished the bar. Chronicler sat at his table and looked down at his page, at the half-finished line of cipher where he had stopped writing when the bell began to ring.

After a while, the innkeeper came back to the table and sat down. He did not say anything about the tinker. He did not say anything about what was in his pocket. He folded his hands on the table and looked at Chronicler.

"Where was I?" he said.

"Tarbean," said Chronicler. His voice was not quite steady. "You'd left Skarpi. You were going back to Imre."

"Yes." The innkeeper nodded. "Yes. I was going back to Imre. With no money and a head full of songs." He smiled faintly. "It was a long way to go on no money. I had to work my passage."

His hand, Chronicler noticed, had gone to the pocket of his apron. It rested there, very lightly, over whatever the tinker had given him. As if he wanted to be sure it was still there.

"Go on," said Chronicler.

And Kvothe went on.
