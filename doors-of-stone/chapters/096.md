# Kote

It took me most of that autumn to make the Waystone into an inn again.

I did it with my hands. Bast helped, when he was in the mood, which was not often. He was very good at some things and very bad at others, and the things he was good at were mostly not the things an inn needs. He could charm a farmer's wife into selling us eggs at half price. He could find the one loose board in a floor by listening to it. He could make the mice leave the walls, simply by walking through the rooms at night and humming. But he could not hammer a nail straight to save his life, and he said the smell of whitewash made him ill.

So I did most of it myself.

I fixed the roof. I climbed up on it with a ladder Graham lent me and a sack of slates from the quarry at Rannish, and I found every leak and patched it, one slate at a time. I fixed the chimney, which was cracked, with mortar I mixed myself from lime and sand and water. I fixed the stable door, and the stable floor, and the pump in the yard, and the shutters on every window.

I cleaned. I swept and scrubbed and dusted every room in the inn, from the cellar to the attic. I washed every window. I whitewashed every wall. I polished every table and every chair and every brass fitting until they gleamed.

I did not think about it. I did not plan it. I simply got up every morning and found the next thing that needed doing, and did it. And then the next thing. And the next. Until one day in the middle of autumn I came down the stairs in the morning and looked at the common room, clean and bright and ready, and there was nothing left to do.

I stood at the bottom of the stairs for a long time, looking at it.

It was grey. But it was clean.

---

Graham came up from his farm that afternoon, the way he did most afternoons, to see how I was getting on. He stood in the doorway of the common room and looked around at it for a long time. At the clean whitewashed walls, and the polished tables, and the gleaming bar, and the fire burning bright in the great stone hearth.

"Well," he said. "God's teeth."

"Is it all right?"

"All right?" Graham came into the room. He ran his big hand along the top of the bar. "It's better than it ever was. Better than when old Bentley had it." He shook his head. "Bentley let it go, the last few years. Drank more than he sold. But this—" He looked around. "This is a proper inn. This is the kind of place a man would walk a mile out of his way to drink in."

"I hope so."

Graham looked at me. "You'll be opening, then?"

"Tomorrow. If anyone comes."

"Oh, they'll come." Graham grinned. "Word's been going round. The stranger who bought the old Crossroads, working day and night like a man possessed. Everyone's curious. They'll come just to look at you." He paused. "You'll need stock. Beer and cider and wine and such."

"I've ordered it. From Baedn. The carter's bringing it tomorrow morning."

"Carter?" Graham laughed. "That's his name, too. Carter. Funny, that. His da was a carter, and his da before him, so they just called him Carter and gave up on anything else." He looked at me. "What about you? What's your name? I never asked. You never said."

I looked at him.

"Kote," I said.

Graham tried it out. "Kote. Hm. Short. Easy to remember." He nodded. "Suits you. You're a short, easy sort of man." He clapped me on the shoulder. "Well, Kote. I'll bring the lads round tomorrow night. First round's on you, mind. For luck."

---

They came.

Not many, that first night. Graham, and his two grown sons. An old man named Cob, grey-bearded and bent, who sat at the bar and told me the whole history of the inn and every innkeeper who had ever kept it, with a great many digressions. A young man named Jake, who had a farm on the north road and a wife who was expecting their first child. A lean, quiet, sour-faced man named Carter, who had brought the beer from Baedn that morning and had decided to stay and drink some of it. A cheerful, round, red-faced man named Shep, who laughed at everything, especially his own jokes.

They sat at the bar and drank and talked. About the harvest, and the weather, and the price of wheat in Baedn. About the war in Vintas, which was over now, and the new king there, who they said was a strange one. Walked barefoot through his own capital, they said, in a sack. Mad as a hatter, they said.

"The Penitent King," said Old Cob, with great authority. "That's what they call him. Because he's always doing penance. My cousin's wife's brother was in Renere last year, and he said the king goes barefoot every Cendling, to this day. Just walks around the palace gardens with no shoes on. For his sins, he says." Old Cob shook his head. "Must have a lot of sins."

"Must have," agreed Shep, and laughed.

I stood behind the bar and poured their drinks and listened. I did not say much. I smiled when they laughed, and nodded when they talked, and filled their mugs when they were empty.

And somewhere near the end of the night, when they were all a little drunk and very cheerful, Jake said, "What about the one who killed the old king? The one before? The Ruh?"

"Kvothe," said Old Cob. "Kvothe the Kingkiller."

"That's him." Jake leaned forward. "My brother-in-law says he wasn't really hanged. He says it was somebody else, with his hair dyed. He says Kvothe got away, and he's hiding somewhere, waiting."

"Waiting for what?" said Graham.

"Dunno. Just waiting." Jake shrugged. "That's what my brother-in-law says."

"Your brother-in-law says a lot of things," said Carter sourly. "He also says there's a dragon living in the hills behind Rannish."

"There might be!"

"There isn't."

They argued about it, cheerfully, for a while. I stood behind the bar and polished a glass. Slowly. In small even circles.

I did not feel anything. The name meant nothing to me. *Kvothe*. It was only a word. A name from a story, like Lanre or Taborlin or Oren Velciter. A man who had done great things, and terrible things, and then vanished.

But my hands, polishing the glass, had stopped moving. I looked down at them and saw that they had stopped. I did not know why.

I made them move again.

---

After that night, they came every night. Graham and Cob and Jake and Carter and Shep, and others too. The smith, a big silent man named Baxter, and his apprentice, a boy of twelve named Aaron who could barely see over the bar. The miller and his wife. A widow named Rena who lived alone at the edge of town and came in on Felling nights for a single glass of wine. Travelers, sometimes, passing through on the road. Tinkers and peddlers and the occasional carter from Baedn or Rannish.

I learned their names. All of them. I learned what they drank and how they liked it. I learned who could be trusted to pay and who needed reminding. I learned whose wife was sick and whose son had gone off to be a soldier and whose barn had burned down in a lightning storm. I learned to listen, and nod, and say the right thing, and pour the right drink.

I learned to be an innkeeper.

It was not hard. It was mostly the same as polishing things. Small and quiet and careful. Doing the next thing, and then the next, and then the next. It kept me small. It kept me quiet.

And it filled the hours. That was the important thing. It filled the long hours between waking and sleeping, when otherwise I would have sat alone in the dark and felt the hole in my chest.

---

In the second month, I hung the sword.

I had not known what to do with it. It had been lying in the bottom of my pack since Imre, wrapped in its oiled cloth, and I had not taken it out. I did not want to look at it. I did not know why.

But one night, after the regulars had gone home and Bast had gone to bed, I went up to my room and took it out of the pack. I unwrapped it. I held it.

It was heavy. Heavier than it looked. A long straight blade, grey and plain, with a simple hilt wrapped in leather. There was no ornament on it at all.

I held it for a long time, standing in my room at the top of the stairs, with the chest at the foot of my bed and the waystone outside the window.

*Folly*, I had called it. A woman in red had asked me to name it, on a riverbank, and I had called it that. I did not remember why. But I remembered the weight of it. The weight in my hands and my arms and my shoulders.

I took it downstairs. I stood in the common room, in the dark, with only the embers of the fire for light. I looked at the wall above the hearth. A great bare stretch of whitewashed stone, above the mantel.

I hung it there. On two iron pegs I drove into the mortar between the stones. I hung it level, with the hilt to the left and the point to the right. And I stepped back, and looked at it.

It looked like nothing. Like an old sword, hung on a wall. The kind of thing you see in a hundred inns across the Four Corners. A traveler's sword, left to pay a debt. A soldier's sword, hung up when he came home from the war.

But I looked at it for a long time.

---

Graham saw it the next night. He stood in front of the hearth with his mug in his hand and looked up at it for a long time.

"That's a good sword," he said at last.

"It's old."

"Old and good." He squinted at it. "Where'd you get it?"

I thought about how to answer.

"It was given to me," I said. "By someone who didn't want it anymore."

Graham looked at it a while longer. "It needs a plaque," he said. "Under it. A sword like that, hanging on a wall like that. It ought to have a plaque. With its name." He looked at me. "Swords have names, sometimes. Old ones. Did you know that?"

"Yes."

"Does this one?"

I looked up at the sword. At the grey blade, and the plain hilt, and the firelight sliding along the edge.

"Folly," I said.

Graham looked at me. Then at the sword. Then back at me. Something moved in his big slow face.

"Folly," he said slowly. "That's a strange name for a sword."

"It's a strange sword."

Graham was quiet for a long moment. Then he nodded.

"I'll make you a plaque," he said. "Oak. Like the sign. I'll burn the letters in, so they last." He looked up at the sword again. "Folly," he said, half to himself. "Hm. There's a story there, I'd wager."

"Maybe."

"You'll tell me, someday?"

I looked at him. At his big slow face and his carpenter's hands.

"Maybe," I said.

He brought the plaque a span later. It was very good work. A board of dark oak, about the length of my forearm, with the edges rounded and the surface polished smooth. The letters were burned in, black and deep. A single word.

*FOLLY*.

I hung it on the wall beneath the sword. I stood back and looked at it. Graham stood beside me and looked at it too.

"That's right," he said at last. "That's just right."

"Yes," I said. "It is."

---

That winter, I began to notice the silence.

It had always been there, I think. Since the day I pushed open the door and saw the dusty common room for the first time. But I had not noticed it. I had been too busy. Too tired. Too full of work.

But in the winter, when the snow came and the roads closed and the travelers stopped coming, and the regulars came later and left earlier because of the cold, there was more time. Long empty hours, in the evenings and the mornings, with nothing to do but sit behind the bar and polish things.

And I began to notice it.

It was not one silence. It was three. I did not know how I knew that. But I knew it, the way I knew how to cut wood or skin a rabbit. Three silences, lying on top of each other, like three layers of snow.

The first was the silence of the inn itself. An empty room, in the night, with the fire burned low. Chairs upside down on the tables. Nothing moving. Nothing making a sound. A plain, simple, ordinary silence, like the silence of any empty room.

The second was the silence of the town. Of Newarre, and the fields around it, and the road running past the door. A small town, in a quiet country, in the dead of winter. No carts on the road. No voices in the street. Only the snow falling, and the wind moving in the bare trees, and the silence of a place where nothing much ever happens.

And the third silence was mine.

I did not know what it was, at first. I only knew it was there. Underneath the other two. Deeper. Quieter. A silence that lived in the long bar of dark wood, and in the bottles behind it, and in the sword above the hearth, and in me. Most of all in me.

It was the silence of a man who is waiting. Who has been waiting for a long time. Who has grown so used to waiting that he has forgotten what he is waiting for.

I did not know what I was waiting for. I did not know if I was waiting for anything.

But the silence knew. And it waited with me.

---

Bast found me one night, very late, sitting behind the bar with a rag in my hand and a glass that I had polished so long it had begun to squeak.

He sat down on a stool across the bar from me. He did not say anything for a long time. He only looked at me with his deep blue eyes.

"Reshi," he said at last. "Do you know what you're doing?"

"Polishing a glass."

"No." He shook his head. "You're waiting. You've been waiting since we came here. I can see it. I can *hear* it." He looked at me. "What are you waiting for?"

I looked down at the glass in my hand. At my own reflection in it, distorted and grey.

"I don't know," I said.

"Then why wait?"

I thought about it for a long time.

"Because an old man told me to," I said at last. "At a crossroads. He said someone would come. Someday. And ask for a story."

Bast went very still.

"Skarpi," he said.

"I think so. I don't remember his name. But he had bright eyes. Like a bird's." I set the glass down. "He said a man would come. A scribe. With ink on his fingers. And he'd ask for the true story of Kvothe." I looked at Bast. "And I'd tell him."

Bast stared at me.

"When?" he said. His voice was very quiet.

"He didn't say. Years. Maybe many years."

"And until then?"

I picked up another glass. I began to polish it. Slowly. In small even circles.

"Until then," I said, "I wait."

Bast looked at me for a long, long time. His face was very strange in the firelight. Full of something I did not understand.

Then he got up and went to bed without another word.

I stayed behind the bar. I polished the glass. The fire burned low. And the silence settled around me, three layers deep, like snow.

Waiting.
