# The Lord of Hollow Hours

The silver string led me out of the Hollow Market and north, into a country of long grey grass and low grey hills and single trees standing far apart, each one a different kind, each one very old. It was a lonely country. I walked for what felt like most of a day without seeing anything move but the grass.

And then, on the crest of a low hill, I came to a table.

It was a long table of dark wood, set out in the open under the twilight sky, with a white cloth on it and silver dishes and crystal glasses and candles burning in tall silver sticks. There were chairs down both sides of it, twelve on each side, all empty. And at the head of the table, in a great carved chair, there was a man.

He was eating.

He was tall and thin and very elegant, in a long coat of grey velvet with silver buttons, and a high collar, and lace at the cuffs. His face was long and pale and handsome, and his hair was the color of ashes, and his eyes were the color of old coins. He was eating a peach with a small silver knife, cutting it into very thin slices and laying each slice on his tongue as if it were a precious thing.

He looked up as I came over the crest of the hill. He smiled.

"A guest," he said. "How delightful. Sit, sit. You've arrived just in time for the second course."

---

I did not sit. I stood at the end of the long table with the silver string floating at my wrist, and I looked at him.

The string, I noticed, had stopped. It was not pointing north anymore. It was hanging slack in the air, drifting slightly, the way a strand of cobweb drifts when there is no wind. As if it had lost the road.

"I'm on the Moon's Road," I said. "Going north."

"Of course you are. Everyone is, eventually." He laid another slice of peach on his tongue. "But the road passes my table. It always has. And no one passes my table without dining." He gestured at the chairs with his little silver knife. "It's the rule."

"Whose rule?"

"Mine." He smiled. His teeth were very white and very even and slightly too many. "I am the Lord of Hollow Hours. This is my hill, and my table, and my road, for as long as the road is on my hill." He cut another slice. "Sit."

I thought about the rules. The ones Felurian had taught me, and the ones I had learned on my own. *Never eat their food. Never give your name. Never make a bargain you do not understand.*

I pulled out the nearest chair and sat down.

---

The Lord of Hollow Hours looked delighted. He clapped his long pale hands, once, and the silver dishes on the table began to steam. I smelled roast meat and fresh bread and spices I did not know, and something sweet, like honey and flowers, that made my mouth water and my head swim.

"Eat," he said. "Please."

"Thank you, my lord. I'm not hungry."

"Everyone is hungry." He tilted his head. "Especially mortals. Especially mortals who have been walking all day through my grey grass with nothing but a little stale bread in their packs." He smiled. "I can hear your stomach from here."

"I'm not hungry," I said again.

He looked at me for a long moment with his old-coin eyes. Then he laughed, very softly.

"Ah," he said. "You know the rules. How refreshing. Most of them don't, these days. They wander in, and sit, and eat, and then they're mine for a hundred years." He cut another slice of peach. "Very well. You won't eat. But you've sat. And the rule is that no one leaves my table without paying for their seat."

"What's the price?"

"Something small." He waved his little knife. "A trifle. Your shadow, perhaps." His eyes went to the shaed, hanging from my shoulders, rippling faintly in a wind I could not feel. "That one. The one you're wearing. It's very fine. Felurian's work, if I'm not mistaken."

"It's not for sale."

"Everything is for sale." He smiled his too-many-toothed smile. "But very well. If not the shadow, then something else. A year. A memory. Your name." His eyes glittered. "No? Then let's make it sporting. Let's play for it."

---

"Play what?"

He reached under the table and brought out a small box of polished wood. He opened it. Inside, there were stones. Black and white, smooth and round, nestled in green velvet.

A tak set.

I sat very still.

"You know the game," said the Lord of Hollow Hours. It was not a question. "I can see it on you. You've played. You've played with someone very good." He set the board on the table between us and began to lay out the stones. "If you win, you may go, with your shadow and your name and all your years. And I'll set your little string back on the road."

"And if I lose?"

He smiled.

"Then you'll stay," he said. "For a little while. And play with me again. And again. Until you win." He placed the last stone in its bowl. "Some of my guests have been playing for a very long time."

I looked down the table. At the twenty-three empty chairs.

They were not empty.

I do not know how I had not seen it before. Perhaps it was the twilight, or the candles, or something in the air. But when I looked down the table now, I saw that there was someone in every chair. Very faint. Very pale. Like reflections in a dark window. Men and women, old and young, in the clothes of every age. Each one sitting very still, with a tak board in front of them, and a hand hovering over a stone, as if about to make a move.

As if they had been about to make that move for a hundred years.

---

I looked back at the Lord of Hollow Hours.

He was watching me with his old-coin eyes, and his smile, and his little silver knife.

"Your move," he said.

I thought about Bredon. About the small garden in Severen and the old oak and the tak board under it. About a hundred games, and a thousand stones, and a kind old man's voice saying *the beautiful game is about time*.

I thought about how I had never, in all those games, truly beaten him. Not once. Not really. The few games I had won, I had won because he let me.

The Lord of Hollow Hours was not Bredon. But he had been playing for a very long time.

I looked at the board. At the stones in their bowls.

And then I did something that I think no one had ever done at that table before.

I reached into my pack, and took out my lute.

---

"What are you doing?" said the Lord of Hollow Hours. His smile had not moved, but something behind his eyes had.

"Paying for my seat," I said.

"We agreed on tak."

"You proposed tak. I didn't agree." I began to tune the lute. My left hand was still stiff; it was only three spans since the fire, and Felurian had not yet found me. But I could make the simple shapes, now. Slowly. "You said no one leaves your table without paying for their seat. You didn't say how."

He looked at me for a long moment.

"And you think," he said softly, "that a song will pay for a seat at the table of the Lord of Hollow Hours?"

"I think," I said, "that you've been sitting here a very long time. With twenty-three guests who never say anything, and a peach you've been cutting since before I was born." I nodded at the fruit in his hand. It was the same peach. I had watched him cut a dozen slices from it, and it was still whole. "I think you're bored. I think that's why you play. And I think nobody has sung at this table in a very long time."

The Lord of Hollow Hours did not say anything. His knife was very still in his long pale fingers.

I began to play.

---

I did not play *Tariniel*. That song was not for a place like this. I played the only song I could think of that was as long and as foolish and as endless as his table.

I played "Tinker Tanner."

All of it. Every verse I knew, and I knew a great many. The tinker and the miller's daughter. The tinker and the baron's wife. The tinker and the goat. The tinker and the priest, the tinker and the sailor, the tinker and the three sisters from Tinuë. I played it slowly at first, because of my hand, and then faster, as my hand warmed. I sang every verse with a straight face. I made up new ones when I ran out of old ones. I sang about a tinker who came to a table on a grey hill, and sat down, and refused to eat the peach.

And somewhere in the middle of the fortieth verse, the Lord of Hollow Hours laughed.

It was not his soft smiling laugh. It was a real laugh. Startled and helpless and loud, bursting out of him the way a bird bursts out of a hedge. He put down his knife. He put down his peach. He put his long pale hands over his face and laughed until his shoulders shook.

And down the long table, very faintly, the pale figures in the chairs began to move.

Not much. A turn of the head. A lift of the eyes. A hand, hovering over a tak stone for a hundred years, drawing back. Twenty-three faint faces, turning toward the sound of a tinker's song as if they had just remembered what music was.

I kept playing.

---

When I finally stopped, the Lord of Hollow Hours was wiping his eyes with a lace handkerchief. The peach lay forgotten on his plate. The pale guests were all looking at me, their faint faces very still.

"*The tinker and the goat*," said the Lord of Hollow Hours, and began to laugh again. "Oh. Oh, my. I haven't laughed like that in—" He stopped. He looked at the peach on his plate. He looked down the long table at his twenty-three guests, all looking at me. "In a very long time."

"Is my seat paid for?"

He looked at me. His old-coin eyes were wet and bright.

"Your seat," he said, "is paid for many times over." He picked up the peach. He looked at it for a long moment. Then he held it out to me. "Take this. Not to eat. Never to eat." He smiled. "Carry it. It's my hour. My hollow one. The one I've been cutting since before your world had a name." He pressed it into my hand. It was warm and heavy and smelled of summer. "If you're ever caught somewhere you can't escape, cut a slice. Just one. And for one hour, time will stop for everyone but you."

"Why would you give me that?"

"Because you gave me an hour of my own back," said the Lord of Hollow Hours. "The first I've had in ten thousand years." He waved his hand, and the silver string at my wrist lifted and pointed north again, bright and taut. "Go on, singer. Your road is waiting."

I stood up. I put the lute on my back and the peach in my pack.

"And them?" I said. I looked down the long table at the twenty-three faint guests.

The Lord of Hollow Hours followed my gaze. He was quiet for a long moment.

"Ah," he said softly. "Yes. Them." He looked at me. "You want me to let them go."

"Yes."

"They lost," he said. "Fairly. At tak."

"They've been sitting there a hundred years."

"Some of them more." He was quiet. Then he sighed. "Very well. One verse. One more verse of your tinker's song, one they've never heard, and I'll let them go. All of them." He smiled. "If it makes me laugh."

---

I sang him one more verse. I will not repeat it. It was about a tinker who came to a table on a grey hill, and found a lord there, eating a peach that never got smaller, and it was very rude about what the tinker suggested the lord do with the peach.

The Lord of Hollow Hours laughed so hard he fell out of his chair.

And when I looked down the long table, the chairs were empty. Truly empty, this time. Twenty-three chairs, and twenty-three tak boards, with the stones still laid out on them in their half-finished games, and no one sitting behind them at all.

I did not see where they went. I hope it was somewhere good.

---

I never cut the peach. I want that written down too. I carried it with me through the twilight and the Stormwal and the war, wrapped in a cloth at the bottom of my pack, and it never rotted and it never grew smaller and it always smelled of summer.

There were times I thought about cutting it. In the burning camp by the river, with Cinder's hand on my wrist. In the chamber beneath the Lackless house, with Denna on her knees and the door screaming. In the Tower of Thorns.

But I never did. Because I did not know what an hour of stopped time would cost, or who would pay for it, and I had learned by then that everything in the twilight costs something.

I gave it away, in the end. To someone who needed an hour more than I did.

But that is a story for another time.
