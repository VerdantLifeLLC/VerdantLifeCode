# Chapter Three: Debts

There are some debts you pay with money, and some debts you pay with time, and some debts you pay with pieces of yourself. The trick of it, the thing that separates the wise man from the fool, is knowing which kind of debt you are in before the collector comes to call.

My debt to Devi was the first kind. Or so I thought.

---

Devi lived above a butcher's shop in a narrow alley on the Imre side of the river. You climbed a set of wooden stairs on the outside of the building, stairs that had been steep and treacherous when they were built and had grown steeper and more treacherous with every passing year. At the top was a heavy door with no sign on it. If you knocked, and if Devi was in a mood to see you, the door would open. If she was not, you would stand there knocking until your knuckles bled, and nobody would ever answer.

I knocked.

The door opened almost at once, as if she had been waiting on the other side. She stood there in the doorway, small and fair and pretty as a doll, with her strawberry-blond hair pulled back and a pair of spectacles perched on the end of her nose. Devi was the most dangerous woman I knew, and I had known Felurian.

"Well," she said. "Look who's come crawling back."

"Hello, Devi."

"Don't you 'hello Devi' me." She put her hands on her hips. "Do you have any idea how long you've been gone? Do you have any idea how much interest has accrued on your loan in that time?"

"I have a fair idea."

"I doubt it. You were never very good at sums when you were panicking." She looked me up and down. Her eyes lingered on the scar on my cheek, then on my new clothes, then on the purse at my belt, which was heavier than it had any right to be. Something shifted in her face. "Oh," she said. "Oh, you've been busy."

"I have."

She stepped aside. "Come in, then. Let's see what you've brought me."

Her rooms were just as I remembered them. Warm, cluttered, and full of books. Shelves lined every wall, and every shelf was crammed so tight you could not have slid a knife between the spines. More books were stacked on the floor, on the chairs, on the table. There was a small alchemical bench in one corner with a rack of glass vials that caught the light from the window and threw it back in pale colors. There was a narrow bed, neatly made, with a cat curled up asleep on the pillow. The cat opened one eye as I came in, looked at me with profound disinterest, and closed it again.

Devi cleared a chair for me by the simple expedient of picking up the stack of books on it and dropping them on the floor. I sat. She sat across from me, behind a desk that was so covered in papers it looked like a snowdrift.

"Well?" she said.

I took out my purse and counted out the coins onto the desk between us. Talents, gleaming silver. Enough to pay back the whole of what I owed her, with interest, and a little more besides. When I finished, I looked up.

Devi was staring at the coins. For once in her life, she did not seem to know what to say.

"You're paying it all," she said at last.

"I'm paying it all."

"At once."

"At once."

She looked at me. Her eyes were very sharp behind her spectacles. "Where did you get this much money?"

"The Maer. I did him a service."

"What kind of service?"

"The kind that pays well."

She laughed, suddenly, a bright sharp sound like breaking glass. "You're learning," she said. "Two years ago you'd have told me the whole story, just to see my face." She reached out and began to count the coins herself, sliding them across the desk one by one with a practiced flick of her fingers. When she finished, she nodded. "It's all here."

"I know."

She scooped the coins into a drawer, locked it, and put the key on a chain around her neck. Then she sat back in her chair and folded her hands across her stomach and looked at me with an expression I had never seen on her face before. It took me a moment to recognize it. It was disappointment.

"Well," she said. "That's that, then."

"You sound sorry."

"I am sorry. I liked having you in my debt." She shrugged. "You were entertaining. And you had interesting friends. And there was always the chance that you'd do something truly spectacular and I'd get to watch." She tilted her head. "And your blood, of course. I still have it."

I had not forgotten. When I had first borrowed money from Devi, she had asked for a drop of my blood as collateral. With a drop of a man's blood, a skilled sympathist can do almost anything to him. Devi was a very skilled sympathist.

"I'd like it back," I said.

"I'm sure you would." She did not move. "But the debt is paid. And our agreement was that I would return the collateral when the debt was paid. So." She opened a drawer, reached inside, and drew out a small glass vial. Inside it, a single dark drop sat at the bottom, dried to a flake. She held it up to the light. "Here you are."

She held it out to me. I reached for it.

She pulled it back.

"Of course," she said sweetly, "there's the question of whether you'd like to borrow again."

I sighed. "Devi."

"I'm serious. You're a man of means now. A man of means has opportunities. And opportunities require capital." She smiled at me. It was a lovely smile. It was the smile of a spider watching a fly bump against the edges of its web. "I could make you a very good rate."

"I don't need to borrow any more money."

"Everyone needs to borrow money eventually. It's one of the great truths of the world."

"Maybe. But not today."

She looked at me for a long moment. Then she laughed again and tossed the vial across the desk. I caught it. The glass was warm from her hand.

"Fine," she said. "Fine. Be responsible. See if I care." She leaned back in her chair. "But I'll tell you something for free, since you're a paying customer now and not a debtor. You're going to need something from me before the year is out. Not money. Something else. I can smell it on you."

"What kind of something?"

"How should I know? I'm not a fortune-teller." She picked up a pen and began to write something in a ledger, not looking at me. "But when you need it, you'll come here. And then we'll talk about what it costs."

---

I should have left then. I had done what I came to do. My debt was paid, and my blood was back in my hand, and there was no reason to stay.

But I did not leave. Because as I stood up, my eye fell on a book lying open on the corner of her desk, half hidden beneath a sheaf of papers, and I saw a word on the open page that stopped me cold.

The word was *Valaritas*.

I knew that word. Everyone at the University knew it. It was carved into the face of the Four-plate door, deep in the heart of the Archives, a door of solid grey stone with no handle and no hinges and no lock. Four copper plates were set into its face, and nobody knew what they were for. Nobody knew what lay behind the door. Nobody had ever opened it, not in living memory, and possibly not ever.

"Where did you get this?" I said.

Devi looked up. She followed my gaze to the book, and her face went very still.

"That," she said, "is none of your business."

"It's about the Four-plate door."

"I know what it's about." She reached out and closed the book, firmly, and set a stack of papers on top of it. "And it's still none of your business."

I sat back down.

Devi glared at me. "What are you doing?"

"Staying," I said. "I want to know why you have a book about the Four-plate door."

"Why?"

"Because I want to know what's behind it."

Something flickered in her eyes. It was only there for a moment, and then it was gone, but I had spent a great deal of time learning to read faces. I knew what I had seen.

It was hunger.

"Everyone wants to know what's behind it," Devi said. Her voice was carefully neutral. "Every first-year in the University wants to know. It's a rite of passage. You get into the Archives, you see the door, you wonder what's behind it, and then you grow up and get on with your life."

"You didn't."

"What?"

"You didn't grow up and get on with your life. You still want to know." I nodded at the book. "You're still looking."

She was silent for a long moment. The cat on the bed stirred, stretched, and resettled itself.

"I was expelled," Devi said at last, "for trying to get into the Archives. Did you know that?"

"I'd heard."

"I was expelled for trying to get into the Archives *through a door I wasn't supposed to use*." She took off her spectacles and rubbed the bridge of her nose. "I was a very good student, you know. The best in my year, some said. I was going to be a Re'lar within a term. I had a gift for sympathy, a real gift. Kilvin liked me. Mandrag liked me. Even Lorren liked me, as much as Lorren likes anyone."

"What happened?"

"I got curious." She put her spectacles back on. "I'd been reading about the Archives. The history of the building. Did you know it's older than the University? Not all of it, just the core. The Stacks were built around something that was already there. Some kind of old stone structure. Nobody knows who built it or what it was for."

I shook my head.

"Nobody knows," Devi repeated. "Because nobody asks. That's the thing about the University. Everybody wants to know everything, except the things that are right under their noses." She leaned forward. "I found an old plan of the building. Very old. Older than the Archives, maybe. It showed a set of rooms beneath the Stacks. Rooms that don't exist anymore. Or rather, rooms that aren't on any map now." She tapped the desk with one finger. "And at the center of those rooms, there was a door."

"The Four-plate door."

"No." She shook her head. "Another door. Below it. Much older. The Four-plate door is just the top of the stair. There's something else, further down." She sat back. "I wanted to find it. So I went looking for a way in. And I found one, through the Underthing. And I got caught."

We sat in silence for a while. I thought about Auri, about the doors that were sleeping. About my dream.

"Did you ever find it?" I asked quietly. "The other door?"

"No. I never got close." Devi looked at me. "Why? What do you know?"

"Nothing," I said. "Not really. Just something someone told me."

"Who?"

"A friend."

She looked at me with narrowed eyes. Then she laughed, a small, humorless sound. "You're a terrible liar, you know. You're a wonderful storyteller and a terrible liar. It's a strange combination."

"I'm not lying. I really don't know anything."

"But you want to."

"Yes."

"How badly?"

I thought about that. I thought about the Chandrian, and the pages cut from every book I had ever read. I thought about the Amyr, whose records had been lost or burned or hidden. I thought about my parents, dead in the grass beneath a sky full of stars, and the smell of blood and burning, and a man with black eyes asking me whose parents had been singing the wrong sort of songs.

"Badly," I said.

Devi looked at me for a long time. Then she smiled. It was not her spider's smile this time. It was something warmer, and sadder, and much more dangerous.

"Well," she said. "Then I suppose we'd better talk."

---

We talked until the sun went down. Devi told me everything she knew about the Four-plate door, which was a great deal more than I had ever heard. She told me that the four copper plates were not decorative, as most people assumed, but were in fact part of a very old and very complex sympathetic mechanism. She told me that the stone of the door was not granite, as it appeared, but something else, something harder and older that she had never been able to identify. She told me that the word *Valaritas* was not in any language she could find, not Tema or Siaru or Yllish or Adem or any of the old tongues of the Aturan empire.

"I think it's a name," she said. "Not a word. A name."

"Whose name?"

"The door's, maybe. Or the thing behind it." She shrugged. "I don't know. That's the problem. I've spent six years trying to find out, and I don't know anything more now than I did when I started."

I told her a little, too. Not about Auri. I would never have told anyone about Auri. But I told her about my dream, and the door without a handle, and the breathing on the other side of it. She listened with her chin on her hand and her eyes fixed on my face, and when I was done, she did not laugh.

"That's a strange dream," she said.

"It felt real."

"Dreams do." She hesitated. "Kvothe. What do you want from this?"

"What do you mean?"

"I mean, why do you care? Why do you want to know what's behind the door?" She gestured vaguely. "You've got a good life here. You've got money now, and a patron, and friends. You've got the Eolian. You're one of the best students in the University, whatever the masters might say about your temper. Why risk all that for a door?"

It was a fair question. I thought about how to answer it.

"Because I think the things I'm looking for might be behind it," I said at last. "Or behind something like it. There are things in the world that people have tried very hard to forget. And I think they've hidden them. And I think if I want to find them, I'm going to have to go looking in the places nobody else will go."

Devi nodded slowly. "What things?"

I did not answer. After a moment, she smiled faintly.

"All right," she said. "Keep your secrets. I'll keep mine." She stood, and I stood too. "But I'll tell you this. If you ever decide to go looking for that door, I want to come with you."

"It's dangerous. If we got caught, they'd expel me. And they'd never let you back in."

"They'll never let me back in anyway." She said it lightly, but there was something bitter underneath. "I'm not asking for permission, Kvothe. I'm telling you. If you go, I go. That's the price."

"The price of what?"

She smiled her spider's smile. "Of my help," she said. "You're going to need it. I told you. I can smell it on you."

---

I walked back across the river in the dark with my blood in a vial in my pocket and my head spinning.

It was a clear night, and cold. The stars were out, bright and hard and very far away. The river rushed beneath the stone bridge with a sound like distant applause. I stopped at the top of the arch and looked back at the lights of Imre, and then ahead at the dark bulk of the University, and above it all the Archives, black against the stars.

Somewhere deep inside it, beneath the Stacks and the Tomes and the long cool rooms full of books, there was a door made of stone. And somewhere beneath that, perhaps, there was another.

I took the vial out of my pocket and looked at it. It was too dark to see the blood inside. But I knew it was there. A drop of me, taken and held and given back again.

I thought about what Devi had said. *You're going to need something from me before the year is out. Not money. Something else.*

I put the vial back in my pocket and went home.

I did not dream that night. Or if I did, I do not remember it. But when I woke in the morning, my hands were very cold, as if I had spent the whole night holding them against a wall of stone.
