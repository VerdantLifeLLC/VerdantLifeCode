# Stapes

Stapes found me in the hallway outside the Maer's chambers, very late, after the dinner. I was standing at a window, looking out at the dark gardens, trying to decide whether I had just been threatened or befriended. With the Maer, it was often both.

"Master Kvothe," said Stapes. "A word, if you would."

I turned. He was standing a few paces away, straight-backed and grey, his hands folded in front of him. He had been standing behind the Maer's chair all through dinner, the way he always did, pouring wine and removing plates and saying nothing. I had almost forgotten he was there. That was the point of Stapes. You were meant to forget he was there.

"Of course," I said.

He did not speak at once. He looked out the window beside me, at the dark gardens and the lights of Severen below the cliff, spread out like coals in a hearth. He was quiet for so long that I began to wonder if I had misheard him.

"You're wondering what he wants from you," Stapes said at last.

"I'm always wondering what he wants from me."

"Yes." Stapes allowed himself the smallest of smiles. "So is everyone. So, sometimes, is he." He looked at me. "I have served His Grace for thirty-one years. Since he was a boy of nine. I have dressed him and fed him and nursed him through the fevers and sat up with him through the nights when he could not sleep. I have seen him married and widowed and married again. I know him better than anyone living." He paused. "And I will tell you something, Master Kvothe, that I have never told anyone. Because I think you need to hear it. And because I think, perhaps, you are the only one who could understand it."

---

"When His Grace was a boy," said Stapes, "he had a brother."

I had not known that. I had read a great deal about the Alverons, in the Archives and elsewhere. I did not remember a brother.

"You won't have heard of him," said Stapes, as if he had heard my thought. "No one has. He was two years older than His Grace. His name was Aurel. He was the heir." Stapes looked out at the gardens. "He was a bright boy. Brighter than His Grace. Quick and clever and laughing, and everyone loved him. He could charm a bird out of a tree, the old servants used to say. He could charm a tax collector out of his ledger. He was going to be a great lord, one day. Everyone said so."

"What happened to him?"

"He drowned." Stapes's voice did not change. "In the mill race. Below the old mill, at the foot of the gardens. You'll have seen it."

I had. A narrow channel of dark water, cut through the rock, running fast and deep down to the great wheel. I had walked beside it only that afternoon.

"He was eleven," said Stapes. "His Grace was nine. They were playing. A game. The kind of game boys play, running along the edge of things, daring each other." He was quiet for a moment. "Aurel slipped. His Grace saw him go in. He ran for help. By the time help came, it was too late."

I did not say anything.

"I was the one who pulled him out," said Stapes. "I was a footman, then. Twenty-two years old. I went into the race with a rope around my waist, and I found him caught against the grate above the wheel, and I brought him up. And His Grace was standing on the bank. Nine years old. Soaking wet, because he'd tried to go in after his brother, and the gardener had pulled him back." Stapes looked at his hands. "He didn't cry. Not then. He just stood there, looking at his brother on the grass. And then he looked at me. And he said, *I should have held on to him.*"

---

The hallway was very quiet. Somewhere far off, a door closed.

"The old Maer was never the same," said Stapes. "He had loved Aurel best. Everyone knew it. And now he had only the second son. The small, quiet, sickly one, who did not laugh, who did not charm anyone, who watched and listened and never said what he was thinking." Stapes paused. "He was not kind to His Grace, after. He did not mean to be cruel. But he looked at His Grace, and he saw the son who had lived, instead of the son who should have. And His Grace knew it. Every day. For fifteen years."

"That's terrible."

"It is." Stapes looked at me. "But I am not telling you this so that you will pity him, Master Kvothe. He would hate that more than anything. I am telling you so that you will understand."

"Understand what?"

"Why he holds on." Stapes's voice was very quiet. "To everything. To his lands, and his title, and his secrets, and his wife. To every thread of every plan. He holds on so tightly that sometimes the things he holds on to cannot breathe." He looked out at the gardens. "He has spent his whole life holding on. Because the one time it mattered, he didn't. And his brother went into the dark water, and did not come back."

---

I thought about the Maer at dinner. About the way he had watched me across the table, with his pale sharp eyes. About the way he had said *Meluan* when he spoke of his wife, as if the name were something fragile he had to carry very carefully. About the box with no lid, and the way he had looked at it.

"Why tell me?" I said. "Why now?"

Stapes did not answer at once.

"Because he is going to ask you to do something," he said at last. "Soon. I do not know what. He does not tell me everything, though he tells me more than anyone. But I have seen that look on his face before, and I know what it means. He is going to ask you to do something dangerous. For him. For his house. For Her Grace." He looked at me. "And he is going to hold on to you, while you do it. Very tightly. Because he cannot help it."

"And you want me to let him?"

"No." Stapes shook his head slowly. "I want you to remember that he holds on because he is afraid. Not because he is cruel. Not because he wants to own you. Because he is afraid that if he lets go, you will go into the dark water too." He paused. "And I want you to remember, when the time comes, that sometimes the kindest thing you can do for a man like that is to hold on back."

---

I did not understand what he meant. Not then. I thought about it later, on a ridge above a burning camp, with a baby in my arms and the Maer's wife walking away into the shadow of the Chandrian. I thought about it in the grey dawn, when I carried that baby back to the Maer, and he took her from me with shaking hands, and held her so tightly I thought she would cry.

She did not cry. She looked up at him with her dark eyes. And he looked down at her, and his face did not move at all. But his hands, holding her, began very slowly to relax. The way a man's hands relax on a rope when he has finally pulled something out of the water, and knows it is breathing.

And Stapes, standing behind him, put one hand on his shoulder. Very lightly. The way you touch someone who might break.

---

But that was later. That night, in the hallway outside the Maer's chambers, I only nodded, and thanked him, and said I would remember.

Stapes inclined his head. He turned to go. Then he paused.

"One more thing, Master Kvothe."

"Yes?"

"The mill race." He did not look at me. "His Grace had the grate above the wheel taken out, after. And the wheel stopped. The old mill has not ground a single sack of grain in thirty years. It just sits there, at the foot of the gardens, with the water running past it." He was quiet for a moment. "The gardeners asked him, once, if they should tear it down. It's an eyesore, they said. And he said no. He said, *Leave it. It's a reminder.*"

"Of what?"

Stapes looked at me. His grey eyes were very calm and very sad.

"Of what happens," he said, "when you let go."

He bowed, very slightly, and went away down the hallway, his footsteps making no sound at all on the thick carpet. And I stood at the window for a long time afterward, looking down at the dark gardens, at the place where I knew the old mill stood, silent, with the water running past it in the dark.
