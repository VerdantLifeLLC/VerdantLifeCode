# A Signet

Auri was ill for nearly a span after that.

It was not an illness I understood. She had no fever, no cough, no rash. She ate a little, when I brought her food, and drank a little, when I brought her water. But she slept most of the day and most of the night, curled up on her bed of blankets in Mantle, and when she was awake she was quiet and distant and strange. She did not arrange her treasures in their patterns. She did not hum. She did not ask me what I had brought her, and when I gave her things, she thanked me politely and set them aside without looking at them.

It frightened me more than I can say.

I went down every day. Sometimes twice a day. I brought her food and water and small gifts, and I sat with her while she slept, and I played my lute for her softly, very softly, so as not to wake her. I did not know what else to do.

On the fifth day, I went to Elodin.

---

He was in his office, for once. It was a small cluttered room in the Rookery, crammed with books and papers and strange objects whose purpose I could not guess. He was sitting at his desk with his feet up, reading a book upside down.

"She's ill," I said. "Auri. Since the Cellarage. She won't wake up properly."

Elodin lowered the book. He looked at me for a long moment. Then he swung his feet down off the desk and stood up.

"Show me," he said.

I took him down. I had never taken anyone into the Underthing before, and I felt strange about it, as if I were betraying a confidence. But Elodin moved through the tunnels as if he knew them, and perhaps he did. He did not need my lamp. He walked in the dark as easily as in the light.

When we came to Mantle, Auri was asleep. Elodin knelt beside her bed and looked at her for a long time without touching her. Then he reached out and laid two fingers very lightly on her forehead.

He closed his eyes. His lips moved, silently.

After a long moment, he took his hand away and sat back on his heels.

"She's dreaming his dreams," he said quietly.

"What do you mean?"

"The Sleeper. He shared his dreams with her, while he held her. Now she can't stop dreaming them." He looked at Auri's pale sleeping face. "It's like a poison. A very slow one. It won't kill her. But if it goes on long enough, she'll forget how to wake up."

My heart turned over in my chest. "Can you fix it?"

"I can try." He was quiet for a moment. "But I'll need something from her. Something that belongs to her. Something that's truly hers, from before. Before the Underthing. Before she came here." He looked at me. "Do you know if she has anything like that?"

I thought about it. I thought about Auri's treasures, arranged so carefully on her shelf. The pebbles and bits of glass. The pressed leaves. The small bottles and boxes and scraps of cloth she had gathered over the years.

"I don't know," I said. "Maybe."

"Look," Elodin said. "Carefully. And respectfully. She won't thank you for pawing through her things."

---

I looked. I went through Auri's treasures one by one, lifting each small thing from its place and setting it down again exactly where I had found it. I did it slowly and carefully, the way you might handle the relics in a church. It felt like a violation. It felt like reading someone's diary while they slept.

But I did it. And at the very back of the shelf, behind a small wooden box with a broken lid, I found a bundle of cloth.

It was very old. The cloth was faded and soft with handling, a pale blue that had once, perhaps, been rich and deep. It was wrapped around something small and hard. I lifted it out and held it in my hand, and I knew, the way you sometimes know things, that this was what Elodin needed.

I unwrapped it.

Inside was a ring. A heavy ring of gold, worn smooth with age, set with a flat oval stone of deep green. There was a design carved into the stone. A seal. I turned it toward the light of my lamp and looked at it closely.

It was a signet ring. The kind of ring that nobles use to seal their letters. The design on the stone was a crest: a tower, and above it three stars, and around it a border of twining leaves.

I did not recognize it. Not then. But I knew what it was. I knew what it meant.

*She is of a royal line*, the Sleeper had said. *A princess. A daughter of kings.*

I stood there in the red light of my lamp with Auri's ring in my hand, and I felt the world shift beneath my feet.

---

I gave the ring to Elodin. He took it without comment, without even looking at the crest, and closed it in his fist. Then he knelt beside Auri again and laid his other hand on her forehead.

He began to speak.

I cannot tell you what he said. I did not understand it. It was not a language, or not one I knew. It was names. A long, slow, winding chain of names, one after the other, like beads on a string. Some of them I almost recognized. Most of them I did not. They seemed to go on forever, rising and falling, twisting and turning, until the air in Mantle hummed with them.

And then, at the end, he said one more name. Very softly. Very gently. The way you might whisper to a sleeping child to wake her.

I knew it was Auri's name. Her true name. I did not hear it clearly; it slipped past my ears like water through fingers. But I knew it.

Auri opened her eyes.

She looked up at Elodin. Then she looked at me. Her face was pale and her eyes were enormous, but they were clear. They were hers.

"Oh," she said softly. "Oh. I was so far away."

"You're back now," Elodin said.

"Yes." She sat up slowly. She looked down at her hands, as if she had never seen them before. "Yes. I'm back." She looked up at Elodin, and something passed across her face. Something shy and wary and old. "You said my name."

"I did."

"Nobody's said my name in a long time."

"I know." Elodin opened his fist and held out the ring to her. "I'm sorry. I had to use this. It was the only way to find you."

Auri looked at the ring. For a long moment she did not move. Then she reached out and took it from his palm, very carefully, the way you might pick up a bird with a broken wing. She held it in both hands and looked at it.

"I'd forgotten this," she said. "I put it away so I wouldn't have to remember."

"I know," said Elodin again.

She looked up at me. Her eyes were wet. "Did you see it?"

"Yes," I said.

"Did you know it?"

I hesitated. "No."

She nodded slowly. Then she wrapped the ring back in its faded cloth and held it against her chest.

"Please don't," she said. "Please don't find out. Please don't ask. I'm Auri now. I'm only Auri. I don't want to be anyone else."

I looked at her. At her pale face, her cloud of hair, her small bare feet. At the ring she held against her heart, and the crest carved into its stone.

"You're only Auri," I said. "I promise."

She smiled at me. It was a small, trembling smile. But it was hers.

---

I kept that promise for a long time. I did not go looking for the crest. I did not search the Archives for books of heraldry. I did not ask Lorren or Herma or any of the other masters what a tower beneath three stars might signify.

But I have a very good memory. I have always had a very good memory. And I could not stop myself from remembering.

It was months later. I was in Severen, at the Maer's court, and I was passing through a long gallery lined with portraits and banners. I was not paying attention to them. I was thinking about something else entirely. And then I passed a banner of deep green silk, hanging from the wall, with a crest embroidered on it in gold thread.

A tower. Three stars. A border of twining leaves.

I stopped. I stood there in the gallery and stared at it for a long time. Then I asked a passing servant whose banner it was.

He looked at me as if I were simple. "That's the banner of the royal house of Modeg," he said. "The Ariel line. Surely you know that?"

I did know it. Now.

There had been a princess of Modeg, some years before. A younger daughter. She had been sent away to study, far from home, at a great school across the sea. Some said she was clever. Some said she was strange. And some said that one day she had simply vanished, and that her family had searched for her for years, and never found her, and had finally given her up for dead.

Her name had been Ariel.

I walked away from that banner and did not look back. And I never told anyone what I had seen. Not the Maer, not Denna, not Sim or Wil or Elodin. I never told Auri.

She was only Auri. I had promised.

---

I have been thinking, lately, about what it means to keep a secret.

A secret is a kind of name. It is the true shape of a thing, hidden away where no one can see it. And like a name, it has power. To know someone's secret is to hold them in the palm of your hand.

I have known a great many secrets in my life. I have kept some of them and sold others. I have used them as weapons and as shields, as currency and as gifts. I have learned things about kings and queens and gods that would shake the world if they were spoken aloud.

But of all the secrets I have ever kept, the one I am proudest of is the smallest. It is a tower and three stars, carved into a green stone. It is a name I have never spoken.

It is the only promise I have ever made that I have kept perfectly.

Everything else, I broke.
