# The Wrong Sort of Songs

Count Threpe found me at the Eolian three nights after Denna left. I was sitting alone at a table near the back, nursing a mug of cider and not playing, which was unusual enough that Stanchion had come by twice to ask if I was ill.

"Kvothe!" Threpe dropped into the chair across from me with the delighted, slightly breathless air he brought to everything. He was a small man, round and bright-eyed, dressed in a coat of such a violent shade of blue that it seemed to hum. He was the only true patron of the arts I had ever known: a man who gave money to musicians because he loved music, and asked for nothing in return but the chance to listen. "My dear boy. I heard you were back and I simply didn't believe it. You've been gone an age. Tell me everything. No, don't. Play me something first, and then tell me everything."

"I'm not playing tonight, Threpe."

He blinked at me. "Not playing? Are you ill?"

"Stanchion asked the same thing."

"Stanchion is a very perceptive man, for someone who spends his life pouring beer." Threpe leaned forward and peered at me. "You look terrible. Is it a girl?"

I laughed despite myself. "Isn't it always?"

"Almost always. The rest of the time it's money, and you look too well-dressed for it to be money." He waved to a serving girl and ordered a bottle of wine, then turned back to me. "Is it the girl? The dark one? Dianne?"

"Denna."

"Denna, yes. She's calling herself that again, is she? I can never keep track." He sighed. "Lovely voice. Lovely girl. Terrible taste in patrons, if the rumors are true."

I went very still. "What rumors?"

Threpe looked at me. He was a kind man, but he was not a stupid one. He had spent his whole life among the nobility of Imre, and he knew how to read a face as well as any courtier.

"Ah," he said. "So it's that kind of trouble." He was quiet while the serving girl brought the wine and poured two glasses. When she had gone, he lifted his glass and looked at it rather than at me. "I don't know much. Nobody does. That's rather the point of him, I think. But I've heard things." He took a sip. "He pays very well, whoever he is. He's been paying for her lessons for years now. Singing, harp, languages. Old languages, mostly. Very old ones. She came to me once, a year or two ago, and asked if I knew anyone who could teach her to read Old Yllish. I didn't, of course. Nobody does. It's a dead tongue. But she seemed to think it was very important."

"Did she say why?"

"Something about a song she was writing. A very long song, about the old days. The Creation War and all that." He shrugged. "I thought it was charming. Most young singers are content to write about love and sheep. She wanted to write an epic." He smiled fondly. "Ambitious girl."

"Have you heard any of it?"

"Bits. Here and there. She played a few verses for me once, at a party at the Cuttlebones'." He frowned slightly. "It was very good. Very powerful. But—" He hesitated.

"But what?"

"But it made me uncomfortable." He set down his glass. "I don't know how to explain it. The music was lovely, and her voice was lovelier. But there was something in the words. Something that got under the skin. I remember I went home that night and couldn't sleep. I kept thinking about it. About Lanre. About how perhaps he'd been treated unfairly by history." He laughed awkwardly. "It's absurd, of course. He's just a character in a story. But for a few days afterward I found myself feeling rather sorry for him."

I looked down into my cider. "It's a good song, then."

"It's a very good song." Threpe peered at me again. "Why? Are you jealous?"

"No," I said. And then, because Threpe deserved honesty: "Yes. A little. But that's not it."

"Then what is it?"

I did not answer right away. I was thinking about my parents. About the song my father had been writing, the song about Lanre, the one he had worked on for years and never finished. I had heard pieces of it as a child, sitting by the fire in the evenings while he played and my mother sang. I had not understood most of it. But I remembered how it had felt. It had not made Lanre a hero. It had not made him a villain either, not exactly. It had made him a man. A man who had done a terrible thing, for reasons that seemed good to him at the time, and who had become something worse than a man because of it.

My father had spent years gathering the pieces of that story. He had talked to old men in taverns and old women in villages. He had read old books in forgotten libraries. He had been trying to find the truth.

And then the Chandrian had come, and he was dead, and his song was dead with him.

"Threpe," I said. "If someone were writing a song about Lanre. A true one. The oldest version of the story, the one that nobody tells anymore. Do you think people would want to hear it?"

Threpe looked at me with sudden, sharp interest. "Are you writing one?"

"I'm thinking about it."

"A rival song. To hers." His eyes lit up. "Oh, that's delicious. That's absolutely delicious. The two best young singers in Imre, dueling over the soul of a legend." He clapped his hands. "Of course people would want to hear it. They'd flock to it. They'd fight over seats." He leaned forward eagerly. "When can I hear it?"

"It's not written yet."

"Then write it! Write it quickly!" He poured more wine into my cup, sloshing some onto the table. "I'll fund it. Whatever you need. Paper, ink, lessons, research. Name it."

I looked at him. He meant it. He always meant it; that was the wonderful thing about Threpe. He was a man who believed in music the way a priest believes in God.

"I'll need to do some research," I said slowly. "Old sources. Very old. The kind that aren't in the Archives."

"Done. I know a man in Anilin with a private library you wouldn't believe. And a woman in Renere who collects old ballads. And—" He paused. "Well. I know a great many people. We'll find what you need." He lifted his glass. "To the true story of Lanre. May it be sung from every rooftop."

I lifted my glass and touched it to his. But I did not drink.

Because as he said it, a cold shiver had gone down my spine. I remembered a man with black eyes, standing over the bodies of my family. I remembered his voice, soft and reasonable and utterly without mercy.

*Someone's parents have been singing entirely the wrong sort of songs.*

---

I went home that night and did not sleep. I sat at my table with a candle and a sheaf of paper and my lute across my knees, and I tried to remember.

It was hard. I had been so young. And I had spent so many years trying not to remember, trying to bury those memories so deep that they could not hurt me anymore. When I had lived on the streets of Tarbean, I had locked them away in a place in my mind that I called the doors of sleep, and I had not opened those doors for a very long time.

But I opened them now. Slowly, carefully, the way you might open a door onto a room where something terrible had happened. I let the memories come.

I remembered my father at the fire, his lute in his lap, his fingers moving on the strings. I remembered his voice, low and rich, singing words I did not understand. I remembered my mother beside him, her head on his shoulder, singing the harmony.

I remembered pieces of the song. A line here, a verse there. A melody that rose and fell like the wind in the trees.

*In the city of Tariniel, where the white towers rise...*

*Lanre, who loved her more than light, more than breath...*

*And Selitos saw, and his eye was a stone...*

I wrote them down. Every scrap I could remember. I wrote until my hand ached and the candle guttered and the sky outside my window began to go grey with dawn.

When I was done, I had three pages of fragments. Lines and half-lines, bits of melody scrawled in my own private notation, notes to myself in the margins. It was not a song. It was not even the beginning of a song. It was the ruin of a song, the broken pieces of something that had once been whole.

But it was a start.

I looked down at the pages in the grey light. At my father's words, rescued from the dark. At my mother's harmonies, scribbled in the margins.

And I made a promise to myself. I would finish it. I would find the rest of the story, the true story, and I would write the song my father had never been able to finish. I would sing it at the Eolian, in front of everyone. I would sing it so loudly that the whole world would hear.

I was seventeen years old. I did not know what I was promising. I did not know that some songs are more dangerous than swords.

Or rather, I did know. I had known since I was eleven years old, standing among the bodies of everyone I had ever loved. I simply did not care.

---

That morning, on my way to the Fishery, I stopped by my room and lit Kilvin's candle.

I had fallen into the habit of lighting it every morning and every night, the way some people say a prayer. I would strike a match, hold it to the wick, and watch the flame catch and steady. And every morning and every night, it burned yellow.

It burned yellow that morning too. A warm, cheerful, ordinary yellow.

I watched it for a long moment. Then I leaned forward to snuff it.

And just before my fingers touched the wick, I saw it flicker.

Not blue. Not the pale moon-silver I had seen once before. It flickered darker, for the barest instant, as if a shadow had passed across it. As if something, somewhere, had turned its head to look at me.

I snuffed the candle. I sat very still for a long moment, listening to my own heart beat.

Then I picked up my three pages of fragments and folded them carefully and put them in the inside pocket of my cloak, next to my heart, and went to the Fishery.

I told myself it was nothing. A draft. A trick of the light.

I have always been a very good liar, when the person I am lying to is myself.
