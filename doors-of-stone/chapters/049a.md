# Stanchion's Wake

They buried Stanchion on the fourth day after the fire, and the whole of Imre came.

I had not known how many people he knew. I suppose I should have. He had been behind the bar of the Eolian for twenty-two years, pouring drinks and taking coins and listening to music, and in that time half the musicians in the Four Corners had passed through his doors, and every one of them had eaten his food and slept under his roof at least once when they were hungry. He had kept the widow's promise. He had never turned one away.

They came back for him.

---

The burial was in the old cemetery on the hill above Imre, among the yews, where the town put its merchants and its minor nobles and its guildmasters. Stanchion was none of those things. He had bought the plot himself, twenty years before, Deoch told me, because it had a view of the river and the bridge and the Eolian's roof. He said he wanted to be able to keep an eye on the place.

There was no roof to keep an eye on now. Only blackened walls, open to the sky.

They carried him up the hill in a plain pine coffin, six of them. Deoch at the front, on the right. Threpe, who was much too small and too old to carry anything and insisted anyway, at the front on the left. Four musicians behind them: a fiddler from the Small Kingdoms, a harpist from Atur, a big bearded piper from the north who had won his talent pipes forty years before, and the old man with the hurdy-gurdy, weeping openly.

I did not carry him. I could not. My left arm was still dead from the elbow down, and Cinder's handprint burned cold on my wrist. I walked behind the coffin with Sim on one side of me and Devi on the other, and Wil and Fela behind us, Wil with his hands wrapped in white bandages and Fela leaning on his arm, still pale and coughing from the smoke.

And behind us, the rest of Imre.

---

I looked back once, on the hill path, and could not see the end of them. They filled the path and spilled over onto the grass on either side. Musicians, dozens of them, with their instruments on their backs. Students from the University. Merchants and tradesmen and their wives. Serving girls from the taverns. Beggars from the docks, who Stanchion had fed on cold nights with the leftovers from the kitchen. A pair of whores from the Wharf Street houses, dressed in borrowed black, holding hands.

And people I did not know at all. Old men and women who had been young when the Eolian was new, and had heard music there on some night forty years gone that they had never forgotten.

"He'd hate this," said Deoch, when we reached the top of the hill and set the coffin down beside the grave. His voice was rough. "All these people. Making a fuss. He always said funerals were a waste of good drinking time."

"He'd love it," said Threpe. He was breathing hard from the climb, and his face was red, and he was weeping without seeming to notice. "He'd pretend to hate it. But he'd love it."

Deoch looked at him. Then he laughed, a broken sound, and wiped his eyes.

"Yes," he said. "Yes, he would."

---

There was no priest. Stanchion had never been a religious man. Deoch said he had once thrown a Tehlin out of the Eolian for trying to preach to the crowd between songs, and had said that if God wanted to be heard in his hall, God could get up on the stage and play like everyone else.

So instead of a priest, there were the musicians.

They played. One after another. They stood at the head of the grave, beside the coffin, in the grey afternoon light under the yews, and they played whatever they had played the first time they came to the Eolian. The fiddler from the Small Kingdoms played a reel so fast and bright that people laughed through their tears. The harpist from Atur played a lament. The big bearded piper played something old and wild and northern that made the hair stand up on my arms. The old man with the hurdy-gurdy played the same four songs he had been playing at the Eolian for thirty years, and everyone sang along, even the whores from Wharf Street, even the beggars.

They played for two hours. More. Dozens of them. Every musician in Imre, and many from farther away, who had heard about the fire and come.

And then, when the last of them had played and stepped back, they all turned and looked at me.

---

I did not move.

I stood at the edge of the crowd, between Sim and Devi, with my cold dead arm hanging at my side, and I looked at them looking at me. Hundreds of faces. Waiting.

"I can't," I said.

Nobody answered.

"I can't," I said again. "My hand. I can't play. Not with one hand."

Deoch came through the crowd toward me. He stopped in front of me and looked at me for a long moment with his handsome ruined face.

"He told me to make sure you kept singing," he said. "That night. Before. He said it was the most important thing." He held out something. It was a lute. Not mine; mine had been lost in the fire, or I thought it had, then. It was an old lute, battered and scarred, with a crack in the soundboard mended with glue. "This was his. The one he brought from the Small Kingdoms. The one he couldn't play." He held it out to me. "Sing, Kvothe. You don't need two hands to sing."

I looked at the lute. At Deoch. At the hundreds of faces behind him, waiting.

I took it.

---

I could not play it. Not properly. My left hand would not form chords; the fingers were stiff and cold and would not bend. But I could hold the lute against my body with my left arm, and I could strike the open strings with my right hand, one at a time, slowly, the way a child plays.

So I did that. I stood at the head of Stanchion's grave with his old cracked lute that he had never been able to play, and I struck the open strings, one by one, and I sang.

I did not sing *Tariniel*. That would have been wrong. It was not his song; it was the song that had killed him. I sang something else. Something I had never sung for anyone, because I had never finished it. A song I had begun on the road back from Severen, the year before, and set aside, and forgotten.

It was about a room.

A round room, with a leaking roof and rats in the rafters and horse dung on the floor three inches deep. A room that a boy of nineteen looked down at from a hayloft, on a rainy night, and thought was beautiful. A room he cleaned with his own hands, and built a stage in with stones he carried up from the quarry on his back, two at a time. A room he filled with music, for twenty-two years, though he could never play a note himself.

A good room.

I did not know the words, when I started. They came as I sang them. They came up out of me from somewhere very far down, the way the name of the wind had come once, the way *Tariniel* had come in Skarpi's lighthouse. I sang about the stable and the widow and the eleven talents. I sang about the promise. I sang about a big bearded man standing on the second balcony of his own hall, with two empty buckets in his hands, telling a Chandrian that she could have him but not the girl, and not his walls.

I sang it with one hand, on the open strings of a cracked lute that its owner had never been able to play.

When I finished, no one on that hill made a sound.

---

They put him in the ground afterward. Deoch and the four musicians lowered the coffin on ropes, and Threpe threw the first handful of earth, and then everyone threw a handful, one after another, hundreds of them, a long slow line winding past the grave. The musicians threw other things too. A broken string. A fiddle peg. A reed from a pipe.

The old man with the hurdy-gurdy threw in a coin.

"For the ferry," he said, to no one in particular. "He always paid his debts."

---

I stayed after the others had gone. I sat on the grass beside the fresh grave with Stanchion's lute across my knees, under the yews, and looked down the hill at the river and the bridge and the blackened ring of the Eolian's walls.

Deoch came back, after a while, and sat beside me. He did not say anything for a long time.

"I'm going to rebuild it," he said at last.

I looked at him.

"The Eolian," he said. "Stone by stone. The way he did. I've got some money saved. Not much. And Threpe will help, and the musicians' guild, and half of Imre, from the look of today." He was quiet for a moment. "It'll take years. But I'm going to do it."

"He'd like that."

"He'd tell me I was doing it wrong. Every step of the way." Deoch smiled faintly. "He'd tell me the balconies were too high and the stage was too low and I'd put the bar on the wrong side." He looked at the blackened walls. "I'll put it exactly where he had it."

He was quiet for a long moment.

"Keep the lute," he said. "His. He'd want you to have it. He always said you were the best he ever heard."

"Deoch, I can't—"

"You can. You will." He stood up. He looked down at me. "And when it's rebuilt, you'll come back. And you'll play on that stage with two hands, the way he wanted. You owe him a song."

"I just sang him one."

"You owe him another." Deoch's face was very still. "You'll always owe him another. That's how it works, with the dead. You never finish paying."

He turned and walked away down the hill.

---

I never played Stanchion's lute on the stage of the rebuilt Eolian. I want you to know that. It is one of the debts I have not paid.

But I kept it. Threpe kept it for me, all through the Fae and the war and the door, through Renere and the Tower of Thorns, and gave it back to me in Imre the autumn I became Kote. I kept it under the bar at the Waystone for seven years, in its case, and never once took it out.

And it is the lute I played, one morning, very recently, in the common room of that inn, for a room full of farmers who had come out of a church at dawn. My own was still upstairs, in the open chest. My hands went to the bar first, and found Stanchion's.

I think he would have laughed at that. I think he would have said it was about time someone played the damned thing properly.
