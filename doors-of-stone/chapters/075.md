# What Came Through

The first thing through the crack was a hand.

It was long and grey and very thin, with too many joints in its fingers and nails like chips of flint. It came out of the silver light slowly, feeling its way, the way a blind man's hand feels along a wall. It curled around the edge of the crack. It gripped the stone.

And then it pulled.

The door groaned. It was a deep sound, a sound that came up through the floor and into my bones. The crack widened. Not much. A finger's breadth. But it widened.

A second hand came through beside the first. Then a third, lower down. Then a fourth.

They were not all the same. That was the worst of it. The first was long and grey and many-jointed. The second was small and black and shiny, like a beetle's leg, ending in a hooked claw. The third was pale and soft and perfectly human, a woman's hand, with a ring on one finger. The fourth was not a hand at all, only a shape, a smudge of darkness with the suggestion of fingers.

They gripped the edges of the crack, all of them, and they pulled.

---

"*Scrael*," said Bast.

He was right. Behind the hands, in the silver light pouring through the gap, I could see them. Black shapes, gleaming, crowding against the other side of the crack. Dozens of them. Hundreds. Their knife-legs clicking and scrabbling at the stone. Trying to get through.

And other things. Things I could not see clearly. Shapes that moved wrong, that bent in ways that nothing should bend. A thing like a man made of wet leather, with no face. A thing like a great pale worm, coiling and uncoiling. A thing like a shadow that had learned to stand up.

They were all pressing against the crack. All reaching through. All pulling.

"*The door*," I said. "*It's still open. Ambrose opened it and nobody closed it.*"

"Close it, then!"

"I can't. Not without the key."

We both looked down at Ambrose's body, lying on the stone at our feet.

The box was still on its chain around his neck. Lying on his chest. Humming. I could hear it, even over the screaming of the wind and the clash of swords and the shrieking of the Surprises. Its long slow *no*.

I knelt. I reached for it.

And a boot came down on the chain.

---

It was Cinder.

He was standing over Ambrose's body, over me, with one foot planted on the silver chain. His ice-pale sword was in his hand. His black eyes were looking down into mine.

"Not yet," he said softly.

I looked up at him. I did not move.

"You can't touch me," I said.

"I know." He smiled. "I'm not touching you. I'm standing on a chain." He pressed down with his boot. The chain bit into the dead king's neck. "And I'm not going to move until the door is open. All the way open. And the lord has passed through." He glanced toward the stair. "He's coming now. Can you feel him?"

I could. A darkness at the top of the stair. A weight. A place where light went to be forgotten.

Haliax was coming down.

---

And then something struck Cinder in the chest, very hard, and knocked him backward off the chain.

It was Vashet.

She had come across the chamber from where she had been fighting the soldiers, and she had not slowed down. She hit Cinder with her shoulder, the way Tempi had hit him in the burning camp, and he staggered. And before he could recover, before he could move with that impossible speed of his, she was on him.

Her sword was a blur. Faster than I had ever seen her move. Faster than I had known anyone could move. It came at Cinder from every direction at once, high and low and left and right, a storm of steel.

Cinder parried. His ice-pale sword flashed. But for the first time, I saw him struggle. I saw him step back. And back. And back.

"*You killed my friend*," said Vashet. Her voice was very calm. Very flat. Adem voices are always flat. "*The quiet one. You said he was better than me.*" Her sword flashed. "*You were wrong.*"

Cinder's sword went wide. For a heartbeat, a single heartbeat, his guard was open.

Vashet's blade went through it.

It did not go into his heart. He was too fast for that, even then. He twisted, impossibly, like smoke. But her blade caught his face. It laid open his cheek from the corner of his black eye to the corner of his mouth.

Cinder screamed.

It was the first time I had ever heard him make a sound that was not soft and reasonable. It was a high, thin, terrible scream, like ice cracking on a winter lake. He staggered back, with his hand pressed to his face. Black blood welled up between his fingers. It steamed in the cold air.

Vashet stood in front of him with her sword raised. Her face showed nothing. But her hands, the one on the hilt and the one at her side, moved. Just slightly.

*I told you*, they said. *I will remember you.*

---

I did not waste the moment. I knelt beside Ambrose's body and took hold of the box.

I lifted it off his chest. I pulled the chain over his head. It came free, and the box was in my hands.

It sang.

I felt it all through me. Its long slow *no*, loud and clear and joyful, like a dog that has found its master. And beneath it, the name of the line. My mother's name. My name. Braided through the stone like a thread through cloth.

And beneath that, the voice from behind the door. Very close now. Very loud.

*YES*, it said. *YES. CHILD OF MY CHILD. YOU HAVE THE KEY. YOU HAVE THE NAME. COME. OPEN. COME HOME.*

I stood up with the box in my hands. I turned to face the door.

The crack was wider now. Much wider. The hands had pulled it open to the width of a man's shoulders. The silver light pouring through it was blinding. The wind was a howling gale. The things behind it were pressing forward, reaching, clawing. A scrael had got its front legs through the gap. The wet leather thing had got its head through, its faceless head, and it was turning slowly from side to side, as if looking for something.

As if looking for me.

---

"Kvothe."

I turned.

Bredon was standing beside me. He had left his table and his tak board. He was leaning on his stick, a few paces away, and his face was calm again. Mild again. The face of a grandfather.

But his eyes were not calm. They were very bright. Brighter than I had ever seen them.

"You have the key," he said softly. "You have the name. You're standing at the door." He smiled. "This is the moment. The one we've all been waiting for. Your whole life has been leading here."

"Get away from me."

"Speak the name, Kvothe. Open the door. Let him through." Bredon's voice was very gentle. "And I'll let her go."

He raised his hand. There was a cord in it. Thin and dark. It ran from his fingers across the chamber, through the air, glowing faintly red, to where Denna knelt beside her harp.

To the knot on her wrist.

"No," I said.

Bredon tightened his fingers.

Across the chamber, Denna screamed.

---

I turned. I could not help it.

She was on her knees on the stone floor, with her wrist held up in front of her. The knot on it was blazing red, bright as a coal. The cord running from it to Bredon's hand was taut as a bowstring. And she was screaming. Her whole body was rigid, arched back, her face twisted in agony. Her other hand was clawing at her chest, over her heart.

"*Stop it!*" I shouted. "*Stop it! You're killing her!*"

"Yes," said Bredon calmly. "I am."

He tightened his fingers a little more. Denna's scream cut off. She made a small choking sound. Her hand on her chest clenched.

"The knot," said Bredon, "is tied to her heart. I told her so, when I tied it. I told her if she ever betrayed me, it would stop." He looked at me. "She has betrayed me. Several times. She has been sending you messages, tied in her hair. She thinks I didn't know." He smiled faintly. "I always know."

"Let her go."

"Speak the name."

"*Let her go!*"

"Speak the name, Kvothe," said Bredon softly. "Or watch her die. Those are your moves. There are no others." He tightened his fingers. "Choose."

---

I looked at Denna. On her knees on the stone, with her face going grey and her hand clawing at her chest and the knot on her wrist blazing red.

I looked at the door. At the crack, gaping wide. At the hands and claws and faceless heads pressing through it. At the silver light, and the freezing wind, and the voice calling my name.

I looked at the box in my hands. Humming. Singing. Waiting.

And I thought about what Selitos had told me, in the round tower at the center of the dead white city.

*Only one of the line, standing before the door, with the key in hand, speaking the door's name. Not to open it. To un-speak their own part of it.*

To un-speak it. To take the name of the line back out of the stone.

But to un-speak a name, you must first speak it.

I understood, then. Standing there with the box in my hands and Denna dying on the floor and Bredon's bright eyes on my face. I understood what he had been waiting for. Why he had needed me here, at the door, with the key and the name. Not to open it. He could not make me open it. No one could make me open it.

He needed me to speak the name. For any reason at all. Because the moment I spoke it, the door would hear. And the door would open. And whatever I meant to do afterward—un-speak it, close it, lock it forever—it would be too late. The Thief would already be through.

That was the beautiful game. That was the shape he had been building all along, stone by stone, move by move. Not a trap to make me open the door.

A trap to make me try to close it.

---

"Reshi."

It was Bast. He was beside me, with his burned hand on my arm. His face was white. His eyes were very bright.

"Reshi, I know what you're thinking. I can see it on you." His voice was shaking. "Don't. Don't do it. If you speak the name, it'll open. You know it will. He knows it will. That's what he wants."

"I know."

"Then *don't*!"

"If I don't, Denna dies."

"Then let her die!" Bast's voice cracked. "Let her die, Reshi. She's one person. One. And if that door opens—"

I looked at him. At his fierce, frightened, beloved face.

"I can't," I said.

"*Why not?*"

I did not answer. I did not need to. He saw it in my face.

He let go of my arm. He stepped back. His face crumpled, just for a moment, and then went very still.

"Then do it right," he said quietly. "Whatever you do. Do it right."

---

I turned to face the door.

I held the box in both hands. I felt it singing. I felt the name of the line braided through it, and the name of the door, and my own name, all wound together like three strands of a single cord.

I closed my eyes.

And I spoke.

I spoke the name of the door. The keeper's door. Not the one Selitos had given me, the one beneath the Archives. This one. I had never heard it. No one had ever told it to me. But I knew it. I had known it since I first touched the box in Meluan's green room. It had been in me all along, the way my own name was in me. Because it *was* my name. Part of it. The part that was the line.

I spoke it. And the door heard.

---

It opened.

Not all the way. Not at once. But the door that had been sealed for ten thousand years heard its name spoken by the one voice in all the world it had been made to answer, and it opened.

The crack became a gap. The gap became a doorway. The grey stone slid back on either side, silently, smoothly, the way a curtain slides back from a stage.

And beyond it, there was nothing.

Not darkness. Not light. Nothing. A place that was not a place. An absence so complete that it hurt to look at, the way the sun hurts to look at. It was the space between the stars. It was the silence after the last note of the last song. It was the place where names go when they are forgotten.

And in the center of it, very far away and very close at the same time, there was something.

I cannot describe it. I have tried. I have lain awake a thousand nights trying to find the words. There are no words. It was not a shape. It was not a color. It was not a sound.

It was a *want*.

The purest, deepest, most terrible want I have ever felt. A hunger so vast and so old that it had become a thing in itself. It wanted everything. It wanted the world. It wanted the moon. It wanted to take all the names of all the things that were, and unmake them, and make them into something else. Something it had chosen. Something that would love it back.

And it turned toward me. And it spoke.

*CHILD OF MY CHILD*, said the Thief. *THANK YOU.*

And the things that had been pressing against the door came pouring through.
