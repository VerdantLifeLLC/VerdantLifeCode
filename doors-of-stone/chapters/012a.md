# The Name of Snow

Elodin held his class on the roof of the Rookery that winter. In the snow.

There were four of us left by then, of the five who had begun the term. Fela and I, and Uresta, the quiet Cealdish boy with long fingers who had never once spoken in class, and Inyssa, the Vintish girl with her hair cropped short as a boy's, who was taking the class, she told us, because her father had said naming was a waste of time. Brey had dropped out at midterm. I do not blame him. It was a very strange class, even by Elodin's standards.

"Today," said Elodin, "we will learn the name of snow."

He was sitting cross-legged on the flat lead roof, in the middle of a drift, in his black master's robe. He had no hat and no gloves and no boots. His feet were bare and blue with cold, and there was snow in his hair and on his eyebrows and melting on his cheeks, and he looked as happy as I had ever seen him.

We stood around him in a half circle, shivering, in cloaks and scarves and every layer of clothing we owned.

"Master Elodin," said Inyssa through chattering teeth. "Couldn't we learn the name of snow indoors?"

Elodin looked at him with bright interest.

"Could we?" he said. "Let's think about that. Where is the snow, Inyssa?"

"Out here."

"And where would we be?"

"In there." Inyssa gestured at the stairwell door. "Where it's warm."

"And would the snow be in there with us?"

Inyssa opened her mouth. She closed it again.

"There you are," said Elodin cheerfully. "Sit down. All of you. In the snow. Go on."

---

We sat. The snow soaked through my trousers in an instant. Beside me, Fela made a small unhappy sound and tucked her hands into her armpits.

"Close your eyes," said Elodin.

We closed our eyes.

"Now," he said. "Tell me what snow is."

"Frozen water," said Inyssa promptly.

"Wrong."

"It *is* frozen water. Master Mandrag says—"

"Master Mandrag is very clever about water," said Elodin. "And very clever about ice. But he is not clever about snow, because he has never sat in it. Try again."

Nobody said anything. The wind moved across the roof, light and cold, and I heard the soft hiss of snow blowing across the lead.

"It's quiet," said Uresta.

We all turned to look at him. It was the first word any of us had ever heard him say. His voice was soft and low and a little hoarse, like a door that has not been opened in a long time.

Elodin turned his head toward her. I could not see him, with my eyes closed, but I heard him turn.

"Yes," he said. "Go on."

"It makes everything quiet." Uresta's voice was very hesitant. "When it falls. Everything goes still. The birds stop. The carts stop. People talk softer." He paused. "It's like the whole world is holding its breath."

"Good," said Elodin. "Very good. What else?"

---

"It's patient," said Fela, after a while. "It doesn't fall all at once. It comes down one flake at a time, and each one is so small it hardly matters. But it keeps coming. And in the morning, the whole world is covered."

"Yes."

"It forgets," I said.

I had not meant to say it. It came out of me the way things sometimes did, in Elodin's class, from somewhere below thinking.

Elodin was quiet for a moment.

"Go on," he said.

"It covers things up," I said slowly. "The roads, and the fields, and the footprints. All the marks people make. It covers them over, and makes everything smooth and new, as if nothing had ever happened there." I paused. "And then it melts. And everything's still there underneath. Just the same as before." I did not know where the words were coming from. "It doesn't really forget. It only pretends. For a while."

The wind moved across the roof. Nobody said anything.

"Open your eyes," said Elodin.

---

We opened our eyes. He was looking at me. His face was very still, and there was something in his eyes I did not understand then, and did not understand for years.

"That is a very sad thing to know about snow," he said. "At your age."

I did not say anything.

"But it's true," said Elodin. "All of it. What Uresta said, and Fela said, and you said. Even what Inyssa said, in its dull little way. Snow is frozen water. And it is quiet. And it is patient. And it forgets, or pretends to." He scooped up a handful of snow from the drift beside him and held it out on his palm. "All of those things are in its name. And more. Much more. More than we could say if we sat here until spring."

We looked at the snow in his hand. It was already beginning to melt.

"That is the first lesson," said Elodin. "A name is not a word. A name is everything a thing is. All at once. All the way down." He closed his hand. Water dripped between his fingers. "You cannot say it. Not with your mouth. You can only know it. And then, sometimes, if you are very lucky and very careful, you can let it speak through you."

---

"Master Elodin," said Inyssa. "Can you do it? Call the name of snow?"

Elodin looked at him for a long moment. Then he smiled. It was not a kind smile, exactly. But it was not unkind either.

"Would you like to see?"

Inyssa hesitated. Then she nodded.

Elodin stood up. He brushed the snow off his robe. He walked to the edge of the roof, barefoot, leaving blue footprints in the white, and stood there looking out over the University. The Archives, grey and windowless. The Mains, sprawling and patched. The Fishery with its chimneys. The whole great muddle of it, roofed in white.

He did not say anything. He did not raise his hands. He only stood there, with his back to us, for a long time.

And the snow stopped.

Not all at once. Not like a door closing. It slowed. The flakes that had been drifting down in their thousands began to drift more slowly, and more slowly still, until they were hanging in the air all around us, motionless, like dust in a sunbeam. Like stars.

I heard Fela draw in her breath.

Every flake. As far as I could see. Over the Rookery and the Archives and the Mains and the river and the town of Imre on the far bank. Hanging in the grey air, perfectly still. Silent. Waiting.

Elodin turned around. He looked at us. His face was pale and there was sweat on his forehead, despite the cold.

"Snow," he said softly, "is also *listening*."

And he let go.

The flakes began to fall again. Slowly at first, then faster, until they were drifting down around us the way they had before, as if nothing had happened. As if the whole world had not, for a moment, held its breath.

---

Inyssa dropped the class the next day. Uresta did not come back either. I heard later that he left the University altogether, at the end of the term, and went home to the mountains of Ceald, and became a weaver of the heavy wool blankets they make there. I have always hoped he was happy. I have always hoped he wove snow into his cloth, sometimes, when no one was watching.

Fela and I stayed.

After the others had gone, that afternoon, I lingered on the roof. Elodin was still sitting in his drift, cross-legged, with his eyes closed. I thought he had forgotten I was there. Then he spoke.

"You said it forgets," he said, without opening his eyes. "Or pretends to."

"Yes."

"Where did that come from, Kvothe?"

I did not answer at once. I looked out over the white roofs. I thought of Tarbean. Of three winters, sleeping in doorways and on rooftops, with the snow coming down over the city and covering everything. The blood in the gutters. The bodies in the alleys, in the mornings. The marks of every terrible thing that had happened in the night, smoothed over, white and clean.

And then the thaw. And everything still there underneath.

"I don't know," I said.

Elodin opened his eyes. He looked at me for a long time.

"Someday," he said, "you are going to want very much to forget something. To cover it over. To make everything smooth and white, as if it never happened." He paused. "And you will find, if you are unlucky, that you can."

I looked at him.

"Don't," said Elodin quietly. "That's all. That's the lesson. When the time comes, don't."

He closed his eyes again. After a while, I went away down the stairs, and left him sitting in the snow.

---

I did not understand what he meant. I did not understand it for years. Not until a chamber beneath an old house, and a woman with a cord in her hands, and a whole life covered over, smooth and white, as if it had never happened.

Seven years of snow.

And then, one autumn, a scribe with bloody knuckles. And the thaw.
