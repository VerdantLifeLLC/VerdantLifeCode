# The Last Singer

I stayed with Skarpi in the broken lighthouse for nine days.

I do not know how he lived there. There was no food that I could see, and no water except the rain that collected in a hollow in the rocks outside, and no bed except the bare stone floor. And yet every evening there was bread beside the fire, fresh and warm, and a jug of cold clean water, and sometimes cheese, and once, memorably, a whole roast chicken, glistening with fat. I asked him where it came from, the first night. He only smiled and said that people in Waterside were very kind to old storytellers.

I did not ask again.

By day, he taught me. Not in the way the masters at the University taught, with lectures and books and examinations. He taught me the way a musician teaches another musician: by singing, and listening, and singing again.

He sang me the true story. All of it. Not in words, the way he had told it to me by the fire that first night, but in song. Long songs, old songs, in languages I did not know and languages so old they were barely languages at all, only shapes of sound. He sang me the fall of Myr Tariniel, and the betrayal at Drossen Tor, and the death of Lyra, and Lanre's grief. He sang me Selitos weeping among the ruins. He sang me the Thief and his stolen moon, and the war that followed, and the sealing of the doors.

He sang them to me over and over, until I could sing them back to him. Until the melodies were in my bones and the words were in my blood. Until I could feel the shape of the story the way you feel the shape of your own hand in the dark.

And then he made me sing them myself.

---

"No," he said, on the third day. "No, no. You're singing it like a performance."

I stopped. I had been singing the fall of Myr Tariniel, standing in the center of the lighthouse with my lute in my hands, and I had thought I was singing it well. The notes were right. The words were right. My voice was strong and clear and full of feeling.

"What's wrong with that?"

"Everything." Skarpi was sitting on his stone by the fire, his long thin hands folded on his knees. "You're singing it to be heard. You're singing it to be applauded. You're thinking about the audience, about how they'll feel, about whether they'll weep in the right places." He shook his head. "That's not how the true song is sung."

"How is it sung, then?"

"The way you sang to the Sleeper," said Skarpi. "The way you sang to the king's hair."

I stared at him. I had not told him about either of those things.

"How do you—"

"I know what I know." He waved it away. "When you sang to the Sleeper, you weren't singing to be heard. You were singing to *him*. You were singing his own loneliness back to him, so that he could hear it and rest. And when you sang to the hair, you weren't performing. You were listening to what it wanted to say, and helping it say it." He leaned forward. "The true song is like that. It's not a story you tell *to* people. It's a story you tell *for* the story. You sing it so that it will be true. So that it will be remembered. So that it will hold."

I thought about that for a long time.

"I don't know how," I said at last.

"Yes you do. You've done it twice already. You just didn't know what you were doing." Skarpi smiled. "That's the trouble with you, Kvothe. You're always doing things before you know how. And then when you try to do them on purpose, you can't, because you're thinking too hard." He gestured at my lute. "Stop thinking. Sing it again. Not for me. Not for anyone. For Myr Tariniel. For the children in the fountains. Sing it so they'll be remembered."

I looked at him. Then I looked down at my lute. I thought about the children in the fountains, playing in the white city on the last day of its life. I thought about Skarpi, a little boy hiding under a slab of marble, listening to the screaming.

I began to sing.

And this time, when I finished, Skarpi did not say anything at all. He sat on his stone by the fire, with his hands folded on his knees and tears running down his face into his long white beard.

---

On the fifth day, he told me about the cost.

We were sitting on the rocks outside the lighthouse, watching the sea. It was a grey day, cold and windy, with low clouds scudding in from the west and the waves breaking white on the rocks below us. Skarpi had brought out the bread and water, and we were eating it slowly, looking at the horizon.

"When you sing the song," he said, "they will come."

"I know."

"No. You don't. You think you know, because they came for your parents. But your parents were singing a piece of it. A fragment. And they came quietly, in the night, in the woods, where no one would see." He looked at me. "You're going to sing the whole of it. In a great hall, before hundreds of people. And the song will be true, and strong, and it will go out into the world like a bell ringing. They will feel it. All of them. Wherever they are. And they will come, and they will not come quietly."

"Then I'll be ready."

"You can't be ready. No one can be ready." He shook his head. "But you can be prepared. That's different."

"How?"

He was quiet for a long time, watching the waves.

"Choose your place carefully," he said at last. "Somewhere with many people, so that the song is heard by many. But somewhere those people can escape, if they need to. Many doors. Open spaces. Somewhere the Seven cannot easily trap them." He paused. "And have friends near. People who know what is coming, and are ready to act."

"And me? What do I do, when they come?"

Skarpi looked at me. His bright eyes were very serious.

"You keep singing," he said. "No matter what. No matter who comes, or what they do, or what they say to you. You keep singing until the song is done. Because if you stop in the middle, the song is broken. And a broken song is worse than no song at all." He paused. "A broken song is a lie."

"And after?"

"After, you run." He smiled faintly. "You're good at that. I've heard."

---

On the seventh day, I asked him the question I had been carrying since the first night.

"Why me?" I said. "You've been alive for thousands of years. You've known hundreds of namers, thousands of singers. Why didn't you teach one of them? Why wait for me?"

Skarpi was silent for a long time. We were inside the lighthouse, by the fire, and night was falling outside.

"I did teach them," he said at last. "Many of them. Over many years. Some of them were very good. Better than you, some of them. Better voices, better hands, better hearts." He looked into the fire. "The Chandrian found them all. One by one. Sometimes before they ever sang a note. Sometimes after. But they found them."

"Why didn't they find me?"

"They did. Six years ago. In a forest near the edge of the Commonwealth." Skarpi looked at me. "But they let you live. Because they needed you. Because you are the key." He smiled, a small, bitter smile. "Do you see? It's the one protection no one else ever had. They cannot kill you. Not until you've opened the door. Haliax has forbidden it."

I sat very still.

"So I'm safe," I said slowly. "From them. As long as I don't open it."

"From death. Not from pain. Not from loss." Skarpi's voice was very quiet. "They'll kill everyone around you, Kvothe. Everyone you love. They'll burn everything you care about. They'll hurt you in every way a person can be hurt, short of killing you. Because they want you broken. They want you desperate. They want you to come to the door on your knees, begging for it to be over." He looked at me. "That's what they did to Lanre, you know. That's how the Thief turned him. Not by force. By grief. By taking everything he loved, one piece at a time, until he would do anything, anything at all, to make it stop."

The fire crackled. Outside, the sea breathed against the rocks.

"You're telling me this," I said, "and you still want me to sing."

"I'm telling you this because you should know," said Skarpi. "Because you deserve to know what it will cost. And because if you're going to do it anyway, you should do it with your eyes open." He paused. "Your mother didn't know, when she started. She thought it was a game. A clever song about an old story. By the time she understood, it was too late."

"Would she have stopped? If she'd known?"

Skarpi looked at me for a long time.

"No," he said. "No, I don't think she would have. She was very stubborn. Like you." He smiled. "But she would have been more careful. She would have hidden you better."

---

On the ninth day, I left.

Skarpi walked with me to the end of the rocky spit, where it met the harbor. The morning was clear and bright, and the sea was calm, and the gulls were wheeling and crying over the water.

"Where will you go?" he asked.

"Imre. The Eolian. It's the best place I know for a song to be heard. It has many doors. And I have friends there."

He nodded. "Good. That's good." He was quiet for a moment. "Kvothe. One more thing."

"Yes?"

"The girl. Denna." He looked at me. "She's singing her song too. All over Vintas, now. In the great halls. People are weeping at it. It's very beautiful, they say."

I did not say anything.

"She doesn't know what it is. Not all of it. She knows some of it now, I think. But she's bound, and she's frightened, and she's alone." Skarpi's voice was very gentle. "When you sing your song, she'll hear of it. And she'll come. Bredon will make her. Because a true song and a false song, sung against each other—" He shook his head. "That's a battle. And whichever song is stronger will win."

"Then mine has to be stronger."

"Yes." He looked at me with his bright old eyes. "But remember. She didn't choose this. She's not your enemy. She's a girl with a knot on her wrist, singing because she has to." He paused. "Don't sing against her, Kvothe. Sing for her. There's a difference."

I looked at him for a long moment. Then I nodded.

"I'll remember," I said.

"I know you will." He smiled. "You always remember. That's your gift. And your curse." He reached out and laid his long thin hand on my shoulder. "Go on, then. Go and sing. And when it's over, if you're still alive, come back and tell me how it went."

"Will you be here?"

"I'm always here. Or somewhere." His smile widened. "I'll find you. I always do."

He let go of my shoulder. I turned and began to walk along the harbor toward the piers, where the ships were loading for the morning tide.

When I looked back, a moment later, he was gone. The rocky spit was empty. The broken lighthouse stood alone at the end of it, black against the bright sea.

But on the wind, very faintly, I thought I heard someone singing. An old voice, rich and warm, singing an old song in a language so old it was barely a language at all.

It was the fall of Myr Tariniel. And it was being sung for the children in the fountains.
