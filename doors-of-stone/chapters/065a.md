# The Shepherd's Song

It was three days' walk through the twilight from Remmen's tree to the greystone he had told us of. Three days, as the Fae counts days, which is to say three long stretches of walking and three short stretches of sleep, beneath a sky that never changed.

Bast did not speak for the first of them.

I did not make him. I knew what Remmen had told him, in the round golden room at the heart of the tree, while I waited outside. I knew because I had asked Remmen to tell him. His mother had not left because of him. She had left because she was dying, slowly, the way mortals die in the twilight. She had gone back through a greystone into the mountains, and married a shepherd, and lived eleven more years. And then she had died. Twelve years ago. While Bast was still a child in his father's court, waiting for her to come home.

He walked beside me along the silver road with his hands in his pockets and his head down, and he did not say anything at all. And I walked beside him, and did not say anything either, and after a while I began to hum. Not a song. Just a hum. The kind of sound you make to fill a silence that is too heavy to carry.

He did not tell me to stop. So I did not.

---

On the second day, he spoke.

We had stopped to rest beside a stream. It was a Fae stream, which meant that the water ran uphill for a little way before it remembered itself, and that the stones in it were very slightly warm to the touch. We sat on the bank and ate the last of the bread from Remmen's kitchens and drank from the stream, which tasted of snow and violets.

"A shepherd," said Bast.

I looked at him. He was looking at the water.

"She married a shepherd," he said. "In the mountains. Father told me. He knew his name, even. He'd watched them. In the pool. For eleven years." He picked up a warm stone from the edge of the stream and turned it over in his fingers. "His name was Aled. He had a flock of black-faced sheep and a dog with one ear and a cottage with a turf roof. And he played the pipes. Every evening, sitting on the wall outside the cottage, while the sun went down." His voice cracked. "And she sang with him."

I did not say anything.

"She sang," said Bast. "Every night. For eleven years. Father said he used to watch her in the pool. Sitting on the wall beside the shepherd with her head on his shoulder. Singing." He threw the stone into the stream. It went in with a small plop, and the water ran uphill around it for a moment, confused. "She never sang at court. Not once. Not in all the years I knew her. Father said she used to, before I was born. But by the time I was old enough to remember, she'd stopped."

"Because she was fading."

"Because she was fading." Bast wrapped his arms around his knees. "She was fading for years, Reshi. The whole time I knew her. And I didn't know. I thought that was just what she was like. Quiet. Pale. Tired all the time." He was quiet for a long moment. "And then she went to the mountains. And she sang again."

---

I did not know what to say. I have never known what to say, at moments like that. I know a great many words. I know how to make them sing and dance and weep and laugh. But when someone I love is hurting, all my words go away, and I am left sitting beside them with nothing in my hands.

So I did what I always do, when I have nothing in my hands. I took out my lute.

Bast looked at it. Then at me.

"Don't," he said. "Don't play me something sad. I can't."

"I wasn't going to."

"Then what?"

I did not answer. I tuned the lute. My left hand was still stiff, from the Stormwal, from the cold. But it was better than it had been. It could hold a chord.

And I played him a shepherd's song.

---

I did not know any songs from the mountains at the edge of the Stormwal. I did not know what Aled had played on his pipes, sitting on the wall outside his turf-roofed cottage, with his black-faced sheep and his one-eared dog. But I knew shepherds' songs. The Ruh know every kind of song there is. And shepherds' songs are all a little the same, the way shepherds are all a little the same, wherever you go. Slow and simple and wandering, like sheep on a hillside. Songs for evenings. Songs for sitting on a wall while the sun goes down.

I played one I had learned as a child, from an old man in the hills above Ralien, who had played it on a pipe made of a hollow reed. It had no words. It did not need them. It was a song about sitting still, at the end of a long day, with someone you love beside you, and watching the light go.

Bast listened. He did not move. He sat on the bank of the uphill stream with his arms around his knees and his chin on his arms and his eyes closed, and he listened.

When I finished, he did not open his eyes.

"Again," he said.

I played it again.

---

The third time, he began to hum.

He did not hum well. Bast has a great many gifts, but singing is not one of them. His voice is too light, and it wanders, and it cannot hold a note for very long before it slides away sideways into something else. But he hummed. Softly. Along with the lute. Following the slow wandering line of the shepherd's song the way a child follows a parent along a path, a step behind, stumbling.

And after a while, his humming began to change.

It was the same song. But it was not. He had found something in it. Some turn of the melody, some small upward lift at the end of a phrase, that I had not played. That was not in the song as I had learned it. He hummed it over and over, that small lift, until I found it on the lute and played it with him.

And then he opened his eyes, and looked at me, and his face was wet.

"That's hers," he said. "That turn. At the end. That's how she used to hum. When I was very small. Before she stopped." His voice was barely a whisper. "I'd forgotten. I'd forgotten it completely. And you played the song and it came back."

---

We sat by the stream for a long time after that. I played the shepherd's song over and over, with Bast's mother's turn at the end of every phrase, and Bast hummed along, and sometimes he wept and sometimes he did not.

"She was happy," he said at last. "Wasn't she? In the mountains. With the shepherd. Singing."

"I think so."

"I'm glad." He wiped his face with the back of his hand. "I thought I'd be angry. When I found out. That she left us. That she was happy somewhere without me." He was quiet for a moment. "But I'm not. I'm glad. She sang again. For eleven years. That's more than she had at court."

"She'd have sung for you, too," I said. "If she could. If she'd been able to stay."

"I know." Bast looked at the lute in my lap. "Father said she asked about me. In the pool. She'd look into the stream behind the cottage, sometimes, and say my name. Hoping it would reach me." He swallowed. "It never did. The twilight's too far from the mountains."

"It reached you now."

Bast looked at me. For a long moment he did not say anything.

"Yes," he said at last. "I suppose it did."

---

We went on, after a while. Along the silver road, toward the greystone on the bare green hill. Bast walked beside me with his hands in his pockets, the way he had the day before. But his head was up now. And every so often, as we walked, he hummed. That small upward turn. His mother's turn. Over and over, very softly, under his breath, as if he were afraid he would forget it again.

"Reshi," he said, on the third day, when we could see the greystone at last, standing alone at the end of the road.

"Yes?"

"Thank you."

"For what?"

He did not answer at once. He looked at the greystone, and the mortal world waiting beyond it, and the war, and the Maer, and everything that was coming.

"For stepping in front of the arrows," he said at last. "And for the song." He looked at me. "I don't know which is bigger. I keep trying to work it out."

"The arrows," I said.

"No," said Bast. "I don't think so."

---

"He still hums it," the innkeeper said. "Did you know that?"

He looked at the kitchen door, where the sound of pots and pans had gone quiet.

"When he's cooking. When he's cleaning. When he thinks no one's listening. The shepherd's song, with his mother's turn at the end." He was quiet for a moment. "He hummed it every day, for seven years. In the kitchen of the Waystone. While I sat behind the bar and didn't remember anything. I heard it every day. I didn't know what it was."

Chronicler did not write. He waited.

"It was one of the first things that came back, these last three days, while I was telling you the rest," said the innkeeper. "Not the song. I knew the song. But the turn. The little lift at the end of the phrase. And a stream that ran uphill, and a boy with wet eyes saying *that's hers*." He looked down at his hands. "I think he hummed it for me, all those years. The way she hummed it for him. Hoping it would reach me."

"Did it?"

"Eventually." The innkeeper almost smiled. "The twilight's a long way from anywhere. But it reached me."
