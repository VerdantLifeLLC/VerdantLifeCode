# Fire in the Horse Lines

I did not know, until much later, what Devi had made.

She told me afterward, sitting by a fire in the Maer's valley with her spectacles pushed up on her forehead and a cup of Mirra's terrible tea in her hands. She had spent the whole road south from Imre working on it, in the back of a wagon, with a portable alchemical bench she had built herself from a traveling chest and a great many stolen glass tubes. She had not slept much. She had burned her fingers a dozen times. She had nearly blown up the wagon twice.

"It's a kind of firework," she said. "Only bigger. Much bigger. And louder. And with a delay." She smiled her spider's smile. "I call it the Surprise."

She had made nine of them. Small clay pots, sealed with wax, each about the size of a man's fist. Inside each pot there were two chambers, separated by a thin wall of something that dissolved slowly when it was wet. When you broke the seal and poured in a little water, the wall began to dissolve. And when it was gone, and the two chambers mixed—

"Well," said Devi. "You saw."

---

I saw.

The first one went off in the horse lines, on the far side of the camp. I did not see the pot. I only saw the light. A great blossom of white fire, rising up into the night sky with a sound like the world cracking open. It was so bright that for a moment the whole valley was lit up like noon, every tent and banner and soldier thrown into sharp black silhouette.

The second one went off a heartbeat later, near the wagon park. Then the third, by the smithy. Then the fourth and fifth and sixth, one after the other, all around the edges of the camp, until the night was full of white fire and thunder.

And the horses screamed.

There were thousands of them in the horse lines. Cavalry mounts and draft horses and pack mules. They had been dozing in the dark, tethered in long rows, and suddenly the world had exploded around them in fire and noise. They went mad. They reared and plunged and screamed. They tore at their tethers. And the tethers broke.

They poured out of the horse lines like a river bursting its banks. Thousands of them, stampeding through the camp in the dark, maddened with terror. They trampled tents and overturned wagons and scattered cookfires. Soldiers ran shouting in every direction. Some of them were trampled. Some of them were knocked into the river. Some of them simply stood and stared.

The seventh Surprise went off in the center of the camp, not fifty yards from the king's pavilion. The eighth went off beside it. The light was blinding. The noise was like being struck by a hammer.

And through it all, the ninth pot sat quietly in a patch of grass at the edge of the firelight, near the Ruh wagons, and did nothing at all.

"That one was for smoke," Devi told me later. "Just smoke. Lots of it. I put it there for you, so you'd have cover." She sighed. "It was a dud. I don't know why. I think the water didn't get in properly."

"It's all right," I said. "The others were enough."

"They were, weren't they?" She looked very pleased with herself. "They really were."

---

In the chaos, I ran.

Not away. Toward. Toward the black and silver pavilion, toward the overturned carved chair, toward Ambrose.

He was standing where his chair had been, with his sword half drawn and his face white in the flashing light. His guards were scattered around him, some of them knocked down by the stampede, some of them running after the horses, some of them standing and staring at the fire in the sky. The lords and ladies of his court were fleeing in every direction, screaming, their fine clothes flapping.

And the box was on his chest. Hanging from its silver chain. Humming.

I felt it call to me. I felt it know me. I ran toward it.

A guard stepped into my way. A big man in black armor, with a halberd. He swung it at me, a great sweeping blow meant to take my legs out from under me.

I did not think. My body moved. It moved the way Vashet had taught it to move, on the hillside at Haert, a thousand times, until the movements were in my bones. *Tree Falling*. *Breaking Lion*. *Water Flows Around*.

I was inside the halberd's swing before it could reach me. I was past the guard before he knew I was there. He stumbled, overbalanced, and fell.

Another guard. Another. I went through them the way water goes through rocks in a stream. I did not draw Caesura. I did not need to. My body knew what to do, even with one arm cold and weak. It had been waiting to do this for a long time.

And then I was in front of Ambrose.

---

He had his sword out now. A fine long blade, with a jeweled hilt and a gleaming edge. He held it in front of him the way a man holds a sword who has been taught by the best masters money can buy, and has never had to use it in earnest.

His face was white. His eyes were wide. The light of Devi's fire flashed across them, white and gold.

"You," he said. His voice cracked. "You."

"Hello, Ambrose."

"*King* Ambrose." He tried to sneer. It did not quite work. "You're under arrest. For murder. For treason. For—for—"

"For what? For singing?"

His sword wavered. "Stay back. I'll kill you. I swear I'll kill you."

"No," I said. "You won't. You can't. You need me." I took a step toward him. "Don't you? That's what he told you. The old man. You need me alive, to open the door."

Something moved in his face. Something I had never seen there before.

"How do you know about the door?"

"I know everything, Ambrose. I always have. That's why you hate me." I took another step. "Give me the box."

"No."

"Give me the box, and I'll let you walk away. Right now. You can go back to Renere and sit on your throne and be king, and I'll never come near you again." I held out my hand. "Just give me the box."

He stared at me. At my hand, held out. At my face, lit by the flashing white fire.

For a moment—just a moment—I thought he would do it. I saw it in his eyes. The fear. The confusion. The part of him that had never wanted any of this, that had only ever wanted to be rich and important and admired, and had not understood what it was getting into.

Then his face hardened.

"No," he said. "It's mine. It's *mine*. He gave it to me. He said I was the only one who could carry it. He said I was *special*." His voice rose. "He said I was of the *line*!"

"You're a drop," I said. "A single drop, from four hundred years ago. He's using you, Ambrose. He's always been using you. The way he used Denna. The way he used everyone."

"*Liar!*"

He lunged at me.

---

It was not a good lunge. It was a lunge made in fear and rage, wild and overextended. I stepped aside and let it pass me, and as it passed, I reached out with my good hand and took hold of the silver chain around his neck.

And the box sang.

I felt it the moment my fingers touched the chain. A great surge of sound, rising up out of the box and into my hand and up my arm and into my chest. Its long slow *no*, suddenly very loud. And beneath it, braided through it, the name of my family. Of the line. Of my mother, and her mother, and all the mothers before, back and back to a woman in the hills who had loved a shaper for a little while.

It knew me. It knew me the way a dog knows its master's hand.

And beneath that, fainter, something else. A voice. Far away. Very deep. Very old. Saying my name.

Not my called name. The other one. The true one.

*Come*, it said. *Come to the door.*

I felt my fingers tighten on the chain. I felt myself begin to pull.

And then a hand closed around my wrist.

---

It was cold.

Colder than anything. The cold of the space between the stars. It went into my wrist and up my arm, and my fingers went numb on the silver chain, and they opened.

The chain slipped out of my grasp. The box swung back against Ambrose's chest.

I turned my head.

Cinder was standing beside me. He was holding my wrist. My good wrist, this time. The right one. His black eyes were looking into mine, and his face was very close, and he was smiling.

"No," he said softly. "Not here. Not yet."

"Let go of me."

"I can't touch you, little key. You know that. The lord forbade it." His smile widened. "But I can stop you. That's different." He tightened his grip. The cold went deeper, into my bones. "You'll have your chance. At the door. Not before."

"*Let go.*"

"Or what?" He tilted his head. "You'll call the wind? You'll sing at me? You'll hum your little song to the stones?" He laughed softly. "Go on. Try."

I opened my mouth.

And something struck Cinder from the side, very fast and very hard, and knocked him away from me.

---

It was Tempi.

He came out of the dark and the smoke and the flashing fire like a red arrow, and he hit Cinder with his shoulder, and Cinder staggered. His hand came away from my wrist. I felt the cold begin to drain out of my arm.

Tempi did not stop. He moved. He moved the way the Adem move, the way I had seen him move a hundred times on the practice grounds, fluid and swift and utterly without waste. He struck at Cinder. Once, twice, three times. His hands were a blur.

Cinder was faster.

I had never seen anything move as fast as Cinder moved in that moment. He was simply *there*, and then he was somewhere else, and then he was behind Tempi, and there was a sword in his hand. A long pale sword, the color of ice.

"Tempi!" I shouted.

Tempi turned. He saw the sword. His face did not change. Adem faces never change.

But his hands moved. Just for a moment. As the sword came down.

*Go*, they said.

---

I do not remember what happened next. Not clearly. I remember the sword coming down. I remember Tempi falling. I remember a sound that I think was me, screaming.

I remember Bast. His hands on my arms, pulling me away. His voice in my ear, shouting. *Reshi, come on, come on, we have to go, Reshi, please*.

I remember Ambrose, standing where I had left him, with the box on his chest and his sword in his hand and his face white as bone. Staring at me.

I remember Cinder, standing over Tempi's body, with the ice-pale sword in his hand. Looking at me. Smiling.

And I remember Bredon. Standing at the door of the plain black tent, a little way off, leaning on his stick. Watching it all with his mild, kind, interested face.

He raised his stick an inch off the ground. His small courtly salute.

And then Bast pulled me into the dark and the smoke, and I did not see anything else.

---

We ran. Through the burning camp, through the stampeding horses and the shouting soldiers and the smoke. Bast's glamour was wrapped around us both, tight and thin and flickering, and people looked at us and saw nothing, or saw only two more shadows fleeing the fire.

At the edge of the camp, where the river bent around a stand of willows, the others were waiting.

Vashet was there. And the other Adem, ten of them, standing in a ring. And in the center of the ring, sitting on the ground with her back against a willow trunk, there was a woman in a pale grey robe, with dark hair and a thin white face.

She was holding a baby.

"Kvothe," said Meluan. Her voice was a thread. "Kvothe. You came."

I fell to my knees beside her. I could not speak. I looked at her, and at the baby in her arms. A tiny thing, wrapped in a grey blanket. Asleep. Its face was red and crumpled and very small.

"Her name is Netalia," said Meluan. She was crying. "I named her Netalia. After my sister. After your mother." She looked at me. "I hope that's all right."

I could not answer. I put my hand very gently on the baby's head. Her hair was soft and fine and dark. She did not wake.

"Tempi," said Vashet. Her voice was very flat. "Where is Tempi?"

I looked up at her. I did not say anything. I did not need to.

Her face did not change. Adem faces never change. But her hands, at her sides, went very still.

Then they moved. One small movement. A sign I had seen only once before, on a hillside in Ademre, when an old woman had died.

It meant *grief*.

---

We could not rest. Not there. The camp behind us was still burning, still roaring with the shouts of soldiers and the screams of horses, but it would not stay that way for long. They would come looking for us. For Meluan. For the baby. For me.

"We have to go," said Vashet. "Now. Before they organize."

"She can't walk," I said. I looked at Meluan. Her face was grey. Her hands, holding the baby, were shaking. "She had the child eleven days ago. She's too weak."

"Then we carry her."

Two of the Adem came forward and lifted Meluan between them, very gently, the way you lift something fragile. She did not protest. She only held the baby tighter against her chest.

We ran. Into the dark, away from the burning camp, along the riverbank and then up into the hills. The Adem carried Meluan. Bast ran beside me, his glamour flickering. I ran with my hand on Caesura's hilt and my heart pounding and Tempi's last sign burned into the backs of my eyes.

*Go.*

---

They caught us at the top of the first ridge.

Not soldiers. Not guards. Something else. Something that came out of the dark ahead of us, standing on the crest of the ridge against the starlight, very tall and very still.

A shadow. Shaped like a man. With no man inside it.

We stopped. All of us, at once. The Adem set Meluan down on the grass and drew their swords and formed a ring around her. Vashet stood at the front of the ring, with her sword in her hand and her face showing nothing at all.

The shadow did not move.

"Singer," said Haliax.

His voice came from everywhere and nowhere. Deep and quiet and very tired.

"You have taken something from us," he said. "The child. The lady."

"They're not yours," I said. "They never were."

"No." The shadow was quiet for a moment. "No, I suppose not." It seemed to look past me, at Meluan on the grass, with the baby in her arms. "The child is very small. Very new. Her name is not yet shaped." A pause. "It would not have worked, I think. Not with her. Not truly. The old man thought it might. I did not."

"Then why did you take her?"

"To bring you." The shadow turned back to me. "As it did. As he knew it would."

I did not say anything.

"Keep the child," said Haliax. "She is nothing to me. But the lady—" The shadow seemed to shift. "The lady has the blood too. Older. Stronger. Shaped by many years. She is not a namer, as you are. But she is enough to wake the door. Enough to crack it. Enough to let a little of what is behind it through." A pause. "Enough to make you come."

"No."

"Yes." The shadow did not raise its voice. It did not need to. "Ferule."

---

He was behind us. I had not seen him come. None of us had. He was standing among the Adem, inside their ring, beside Meluan. His ice-pale sword was in his hand. His black eyes were on me.

"Lady," he said. He bowed, very slightly, to Meluan. "You will come with me."

"No," said Meluan. She held the baby against her chest. "No. No."

"You will come," said Cinder. "Or the child will not live to see the morning." He looked down at the baby in her arms. "I cannot touch the key. But I can touch *her*."

The Adem moved. All ten of them at once, swords flashing. They moved toward Cinder like a closing fist.

Cinder did not move. He only smiled.

And the ground beneath the Adem went cold. Frost bloomed out from Cinder's feet across the grass, white and crackling, faster than a man could run. It crept up the Adem's boots. Their legs. They stopped moving. Their swords hung in the air, frozen in mid-swing. Their breath plumed white.

They were not dead. I could see their eyes moving. But they could not move anything else.

Only Vashet was still free. She had been standing at the front of the ring, beside me, beyond the reach of the frost. She stood very still, with her sword in her hand, and looked at Cinder.

Cinder looked back at her.

"You are the teacher," he said. "The one who taught him the Ketan." He smiled. "I killed your friend tonight. The quiet one. He was very good. Better than you, I think."

Vashet did not answer. Her face showed nothing.

But her hands moved. One small, sharp gesture. I had never seen it before.

It meant *I will remember you*.

---

"Stop," said Meluan.

She was standing up. I do not know how. She had been too weak to walk a moment before. But she was standing, with the baby in her arms, in the center of the frozen ring of Adem. Her face was white as bone. Her eyes were very steady.

"Stop," she said again. "I'll come."

"Meluan, no—"

"I'll come." She turned and looked at me. Her face was calm. Her eyes were wet. "Take her. Please. Take her and keep her safe."

She held out the baby.

I took her. I do not remember deciding to. I only remember that one moment Meluan was holding the baby out to me, and the next I was holding her against my chest, small and warm and asleep, wrapped in her grey blanket.

"Keep her safe," Meluan whispered. "The way your mother kept you. Hide her. Wrap her name. Don't let them find her." She reached out and touched my face. Her fingers were ice cold. "She's the last. After you. After me. She's the last of us."

"I'll get you back," I said. My voice cracked. "I swear. I'll come to the door. I'll get you back."

Meluan looked at me for a long moment.

"I know you will," she said softly. "That's what they're counting on."

She turned away. She walked out of the frozen ring, past Cinder, toward the shadow on the ridge. Cinder followed her, with his ice-pale sword.

At the crest of the ridge, the shadow waited. When Meluan reached it, it seemed to open, like a cloak. She stepped into it.

And then they were gone. All of them. The shadow, and Cinder, and Meluan. Gone into the dark, as if they had never been there.

The frost on the grass began to melt. The Adem stumbled forward, gasping, as their limbs came free.

I stood on the ridge in the starlight with my aunt's daughter in my arms, and I did not move for a long time.
