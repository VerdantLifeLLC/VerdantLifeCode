# What the Fae Know About Mortals

Bast talked. That was the first thing I learned about him, on the Moon's Road, in the days after the Sithe let us go. He talked the way a stream talks, all the time, about everything, without ever seeming to need a reply.

He told me about the Twilight Court. About his father's house, grown out of a tree so old it had been a sapling when the Thief was still a man. About his thirteen brothers and sisters, most of whom he did not like, and the two he did. About the dark river that ran through the valley, and the lanterns, and the bridges of living vine. About a girl at his father's court with fox-red hair who had broken his heart when he was very young, and another girl with eyes like a cat who had broken it again, and a third, whose name he would not say, who had broken it so thoroughly he was still picking up the pieces.

"You fall in love a great deal," I said.

"All the time." He did not seem embarrassed. "It's the mortal blood. Makes us soft. The Sithe don't fall in love. Felurian doesn't, not really. She falls in *interest*." He grinned. "The Telwyth Mael fall in love the way mortals catch colds. Constantly, and with a great deal of sneezing."

I laughed. He looked pleased.

---

He asked questions, too. Hundreds of them. About the mortal world, mostly. What it was like, having a sun that went up and down. Whether it was true that mortals ate cows. Whether it was true that mortal women had to carry their babies for nine whole months.

"Yes," I said.

"*Nine months?*" He was horrified. "Inside them? The whole time?"

"Yes."

"That's barbaric." He shook his head. "My mother told me, but I thought she was joking. She had a very strange sense of humor." He was quiet for a moment. "She carried me for nine months. In the twilight. It's supposed to be three, for us. She said I was very stubborn and wouldn't come out." He smiled faintly. "She said I've been stubborn ever since."

"Have you?"

"Terribly." He looked at me sidelong. "So are you. I can tell. We're going to argue a great deal, Reshi."

"Don't call me that."

"Why not? It's what you are."

"I'm not anyone's master."

"Teacher, then." He shrugged. "Or fool who steps in front of arrows. Pick one. But you're something, and I'm going to call you something, and Reshi is the only word I know that fits." He grinned again. "You'll get used to it."

I did not think I would. I was wrong about that, as I was wrong about a great many things that year.

---

He did not know anything about music. That surprised me more than anything.

"We have music," he said, when I asked. "Of course we have music. The whole twilight is music, if you listen. The trees sing. The rivers sing. The wind sings." He frowned. "But we don't *make* it. Not the way you do. Not with wood and string and fingers." He looked at my lute case with deep suspicion. "Felurian likes it. Mortal music. She says it's the only thing mortals make that's worth the trouble of stealing them."

"She said that?"

"She says it about you." He grinned. "Everyone in the twilight knows about you, you know. The mortal who went to Felurian's glade and came out alive, with a shaed and his wits. They sing about you at my father's court." He paused. "Badly. They don't know the tune."

"There's a song about me?"

"There's a dozen." Bast looked at me. "Didn't you know? You're famous. In the twilight. More famous than in your world, probably." He tilted his head. "Most of them are rude."

---

So I taught him "Tinker Tanner."

We had sung it past the Grove, of course, to drown out the Cthaeh. But he had only sung the chorus then, and nonsense syllables for the verses. Now, walking through the grey-green hills with the silver string floating ahead of us, I taught him the verses. All of them. Every one I knew.

He was a terrible student. He forgot the words. He sang the wrong tune. He made up his own verses when he could not remember mine, and they were worse than mine, which is saying something. He laughed so hard at the verse about the tinker and the three sisters from Tinuë that he had to sit down in the grass.

But he learned. By the end of the second day, he could sing forty verses without a mistake. By the end of the third, he was making up new ones that were, I had to admit, very nearly as rude as the old ones.

"This is the best song in the world," he said, lying on his back in the grey-green grass with his hooves in the air. "The absolute best. Why didn't anyone tell me mortals had songs like this?"

"It's a children's song."

"Then mortal children are much wiser than mortal grown-ups." He rolled over and looked at me. "Teach me another."

I taught him another. And another. By the time we reached the Northern Gate, he knew thirty songs, and he sang them all, loudly and badly, at every opportunity.

I have never had a better student. Or a worse one.

---

On the last night before the Gate, we sat by a small fire in a hollow of silver-barked trees, and Bast asked me the question I had been expecting since the birch wood.

"Why did the Cthaeh talk to you?" he said. "The first time. When you went to it. What did it want?"

I did not answer right away. I looked into the fire.

"It wanted me to go looking for someone," I said at last. "It told me things about a girl I love. About the man who keeps her. Things that would make me angry. Things that would make me go and find him, and do something about it."

"And did you?"

"Not yet."

Bast was quiet for a long time.

"It didn't want you to find him," he said at last. "Not really. It wanted you to find something else. Along the way." He looked at me across the fire. "That's how it works. It doesn't send you where it wants you. It sends you somewhere else, and lets the road take you where it wants." He shivered. "My father told me that. When I was small. He said the Cthaeh is like a river. It doesn't push you. It just makes the ground slope."

I looked at him for a long time.

"And the road took me here," I said slowly. "To the Moon's Road. Past the Grove. To you."

Bast went very still.

"I didn't think of that," he said.

"Neither did I."

We sat by the fire, the two of us, and looked at each other, and I think we were both thinking the same thing. That perhaps the Cthaeh had wanted us to meet. That perhaps everything that happened after—every choice, every kindness, every step we took together—was a step down a slope it had made.

"Does it matter?" said Bast at last. His voice was very small. "If it did? If it wanted us to be here?"

I thought about Vashet. About the Lethani. About choosing, and bearing what comes after.

"No," I said. "It doesn't matter. I stepped in front of those arrows because I chose to. Whatever the Cthaeh wanted. Whatever slope it made." I looked at him. "And you're here because you chose to follow. Didn't you?"

Bast looked at me for a long moment. His deep blue eyes were very bright in the firelight.

"Yes," he said. "Yes. I chose."

"Then that's what matters."

He was quiet for a while. Then he smiled. A small, crooked, relieved smile.

"You'd make a terrible Fae," he said. "You believe in choosing too much."

"I'm not Fae."

"No." He lay back in the grass and looked up at the silver trees. "No, you're something much stranger than that." He was quiet for a moment. "Good night, Reshi."

"Don't call me that."

"Good night, Reshi," he said again, and closed his eyes, and was asleep in a moment, the way the young can sleep.

I sat by the fire and watched him for a long time.

I did not know, then, what the Cthaeh had said to him in the net. I would not know for many years. Not until a cold night at a table by a hearth, with a scribe writing it all down.

But I think I felt it, even then. The weight of it. Something he was carrying, that he would not put down.

And I thought: whatever it is, I'll help him carry it. Whatever it costs.

It was a promise. I did not say it aloud. But I kept it.

He kept his too.
