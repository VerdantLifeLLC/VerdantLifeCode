# The Fair at Tinuë Crossing

Bast tried very hard, that summer, to make me laugh.

I did not know that was what he was doing. I did not know much, then. We traveled through the Commonwealth with the cart and the chest and the mule, from town to town, from inn to inn, looking for somewhere quiet, and Bast talked. He talked all day long. He told jokes and stories and outrageous lies. He sang songs, badly. He made faces at me when I was not looking and then, when I looked, pretended he had not.

I did not laugh. I did not feel anything. I sat on the seat of the cart beside him and watched the road go by, and when he told a joke, I looked at him and said, "Yes," and he would sigh, and look away, and a while later he would try again.

He never stopped trying. I think that is the thing about Bast I most want you to understand. In all those years, the grey years on the road and the greyer ones behind the bar, he never once stopped trying.

---

In the middle of summer, we came to a town called Tinuë Crossing, and there was a fair.

It was not a large fair. A midsummer market, the kind every town of any size in the Commonwealth holds, in a field outside the walls, with tents and stalls and a great deal of noise. Cheese sellers and cloth merchants and tinkers, a man selling hot pies from a barrow, a woman with a dancing bear that did not dance so much as sway, a pair of jugglers, a puppet show. Children running everywhere, underfoot, shrieking.

Bast's eyes lit up when he saw it.

"Reshi," he said. "We're stopping."

"We need to go on," I said. It was the kind of thing I said, then. Not because I wanted to go on. Because going on was what we were doing, and I did not know how to want anything else.

"We're stopping," said Bast firmly. He was already pulling the cart off the road, into the field, among the other carts and wagons. "Patience needs rest. I need pie. And you—" He looked at me. "You need to see something."

---

He took me by the arm and led me through the fair. I let him. I let him lead me past the cheese sellers and the cloth merchants and the man with the pies, from whom Bast bought two, and handed me one, and watched me eat it without tasting it, and sighed. I let him lead me past the dancing bear and the jugglers.

He stopped in front of the puppet show.

It was a small one. A painted booth, red and gold and faded, with a little stage at the top and a curtain across the front. A crowd of children sat on the grass in front of it, cross-legged, staring up. And on the stage, two puppets were fighting with little wooden swords.

"Watch," said Bast.

I watched.

---

The puppets were very old. You could see that, even from the back of the crowd. Their paint was chipped and their clothes were faded and patched. But the puppeteer, whoever was behind the curtain, was very good. The little wooden figures moved as if they were alive. They lunged and parried and ducked and spun, and their tiny swords clacked together, and the children shrieked with delight.

One of the puppets had red hair.

I looked at it. It was a small wooden man with a shock of bright red yarn for hair and a black painted cloak. It had a little wooden lute on its back. It was fighting a much larger puppet, a great hulking figure with a crown on its head and a painted face twisted into a snarl.

"And lo!" cried a voice from behind the curtain. A high, cracked, old man's voice, full of theatrical relish. "The wicked king of Vintas raises his sword! Will the brave hero fall?"

"*No!*" shrieked the children.

"Will he flee?"

"*No!*"

"Will he—*sing?*"

"*Yes!*"

And the little red-haired puppet swung the lute off its back and began to play. The puppeteer made a jangling sound behind the curtain, a terrible out-of-tune strumming, and the children laughed and laughed. And the big crowned puppet clutched its ears and staggered and reeled, and fell over backward off the stage with a clatter.

The children cheered.

---

I stood at the back of the crowd and looked at the little red-haired puppet, bowing on the stage.

I did not know who it was meant to be. I want to be honest about that. I knew my name was Kvothe, because Denna had told me. I knew I had done things, because people had told me. But I did not know what those things were, not really, not in any way that meant anything. The name *Kvothe* was just a word to me, then. Like *Tuesday*, or *salt*.

So I did not know that the little red-haired puppet was me. That the big crowned one was Ambrose. That the story the old man was telling, in his cracked theatrical voice, was a story that had spread through the Four Corners in the year since Renere, growing and changing as it went, until it had become a thing that puppeteers told to children at midsummer fairs.

I did not know. I only looked at it.

And Bast, beside me, was looking at me.

---

"The Kingkiller!" cried the puppeteer. "The Bloodless! Kvothe the Arcane, who sang the king to death!" The little red-haired puppet bowed again. The children cheered again. "And do you know what happened to him, children? Do you know where he went?"

"*No!*"

"Nobody knows!" The puppeteer's voice dropped to a whisper. The children leaned forward. "Some say they hanged him, in Renere, in the great square. Some say he escaped, and went into the Fae, and married a faerie queen. And some say—" He paused. The children held their breath. "Some say he's still out there. Wandering the roads. In disguise. And that he might be anywhere." The little puppet turned, very slowly, and looked out over the crowd. "He might be in *this very field!*"

The children shrieked and looked around at each other, delighted and terrified.

And the little wooden puppet, with its chipped paint and its red yarn hair, looked straight at me.

---

It was an accident. It must have been. The puppeteer could not see the crowd from behind his curtain. He had turned the puppet at random. It happened to be facing my way.

But I looked at it. And it looked at me. And for a long moment, standing at the back of that crowd of shrieking children in a field outside Tinuë Crossing, with the smell of hot pies and trampled grass all around me, I felt something.

It was very small. It was not recognition. It was not memory. It was more like the feeling you get when you walk into a room and know, without knowing how, that someone has just left it. A warmth in the air. A shape in the dust.

And I heard myself make a sound.

It was not a laugh. Not really. It was a small, dry, surprised exhalation, through my nose. The kind of sound you make when something is absurd. When you see a little wooden man with red yarn hair, playing a toy lute to make a king fall over, and some small forgotten part of you thinks: *that's ridiculous. It wasn't like that at all.*

It was the closest thing to a laugh that came out of me for seven years.

---

Bast heard it.

He went absolutely still beside me. I felt him go still. And then, very slowly, he turned his head and looked at me, and his face was doing something I had never seen it do before. It was crumpling. Slowly, from the eyes down, the way paper crumples when it gets wet.

"Reshi," he said. His voice was a whisper. "Reshi. Did you—"

But it was gone. Whatever it had been. The warmth in the air, the shape in the dust. Gone. And I was standing at the back of a crowd, looking at a puppet show, and I did not feel anything at all.

"Did I what?" I said.

Bast looked at me for a long moment. His eyes were very bright and very wet.

"Nothing," he said at last. "Nothing, Reshi." He wiped his face with the back of his hand. "Come on. Let's go and get another pie."

---

He did not talk much, the rest of that day. He bought us both another pie, and we ate them sitting on the tailboard of the cart, watching the fair. He did not tell any jokes. He did not make any faces. He sat beside me in silence and ate his pie and watched the children running past.

But that night, camped at the edge of the field, when he thought I was asleep, I heard him crying. Very quietly, wrapped in his blanket on the other side of the fire. Not sad crying. The other kind. The kind that comes after a long time of holding on, when you find out that what you were holding on to is still there.

I did not know why he was crying. I lay in my blanket and looked at the stars and did not understand.

I understand now.

---

"You laughed," said Chronicler. He had stopped writing. "At a puppet of yourself."

"I snorted." The innkeeper's mouth twitched. "There's a difference."

"Bast told me," said Chronicler slowly, "on the first night. When he came to my room. He said you hadn't laughed in seven years." He looked at Bast. "Was that—"

"It doesn't count," said Bast. He was not looking at either of them. He was looking at the fire. "It was a snort. He's right. It doesn't count." He was quiet for a moment. "But I've been waiting for the next one ever since."

The innkeeper looked at him for a long moment. Something moved across his face. Something warm, and sad, and very fond.

"Bast," he said.

"Yes, Reshi?"

"It really wasn't like that. With Ambrose. At all."

Bast looked up. Their eyes met. And then, slowly, the corner of Bast's mouth began to curl.

"What, no toy lute?" he said.

"No toy lute."

"He didn't clutch his ears and fall off the stage?"

"He did not fall off the stage."

"Pity," said Bast. "It was the best part."

The innkeeper did not laugh. But something happened at the corner of his mouth. Something small and crooked and brief, there and gone again, like a fish turning once in dark water.

It was not a smile. Not quite. But it was the shape a smile leaves behind, when it has been somewhere recently.

Bast saw it. He did not say anything. He did not have to. He sat in his backward chair with his arms folded across its back, and looked at the innkeeper, and his eyes were very bright.

Chronicler, very quietly, picked up his pen and wrote it down.
