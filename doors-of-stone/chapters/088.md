# A Fire Far from Renere

It was on the road to Imre, somewhere in the Commonwealth hills, that Denna told me about the nightingale.

I have told you that part already. The duke's feast, and the black silk dress, and the true song sung in the great hall. I put it where it belonged, in the middle of the grey, because it was one of the bright things. But I did not hear it until later. Until that night. Beside a fire, in a hollow between two hills, far from Renere.

The troupe had stopped for the night in a sheltered place off the road, where a stand of old oaks grew around a spring. The wagons were drawn up in a half circle. The horses were hobbled and grazing. Mirra was cooking something in a great iron pot that smelled of onions and rabbit, and Brin was sawing at his fiddle, and Nell was chasing Kellen's youngest around the wagons, shrieking.

Denna and I sat a little apart, at the edge of the firelight, under one of the oaks. She had built a small fire of our own, just for the two of us. She said the big fire was too loud. She said she wanted to talk to me, and she wanted it to be quiet.

So we sat by our small fire, and she held my hand, and she talked.

---

She told me about the duke, and the feast, and the song. She told me about singing Ambrose with love. She told me about the duke weeping at his high table, and walking out alone, and never threatening her again.

I listened. I did not understand all of it. I did not remember the duke, or Ambrose, or the song. But I listened, and when she was done, I asked her why.

"Why what?"

"Why you did it. You knew he could have killed you."

She told me. I have told you what she said. *Because it was true. I owed the truth something. After that. I owed it a song.*

And then she was quiet for a long time, looking into the fire.

"Can I tell you something else?" she said at last. "Something I've never told anyone?"

"Yes."

She did not speak right away. She sat with her knees drawn up and her arms around them, the way she always sat when she was uncomfortable, and she looked into the small fire.

"My name," she said. "My real one. Not Denna, or Dianne, or Dinael, or any of the others. The one I was born with."

I waited.

"I don't know it," she said.

---

She told me the rest slowly. In pieces. The way you tell something you have kept locked away for a very long time.

She did not know where she had been born. She did not know who her parents were. Her earliest memory was of a ditch, beside a road, in the rain. She had been four, perhaps. Or five. She had been cold and hungry and alone, and she did not know how she had got there.

A woman had found her. A peddler, traveling the roads with a cart full of pots and pans. The woman had taken her in, and fed her, and called her *girl*, because she did not know what else to call her. They had traveled together for three years. Then the woman had died, of a fever, in a town whose name Denna did not remember. And Denna had been alone again.

"I made up a name," she said. "That first night, after she died. I was eight, I think. Sitting in a stable, in the straw, with the horses. I thought, I have to be called something. Everyone's called something." She smiled faintly. "So I called myself Dinnah. After a horse. There was a horse in the stable called Dinnah, and she was very beautiful, and very gentle, and she let me sleep against her side."

"A horse."

"A horse." Denna laughed softly. "And the next town I went to, I was someone else. And the next. And the next. I changed my name every time I changed towns. Because I didn't have a real one. Because none of them were me." She was quiet for a moment. "Because if I had a name, someone could find me. Someone could hold on to me. And every time anyone had ever held on to me, they'd let go."

I did not say anything. I held her hand.

"And then Bredon found me," she said. "When I was fourteen. In a tavern in Anilin, singing for coppers. He came up to me afterward and said I had a remarkable voice, and that he could help me. And I believed him." She looked at the fire. "He gave me lessons. He gave me clothes. He gave me a place to live. And one night, a few months after he found me, he sat me down and told me he was going to teach me something very special."

"The knots."

"The knots." She nodded. "He said the Yllish knots weren't just writing. He said they could hold things. Names, and promises, and stories. He said that if I learned them well enough, I could tie my own name into a cord, and then I'd always have it, and no one could ever take it away." Her voice was very quiet. "I wanted that more than anything. More than food, more than music. A name that was mine. That I could hold."

"Did he teach you?"

"He taught me." She looked down at her hands. "And then, when I'd learned enough, he helped me tie it. My name. The one I didn't know. He said the knots would find it, if I tied them right. He said they'd reach down inside me and pull it out and hold it, so I could see it."

"And did they?"

Denna was quiet for a very long time.

"Yes," she said at last. "They did. I tied the cord, and I felt it. Something reaching down, and catching hold, and pulling. And there it was. My name. My real one. In the knots." She looked at me. Her eyes were wet. "And then he took it."

---

I sat very still.

"He took it," said Denna. "The cord. With my name in it. He said he'd keep it safe for me. He said I wasn't ready to carry it yet. He said I'd lose it, or someone would steal it, or I'd use it wrong." She laughed, a small broken sound. "And I believed him. I was fourteen. I believed everything he said."

"And then he tied the knot on your wrist."

"The same night. With a piece of the same cord." She held up her wrist. The knot was gone now, fallen off in the chamber when Bredon went through the door. But there was a mark where it had been. A thin pale line around her wrist, like a bracelet. Like the mark on mine would be, someday. "He said it was a promise. That I'd serve him, and he'd keep my name safe. And as long as I kept my promise, he'd keep his." She lowered her hand. "It was my own name, Kvothe. Tied around my own wrist. That was what held me. That was what he pulled on, all those years. Not his power. Mine."

I looked at her for a long time.

"Where is it now?" I said. "The cord. With your name."

Denna looked into the fire.

"I don't know," she said. "He always kept it with him. In a pocket, or a pouch, or in the head of his stick. I never saw where." She was quiet for a moment. "I think he took it with him. Through the door."

---

We sat in silence for a long time after that. The fire burned low. Across the hollow, at the big fire, the troupe was singing. "Tinker Tanner," with all the rude verses. Nell's voice rose above the others, high and piercing and slightly off key.

"So I don't have a name either," said Denna at last. "Not really. It's on the other side of a door that can never be opened." She laughed softly. "We're a fine pair, aren't we? Two people with no names, sitting by a fire in the middle of nowhere."

"You have a name," I said.

She looked at me.

"Denna," I said. "That's your name. That's the one I—" I stopped. I did not know what I had been going to say. There was a feeling in the hole in my chest, very faint, like a word on the tip of my tongue.

"The one you what?"

I looked down at the cord on my wrist. At the knots, glowing faintly in the firelight. I felt the feeling again. Stronger, this time. A tug. A pull. Something in the cord reaching out toward her.

"The one I remember," I said slowly. "Sometimes. When the cord hums. That's the one I remember."

Denna stared at me.

"You remember me?"

"Sometimes. For a moment." I touched the cord. "When you're close. It's like—a word I almost know. A face I almost recognize." I looked up at her. "It's always *Denna*. Never any of the others. Just that."

She looked at me for a long time. Her face was very strange in the firelight. Wet, and shining, and full of something I did not have a name for.

"Then that's my name," she said softly. "That's the one. If you remember it, that's the one that's real."

---

She reached into the neck of my shirt. I did not stop her. She found the thread there, the one I had worn around my neck since before the Eolian, and drew it out. There was a knot on it. A small knot, tied in dark cord. *Road. Promise. A story told many times, and changes in the telling.*

"Do you know what this is?" she said.

"No."

"I gave it to you. A long time ago. In Imre. The first time I taught you knots." She held it in her palm. "I didn't know, then, what it was. I thought it was just a keepsake. A pretty knot. But I tied it the way he'd taught me to tie my own name, and I put a little of myself in it without knowing." She looked at me. "There's a knot at the heart of it. Very small. Do you see?"

I looked. There was. A tiny knot, so small and so tight I could barely see it, at the very center.

"That's me," said Denna. "The part of me that knew you. That's what brought you back, at the Eolian, when the song emptied you out. You held onto it, and it held onto you." She closed her hand around the knot. "I didn't know I'd done it. But I'm glad I did."

She put it back inside my shirt. She laid her hand flat over it, over my heart.

"Keep it," she said. "Always. Whatever else happens. Wherever you go. Keep it."

"I will."

"Promise me."

I looked at her. At her dark eyes, and her wet face, and her hand on my chest.

"I promise," I said.

---

She told me, then, what she thought we should do.

She had been thinking about it, she said, all the way from Renere. Lying awake at night in the back of the wagon, holding my hand, feeling the cord on my wrist hum.

"It's too loud," she said. "The cord. It's got your whole name in it. The whole line. Everything the door used to answer to." She touched it, very gently. "And it's right here. On your wrist. Where anything that's listening can hear it. The Seven. The Thief. Whatever else is out there." She looked at me. "And you're right here with it. Walking around. Where anyone could find you. And if they found you, and took it, and carried it to the door—"

"The door would open."

"And there'd be no one left to close it." She was quiet for a moment. "We need to put it somewhere. Somewhere safe. Somewhere that says no to everyone but you. And then you need to go somewhere else. Somewhere no one would ever look."

"Apart from it?"

"Not too far. It's your name. It needs to be near you, or you'll fade. Like—" She hesitated. "Like a candle without a wick. Elodin said so, I think. Or someone did." She shook her head. "Near you. But not on you. In something that holds."

"What?"

Denna looked at me. Then she looked across the hollow, at the big fire, at the Ruh singing around it.

"I don't know," she said. "Not exactly. But I know who might." She smiled faintly. "A man who makes things. And a girl who knows stone. And a master who knows names. And a woman with spectacles who can make a dead man walk to the gallows." She looked back at me. "You have very clever friends, Kvothe. Even if you don't remember them."

---

We sat by the fire until it burned down to embers. Across the hollow, the big fire burned down too, and the singing stopped, and the troupe went to their wagons and their blankets. The night grew quiet. The stars came out, very bright and very far away.

At last Denna lay down beside me in the grass, with her head on my shoulder and her hand on my chest, over the knot.

"Kvothe," she said sleepily.

"Yes?"

"When we get to Imre. When it's done. When your name's put away somewhere safe." She was quiet for a moment. "I'm going to have to go."

I did not say anything.

"I'm the one who tied it," she said. "I'm the only one who knows how. If anyone ever wanted to open it, they'd need me. And if they found me, they'd find you." Her voice was very soft. "So I have to go. Far away. Somewhere no one knows me. And I have to stay there." She paused. "And I have to not come back. Not ever. Not even if I want to. Especially not if I want to."

"Is that what you want?"

She laughed softly against my shoulder.

"No," she said. "No. It's the last thing I want." She was quiet for a long time. "But it's the right thing. You taught me that. Choose, and bear what comes after."

"I don't remember teaching you that."

"You didn't. Not in words." She pressed her face against my shoulder. "You taught me by doing it. In the chamber. When you pulled your name out and handed it to me." Her voice cracked. "I never saw anyone do anything so brave. Or so stupid."

"Those are often the same thing."

She laughed. It was a broken, astonished, beautiful laugh.

"Yes," she said. "Yes. They are."

She fell asleep, after a while. I lay awake beside her with her head on my shoulder and her hand on my chest, and I looked up at the stars, and I did not sleep.

The stars were grey. Everything was grey.

But the cord on my wrist was humming. Very softly. All night long.

And somewhere very far down, at the bottom of the hole where my name had been, something was humming back.
