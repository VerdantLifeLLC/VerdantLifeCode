# The Stormwal

Nobody lives in the Stormwal. Nobody sane.

It is a range of mountains in the far north of the Four Corners, beyond the last of the Commonwealth's cities, beyond the last of the Small Kingdoms, beyond the last village where anyone speaks a language anyone else understands. It is high and cold and bare, a great jagged wall of grey stone and white snow stretching from east to west as far as anyone has ever traveled. The winds there never stop. They come howling down off the peaks, day and night, summer and winter, so fierce and so cold that they can freeze a man's breath in his lungs.

That is why it is called the Stormwal. Because it is a wall made of storms.

There are stories about it, of course. There are always stories. They say that the mountains are the bones of a giant who died in the Creation War. They say that the winds are the ghosts of the namers who fought there, still screaming their names into the sky. They say that there are things in the high passes that are older than the mountains themselves, things that crawl out of the ice at night and hunt anything warm.

Some of the stories are true.

---

We came out of the twilight into the Stormwal through a ring of greystones on a high bare shoulder of the mountains, and the first thing I felt was the cold.

I had been cold before. I had been cold on the streets of Tarbean, sleeping on rooftops in the winter rain. I had been cold in the Eolian, with Cinder's hand around my wrist. I thought I knew what cold was.

I did not.

It hit me the moment I stepped out of the ring. It went through the shaed and through my clothes and through my skin and into my bones, all at once, like a hammer blow. My breath froze in my throat. My eyes watered and the tears froze on my cheeks. My cold left hand, which had been almost bearable in the twilight, went dead and white and useless in an instant.

Beside me, Bast gasped. It was a thin, high, strangled sound, the sound of someone who has jumped into a winter lake.

And then he looked up.

It was snowing. Great soft flakes, drifting down out of a white sky, falling slowly and silently all around us. They settled on the grey stones of the ring, and on the bare rock of the mountainside, and on our hair and our shoulders and our eyelashes. The world was white and grey and silent, except for the distant howling of the wind in the peaks.

Bast stood in the snow with his mouth open. He held out his hands, palms up, and watched the flakes land on them and melt. He tilted his head back and let them fall on his face.

"What is it?" he whispered.

"Snow."

"Snow." He said the word as if it were a prayer. "We don't have snow. In the twilight. We don't have weather at all." He caught a flake on his tongue. His eyes went wide. "It's *cold*."

"That's the point of it."

"It's beautiful." His voice cracked. His eyes were wet. "Reshi, it's so beautiful."

I looked at him, standing in the snow with his face turned up and tears on his cheeks, and I did not tell him that the snow would kill us both if we stood there much longer. I let him look.

For a little while.

---

The silver string still pointed north. It floated in the air ahead of us, glowing faintly against the white, pointing up the mountainside toward a high pass between two great peaks. I did not know how far it was. I did not know what lay beyond.

But I knew that we could not stay where we were. Not in that cold. Not in that wind.

So we climbed.

It was the hardest climb of my life. The mountainside was steep and bare and slick with ice. The snow grew deeper as we went, first to our ankles, then to our knees. The wind came howling down from the pass, driving the snow into our faces like handfuls of needles. My cold hand was useless. I climbed one-handed, clutching at the rocks with my good hand, dragging myself up.

Bast was better at it than I was. His hooves, it turned out, were made for mountains. They found purchase on the ice where my boots slipped. He went ahead of me, picking the way, reaching back to pull me up over the hardest places. He did not complain. He did not say anything at all. But every so often, when he thought I was not looking, he tilted his head back and caught a snowflake on his tongue.

We climbed all day. At dusk, if you could call it dusk—the white sky only grew a little greyer—we found a shallow cave in the lee of a great boulder, and we crawled inside, and we huddled together against the back wall with the shaed wrapped around us both.

I tried to light a fire. There was nothing to burn. I tried sympathy, binding the heat of my own body to a stone to warm it. It worked, a little. Not enough.

"We're going to die," said Bast, through chattering teeth. He did not sound frightened. He sounded interested. "Aren't we? Up here. In the snow."

"Not tonight."

"How do you know?"

"Because I haven't finished what I came to do." I pulled the shaed tighter around us. "And I always finish what I came to do. Eventually."

Bast laughed. It came out as a cloud of white breath.

"You're insane," he said. "Do you know that?"

"I've been told."

"Good. I like insane." He leaned his head against my shoulder. "Insane people are the only ones who ever do anything interesting."

---

The scrael came in the night.

I heard them first. A sound in the darkness outside the cave, beneath the howling of the wind. A clicking. A rattling. Like dry bones knocking together. Like beetles scuttling over stone. It came from somewhere below us, on the mountainside, and it was getting closer.

I sat up. Beside me, Bast sat up too. His eyes were wide in the dark.

"What is that?" he whispered.

I did not answer. I knew what it was. I had heard it before, in the Commonwealth, in the stories of travelers. I had read about it in the Archives. I had never heard it myself.

Scrael.

They are things from the mountains. Black and hard and shiny, like beetles, but the size of a dog or a large cat. They have many legs, long and thin and sharp as knives. They have no eyes that anyone has ever found. They have no mouths either, or not the kind of mouth that eats. They do not eat what they kill. They only kill it.

They come in swarms. Dozens. Hundreds. They come down out of the high places when the winter is hard, and they kill everything they find. Sheep, cattle, horses, men. They cut them to pieces with their knife-legs and leave the pieces lying in the snow.

No one knows where they come from. No one knows what they are. They are not natural. That much everyone agrees on. They are not animals, and they are not Fae, and they are not anything else that has a name.

And they are hard to kill. Very hard. Swords glance off their shells. Arrows bounce. Fire does not burn them.

But I knew something about them that most people do not know. I had read it in an old book in the Archives, years ago, a book about the monsters of the northern mountains. I had not thought about it since.

They do not like iron.

---

"Bast," I said. My voice was very quiet. "Do you have any iron on you?"

He stared at me in the dark. "*Iron?* Reshi, I'm *Fae*."

Of course. Of course he did not. Iron burns the Fae. They cannot touch it. They cannot bear to be near it.

I had a little. Not much. The buckle of my belt. The nails in my boots. The small knife I kept in my pack for cutting bread. And Caesura, of course. But Caesura was not iron. The Adem make their swords of something else, some alloy of their own that they do not share with outsiders.

The clicking was getting louder. Closer. I could hear individual sounds now, sharp little taps of knife-legs on stone. Dozens of them. Coming up the mountainside toward the cave.

I took off my belt. I pulled the knife out of my pack. I looked at the buckle, and the blade, and the nails in my boots.

It was not enough. It would never be enough. Not for dozens of them.

"Stay behind me," I said to Bast. "Stay against the back wall. Don't move."

"What are you going to do?"

"I don't know yet."

---

The first one came into the mouth of the cave a moment later.

It was bigger than I had expected. The size of a large dog, black and gleaming, with its many legs folded around it like the ribs of an umbrella. It had no head that I could see. Just a smooth black shell, faintly iridescent, like oil on water. It paused in the mouth of the cave. Its legs tapped on the stone, feeling. Searching.

Then it came in.

It moved very fast. Faster than anything that size should be able to move. It was across the cave floor before I could draw breath, its knife-legs flashing, and I threw myself sideways and felt one of them slice through the edge of the shaed and graze my shoulder.

I rolled. I came up on one knee with the bread knife in my good hand. The scrael turned, all its legs moving at once, and came at me again.

I stabbed at it. The knife hit its shell and skittered off, the way a knife skitters off a pane of glass. But where the iron touched it, the shell smoked. Just a little. A thin grey wisp, rising from the black surface.

The scrael shrieked. It was a horrible sound, high and thin and grinding, like metal on metal. It reared back, its legs flailing.

I did not wait. I lunged forward and drove the knife into the place where its legs met its body, where the shell was thinnest. I felt the blade go in. I felt something give.

The scrael shrieked again. Then it collapsed, its legs folding in on themselves, and lay still.

I knelt beside it, breathing hard, with the knife in my hand. The blade was smoking. Black ichor dripped from it, thick and foul-smelling.

"Reshi," said Bast. His voice was very small. "Reshi, look."

I looked up.

The mouth of the cave was full of them.

---

I do not know how many there were. Dozens. Maybe a hundred. They filled the mouth of the cave and spilled out onto the mountainside beyond, black and gleaming in the grey light, their legs clicking and tapping on the stone. They were not moving. Not yet. They were waiting. As if the first one had been a scout, and they were waiting to see what had happened to it.

I had one bread knife. One belt buckle. A handful of boot nails.

I could not kill them all. I could not kill even a few of them. They would pour into the cave like water and cut us to pieces, and there would be nothing I could do.

Behind me, Bast was pressed against the back wall. I could hear him breathing, fast and shallow.

"Reshi," he whispered. "I can do something. Maybe. I think."

"What?"

"A glamour. A seeming. I can make us look like—like nothing. Like stone. Like part of the wall." His voice was shaking. "They don't have eyes. I don't know if it'll work on something with no eyes. But I can try."

"Try."

I heard him take a breath. I heard him begin to murmur, very softly, in a language I did not know. It was not the Sithe's liquid tongue, or the old language of names. It was something else. Something light and quick and sly, like a fox moving through tall grass.

And I felt the air change around us. Felt it thicken, and shimmer, and fold. Felt the edges of things blur and soften. I looked down at my own hand, holding the knife, and I could not quite see it. It was there, but it was hard to look at. Like trying to see something at the corner of your eye.

The scrael at the mouth of the cave shifted. Their legs tapped on the stone. They seemed—uncertain. As if they had been following something, and it had suddenly disappeared.

They came in anyway.

---

They came in slowly, this time. Carefully. Feeling their way along the floor with their knife-legs, tapping and clicking. They could not see us. Bast's glamour was working, or partly working. But they could feel us. Could sense us somehow, the warmth of us, the life.

They were spreading out. Filling the cave. Moving toward the back wall, where we crouched.

I held my breath. I held the knife. I held Fela's stone in my cold dead hand, so tight that its edge cut into my palm, though I could not feel it.

*No*, the stone said. *No. No. No.*

And suddenly, I understood.

---

I have told you that I learned to hear stone in the round room at the top of the Rookery. That I heard it saying no, its long slow patient no, and that beneath it, far below, I heard something else. Something hungry.

I had heard that hungry thing again, in the Chamber of Watches, behind the door. I had felt it in the Cellarage, in the Sleeper's dreams. I had felt it in the Lackless box, humming its no to a door.

And I felt it now. Here. In the scrael.

They were not natural. They were not animals, or Fae, or anything else that had a name. They were *made*. Shaped. Twisted out of nothing by someone who wanted things that would kill.

They were the Thief's.

I do not know how I knew. But I knew. The way you know your mother's voice. They had the same hunger in them that I had heard behind the door. The same wrongness. They were things the Thief had made, long ago, before the war. Or things that had come through from behind the door, slipping through the cracks, the way water seeps through a wall.

And they were afraid of stone.

Not iron. Iron hurt them, but it did not frighten them. Stone frightened them. Because stone said no. And they were made of *yes*. Of wanting. Of hunger. Of change. Everything they were was the opposite of stone.

I opened my mouth. And I began to hum.

---

I hummed the stone's no. The long slow patient no that I had learned in the round room and sung to the Sleeper and whispered to the king's hair. I hummed it into the cave, into the mountain, into the great grey bones of the Stormwal all around us.

And the mountain heard me.

I felt it. Felt the stone of the cave walls wake up, slowly, the way stone does everything. Felt it hear my hum and recognize it. Felt it answer.

*No*, said the mountain.

It was not a sound. It was a vibration. Deep and low, rising up out of the cave floor, out of the walls, out of the ceiling. It was the voice of the Stormwal itself, the great grey wall of stone that had stood there since before the world had a name, saying its long slow patient *no* to everything that was not stone.

The scrael froze.

All of them, at once. Their legs stopped moving. Their shells trembled. The clicking and tapping stopped, and there was only the vibration, the mountain's voice, rising up through the stone and filling the cave.

*No*, said the mountain. *No. No. No.*

The scrael began to shriek.

It was the most terrible sound I have ever heard. A hundred high thin grinding voices, all screaming at once. They reared back, their legs flailing. They scrabbled at the stone floor, trying to get away from it. Trying to get away from the voice.

And they fled.

They poured out of the cave like water from a broken jar. They tumbled over each other in their haste, shrieking, clicking, scrabbling. They went out through the mouth of the cave and down the mountainside, into the grey dark, into the snow, and they did not come back.

---

I stopped humming. The mountain's voice faded, slowly, sinking back into the stone the way water sinks into sand.

The cave was silent. Empty. Except for the one dead scrael lying on the floor, and Bast and me pressed against the back wall.

Bast's glamour flickered and went out. He slumped against me, exhausted.

"What," he said. His voice was a thread. "What *was* that?"

"Stone," I said. My own voice was not much better. "I asked the mountain to say no."

He stared at me in the grey dark. Then he began to laugh. Weakly, helplessly, with his head against my shoulder and tears running down his face.

"You asked the *mountain*," he said. "You asked the mountain to say no. And it *did*."

"Mountains are very reasonable," I said. "If you ask them properly."

He laughed harder. I held him while he laughed. And after a while, I found that I was laughing too.

We did not sleep that night. But we did not die, either. And in the morning, when the grey light came, the snow had stopped, and the sky was clear and blue and very cold, and the silver string on my wrist was pointing up toward the high pass between the peaks.

We followed it.
