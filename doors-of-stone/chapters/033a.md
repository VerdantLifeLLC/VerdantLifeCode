# Harl

The road from Severen to Renere runs northeast across the heart of Vintas, through a country of vineyards and orchards and old walled towns, and it takes six days if the weather is kind. The weather was not kind. It rained for four of the six, a steady grey soaking rain that turned the road to paste and the fields to lakes and got into everything we owned.

I rode the Maer's tall grey gelding, whose name, I discovered on the second day, was Dignity. Harl and Corrin rode behind me on two heavy bay cobs with no names at all, or none they would tell me.

Corrin talked. He was the younger of the two, lean and quick and freckled, and he had served in the city guard of Renere before he came to the Maer, and he was full of stories about it. Drunken lords and knife fights in the Weir and a baker's wife who had once chased him three streets with a rolling pin. He told them well. By the second day I was laughing at them in spite of the rain.

Harl did not talk. Harl rode.

He was a big man, older than Corrin by twenty years, with a broken nose and a grizzled beard and a face like a closed door. He rode with his hood up and his hands loose on the reins and his eyes moving, always moving, over the hedges and the ditches and the dark lines of woods at the edges of the fields. When we stopped, he checked the horses' feet before he checked his own supper. When we slept, he slept last and woke first, and I never once caught him with his eyes closed.

"Does he ever say anything?" I asked Corrin, on the third night. We were in the common room of an inn in a town called Pell, and Harl was outside, seeing to the horses.

Corrin grinned. "Sometimes. When it matters."

"How often does it matter?"

"Not often," said Corrin. "Harl says most talk is just noise. He says a man should only open his mouth to eat, drink, or say something that'll keep someone alive." He took a pull of his beer. "I've known him nine years. I think he's said maybe a thousand words to me. Most of them were *duck*."

---

On the fourth day, we were ambushed.

It was not much of an ambush. Six men, ragged and thin, deserters or out-of-work soldiers from some lord's disbanded levy. They came out of a copse of trees where the road ran through a narrow cut between two hills, with rusty swords and one crossbow, and they shouted at us to stop and throw down our purses.

Corrin swore and reached for his sword.

Harl did not reach for anything. He looked at the six men for a long moment. At their thin faces and their rusty swords and the crossbow, which was pointed at me, and which was shaking.

Then he said, in a voice like gravel, "Boy with the bow. What's your name?"

The boy with the crossbow stared at him. He could not have been more than sixteen.

"Dell," he said.

"Dell," said Harl. "When did you last eat?"

The boy's mouth fell open. The men around him shifted and muttered.

"Two days," said the boy.

Harl nodded slowly. He reached into his saddlebag, very slowly, so that no one would mistake it, and took out a loaf of bread. A whole loaf, and a wedge of cheese wrapped in cloth. He held them up.

"Put down the bow," he said, "and this is yours. All of you. Share it." He looked at the others. "Or pick it up again and I'll kill four of you before the other two can run. Your choice."

There was a long silence in the cut between the hills. Rain dripped from the trees.

The boy put down the bow.

Harl rode forward and handed him the bread and cheese. Then he reached into his saddlebag again and took out a few coppers and gave them to the oldest of the men.

"Farm at the bottom of the next valley," he said. "Man named Tobin. Needs hands for the harvest, and he's too proud to ask. Tell him Harl sent you." He looked at them all. "And throw away those swords. They'll get you hanged."

He turned his horse and rode back to us, and we went on through the cut between the hills, and the six men stood at the side of the road with the bread in their hands and watched us go.

---

"You knew there'd be a farm," I said to him that evening, when we had stopped. It was the first time I had spoken to him directly in four days.

Harl was crouched by the fire, turning a rabbit on a spit. He did not look up.

"Grew up two valleys over," he said.

"You were going to kill them."

"Four of them." He turned the rabbit. "Wouldn't have come to it."

"How did you know?"

He was quiet for so long that I thought he was not going to answer. Then he said, "Boy's hands were shaking. Men whose hands shake don't want to shoot. They want to eat." He looked at me for the first time, across the fire. "You learn it. After a while. What men want."

"What do I want?"

Harl looked at me for a long moment with his small hard eyes.

"To be too late for nothing," he said. "Never again."

I did not say anything. I did not know how he knew. I had told him nothing about my family, or Tarbean, or the Deller farm.

He went back to turning the rabbit.

"Same as me," he said.

---

He did not say anything else that night. Or the next day. But on the sixth morning, as we came over the last rise and saw Renere spread out below us in its bowl of green hills, he rode up beside me.

"The city's bad," he said. "Worse than Severen. Everyone smiling. Everyone with a knife." He looked at me. "You'll want to go places alone. Don't."

"I can take care of myself."

"I know." He looked down at the city. "Take care of us anyway. Me and the boy. We're paid to die for you. I'd rather not."

It was, Corrin told me later, the longest speech he had ever heard Harl make.

---

I have told you how he died. In the laundry doorway of the palace at Renere, with his sword drawn, holding a narrow door against a dozen men while I ran.

I did not tell you what he said to me, in that doorway, before I ran. I have not told anyone.

He said, *Go on, sir.* And then, as I turned, one more thing, very low, almost to himself.

*Not too late this time.*

I did not understand it then. I thought about it for a long time afterward, on the barge, and in Tarbean, and in all the years after. And I think now that he had a story too. Someone he had come too late for, once, a long time ago. Two valleys over, perhaps. And that in that doorway, holding it, he had found a way to be on time.

I never learned who. He never told me, and there was no one left to ask.

But I have his name. Harl. Four letters, spoken very seldom. And I have put it in this story, where it belongs, so that someone will remember.

He would have said it was just noise.

It isn't.
