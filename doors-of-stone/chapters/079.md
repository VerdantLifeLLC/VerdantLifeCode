# The Duke of Hallowmere

They did not kill me. I have thought, since, that they should have. It would have been simpler for everyone.

The soldiers came up the slope toward the door of the Lackless house in a great grey tide, shouting, with their swords drawn and their pikes leveled. I stood in the doorway with the woman beside me and watched them come, and I did not feel afraid. I did not feel anything. Fear needs a self to be afraid for, and mine was tied up in a cord around my wrist.

They surrounded us. A wall of grey steel and grey faces, pressing close. Someone struck me in the face with the butt of a pike, and I fell to my knees on the grey stone of the doorstep, and tasted blood. I did not feel that either. Not really. I knew it hurt. I could see the blood dripping onto the stone. But it was happening to someone else. Some grey man, kneeling on a doorstep, with blood on his mouth.

The woman was shouting. She had put herself between me and the soldiers, with her arms spread wide, and she was shouting at them. I did not understand what she said. But the soldiers stopped. They stood with their pikes leveled and their faces twisted with rage, and they did not strike again.

And then the crowd parted, and a man came through it on a horse.

---

He was old. Older than the Maer, younger than Bredon. A big man, heavy through the shoulders and the belly, with a thick grey beard and a heavy grey face. He was dressed in fine armor, black and silver, with a black cloak and a circlet of silver on his brow. His horse was black too. Everything about him was black and silver.

I did not know him. But the woman beside me went very still.

"Duke Jakis," she said softly. "His father."

The duke reined in his horse at the foot of the doorstep. He sat very still in the saddle, looking down at me. His face was the color of old ashes. His eyes were red-rimmed and swollen.

"Where is he?" he said.

His voice was very quiet. Much quieter than I expected, from a man so big.

No one answered.

"Where is my son?" said the duke.

"In the cellars," said the woman. Her voice was steady. "At the bottom of the stair. By the door."

The duke looked at her. Then he looked at me.

"You did it," he said. "You."

I looked up at him. I tried to think of something to say. Nothing came. There was nothing in me to say it with.

"Yes," I said.

---

He got down off his horse. He did it slowly, heavily, the way an old man gets down off a horse. He walked up the steps toward me. His soldiers parted to let him through.

He stopped in front of me. I was still on my knees on the doorstep. He looked down at me for a long time.

Then he struck me.

Not with a sword, or a pike, or anything else. With his hand. His open hand, across my face. It was a heavy hand, and he put all his weight behind it, and it knocked me sideways onto the stone.

I lay there. I did not get up. I looked at the grey sky.

"He was twenty years old," said the duke. His voice was shaking now. "Twenty years old. He was my only son. His mother died when he was six. I raised him myself." He struck me again, a kick this time, in the ribs. I felt something crack. "He was proud. He was vain. He was foolish. He was cruel, sometimes. I know that. I know all of that." Another kick. "But he was *mine*. And you killed him. In a hole in the ground. Like a dog."

"He was going to kill her," I said. My voice was flat. "The lady. With a knife."

The duke went very still.

"What lady?"

"The Maer's wife. Meluan. He had a knife at her throat. He was going to give her to the door." I looked up at him. "I didn't have time. I didn't have time to do anything else."

The duke stared at me.

"You're lying," he said.

"No."

"My son would never—"

"He would," said the woman. She was standing beside me, very straight, with her chin up. "He did. I saw it. Everyone in that chamber saw it." Her voice cracked. "He wasn't himself, your grace. He hadn't been himself for months. Not since the old man came. Not since he put that box around his neck."

The duke looked at her. Something moved in his heavy grey face.

"The old man," he said slowly. "Bredon."

"Yes."

"Where is he?"

The woman was quiet for a moment. "Gone," she said at last. "Through the door. Just before it closed."

The duke looked at her for a long time. Then he looked at me. Then he looked up, past me, into the dark hallway of the Lackless house.

"Bring my son up," he said to his soldiers. "Now. I want to see him."

---

They brought Ambrose up an hour later.

Four soldiers carried him on a litter made of spears and cloaks. They came out of the dark doorway into the grey morning light and set him down on the stone of the courtyard, very gently, the way you set down something that can break. They had covered him with a black cloak. Only his face showed. It was very pale and very still, and his eyes were closed, and he looked younger than I had ever seen him. He looked like a boy.

The duke knelt beside him.

He did not say anything. He knelt there on the grey stone with his hand on his son's chest, over the black cloak, over the place where Caesura had gone in. His heavy shoulders were shaking.

I lay on the doorstep where he had left me, with my ribs cracked and my face bleeding, and I watched him.

And I felt something.

It was very faint. Very far away. Coming up out of the hole in my chest like water seeping up into a footprint in wet sand. I did not know what it was. It had no name. I had nothing to name it with.

But I watched the duke kneel beside his son and weep, and I felt it. And it hurt more than his hand, or his boot, or anything else.

I have thought since that it was grief. His, not mine. I think that when you have no name of your own, sometimes other people's feelings can seep into the empty place where yours should be. Like water into a hole.

I think I grieved for Ambrose that morning. Not because I had loved him. I never did. But because his father did. And for a little while, lying on that doorstep with nothing inside me, I had room for it.

---

The duke stood up at last. His face was grey and wet and very hard.

"Hang him," he said. "Here. Now. From the gate."

"Your grace." It was one of his officers. A lean man with a careful face, in the colors of some lesser lord. "Your grace, we can't."

The duke turned on him. "Can't?"

"He killed the king, your grace. The *king*. Not a lord, not a soldier. The king of Vintas." The officer's voice was very careful. "There are laws. Very old laws. A regicide must be tried in the capital, before the lords of the realm. If we hang him here, in a field, without trial—"

"I don't care about laws."

"The other lords will, your grace." The officer was sweating. "They'll say you did it to silence him. They'll say you didn't want him to talk. They'll say—" He hesitated.

"They'll say what?"

The officer glanced at the woman beside me. Then back at the duke.

"They'll say the king was killed at a door," he said quietly. "In a cellar. With a box around his neck and a woman's blood on his hands. They'll say the king was doing something that no king should do. And that you knew." He paused. "They'll want to know why, your grace. And if the only witness is dead, they'll assume the worst."

The duke stared at him for a long time.

"You think I'm afraid of what they'll say?"

"I think your son's name matters to you, your grace." The officer's voice was very soft. "I think you want it remembered well. And I think the only way that happens is if there's a trial. A public one. Where everyone can hear what he did, and what was done to him." He glanced at me. "Where everyone can see the Ruh who killed him hang for it, in the great square at Renere, in front of the whole kingdom."

The duke was silent. He looked down at his son's body, lying on the stone under its black cloak. He looked at me, lying on the doorstep.

"Chain him," he said at last. "Take him to Renere. We'll try him there." He looked at the woman. "And her too."

---

They chained me. Heavy iron chains, on my wrists and my ankles. They put the one on my left wrist right over the cord, and I felt the iron press the knots against my skin. The cord was humming very faintly. Unhappily. Like a cat pressed too tight.

The woman saw. She said something to the soldier who was fastening the chain. Sharp and angry. He ignored her. She said it again, louder. He struck her across the face.

I do not remember deciding to move. I do not remember deciding anything. One moment I was sitting on the doorstep with the chains on my wrists, and the next I was on my feet, and the soldier who had struck her was on the ground, and I was standing over him with the chain between my wrists pulled taut, and I did not know how I had got there.

Every soldier in the courtyard had their sword out.

The woman caught my arm. "*Stop*," she hissed. "*Stop, stop, it's all right, I'm all right, stop.*"

I stopped. I stood very still. I did not know why I had moved. I did not know what I was feeling. There was something in my chest, hot and fierce, rising up out of the hole like fire out of a well. I did not know what it was called.

The soldier on the ground was staring up at me. His face was white.

"He's a demon," he whispered. "Look at his eyes. He's a demon."

I did not know what my eyes looked like. I did not know what a demon was. I stood there with the chains on my wrists and the heat in my chest, and I looked at him.

Then the woman's hand tightened on my arm, and the heat went out of me as quickly as it had come, and I was empty again. And grey. And I sat back down on the doorstep, and I did not move again until they came to take me away.

---

They took us to Renere in a cart.

It was a prison cart, with iron bars on all four sides and an iron roof, the kind they use to carry criminals to the gallows. They put me in it, in my chains, and they put the woman in with me. They did not chain her. I think they did not think they needed to.

The cart was drawn by two grey horses, and it was guarded by fifty soldiers on horseback, and it went at the head of the duke's army, behind the black cart that carried his son's body.

It was a long way to Renere. Nine days. I do not remember most of it. I remember the grey sky, and the grey road, and the grey hills rolling by beyond the bars. I remember the rain, which came on the third day and did not stop. I remember the woman sitting beside me in the cart, holding my hand, singing very softly under her breath.

I remember the villages.

---

We passed through many of them. Small places, farms and hamlets and market towns, strung along the road like beads on a cord. And in every one, people came out to see.

Word had gone ahead of us. The king was dead. The kingkiller was coming, in a cage, to be tried in Renere. People came out of their houses and lined the road to watch us pass. Men and women and children. Old people. Young people. Farmers and shopkeepers and priests.

Some of them threw things. Mud. Stones. Rotten fruit. A dead cat, once, which struck the bars of the cart and fell back into the road. They shouted. *Kingkiller. Murderer. Demon. Ruh.* The soldiers let them.

Some of them did not throw anything. They only stood and watched, with their faces closed and wary. The way people watch a storm passing on the horizon.

And some of them—not many, but some—did something else.

In a village called Brennock, on the fifth day, a girl came out of the crowd. She was very young, seven or eight. She had a cup in her hands. She walked right up to the bars of the cart, past the soldiers, before anyone could stop her, and she held the cup up to me.

It was water.

I looked at it. I did not understand. The woman beside me took it, gently, from the girl's hands, and held it to my lips, and I drank.

"My mam says you sang a song," said the girl. She was looking up at me through the bars with wide brown eyes. "In the big city. About the white town. She says it's the best song there ever was. She says the king's men burned our neighbor's house because he was singing it."

I looked down at her. I did not know what to say. I did not know what a song was. I knew the word, but not the meaning.

"Thank you," I said.

The girl smiled. Then a soldier grabbed her by the arm and hauled her away, and she was gone into the crowd.

The woman beside me was crying. I did not know why.

---

In another village, on the seventh day, I saw a fox.

It was sitting at the edge of a wood, beside the road, watching the cart go by. A red fox, bright against the grey. It sat very still, with its tail curled around its feet, and it watched me. Its eyes were a very deep blue.

I did not know foxes did not have blue eyes. I did not know anything. But I looked at it, and something in the cord on my wrist hummed.

The fox watched until the cart was out of sight. Then, when I looked back, it was gone.

I saw it again the next day. And the next. Always at the edge of a wood, or on a hill, or at the corner of a barn. Always watching. Always with those deep blue eyes.

On the ninth day, as we came over the last rise and saw the city of Renere spread out below us in its bowl of green hills, I saw it one more time. Sitting on a stone at the side of the road, with the city behind it.

It looked at me. I looked at it.

And very faintly, so faintly I could not be sure I had heard it at all, I thought I heard a voice. Soft and cracked and very young.

*I'll find you, Reshi. Wherever they take you.*

The cart rolled on. When I looked back, the stone was empty.
