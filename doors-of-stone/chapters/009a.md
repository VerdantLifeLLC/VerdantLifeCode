# A Sonnet for Fela

"It's terrible," said Wil.

"It's not terrible," said Sim.

"It's the worst thing I have ever read. And I have read Master Hemme's treatise on the rhetorical uses of silence, which is four hundred pages long and contains no silences whatsoever."

"It's a sonnet," said Sim, with great dignity. "It's supposed to be difficult."

"It's supposed to be *good*."

We were sitting at our usual table in the corner of Anker's, late on a Theden evening, with three mugs of cider and a plate of Anker's terrible fried bread between us. Sim had a sheet of paper in his hand. He had been holding it for most of an hour. He had read it to us three times, each time with more feeling than the last, and each time Wil's face had grown a little more pained.

I had not said anything yet. I was trying very hard not to.

"Kvothe," said Sim. He turned to me. His thin eager face was flushed. "You're a musician. You understand meter. Tell him it's good."

I looked at the paper. At Sim's cramped untidy handwriting, and the lines that had been crossed out and rewritten and crossed out again, and the small blot of ink at the bottom where he had apparently wept.

"It's heartfelt," I said carefully.

Wil laughed so hard he choked on his cider.

---

It was about Fela. Of course it was about Fela. Sim had been in love with Fela for as long as I had known him, and possibly longer, the way the moon is in love with the sea, which is to say helplessly and with a great deal of pulling this way and that. They had been walking out together since before I left for Vintas, and I had been glad for them both.

But while I was away, they had quarreled. Sim would not tell me about what, except that it had been very stupid, and entirely his fault, and had involved a remark about Elodin's class that he had meant as a joke and she had not taken as one. She had not spoken to him in two months. She passed him in the Archives without looking at him. She had moved to the other side of the round room at the top of the Rookery.

And now, he said, he was going to win her back. With a sonnet.

"She loves poetry," he said. "She told me so. Last term, in the Archives. She said there's nothing more romantic than a man who can tell you how he feels in fourteen lines."

"She may have been joking," said Wil.

"She wasn't joking. Fela doesn't joke."

"Everyone jokes."

"Fela doesn't. She's very serious. That's one of the things I love about her." Sim looked at the paper in his hand. His face fell. "Is it really that bad?"

Wil opened his mouth. I kicked him under the table.

"Read it again," I said. "Slowly. And this time, let me listen."

---

He read it again.

I will not reproduce it here. Sim would never forgive me. But I will tell you that it compared Fela's eyes to the dark water of a mountain lake, and her hair to the night sky, and her voice to the sound of a river running over stones. I will tell you that it rhymed *heart* with *apart* and *eyes* with *skies* and, in one memorable couplet, *Fela* with *a stellar*. I will tell you that the meter lurched and stumbled like a drunk man on a frozen road.

And I will tell you that by the end of it, I had stopped wanting to laugh.

Because under the terrible rhymes and the lurching meter, there was something true. Something that shone through the bad writing the way a candle shines through a dirty window. It was clumsy and awkward and ridiculous, but it was Sim, all the way through. It was the way he looked at her when she wasn't looking. The way he went quiet when she came into a room. The way he had once walked all the way across the river in the rain to bring her a book she had mentioned wanting, and left it on her doorstep without a note, and never told her it was from him.

"Well?" said Sim, when he was done. He looked at me anxiously.

I thought about it.

"The rhymes are bad," I said. "And the meter needs work. And you should never, under any circumstances, rhyme *Fela* with *a stellar*."

Sim's face fell.

"But," I said, "the thing underneath it is good. The thing you're trying to say. That's good." I held out my hand. "Give it to me. Let's fix the parts that aren't."

---

We worked on it until closing. And then Anker threw us out, and we went up to my room under the eaves and worked on it until the candle guttered. Wil came with us, grumbling, and sat on my bed and made acid remarks, some of which were actually helpful.

It was hard work. Harder than I expected. It is one thing to write a song; you have the melody to carry you, and the rhythm, and the listener's ear forgiving a great deal for the sake of the tune. A sonnet has nothing but itself. Every word has to earn its place.

And it was Sim's sonnet. That was the difficult part. I could have rewritten it from top to bottom in an hour, and it would have been better, and it would not have been his. Fela would have known. She would have read it and seen my hand in it, and it would have been worse than the bad version, because it would have been a lie.

So I did not rewrite it. I sat beside Sim at my narrow table and asked him questions. *What do you mean here? What does it feel like when she does that? What's the thing you'd say to her if you weren't afraid?* And he answered, haltingly at first and then faster, and I helped him find the words that were already in him, under the bad rhymes, waiting to be dug out.

The mountain lake stayed. Sim insisted, and he was right; Fela did have eyes like dark water, and it was the first thing anyone noticed about her. The night sky went. The river over stones stayed, but changed: Sim said her voice was not like a river but like the stones themselves, patient and quiet and very old, and I told him to write that down, and he did.

*A stellar* went. I made him promise.

---

By the time the candle guttered out, we had fourteen lines that were almost good. Not perfect. Sim would never be a great poet; his gift was for feeling, not for form. But they were honest, and clean, and they scanned, and every word in them was his.

He read it aloud one last time, in the dark, with the moon coming through my window.

When he finished, nobody said anything for a long moment.

"Well," said Wil at last, from my bed. His voice was rough. "That's not terrible."

Sim turned to him. "Really?"

"Really." Wil cleared his throat. "It's still not *good*. But it's not terrible." He paused. "If a man wrote that for me, I'd think about it."

Sim stared at him. Then he laughed, a startled, delighted laugh, and folded the paper very carefully and put it inside his shirt.

"Tomorrow," he said. "I'll give it to her tomorrow. In the Archives. After her shift."

---

He did not give it to her tomorrow. Or the next day. Or the next. He carried it around inside his shirt for nine days, and every day he came back from the Archives with his face pale and his hand pressed against his chest where the paper was, and every day he said *tomorrow*.

On the tenth day, Wil lost patience.

"Give it to me," he said.

"What?"

"The sonnet. Give it to me. I'll give it to her."

Sim went white. "You wouldn't."

"I would." Wil held out his hand. "I work the desk in the Archives. She comes past it every afternoon. I'll give it to her, and I'll say it's from you, and then it'll be done, and you can stop walking around like a man with a stomach ache."

"No!" Sim clutched his chest. "No, no, no. You can't. What if she laughs? What if she hates it? What if she—"

"What if she doesn't?" said Wil.

Sim stopped.

"You've been in love with her for three years," said Wil. His voice was very calm. "Three years, Simmon. You've brought her books and left them on her doorstep. You've walked her home in the rain. And then you said one stupid thing, and instead of saying you were sorry, you've spent two months writing poetry about it." He looked at Sim with his dark patient eyes. "What if she's been waiting for you to say something? What if she's been waiting all this time for you to just come and say it?"

Sim stared at him. His face was very pale.

"She hasn't," he said. "She can't be. She's Fela. She's—she's beautiful and brilliant and she knows the name of stone. And I said that stupid thing, and I'm—" He stopped. "I'm me."

"Yes," said Wil. "You are." He held out his hand again. "Give it to me."

---

Sim did not give it to Wil.

He gave it to Fela himself. That same afternoon. In the Archives. He walked up to her while she was shelving books in the Stacks, with his face white and his hands shaking, and he took the paper out of his shirt and held it out to her without a word.

I know, because Wil told me. He had followed at a discreet distance, to make sure Sim did not lose his nerve and run.

"She read it," said Wil. We were at Anker's that evening, the two of us. Sim was not with us. "Right there. Standing in the Stacks, with an armful of books. She read it twice. And then she looked up at him."

"And?"

Wil was quiet for a moment. His dark face was very still. But there was something in his eyes. Something warm.

"She said, *What took you so long?*" he said.

I stared at him. Then I began to laugh.

Wil did not laugh. Wil almost never laughed. But the corner of his mouth twitched, and kept twitching, and he picked up his mug of cider and drank from it to hide it.

"They're walking along the river," he said. "Right now. Holding hands." He set down his mug. "It's disgusting."

"It's wonderful."

"It's both." Wil looked at me. "Most wonderful things are."

---

I have thought about that sonnet many times since. About Sim, sitting at my narrow table in the dark, digging the true words out from under the bad ones. About Fela, standing in the Stacks with her arms full of books, reading it twice.

*What took you so long?*

I have thought about it because of what came after. Because of the Eolian, and the fire, and a girl kneeling in the dark with her hands pressed against the stone, holding up a building for three hours while it burned above her. Because of a young man with one eye and a black patch with a white star on it, weeping openly at the foot of a bridge. Because of a poem, very bad, that does not scan, lying folded in the bottom of a chest of roah wood in a room at the top of the stairs of an inn.

I have thought about it because it was the last easy thing. The last simple thing. A boy and a girl and an apology in fourteen lines and a walk along the river.

Everything after that was harder.

But they had that. Sim and Fela. They had the walk along the river, and the holding hands, and *what took you so long*. And no matter what came after—no matter the fire, or the war, or the door—no one could ever take that away from them.

I am glad I helped. It is one of the few things I did in those years that I have never regretted.
