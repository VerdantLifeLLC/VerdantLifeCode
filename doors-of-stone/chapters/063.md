# Selitos

The inside of the tower was warm.

That was the first thing I noticed, stepping through the door out of the glare and the cold. Warm and dim and smelling of woodsmoke and herbs and old paper. There was a fire burning in a round hearth in the center of the room, with a copper kettle hanging over it on an iron chain. There were shelves around the walls, curving with the curve of the tower, and they were full of books. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Old books, bound in leather and wood and things I did not recognize, their spines cracked and faded.

There was a table beside the fire, and three chairs. Three. As if he had known how many of us there would be, counting himself.

Selitos was standing at the hearth with his back to us, taking the kettle off its chain. He did not turn around as we came in.

"Sit," he said. "The tea is nearly ready."

We sat. I do not remember doing it. I remember only that one moment I was standing in the doorway, and the next I was sitting in a chair beside the fire with Bast in the chair beside me, and Selitos was setting three cups of tea on the table in front of us.

He sat down in the third chair. He looked at me.

Up close, his face was even older than it had seemed from across the plaza. The lines in it were so deep they looked carved. His skin was brown and weathered, like old leather. His beard was white and long and smelled faintly of smoke. And his eye—his one good eye—was grey and clear and very sharp.

The other eye, the stone, was worse up close. It was smooth and round and grey, polished like a river stone, set into the socket so perfectly that it seemed to have grown there. It caught the firelight and threw it back. And I had the strangest feeling, looking at it, that it was looking at me too. Not the way an eye looks. Some other way. Deeper.

"Drink," said Selitos. "You are cold."

I drank. The tea was hot and strong and tasted of pine needles and honey. It went down into me like fire, and I felt the warmth of it spread out through my chest and into my arms and legs. Even into my cold dead hand, a little.

Beside me, Bast was holding his cup in both hands and staring at Selitos over the rim of it with enormous eyes.

---

"You know who I am," said Selitos.

"Yes," I said.

"And you know why you have come."

"I think so."

"Tell me."

I told him. It came out of me the way it had come out for the Sithe, all in a rush, as if it had been waiting behind a dam. The Archives and the door. The line and the key. Bredon and Ambrose and Denna. The Eolian and the song and Haliax. The war, and Meluan, and the box in Ambrose's tent.

He listened without moving. His one good eye stayed on my face. The stone eye glinted in the firelight. When I was done, he was silent for a long time.

"You sang the song," he said at last. "The true one. In a great hall, before many people."

"Yes."

"And you put in the child."

I stared at him. "How do you know that?"

"Because I heard it." He touched the stone in his eye socket, very lightly, with one long finger. "This does not see the world, as my other eye does. It sees names. True things. And when a true thing is spoken, anywhere in the world, loudly enough—" He paused. "I hear it. The way you hear a bell ringing in a distant town."

"You heard the song. From here."

"I heard it. From here." His voice was very quiet. "I heard the white city on the last day of its life. I heard Lyra. I heard Lanre. I heard myself, weeping among the ruins." He was silent for a moment. "And I heard the child. The boy in the fountain. Asking to be a singer." He looked at me. "No one has ever put in the child. Not in all these years. Not in all the songs."

"Skarpi told me."

"Yes. I thought he might have." Something moved in Selitos's face. It was very small. It might have been a smile. "He was always a good boy. A little too fond of taverns, perhaps. But a good boy."

---

He asked me to sing it.

Not all of it. Just the end. The last verse. The one about the child.

I did not want to. My throat was still raw from the cold, and my left hand was still stiff, and I was more tired than I had ever been in my life. But I could not refuse him. I do not think anyone could.

So I took out my lute, and I tuned it with my stiff cold fingers, and I sang it. There, in the round tower at the center of the ruins of Myr Tariniel, before the man who had dug the child out of the rubble with his own hands.

When I finished, Selitos was very still. His one good eye was closed. There was a single tear on his cheek, running down into his white beard.

The stone eye was open. It was looking at me.

"Thank you," said Selitos, without opening his good eye. "Thank you, singer."

---

We sat by the fire for a long time after that. Selitos did not speak. Neither did I. Bast sat very still in his chair, holding his cup of tea, which had long since gone cold.

At last Selitos opened his eye.

"You have come to ask me how to close the door," he said. "For good. So that no one can ever open it again."

"Yes."

"And you think I know."

"You made it."

"I did." He nodded slowly. "I made it. And the key. And the line." He looked into the fire. "I made a great many things, in those days. After the war. When everything was broken, and someone had to put it back together." He was silent for a moment. "Some of them I would unmake, if I could."

"Can you?"

"No." He shook his head. "I am very old, singer. Older than anything you have ever met, except perhaps the thing behind the door. And the Tree. And one or two others." He smiled faintly. "My power is not what it was. I could not unmake the door now if I wanted to. I could not even reach it. It is very far from here, and I cannot leave this valley."

"Why not?"

He looked at me with his one good eye. "Because I am keeping watch," he said. "Over this place. Over the ruins of the city Lanre burned. I promised, that night, among the ashes. I promised I would stay here and remember. So that someone would." He was quiet for a moment. "I have kept that promise for a very long time."

"Alone?"

"Mostly." He glanced at Bast. "Sometimes the Fae come. Sometimes mortals. Very rarely. The mountains are not kind to visitors." He looked back at me. "You are the first in nearly three hundred years."

---

"Tell me about the door," I said. "Please. Tell me how it works."

Selitos looked at me for a long time. Then he set down his cup, and folded his long hands on the table in front of him.

"The door," he said, "is a name."

I waited.

"All doors are names. You know that, I think. You have heard stone. You know that everything that is, is its name, and that to know a thing's name is to know what it is." He looked at me. "The doors of stone are names too. Seven of them. Seven names, spoken into seven stones, in seven places. And together they make a single name. A very long one. A very old one. The name of the prison."

"And the keeper's door?"

"The keeper's door is different." Selitos was quiet for a moment. "When I made it, I spoke its name into the stone, as I did with the others. But I did something else, too. I wove another name into it. A second name, braided through the first, like two threads in a cord." He looked at me. "The name of the line."

I sat very still.

"The line of the Thief's daughter," said Selitos. "Loeclos, and Lockless, and Lackless. I took the name of that line—not the name of any one person in it, but the name of the line itself, the thing that runs through all of them like a river through a valley—and I wove it into the door. So that the door and the line would be bound together. So that the door would know them, and they would know the door."

"So that only they could open it."

"So that only they could open it. And so that only they could *keep* it." He paused. "A door must be tended. It must be watched. It must be checked, from time to time, to make sure it holds. And the only ones who could do that were the ones the door would answer to."

"And the key?"

Selitos was silent for a long time. Then he raised his hand, and touched the stone in his eye socket.

"This," he said.

---

I stared at him.

"When Lanre burned the city," said Selitos quietly, "and I stood among the ruins and cursed him, I took up a stone. A shard of stone from the city's heart, the great white stone at the center of the plaza, the one that had held the name of Myr Tariniel since it was founded. And I put out my own eye with it."

"I know."

"Do you know why?"

"So you would never be deceived again. So you could see true."

"That is what the stories say. And it is true, in its way." He lowered his hand. "But there was another reason. I did not know it at the time. I only knew that I needed to do it. That I needed to take something from myself, to pay for what I had done. For the curse." He was quiet for a moment. "Later, I understood. When I made the doors. When I made the key."

"The stone," I said slowly. "You split it."

"I split it." He nodded. "One half I kept, here, in my eye. So that I could see. So that I would always know what the key was doing, where it was, whose hands held it. The other half I put into a box with no lid and no lock, and I gave it to the Thief's daughter, and I told her to keep it forever."

"The key of stone."

"The key of stone. Half of my own eye. Half of the heart of Myr Tariniel." Selitos looked at me. "It knows the door, because I made the door. It knows the curse, because I spoke the curse with it in my hand. It knows me, and it knows Lanre, and it knows the line." He paused. "It is the most dangerous thing in the world, singer. And it is in the hands of a boy king who does not know what it is."

---

"You've been watching it," I said. "Through your eye. You've seen where it went."

"I have seen." His voice was very quiet. "I saw it in the Lackless house, for a thousand years, passed from hand to hand, from mother to daughter. I saw it in the hands of a frightened woman in a green room, who slept with it under her pillow." He looked at me. "I saw it in your hands. For one night. And then in a girl's hands, riding through the dark. And then in an old man's hands. And then in a boy king's tent."

"You saw all of that."

"I saw all of that." He was silent for a moment. "And I could do nothing. I cannot leave this valley. I cannot reach the key. I can only watch." His one good eye was very steady. "I have watched the old man for a very long time, singer. The one you call Bredon. I watched him take the Amyr apart, one by one, over centuries. I watched him cut the truth from the world's books. I watched him move his pieces toward the door." He looked at me. "I knew his name, once. Before. When he was young, and one of mine."

My heart stopped.

"One of *yours*?"

"He was Amyr," said Selitos. "One of the first. One of the finest. I gave him his tattoo with my own hands." He looked into the fire. "He was the one who carried the Book of Watches down to the Chamber, beneath the place that became your Archives. He was the first to keep that watch."

"And then?"

"And then he sat in that chamber, alone, in the dark, beside the door. For years. Listening to the breathing on the other side." Selitos's voice was very soft. "And one day, the thing behind the door spoke to him. And he answered."

---

The fire crackled. Outside, the wind moaned softly through the ruins.

"He was the Knife," I said. "The one in the last page of the Book of Watches. *He comes as a friend. He comes as a teacher.*"

"He was the Knife." Selitos nodded. "He cut his own order apart, from the inside. He knew where they all were, and what they knew, and how to find them. He gave them to the Seven. One by one." He looked at me. "The last of them wrote that page, in the Chamber of Watches, the night before he came for her. She knew he was coming. She wrote the warning so that someone, someday, would know."

"Devi found it."

"Yes. I saw that too." A very faint smile. "She is a clever girl. Too clever for her own good. Like you."

I sat very still with my cold hands wrapped around my cup. I thought about Bredon. About his kind grandfather's face, and his flat cold eyes. About the tak board in the garden, and the shape of the door built along its edges.

"What's his name?" I asked. "His true name. Before he was Bredon."

Selitos looked at me for a long time.

"I will not tell you that," he said.

"Why not?"

"Because a name is a weapon, singer. And you are angry. And angry men should not be given weapons." He picked up his cup. "Besides. You do not need his name to stop him. You need something else entirely."

"What?"

Selitos looked at me over the rim of his cup. His one good eye was grey and clear and very old. The stone eye glinted in the firelight.

"You need to give up your own," he said.
