# Snow on the Underthing

The snow came the night before Ambrose left the University, without any wind, and by morning the whole of it was white and silent and strange.

I went to see Auri before breakfast. I had not meant to. I had meant to go to the Archives, where I had a stack of scriv ledgers waiting for me and a headache already waiting behind them. But when I came out of my room at Anker's and saw the rooftops, white and soft and unbroken as a sheet of new paper, I found my feet taking me the other way.

She was on the roof of the Mains. Of course she was. She was standing at the very edge of the great slanting slope of lead and slate above the courtyard, barefoot in the snow, with her arms held out from her sides like a child pretending to be a bird. Her hair was a pale cloud around her head, and there were snowflakes caught in it, and she was looking up at the sky with her mouth open.

I climbed up by my usual way, over the apple tree and the gutter and the long low roof of the tanning shed. When I came over the last ridge, she heard me and turned, and her whole face lit.

"Kvothe!" she said. "Look! Look what it did!"

"I see it."

"It came in the night, without asking." She turned in a slow circle, her arms still out. "It didn't knock or anything. It just came down and sat on everything. Very politely. Very quietly." She stopped and looked at me, delighted. "It's the quietest thing there is. Did you know that? Quieter than moonlight. Quieter than the Underthing."

I looked at her bare feet in the snow. They were pink with cold.

"Auri," I said. "Aren't your feet cold?"

She looked down at them as if she had not noticed they were there.

"Yes," she said, with great seriousness. "Very. It's proper. You shouldn't walk on snow unless your feet know it. Otherwise you're being rude." She wiggled her toes. "I'm introducing myself."

---

I sat down on the ridge of the roof. The slates were cold through my trousers. After a moment, Auri came and sat beside me, tucking her feet up under the hem of her dress.

"I brought you something," I said.

It was a pair of socks. Thick grey wool, knitted by the old woman who sold them from a basket on the Stonebridge. I had bought them with the last jot in my purse the week before, thinking of exactly this.

Auri looked at them for a long time. She did not take them.

"They're very nice socks," she said at last.

"They're for you."

"I know." She looked at them sadly. "But they're not *for* me. Do you see? They're for someone who wears socks." She reached out and touched one with a fingertip, very gently, the way you might touch a sleeping bird. "I'm not someone who wears socks. Not yet. Maybe someday. But not now."

I did not know what to say. I held the socks in my lap.

"You could give them to the stones," Auri suggested. "The ones by the chimney. They're always cold. They'd like socks."

So I got up and went to the chimney at the end of the roof, and I tucked the socks into the crack between two of the stones, where the snow had not reached. Auri watched me with grave approval.

"There," she said. "Now they know someone cares about them. That's important. Everything should know someone cares about it."

---

We sat for a long time after that. Below us, the University was waking. Students crossed the courtyard in dark cloaks, leaving lines of footprints in the snow. Someone was laughing somewhere. The bells in the Chancellor's tower rang the hour, and the sound was muffled and strange, as if the snow had wrapped it in wool.

"Kvothe," said Auri. "Can I tell you a secret?"

"Always."

She leaned close. Her breath smelled of apples.

"The snow goes all the way down," she whispered.

I looked at her.

"Into the Underthing," she said. "Not the snow itself. The snow stays up here, where it belongs. But the *quiet* goes down. Through the grates, and the cracks, and the old drains. All the way down, into the deepest places." Her eyes were very wide. "Places that are never quiet. Places that are always humming, or ticking, or sighing. When the snow comes, they go quiet too. Just for a little while."

"Which places?"

Auri looked at me for a long moment. Then she looked away.

"The door," she said. "The one that isn't a door. Down past Tumbrel and the Old Throat. It hums, all the time. Very low. You can hardly hear it. But I can." She hugged her knees. "Not when it snows. When it snows, it goes quiet. And I can sleep."

I did not say anything for a moment. My heart was beating hard.

"Auri," I said carefully. "Does the door ever do anything else? Besides humming?"

She did not answer right away. She picked up a handful of snow from the slates beside her, and looked at it, and let it fall between her fingers.

"Sometimes it listens," she said. "That's worse than the humming." She brushed her hands off on her dress. "When it listens, I sing to it. Very softly. Nursery songs. The kind you sing to babies so they'll sleep." She looked at me. "It likes that. It stops listening, and goes back to humming, and I can go away."

"Who taught you to do that?"

"No one." She looked surprised. "It's just proper. When something is listening in the dark and it's frightened, you sing to it. Everyone knows that."

---

I did not know that. Not then. I thought about it later, many times. I thought about it on the ridge above a burning camp with a baby in my arms, and I thought about it in a cell in the Tower of Thorns, listening to a man tap on the stones, and I thought about it in an inn at a crossroads, on a night when the wind came looking for me and I told it, very gently, to go away.

*When something is listening in the dark and it's frightened, you sing to it.*

It was the truest thing anyone ever told me about doors. I did not understand it for years.

---

"Will you come down?" said Auri. "Into the Underthing? I want to show you."

"Show me what?"

"The snow-quiet." She stood up, brushing snow off her dress. "You have to see it. It only lasts a day, maybe two, and then the humming comes back." She held out her hand. "Please?"

I took her hand. It was very cold and very small. And I followed her down off the roof of the Mains and across the snowy courtyard, where students turned to stare at the barefoot girl and the red-haired boy walking hand in hand, and through a grate beside the Masters' Hall that I had not known opened, and down.

---

It was quiet in the Underthing.

I had been there many times by then. I knew the Underthing the way you know a house you visit often: not all of it, but the rooms that matter. I knew Mantle and the Twelve and Billows. I knew the Crumbledown and the Downside and the Grey Tree. I knew that the Underthing was never silent. There was always water running somewhere, and wind moving through old pipes, and the settling and creaking of stone, and a hundred other small sounds that I had long since stopped hearing.

That day, they were gone.

Not all of them. The water still ran. The wind still moved. But underneath them there had always been something else, something I had not known I was hearing until it stopped. A low, faint, constant hum, so deep it was more a feeling than a sound, like the vibration of a great bell long after it has been struck.

It was gone. And the silence it left behind was enormous.

We walked through Billows with our footsteps echoing, and through the Twelve, and down the long sloping tunnel that Auri called Tenders. She did not speak. Neither did I. It did not seem like a place for speaking.

At the end of Tenders, there was a place where three tunnels came together. Auri stopped there. She pointed down the left-hand tunnel, which sloped steeply into darkness.

"That way," she said, very softly. "Past the Old Throat. That's where the door is." She looked at me. "We won't go all the way. It's not polite, when it's sleeping. But you can listen from here."

I listened.

And there, very faint, very far away, at the very edge of hearing, I heard something. Not a hum. Something else. Something slow and steady and soft, rising and falling, rising and falling.

Breathing.

I felt the hair rise on my arms.

"It's asleep," Auri whispered. "The snow makes it sleep. Isn't it lovely?" She was smiling. "It's like a baby. When it's asleep, it's just a baby. You'd never know it could be anything else."

---

We went back up after a while. Auri took me the long way, through the Grey Tree and the Crumbledown, and showed me a place where snow had drifted down through a broken grate and made a little white hill on the stone floor, pristine and perfect, like something in a story. She would not let me touch it.

"It came all this way," she said. "It should be allowed to rest."

When we came out into the courtyard, the light was already fading. I had missed breakfast and lunch and three shifts in the Archives, and Lorren would be furious, and I did not care at all.

"Thank you," I said. "For showing me."

Auri looked at me very seriously.

"You needed to know," she said. "Everyone who walks in the Underthing should know that it's asleep sometimes. So they can be quiet." She tilted her head. "And so they know it's there. When it's awake."

She turned to go. Then she stopped, and turned back.

"Kvothe," she said. "The socks. Will you check on them? In the spring? To see if the stones liked them?"

"I promise."

She smiled. And then she was gone, back through the grate beside the Masters' Hall, into the dark.

---

I did check on the socks, that spring. They were still there, tucked into the crack between the chimney stones, damp and grey and a little moldy.

And the stones on either side of them, I swear, were warm to the touch.

I told Auri. She was not surprised at all.
