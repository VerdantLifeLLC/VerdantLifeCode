# The Night of the Chest

Once a year, I tried to open the chest.

I do not know why I chose the night I chose. It was not the anniversary of anything, that I knew of. It was not the night we came to Newarre, or the night the chest was made, or the night on the bridge when Denna walked away. It was a night in early spring, when the snow had begun to go grey at the edges and the icicles dripped all day long from the eaves of the Waystone. I did not choose it. It chose me. Every year, on that night, I would wake in the dark with my heart pounding and my left wrist aching, as if something had pulled on it, and I would know.

I found out, years later, what night it was. Bast told me. He had worked it out, the second year, counting on his fingers in the kitchen.

It was the night of the Nighting. The night of lights. The night, years before, when I had gone down through the Underthing with Auri's lamp and stood in front of a door that was not a door, beneath the Archives, and heard my name.

---

The first year, I did not know what I was doing. I woke in the dark and I got up and I went to the foot of my bed, where the chest sat under its canvas cover, and I knelt in front of it. I pulled the cover off. I looked at it.

It was a beautiful thing. Even I could see that, grey as I was. Dark reddish wood, smooth as glass, without a single nail or seam. It smelled faintly of cinnamon and old rain. There was a copper lock on the front, shaped like a closed eye. And beside it, a lock of grey stone, carved with a small, smooth hollow, like the print of a thumb. And between them, sunk into the wood itself, a knot. Dark and small and very tight. I could not tell where it began or where it ended.

I put my hand on the lid.

It was cold. Not the cold of wood in an unheated room. A deeper cold. The cold of a thing that does not want you. I felt it in my palm and up my arm and into my chest, where the hole was.

I tried to lift the lid. It did not move. Of course it did not move. It was locked. I had watched them lock it. I had stood in the Fishery, the night it was made, and watched Kilvin set the copper lock and Fela set the stone and Denna sink her knot into the wood between them.

I did not remember that. Not then. But my hands did. They went to the copper lock, and found the keyhole shaped like a closed eye, and felt it. They went to the stone lock, and pressed a thumb into the smooth hollow. They went to the knot in the wood, and touched it, very lightly.

Nothing happened.

I knelt there until morning. Then I put the cover back on and went downstairs and opened the inn.

---

The second year, Bast was waiting for me.

I did not know he would be. I woke in the dark, the way I had the year before, with my wrist aching, and I got up and went to the chest and knelt in front of it. And when I pulled off the cover, I heard a small sound behind me. A breath, quickly caught.

I turned. Bast was sitting in the corner of my room, in the dark, with his knees drawn up to his chin. He had been there, I think, all night.

"Reshi," he said. His voice was very soft. "What are you doing?"

"I don't know."

"You did this last year. I heard you. On the same night." He did not move from the corner. "Do you know what you're trying to do?"

I looked at the chest.

"Open it," I said.

"Why?"

I did not answer. I did not know. I only knew that my wrist ached, and the hole in my chest ached, and there was something inside the chest that I needed, the way a thirsty man needs water. I did not know what it was. I did not know how I knew.

"You can't," said Bast. "Reshi. You know you can't. Only you can open it. The real you. Not—" He stopped.

"Not Kote."

"Not Kote." Bast's voice cracked. "I'm sorry."

---

I tried anyway. I put my hand on the copper lock. It was cold. I put my thumb in the hollow of the stone lock. It was cold. I touched the knot.

And the stone lock spoke.

Not in words. Stone does not speak in words. But I had spent a winter in the Underthing, years before, learning to listen to stone. My hands remembered, even if I did not. And when I pressed my thumb into the hollow of Fela's lock, I heard it. Very faint. Very deep. Very patient.

*No.*

It was not unkind. That was the thing I remember most. It did not say *no* the way a door says it, or a guard, or an angry master at the University. It said it the way a mother says it, to a child reaching for a hot stove. Gently. Firmly. With love.

*No. Not yet. Not you.*

I took my hand away. I sat back on my heels on the floor of my room in the dark and looked at the chest for a long time.

"It said no," I said.

Bast made a small sound in the corner.

"I know," he said. "I heard it too."

---

After that, he always waited with me. Every year. On the night of the Nighting. He never said anything about it, during the day. He never asked if I was going to try. But when I woke in the dark with my wrist aching and went to the chest, he was always there. In the corner. With his knees drawn up. Keeping me company.

The third year, I tried the copper lock. I tried to remember what Kilvin had told me about sympathy locks. I tried to hold the binding, the way I used to, with my mind split in two, the Heart of Stone. I could not. I did not know how anymore. The lock stayed cold under my hand.

The fourth year, I tried the knot. I sat for hours with my fingers on it, feeling the shape of it, trying to find the end. There was no end. There was no beginning. It was a knot tied by someone who knew more about knots than anyone in the world, and it did not want to be found.

The fifth year, the year the scrael came, I did not try anything. I knelt in front of the chest with my hands in my lap and I did not touch it at all. I only looked at it.

"What are you doing, Reshi?" said Bast, from the corner.

"Listening," I said.

And I was. I was listening to the chest. To the wood, and the locks, and whatever was inside. I did not hear anything. The waystone outside the window drowned it all out, the way it drowned everything. But I listened anyway, all night, the way you listen at a door for someone you love, on the other side, breathing.

---

The sixth year was the year Shep died.

I did not wait for the Nighting that year. I went to the chest the night after we buried him. I knelt in front of it and pulled off the cover and put both hands flat on the lid, the way you put your hands on a door you want to open very badly, and I pushed.

I did not push with my hands. I pushed with everything. With the hole in my chest, and the grey, and the memory of the thing in the stranger's skin, and the nothing that had come out of my mouth when I tried to name it. I pushed with Shep's face, and his widow's weeping, and the fresh raw earth over his grave.

*Open*, I said. Not out loud. Somewhere else. Somewhere deeper. *Open. Please. I need it. I need it back. People are dying. Open.*

The chest did not open.

But the stone lock spoke again. Louder than before. So loud I felt it in my teeth.

*No. Not you. Not yet.*

And then, very faint, almost too faint to hear, something else. Not from the stone. From the knot. From the small dark knot sunk into the wood between the locks. A voice I did not recognize, and knew better than my own.

*Not like this. Not alone. Wait.*

---

I did not remember, then, whose voice it was. I only knew that I had heard it. And that it had said *wait*. And that there had been something in it that made the hole in my chest ache in a new way. Not the old dull grey ache. Something sharper. Something that felt almost like hope.

I took my hands off the lid. I sat back. And I wept. For the first time in six years. For Shep, and for his widow, and for Aaron standing over the thing with the iron bar in his shaking hands. For myself, kneeling on the floor in front of a box I could not open, with my name inside it. For all of it.

Bast came out of the corner. He knelt beside me on the floor. He did not say anything. He only put his arms around me and held on, the way you hold on to someone who is drowning.

"She said wait," I said, into his shoulder. "She said *not alone*."

Bast went very still.

"Who, Reshi?"

"I don't know." I did not. I truly did not. "But she said wait."

Bast held me for a long time. And then he said, very quietly, so quietly I almost did not hear it:

"Then we'll stop waiting alone."

---

That was the night Bast began to plan. He told me so, later, on the last night, kneeling on the floor of the common room with his face wet. That was the night he went looking for Skarpi, and found him, and was told about a scribe on the road. That was the night he started talking in his room at the back of the inn, to someone who was not there.

I did not know. I did not know anything. I got up off the floor in the morning and put the cover back on the chest and went downstairs and opened the inn. The regulars came, and I served them, and I polished the glasses, and I was Kote.

But I did not try to open the chest again. Not the next Nighting. Not the one after. I did not need to. It had told me what it had to tell me.

*Not like this. Not alone. Wait.*

So I waited. And I was not alone. And one autumn, a scribe came up the road with his satchel and his bloody knuckles.

---

"The knot," said Chronicler slowly. "The voice in the knot. That was—"

"Denna." The innkeeper looked at the fire. "Her knot. The one she sank into the wood, the night we made it. She tied something of herself into it. Not her name. Something smaller. A little piece of her voice, maybe. A little piece of what she wanted." He was quiet. "She knew. She knew I'd try to open it, someday, alone, when things got bad enough. She knew I'd break myself on it, trying. So she tied a message into the lock. For the day I did."

"*Not alone. Wait.*"

"Yes." The innkeeper looked at his hands. Then he reached into the pocket of his apron and took out something small, and set it on the table between them.

It was a knot. A single knot, tied in a short length of cord braided from dark hair. Small and neat and very tight.

Chronicler had seen it before. When the old tinker with the bright black eyes had pressed it into the innkeeper's hand across the bar, and the innkeeper had gone white.

"I didn't know what it was," said the innkeeper quietly. "When he gave it to me. I only knew that when I held it, the hole in my chest stopped aching. For the first time in seven years." He looked at the knot. "And tonight, telling you this, I think I finally understand what she meant. That night. In the lock."

"*Not alone. Wait.*"

"There was more," said the innkeeper. "I couldn't hear it, then. It was too faint. But I think I know what it was." He touched the knot with one finger, very gently. "*Not alone. Wait. I'll send someone.*"

He looked up at Chronicler. At the scribe, and the pen, and the pages.

"She sent two," he said. "A tinker with a knot. And a scribe with a pen." He almost smiled. "She always did like to be sure."
