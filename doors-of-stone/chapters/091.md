# Roah

They made the chest in the Fishery, on a single night in late autumn, with the doors locked and the windows shuttered and a fire burning in every forge.

Kilvin allowed it. I learned later that he had argued with Herma for two spans about it, and with Lorren for three, and that in the end he had simply stopped arguing and told them that he was the Master Artificer, and that the Fishery was his, and that if he chose to let a man who had been expelled and hanged and was supposedly dead come into it for one night, that was his business and no one else's. And then he had gone back to his office and closed the door, and no one had dared to knock on it for a span.

I did not know any of this at the time. I did not know Kilvin. I did not know the Fishery. I was only brought across the bridge in the dark, under a hood, with Bast on one side of me and Elodin on the other, and led through the silent courtyards of the University to a great stone building that smelled of hot metal and coal smoke and quenching oil.

And something in me stirred when I smelled it. Very faintly. The way a sleeping dog stirs at a familiar voice.

---

The wood was roah.

Kilvin showed it to me when I came in. He was standing beside a long workbench in the center of the great room, with his hands resting on a stack of dark planks. He was a very big man, broad and bearded and shaggy, with hands like shovels, blackened and scarred by a lifetime of work. When he saw me, his face did something complicated beneath the beard.

"Kvothe," he said. His voice was a low rumble.

"Kote," said Denna, beside me. "He's Kote now. For a while."

Kilvin looked at her. Then at me. He was quiet for a long moment.

"Kote," he said at last. He said it carefully, the way you say a word in a language you are not sure of. "Very well." He looked down at the planks under his hands. "This is roah. Do you know it?"

"No."

"It is a very rare wood. Very dense. Very heavy. It grows only in one valley in the far north, beyond the Small Kingdoms, and it takes three hundred years for a tree to grow thick enough to cut." He ran his broad hand along the top plank. It was almost black, with a faint reddish tinge in the grain, and it was so smooth that it seemed to drink the light. "It does not burn easily. It does not rot. It does not warp or crack or split. It is very stubborn wood." He looked at me. "It says no. Like stone. But it is alive, or was. It remembers being a tree. It remembers growing. It knows how to hold something that is alive."

He was quiet for a moment.

"I have had these planks for eleven years," he said. "I bought them from a trader who did not know what they were. I have been saving them. I did not know for what." He looked at me with his dark eyes under his shaggy brows. "Now I know."

---

They worked all night.

I cannot tell you everything they did. I did not understand most of it, and I have forgotten more. But I remember pieces. I will tell you the pieces.

I remember Kilvin at the workbench, with a plane in his great hands, shaving the roah planks smooth. Long pale curls of wood falling to the floor around his feet. He worked slowly and steadily, without hurry, the way a man works who has been doing a thing for forty years and knows that hurry is the enemy of good work. He did not use a single nail. He cut the joints by hand, dovetails and mortises, so tight and so perfect that when he fitted the planks together, you could not slide a hair between them.

I remember Wil, sitting at a smaller bench beside him, with his scarred hands working carefully on a lump of copper. He could not do fine sympathy anymore. But he could still do fine metalwork, if he went slowly. He was making a lock. A plain copper lock, heavy and solid, with a keyhole the shape of a small closed eye.

"Kilvin designed it," he told me, without looking up from his work. "I'm just making it. It's a sympathy lock. Very old design. The key has to be bound to the lock by the one who owns it. Once it's bound, only that person can turn it." He paused. "We'll bind it to you. Tonight. While you're still—" He stopped.

"While I'm still what?"

Wil looked up at me. His dark face was very still.

"While you're still you," he said quietly. "Enough of you. With the cord on your wrist. So the lock knows you." He looked back down at the copper. "And then, when the cord's in the chest, and you're just Kote, you won't be able to open it. The lock won't know you. It'll be waiting for the other one."

---

I remember Fela, at a bench in the corner, with a block of grey stone in front of her and a small steel chisel in her hand. She was carving it into the shape of a lock. A stone lock, to sit beside the copper one on the front of the chest. She carved very slowly and very carefully, and every so often she stopped and put down her chisel and laid her palms flat on the stone and closed her eyes.

I watched her for a long time. At last she looked up and saw me watching.

"I'm asking it," she said. "To remember. What it's for. Who it's for." She held up the two small flat stones, the ones she had given me, the ones from the foundation of the Eolian. "These are going in it. At the heart of the lock. They remember you. They remember the night you sang, and the fire, and how they held." She fitted them carefully into a hollow she had carved in the center of the block. "They'll tell the lock who to open for."

"And who not to open for?"

Fela looked at me with her dark serious eyes.

"Everyone else," she said. "Everyone in the world. Even Kote."

---

I remember Devi, at the alchemical bench by the far wall, surrounded by glass vials and copper tubes and strange-smelling bottles. She was making something. She would not tell me what.

"Insurance," she said, when I asked. She did not look up. "In case anyone gets past the locks. In case anyone tries to force it, or break it, or burn it." She smiled her spider's smile. "They'll get a very nasty Surprise."

"What kind?"

"The kind that means nobody ever tries it again." She held up a small sealed vial, full of something that glowed faintly green. "I'm going to line the inside of the lid with this. Very thin. Very fine. You won't even know it's there." She paused. "Unless someone opens it without the key."

---

I remember Sim.

He was not making anything. He could not; he was a poet, not an artificer, and his hands were no good at wood or metal or stone. So he sat on a stool near the door, out of the way, with a sheaf of paper on his knee and a pen in his hand, and he wrote.

I went and sat beside him, after a while. He looked up at me with his one bright eye.

"What are you writing?" I asked.

"A poem." He looked down at the paper. "For the chest. To go inside it. With your name and everything else." He smiled crookedly. "Elodin says it can't hurt. He says names like company. He says a name shut up alone in a box might get lonely, and lonely names do strange things."

"What's it about?"

Sim was quiet for a long moment.

"You," he said at last. "The real you. The one in the cord. Everything I remember about you. The day we met, in the Mews. The day you played at the Eolian and won your pipes. The day you broke Ambrose's arm, and set his rooms on fire, and got whipped for it and didn't cry." He laughed softly. "All the stupid things you did. All the brave ones. All the times you were an idiot and all the times you were a hero." He looked down at the paper. "So your name will have something to read. While it's waiting."

I looked at him. At his thin eager face, and his black eye patch with the white star, and the paper on his knee covered in his cramped untidy handwriting.

"Sim," I said.

He looked up.

"Thank you."

He looked at me for a long moment. Then his face crumpled, just a little, and he looked back down at his paper.

"It's a terrible poem," he said thickly. "It doesn't scan. The rhymes are awful." He wiped his eye with the back of his hand. "You'd hate it."

"I'm sure I wouldn't."

"You would. You always hated my poems." He laughed wetly. "You used to tell me they sprawled. Like weeds."

"I don't remember."

"I know." He went back to writing. "That's why I'm writing it down."

---

And I remember Denna.

She did not make anything with her hands that night. Not wood, not metal, not stone. She sat beside me at the edge of the great room, holding my left hand, with the cord on its wrist. And she waited.

When the others were done—when Kilvin had fitted the last plank, and Wil had finished the copper lock, and Fela had set the stone lock beside it, and Devi had lined the inside of the lid with her glowing green insurance—they all came and stood around the chest. It sat on the long workbench in the center of the Fishery, in the firelight. A plain dark box, about the size of a small trunk. Smooth and heavy and without ornament. Two locks on its front: one copper, with a keyhole shaped like a closed eye; one stone, grey and plain, with no keyhole at all.

And a third place. Between the two locks. A small hollow, carved into the wood, the size of a thumb.

"That's for me," said Denna.

She let go of my hand. She reached into her sleeve and took out a cord. Thin and dark and braided. Her own hair.

She began to tie.

---

She tied a knot. A single knot. It took her a long time. Her hands moved very slowly, very carefully, looping and twisting and doubling back. When it was done, it was about the size of a thumbnail, very tight and very complex. It seemed to shift, when I looked at it, the way the knots in the king's hair had shifted.

She pressed it into the hollow between the two locks. It fit perfectly. She held it there with her thumb for a long moment, with her eyes closed, and her lips moving.

When she took her thumb away, the knot was part of the wood. I could still see it, faintly, the loops and twists of dark hair against the dark roah. But it was sunk into the grain, the way a fossil is sunk into stone.

"That's the third lock," she said. "Mine. It won't open unless the other two are open. And it won't open unless someone unties it." She looked at me. "And only one other knot in the world can untie it."

"Where's that knot?"

Denna did not answer. She reached into her sleeve again and took out another cord. Short, this time. Barely as long as her finger. With a single knot tied in it. The twin of the one in the lid.

She held it up.

"Here," she said. "I'm keeping it. Far away from here. Far away from you." She closed her hand around it. "If you ever need it—if the time ever comes when it's safe for you to be yourself again—it'll find its way to you."

"How?"

She smiled. It was a small, sad, secret smile.

"Knots are very good at finding their way home," she said.

---

Then they put things into the chest.

Kilvin opened the lid. The inside was dark and smooth and smelled of old wood. The faint green glow of Devi's insurance showed along the underside of the lid, like moss on a stone.

Sim went first. He folded his poem very carefully and laid it in the bottom of the chest.

Then Bast came forward. He had been standing at the back of the room all night, near the door, not speaking. His face was pale. His hands were bandaged, from the iron knife. He came forward now, and he had something in his hands. A small box of dark wood, smooth and polished, about the size of a loaf of bread. No lid. No lock. No seams.

It was humming. I could hear it, even from where I stood. Its long slow *no*.

"I picked it up," Bast said. His voice was very quiet. "In the chamber. After. From the floor, where you dropped it." He looked at me. "I've been carrying it ever since. In my pack. The whole way. Through the war, and Renere, and everything." He swallowed. "It's very heavy."

He laid it in the chest, beside Sim's poem.

Then Denna took my lute out of its case. She held it for a long moment, looking at it. Then she laid it in the chest too, very gently, on top of the box and the poem.

"Why?" I said. "Why the lute?"

Denna looked at me.

"Because it's yours," she said. "The real you. Not Kote. When you play it now, it's just a lute. But when you're you again—" She stopped. "When you're you again, you'll want it. And it'll be waiting."

---

And then it was my turn.

I stood in front of the chest. I held out my left hand. Denna took it. She held my wrist in both of her hands, and she looked at the cord for a long time.

"This is going to feel strange," she said. "Maybe worse than strange. It's been on your wrist for a year. It's gotten used to you. And you've gotten used to it." She looked up at me. "When I take it off, you'll feel—less. Even less than now. The hole will be bigger."

"I understand."

"Do you?"

"No," I said. "But do it anyway."

She looked at me for a long moment. Then she bent her head over my wrist. Her fingers found the knots. They moved, slowly, carefully, looping and twisting and undoing.

The cord came off.

---

I will tell you what it felt like, because I promised to tell the truth.

It felt like dying.

Not the pain of it. There was no pain. But the going. The going out. Like a candle flame, guttering. Like the last light going out of the sky at dusk. The cord came off my wrist, and something went with it. Something I had not known was still there. Some last thread, some last faint humming string, connecting the hole in my chest to the name in the cord.

It snapped. And I was empty.

Truly empty. For the first time. Not grey, now. Not ash. Nothing at all. Like the space behind the door, in the chamber beneath the Lackless house. Like the place between the stars.

I did not fall. I stood very still in front of the chest, with my empty wrist held out in front of me, and I looked at the cord in Denna's hands. At the knots, glowing faintly silver. At the thing that had been me.

It meant nothing to me. Nothing at all. It was a piece of string.

Denna laid it in the chest. On top of the lute. On top of the humming box, and Sim's poem.

Kilvin closed the lid.

The copper lock clicked. The stone lock settled. The knot in the wood between them seemed to tighten, very slightly, and go still.

And the Fishery was silent.

---

Everyone was looking at me. I could feel them. Kilvin, and Wil, and Fela, and Devi, and Sim, and Elodin by the door, and Bast beside him. And Denna, beside me, with her hands empty now, and her face white.

I looked back at them. I did not know any of them. I did not know where I was, or why, or what the dark box on the workbench was, or why everyone was looking at me.

"Kote," said Denna softly. "Kote. Can you hear me?"

*Kote*. I knew that word. It was a small word. A little one. A girl with cloud-hair had given it to me, on a roof, in the dark.

"Yes," I said.

"Do you know who I am?"

I looked at her. I did not know.

"No," I said.

She did not cry. I will always remember that. She had cried so many times, in the months since the cellar. But she did not cry then. She only nodded, very slowly. As if she had expected it.

"That's all right," she said. "That's all right, Kote. You don't have to."

She took my hand. My left hand. There was a mark on the wrist, where the cord had been. A thin red line, around and around, like a bracelet.

"Come on," she said. "It's late. Let's go home."

"Where's home?"

She looked at me for a long moment.

"I don't know yet," she said. "But we'll find it."
