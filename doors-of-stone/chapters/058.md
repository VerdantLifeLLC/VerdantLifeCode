# Bast

They did not let us go. Not right away. The Sithe are not a people who make hasty decisions, and they had two poisoned creatures in front of them instead of one, and they needed to think.

They took us to a place deeper in the birch wood, a small round clearing with a ring of white stones around its edge. They cut the young man out of the silver net, very carefully, and bound his hands with a cord of the same silver stuff. They did not bind mine. I think they did not think they needed to.

Then they sat down around the ring of stones, the five of them, and they talked.

They talked in their own language, which was not a language I knew, or anything like one. It was soft and cold and liquid, like water running under ice. They talked for a long time. I could not tell what they were saying. But I could tell, from the way the tall one kept looking at me, that they were talking about me.

The young man sat beside me in the center of the circle, with his bound hands in his lap. He did not speak. He was trembling. Every so often, he glanced at me sidelong with his deep blue eyes, and then glanced away again.

I looked at him when he was not looking at me.

He was beautiful. Not the way Felurian was beautiful, overwhelming and terrible. A quieter beauty. Fine-boned and graceful, with dark hair that fell over his forehead and a mouth that looked as if it had been made for laughing. He was dressed in clothes of deep green and brown, rich and soft, stained with mud from his struggles in the net. There was a thin circlet of dark metal on his brow, half hidden by his hair.

And his feet were hooves. Small and neat and cloven, dark as polished wood.

"Thank you," he said at last. His voice was soft and a little hoarse from screaming. "For stepping in front of me. That was very brave."

"It was very stupid."

"Those are often the same thing." He almost smiled. Then he looked at the Sithe, still talking in their liquid language, and the smile went away. "They're going to kill us both. You know that."

"Maybe."

"No. They are. I know the Sithe. They don't let anyone go who's been near the Tree." He looked down at his bound hands. "I was so stupid. I knew better. My father told me, a hundred times. *Never go near the Grove, Bast. Never, never, never.* And I went anyway."

"Why?"

He was quiet for a long time.

"I wanted to ask it something," he said at last. "Something I couldn't ask anyone else." He looked at me. "Why did you go? The first time?"

"I didn't mean to. I was lost. It called to me."

"It called to me too. That's the thing. It calls to everyone, if they're sad enough. Or lonely enough. Or curious enough." He looked away. "I was all three."

---

The Sithe finished talking. They stood up, all five, and came and stood in a circle around us again. The tall one looked down at us with her twilight eyes.

"We have decided," she said.

I waited.

"You," she said, looking at me, "are the key. The Thief's blood. The one the dark one needs. If we kill you, perhaps the door stays closed forever. Perhaps that is the best ending." She paused. "Or perhaps the dark one finds another way. Another child of the line. The one that is to be born, in the besieged city. And then the door opens anyway, and there is no one left who knows how to close it."

"Yes," I said.

"And perhaps," she went on, "that is the path the Tree wants. The one where we kill you. Perhaps it brought you here for that." She looked at me. "We cannot know. As you said. No one can know."

"No."

"So we have decided to do what the Adem would do. What you would do." She was silent for a moment. "We will choose. And we will bear what comes after."

"And what do you choose?"

The tall Sithe looked at me for a long time. Then she looked at the young man beside me.

"We will let you go," she said. "Both of you. On two conditions."

"What conditions?"

"First. You will never come back to the Moon's Road. Neither of you. Never again. If we ever see you here, we will kill you without a word." Her voice was very cold. "Second. You will take him with you."

I stared at her. "Take him?"

"He has been near the Tree. Very near. Whether he heard it or not, we cannot know. We cannot let him go back to the Twilight Court. If he is poisoned, he will poison them." She looked at the young man. "And we cannot let him wander the twilight alone. He is too young, and too curious, and too foolish. He will find his way back to the Grove, sooner or later. They always do." She looked back at me. "So you will take him. Into the mortal world. Where the Tree cannot reach. And you will keep him there."

"For how long?"

"Until he is no longer foolish," said the tall Sithe. "Which may be a very long time."

The young man made a sound. Half a laugh, half a sob.

"You're giving me to a *mortal*?" he said. "As a—what? A pet?"

"As a charge," said the tall Sithe. "He has shown he will die for you. That is more than most would do." She looked at me. "Will you accept?"

I looked at the young man beside me. At his deep blue eyes and his fine-boned face and his small dark hooves. At the circlet on his brow, and the silver cord around his wrists.

"Yes," I said.

---

The tall Sithe cut the cord around his wrists. She did it with a small knife of grey metal, very quickly, and then she stepped back.

"Go," she said. "North, on the Moon's Road. We will watch you until you reach the Northern Gate. If you stray, we will know." She looked at me. "And if you are wrong, mortal—if you are poisoned, and everything you do is the Tree's will—then may whatever gods you have forgive us. Because we will not forgive ourselves."

"And if I'm right?"

She looked at me for a long time. Something moved in her twilight eyes.

"Then we will sing of you," she said. "For a thousand years. As the mortal who walked the Moon's Road and came out clean."

She turned away. The other four turned with her. And then, between one breath and the next, they were gone, back into the birches, and the clearing was empty except for the two of us.

---

We walked north.

For a long time, neither of us spoke. I followed the silver string, and the young man walked beside me, a little behind, his hooves making soft clicking sounds on the stones of the path. He kept glancing at me sidelong. I kept pretending not to notice.

At last he said, "What's your name?"

I hesitated. The rules. *Never give your name.*

"You don't have to tell me," he said quickly. "I know the rules. I know mortals aren't supposed to. I just—" He stopped. "I just thought, since you're going to be keeping me, I should know what to call you."

"Kvothe," I said. "It's the name I'm called by. Not my true name."

"Kvothe." He tried it out. "That's a strange name."

"It's a strange life."

He laughed. It was a real laugh, sudden and bright, and it made him look younger. "I'm Bast," he said. "Bastas, really. Bastas, son of Remmen, Prince of Twilight and the Telwyth Mael." He made a small, ironic bow. "At your service. Apparently. Forever. Or until I'm no longer foolish, which my father would tell you is the same thing."

I looked at him.

"A prince," I said.

"Of a very small court. Very unimportant. Very far from anything." He shrugged. "My father has fourteen children. I'm the youngest. No one will miss me."

"Someone will."

He looked at me for a long moment. Then he looked away.

"Maybe," he said quietly.

---

We walked. The birches gave way to open country, rolling hills covered in grey-green grass that rippled in a wind I could not feel. The sky above was paler here, more silver than violet. Far ahead, on the horizon, I could see mountains. Or the shapes of mountains, faint and grey and very far away, like mountains in a dream.

"What did you want to ask it?" I said at last. "The Cthaeh."

Bast did not answer for a long time. I thought he was not going to. Then he said, very quietly, "My mother."

I waited.

"She was mortal," he said. "Did you know that? The Telwyth Mael have mortal blood in them. That's why we have these." He lifted one hoof and set it down again. "A long time ago, one of my ancestors fell in love with a mortal woman, and brought her into the twilight, and she bore him a son. We've been half-and-half ever since. Not quite Fae, not quite mortal. The other courts look down on us for it."

"And your mother?"

"My mother was mortal too. My father found her in your world, in the mountains, and brought her back. She lived with us for a long time. Longer than mortals are supposed to live." He was quiet for a moment. "And then one day she wasn't there anymore. She'd gone back. Through a greystone. To the mortal world. She didn't say goodbye."

"I'm sorry."

"I was very young. I didn't understand. I thought she'd come back. I waited and waited." He looked down at his hooves. "She never came back. My father wouldn't talk about it. Nobody would talk about it. And I thought—" He stopped.

"You thought the Cthaeh would know."

"It knows everything. That's what they say. It sees everything that's ever happened, and everything that ever will." He laughed bitterly. "I thought if I asked it, it would tell me why she left. Where she went. Whether she ever thought about me."

"It would have told you," I said. "But not the way you wanted."

He looked at me. "What do you mean?"

"It tells the truth," I said. "Always. That's what makes it so dangerous. It never lies. It just tells you the truth in the way that will hurt you most." I was quiet for a moment. "It would have told you exactly why your mother left. And it would have told you in a way that made you hate her. Or hate your father. Or hate yourself. And you would have gone back to the Twilight Court with that hate inside you, and it would have grown, and it would have done something terrible."

Bast was silent for a long time.

"Is that what it did to you?" he asked at last.

I did not answer.

---

That night, or what passed for night in the twilight, we stopped to rest in a hollow between two hills. There was a small stream there, and a stand of trees with leaves like silver coins, and soft grass to lie on.

I did not sleep. I sat with my back against a tree and my cold hand in my lap and Fela's stone in my good hand, and I watched the sky.

Bast lay in the grass a little way off, curled on his side with his hooves drawn up. I thought he was asleep. But after a while he said, without opening his eyes, "Kvothe."

"Yes?"

"Why did you do it? Step in front of me?" He opened his eyes and looked at me. "Really. Not the thing you told the Sithe. The real reason."

I thought about it for a long time.

"Because I was twelve once," I said at last. "And alone. And there was no one to step in front of me." I looked at him. "And I thought, if I ever had the chance, I'd be the one who did."

Bast looked at me for a long time. His deep blue eyes were very bright in the twilight.

"Then I owe you," he said. "A life-debt. The old kind." He sat up. "Do you know what that means, in the twilight?"

"No."

"It means I'm yours," said Bast simply. "Not as a pet. Not as a charge. As—" He struggled for the word. "As a student. A follower. Someone who goes where you go and learns what you know. Until the debt is paid."

"How long will that take?"

He smiled. It was a strange smile, sad and fond and a little wicked, all at once.

"Forever," he said. "Probably. You've got very bad luck, Kvothe. I can see it on you. You're going to need someone to watch your back."

"I don't need—"

"Everyone needs." He lay back down in the grass and closed his eyes. "Good night, Reshi."

"What does that mean? Reshi?"

"It's an old word," said Bast, without opening his eyes. "In the old tongue. It means *teacher*." A pause. "Or *master*. Or *one who is followed*." Another pause. "Or *fool who steps in front of arrows*. It depends on the context."

I laughed. I could not help it.

And Bast, lying in the silver grass with his eyes closed, smiled.
