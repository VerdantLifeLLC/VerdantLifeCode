# Chapter Five: Patience

Elodin's class met that term in a small round room at the top of the Rookery, a room I had never been in before and was never able to find again afterward. I do not mean that it moved. I mean that I climbed the stairs of the Rookery a hundred times in the years that followed, and I never once came to a door that led to that room. Elodin, when I asked him about it later, said only that some rooms are shy.

There were five of us that term. Fela, of course, who had been with Elodin longest and who sat in the chair nearest the window with her hands folded in her lap like a novice at prayer. Uresta, a quiet Cealdish boy with long fingers who had been studying with Elodin for two terms and had not, as far as anyone knew, spoken a single word in class. Brey, whom I had met before, a tall and cheerful young man from the Small Kingdoms who laughed too loudly at everything. Inyssa, a Vintish girl I did not know, who wore her hair cut short as a boy's and watched everything with eyes the color of slate.

And me.

Elodin came in late, as he always did. He was carrying a stone.

It was a plain stone, grey and roughly round, about the size of a man's fist. The kind of stone you might find in any field or riverbed. He set it on the floor in the center of the room and stood looking down at it with his hands in his pockets.

"This," he said, "is a stone."

We waited.

"It is a very old stone. Older than anyone in this room. Older than the University. Older than the Aturan Empire, or the empire before that, or the empire before that. It was here before there were people to name it, and it will be here after there are no people left to remember what it was called." He looked up at us. "What is it thinking?"

Nobody answered.

"Come now," Elodin said. "Somebody guess."

"Stones don't think," said Brey, and laughed.

Elodin looked at him. Brey stopped laughing.

"How do you know?" said Elodin.

"Because they don't have brains."

"Neither does the wind. Neither does fire. Neither does the sea. And yet I have known all three of them to have very strong opinions." Elodin crouched beside the stone. "Stone thinks. It thinks very slowly, and it thinks about very few things, and it almost never changes its mind. But it thinks." He looked around at us. "Your task for this term is to find out what."

"How?" I asked.

Elodin smiled at me. "By listening."

"To a stone."

"To a stone."

"For a term."

"For as long as it takes." He stood. "Some of you will hear it in a span. Some of you will hear it in a year. Some of you will never hear it at all, and you'll go back to your alchemy and your sympathy and your nice safe lives, and you'll be the better for it." He looked at me again. "And one of you will hear it, and then you'll wish you hadn't."

He walked to the door. "Class dismissed," he said, and left.

We sat in silence for a long moment. Then Brey laughed nervously. "Well," he said. "That was helpful."

Nobody answered him. We were all looking at the stone.

---

I want to tell you that I heard the stone that first day. I want to tell you that I sat in that round room and closed my eyes and let my mind go still, and the name of stone rose up in me like water in a well.

But it did not happen that way. It did not happen that way at all.

I listened to that stone for three spans. I sat with it in the round room for hours at a time, until the light through the window went from gold to grey to black. I carried it with me back to my room at Anker's and set it on the windowsill beside Kilvin's candle and stared at it until my eyes ached. I took it to the Fishery and set it on my bench while I worked. I took it to the Archives and set it on the table beside my books. I even, on one memorable occasion, took it to the Eolian and set it on the stage beside me while I played, which caused a great deal of confused whispering in the balconies and did nothing whatsoever to help me hear it.

It did not speak to me. Not a word, not a whisper, not so much as a sigh.

I tried everything. I tried the Heart of Stone, holding my mind still and cold and clear. I tried the opposite, letting my mind wander, letting it drift and dream, the way Elodin had once told me to do when I was trying to find the name of the wind. I tried Spinning Leaf, the mental trick that had helped me once before. I tried the breathing exercises the Adem had taught me, the long slow breaths that were meant to still the body and open the mind.

Nothing.

The stone sat on my windowsill and was a stone, and nothing more.

---

"You're trying too hard," said Fela.

We were sitting on the roof of the Mains together, where I had gone to get away from the stone for an hour and where she had come, she said, to see what I was doing. It was a cold, bright afternoon. The wind was sharp. Below us, the University spread out like a map.

"How can I try too hard?" I said. "I'm not trying at all. I'm just sitting there. Listening."

"No," Fela said. "You're waiting. That's different."

"What's the difference?"

She thought about it. "When you listen to someone," she said slowly, "you're not waiting for them to say something. You're just—there. With them. You're paying attention to them. You're not thinking about what you want them to say, or when they're going to say it. You're just letting them be." She looked at me. "When you sit with that stone, you're waiting for it to talk. You want it to talk. You're hungry for it. And the stone can feel that, and it doesn't like it."

"The stone can feel that."

"Of course it can." She shrugged. "Stone doesn't like hunger. Stone doesn't like anything that wants something from it. It's been here forever, and it'll be here forever, and everything that wants something from it eventually goes away. Rain wants to wear it down. Roots want to crack it open. Men want to cut it and carve it and build things out of it. And the stone just waits. It waits for all of them to go away." She smiled faintly. "It's very good at waiting. Much better than you."

I thought about that. "So how did you hear it?"

She was quiet for a long time. The wind blew her hair across her face and she pushed it back with one hand.

"I stopped wanting to," she said at last.

"What do you mean?"

"I mean I gave up." She laughed softly. "I'd been trying for months. I was so frustrated I wanted to scream. And one day I was sitting with it in the round room, and I just thought, *fine*. Fine. I'm never going to hear it. I'll just sit here with it, because it's quiet and it's peaceful and I like it here. And I stopped trying. I just sat with it." She looked out over the rooftops. "And after a while, I don't know how long, I realized I could hear it. It had been talking all along. I just hadn't been quiet enough to hear."

"What did it say?"

She looked at me. Her eyes were very dark.

"It said *no*," she said.

"No?"

"That's all stone ever says, really. No. No, I will not move. No, I will not change. No, I will not break." She shook her head. "Except that when you really hear it, it's not just no. It's—" She struggled for the words. "It's the most patient no in the world. It's a no that's been saying itself since before there was anyone to hear it. It's a no that will keep saying itself long after the last person is dead. It's not angry, and it's not afraid. It just *is*." She hesitated. "It's the most beautiful thing I've ever heard."

We sat in silence for a long time after that.

"I don't think I can do that," I said at last. "Stop wanting."

"I know." Fela smiled. "You've never stopped wanting anything in your life. It's one of the things I like about you." She stood up and brushed off her skirt. "But you'll have to learn. Elodin wouldn't have given you stone if he didn't think you needed it."

She started to walk away along the ridge of the roof. Then she stopped and turned back.

"Kvothe," she said. "When you do hear it. Be careful."

"Careful of what?"

She hesitated. "When I split that stone. The first time. I didn't mean to. It just happened. I heard the name, and I said it, and the stone broke." She looked down at her hands. "I've never been able to do it again. Not on purpose. But sometimes, late at night, I can still hear it. Stone. All around me. The walls, the floor, the foundations of the buildings. It's all saying no, all the time, very quietly. And sometimes—" She stopped.

"Sometimes what?"

"Sometimes," Fela said, "I hear something else. Underneath. Something that isn't saying no."

"What is it saying?"

She looked at me, and there was something in her face I had never seen before. It was fear.

"I don't know," she said. "I don't want to know."

And then she was gone, down the far side of the roof, and I was alone.

---

That night I went down into the Underthing.

I told myself that I was going to see Auri, and that was true, in its way. I brought her a pear and a small paper twist of salt and a ribbon of blue silk I had bought in Imre. But I also brought the stone. I carried it in my pocket, a hard round weight against my hip, and as I climbed down through the grate and into the dark, I felt it grow heavier.

Auri found me in the place she called Mantle, a long low room with a curving ceiling where an old chimney came down through the stone. She was sitting on a ledge with her feet dangling, humming to herself, and when she saw me her face lit up.

"Kvothe!" she said. "You came down."

"I did."

"You never come down." She tilted her head. "You always call me up."

"I wanted to see where you live," I said. "If that's all right."

She considered this seriously. Then she nodded. "That's all right," she said. "But you have to be polite. The Underthing doesn't like rude people."

"I'll be polite."

"And you have to be quiet. Some of the rooms are sleeping."

"I'll be very quiet."

She smiled at me and slid down off the ledge. "What did you bring?"

I gave her the pear, and she pronounced it "earnest." The salt was "honest." The ribbon made her go very still, and she held it in both hands and looked at it for a long time without saying anything. Then she tied it in her hair, carefully, and looked at me with shining eyes.

"Thank you," she said softly. "It's the color of being brave."

We walked through the Underthing together. Auri led the way, her bare feet silent on the stone, and I followed with my sympathy lamp held low. She showed me the places she loved. The Crumbling, where the ceiling had fallen in long ago and a thin shaft of moonlight came down from somewhere far above. The Twelve, a great round room with twelve doorways, each leading somewhere different. Tumbrel, with its family of little grey moths, who fluttered up around us as we passed and settled again on the damp walls like a scattering of ash.

And as we walked, I felt the stone in my pocket grow heavier and heavier.

It was not a real weight. I knew that. It was the same stone it had always been, no bigger, no denser. But it felt heavier, the way a word can feel heavier when you are about to say something you know you should not.

At last we came to a place I had never been before. It was a narrow passage that sloped downward, steeper than any I had seen in the Underthing. The walls were rough and unfinished. The air grew colder as we went.

"Where does this go?" I asked.

Auri stopped. She turned and looked at me, and her face was very serious.

"Down," she said.

"Down where?"

"Just down." She hesitated. "It's not a proper place. It doesn't have a name. I don't go there very often."

"Why not?"

She looked at the passage, sloping away into darkness. "Because it's too loud," she said.

I listened. I heard nothing. The passage was utterly silent, silent the way only deep places are silent, a silence so complete it seemed to have weight.

"I don't hear anything," I said.

Auri looked at me. There was something almost pitying in her eyes.

"You're not listening," she said.

And then, very quietly, she began to walk away, back the way we had come. After a moment I followed her. But as I turned, I felt the stone in my pocket shift, very slightly, as if it wanted to go the other way.

---

I did not hear the stone that night. I did not hear it for many nights after.

But I began to notice things. Small things. The way the walls of the Archives felt cooler under my hand than the walls of other buildings. The way the paving stones of the courtyard seemed to hum faintly underfoot on very cold mornings. The way the great grey bulk of the stone bridge seemed, when I stood at the top of its arch and looked down at the river, to be holding its breath.

I did not know what any of it meant. I told myself it was nothing. I told myself it was the product of too little sleep and too much listening, the way a man who stares too long at a blank wall will begin to see faces in it.

But at night, lying in my narrow bed with the stone on the windowsill beside the candle, I would sometimes wake with a start, my heart pounding, certain that I had heard something. A voice. A word. A single long syllable, very deep and very slow, spoken from somewhere far below me.

And every time, when I lay still and listened, there was nothing. Only silence. Only the wind moving across the rooftops, and the distant sound of the river, and the slow patient silence of stone.

It would be months before I understood what I had been hearing. And by then, of course, it was too late.
