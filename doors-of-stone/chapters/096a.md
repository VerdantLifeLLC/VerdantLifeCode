# The Priest

The priest came to see me in my second month at the Waystone.

His name was Father Anselm, and he was the only Tehlin priest within a day's ride of Newarre. He served four villages, riding from one to the next on a patient grey mule, holding services in each on a different day of the span. In Newarre, it was Mourning. He said the service in the little stone church behind the square, and then he came to the inn for his dinner, the way he had done, Graham told me, for twenty years. Old Bentley had always given it to him free.

He was a small man, Father Anselm, and very old, with a round pink face and wispy white hair and eyes the washed-out blue of a winter sky. His robes were grey and patched at the elbows. He came into the common room on my first Mourning with his hat in his hands, and stood inside the door, and looked at me behind the bar.

"You'll be the new one," he said.

"Yes, Father."

"Kote, they tell me." He came over and sat down on a stool at the bar with a small grunt of relief. "Just Kote?"

"Just Kote."

He looked at me for a moment with his faded blue eyes. Then he nodded.

"Well, Kote," he said. "Bentley used to give me the stew and a cup of the brown. I don't suppose you'd be willing to carry on the tradition? I'm a poor man, and the mule eats more than I do."

I gave him the stew and a cup of the brown. I did not charge him. I did not charge him the next Mourning, either, or any Mourning after, for seven years.

---

He came every span. He sat at the same stool, at the end of the bar near the fire, and ate his stew slowly, and talked. Not to me, particularly. To anyone. To the room. He talked about his other villages, and the weather, and the price of barley, and the doings of his flock. Who was sick. Who was married. Who had quarreled with whom. He talked in a mild, rambling, pleasant way, like a stream running over stones, and I polished glasses and listened, and did not say much.

He did not ask me questions. That was the first thing I noticed about him. Everyone in Newarre asked me questions, those first months. Where I was from. What I had done before. Why I had come. I gave them small answers, grey and dull, and they lost interest. But Father Anselm never asked. He ate his stew and talked about the price of barley and looked at me, sometimes, with his winter-blue eyes, and did not ask anything at all.

---

It was in the second winter that he finally did.

It was a bad Mourning. Snow and wind, and the roads half closed. Father Anselm had come in late, shaking snow from his hat, and there was no one else in the common room. He sat on his stool and ate his stew in silence, for once. He did not talk about anything.

When he had finished, he set down his spoon and looked at me.

"Kote," he said. "Would you like to make confession?"

I stopped polishing the glass in my hands.

"I'm not a Tehlin, Father," I said.

"No. I didn't think you were." He folded his small pink hands on the bar. "But confession isn't only for Tehlins. Not really. It's for anyone who's carrying something too heavy." He looked at me. "I've been a priest for fifty-one years, son. I know what it looks like, when a man is carrying something too heavy. It looks like you."

I did not say anything. I set down the glass.

"You don't have to tell me what it is," said Father Anselm. "That's not the point of confession. People think it is, but it isn't. God already knows what it is. I don't need to." He smiled faintly. "The point is to set it down. Just for a moment. In front of someone. So you can remember that it *can* be set down. That it's not a part of you. That it's something you're carrying."

---

I looked at him for a long time. The wind howled around the inn. Snow hissed against the shutters.

"I can't," I said at last.

"Can't?"

"There's nothing to set down." I heard my own voice, flat and grey. "I don't remember what it is. I know there's something. I can feel the weight of it. But I don't know what it is." I looked at my hands on the bar. "Someone took it from me. To keep it safe. And now I carry the weight, and not the thing."

It was more than I had said to anyone in Newarre. It was more than I had said to Bast, in months. I do not know why I said it to him. Perhaps because he had not asked, all those spans. Perhaps because of the snow.

Father Anselm did not look surprised. He looked at me with his winter-blue eyes for a long moment.

"That's the hardest kind," he said quietly. "I've seen it before. Not often. Soldiers, mostly. Men who came back from the wars and couldn't remember what they'd done there. Only that it was heavy." He was quiet. "One of them told me it was like carrying a sealed box. You can feel the weight. You can hear something moving inside. But you can't open it. And you can't put it down, because what if it's something important. So you just carry it. Forever."

"Yes," I said. "Like that."

---

Father Anselm was quiet for a long time. Then he reached into the pocket of his patched grey robe and took out something small and set it on the bar between us.

It was a wheel. A little Tehlin wheel, the kind every priest carries, carved from dark wood and worn smooth by years of handling. The kind of thing you see hanging on a cord around the neck of every devout farmer's wife in the Commonwealth.

"I'm not going to tell you to pray," he said. "You're not a Tehlin. It wouldn't mean anything." He pushed the wheel across the bar toward me. "But I'll tell you what I told the soldier. What my own teacher told me, when I was young and carrying something too heavy of my own."

I looked at the wheel.

"The wheel turns," said Father Anselm. "That's all it means. That's all it's ever meant, underneath all the theology. Nothing stays at the top, and nothing stays at the bottom. Whatever you're carrying, however heavy it is, the wheel is turning. Slowly. So slowly you can't feel it. But turning." He tapped the little wooden wheel with one finger. "Someday it'll come round. And the box will open. And you'll find out what's inside." He looked at me. "And whatever it is, son, you'll be strong enough to hold it. By then. The wheel makes sure of that. It never brings anything round before you're ready."

---

I did not believe him. I want to be honest about that. I did not believe in Tehlu, or in the wheel, or in anything very much, those days. I did not believe in much of anything.

But I took the little wheel. I put it in my apron pocket. And I carried it there, for years, the way I carried the tinker's knot later, without ever taking it out or looking at it. A small smooth weight against my hip.

Father Anselm died in my fifth winter at the Waystone. He fell from his mule on an icy road between Newarre and Rannish, and broke his hip, and lay in the cold for a night before anyone found him. He lived three days after that, in the back room of the smithy, where Baxter had carried him. The whole town came to see him. He talked the whole time. About the price of barley, and the weather, and who had quarreled with whom.

I went on the second day. I stood by his bed and did not say anything. He looked up at me with his winter-blue eyes, very faded now, very tired.

"Kote," he said. "Do you still have it?"

I took the little wooden wheel out of my apron pocket and showed him.

He smiled.

"Good," he said. "Keep it turning."

He died that night. They buried him in the churchyard behind the little stone church, beside Bentley, who had given him his dinner for twenty years. There was no new priest for most of a year after that. The church stood empty on Mourning, and nobody came to the Waystone for stew and a cup of the brown. When a new one came at last, from Rannish, he was young and thin and nervous, and he said his prayers quickly, and he never stayed for dinner.

I kept a stool empty for him, at the end of the bar by the fire. For a year. I did not tell anyone why.

---

The innkeeper took something out of his apron pocket and set it on the table. A small wooden wheel, dark and worn smooth.

"I still have it," he said. "I don't know why. I'm not a Tehlin. I don't pray." He turned it over with one finger. "But I think he'd be pleased to know it came round. The wheel. Eventually." He looked at Chronicler. "It took seven years. And a scribe. And a knot from a tinker." He was quiet for a moment. "But it came round."

He put the little wheel back in his pocket. Beside the knot.
