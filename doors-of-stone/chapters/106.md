# EPILOGUE: A Silence of Three Parts

Dawn came again to the Waystone Inn, and it found the quiet there divided, as it had been three mornings before, into three.

The first part was the silence of a house that has just been left. The fire in the great stone hearth had burned down to a bed of red and grey, and the red was still winning, a little. The chairs sat upside down on the tables, the way they always did at night, but one of them had been turned right side up and pushed back from a table near the hearth, as if someone had been sitting there until the very last moment, and then stood, and gone. There was a cup on the table, empty. There was a scatter of crumbs. There was a single blank sheet of paper, weighted down with a salt cellar, and nothing written on it at all.

It was a warm silence. The silence of a room where people have been, and talked, and laughed, and wept, and will again. The silence of a held breath, not a stopped one.

---

The second part was the silence of the town. Of Newarre, and the fields around it, and the road running past the door.

It was a different silence than it had been. For months, for years, the silences of Newarre had been hard ones. The silence of a road where no carts came. The silence of a mother who has heard something in the dark and knows better than to look. The silence of a town that has learned to hold its breath.

This was softer. This was the silence of a town asleep. Of people lying in their beds after a long night, with their doors unbarred and their children beside them, breathing slow and even. The silence of a field of dead cows that were only dead now, and not frozen, and would be buried properly in the morning. The silence of a road with mud on it instead of frost, and footprints in the mud, two sets, heading north.

It was the silence of a town that had been told a story. And would tell it, over and over, for the rest of its life. And was resting now, before it began.

---

The third silence was harder to notice.

If you had walked into the Waystone that morning, you might not have noticed it at all. You might have stood among the upturned chairs and looked at the fire and the empty cup and the blank sheet of paper, and heard only the first two silences, the house and the town, and thought that was all there was.

But it was there. Underneath. It lived in the long bar of dark wood, and in the bottles behind it, the ones that had not cracked in the cold. It lived in the plaque on the wall above the hearth, with a single word burned into it, and the two empty iron pegs above it where a sword had hung. It lived in the room at the top of the stairs, at the foot of the bed, in a chest of roah wood with its lid standing open and its locks undone.

It was not the silence of a man who is waiting.

That silence was gone. It had walked out the door at dawn, with a sword at its hip and a lute on its back and a Fae prince leaning on its shoulder, and it had taken the road north. It had not looked back.

What was left was something else. Something quieter. Something that had been there all along, underneath the waiting, and had only now been uncovered.

It was the silence after a song.

The silence that comes when the last note has faded, and no one has yet moved, and no one has yet spoken. When the song is over, and everyone who heard it is sitting very still, holding it in themselves, not yet ready to let it go.

It was the silence of a story that has been told all the way to its end.

Or as far as the end had gone.

---

Outside, across the road, the waystone stood at the crossroads in the grey dawn light. Tall and grey and very old, worn smooth by the wind and the rain and the years. It said its long slow *no* to the world, the way it always had, the way it always would.

And for the first time in seven years, there was nothing beside it for it to hide.

---

Upstairs, in the best room the Waystone had to offer, a man named Devan Lochees sat on the edge of his bed with a great untidy stack of paper in his lap, and did not move.

He had not slept. He did not think he would ever sleep again. He sat on the edge of his bed and looked down at the paper. Three days of it. More than three days. The first page was dated in his careful hand, and the last page had no date at all, only a single line of his tiny crabbed cipher, halfway down, and then nothing.

He had written it the night before, after the others had gone to bed. After the innkeeper—after Kvothe—had sat with him by the fire for one last hour, and talked about nothing in particular. The weather. The road. What Chronicler would do with the story, now that he had it.

"Tell it true," Kvothe had said. "That's all I ask. Don't make me better than I was. Don't make me worse. Just tell it the way it happened."

"People won't believe it."

"No." Kvothe had smiled. "They never do. That's all right. Tell it anyway." He had been quiet for a moment. "And leave out the part about the ring. The signet. The tower and three stars. You promised."

"I remember."

"Good." Kvothe had stood up. He had looked down at Chronicler for a long moment, in the firelight. "Thank you, Devan Lochees. For listening. For holding it. For bringing it up the stairs when I asked." He had smiled, a small crooked smile. "Skarpi chose well."

And then he had gone up to bed, and Chronicler had sat alone by the fire, and written one more line.

He looked at it now, on the last page. His own tiny crabbed cipher. He read it over, and over, and over.

*And that is where the story stops. For now.*

---

He did not know, sitting on the edge of his bed with the pages in his lap, what would happen next. He did not know if Kvothe would reach the Stormwal, or the old man in the tower, or the door. He did not know if Kvothe would find the other way, the one that ended the curse without opening the door. He did not know if the others would come, the rest of the Seven, with their rot and their rust and their blue flame. He did not know if Haliax would come himself.

He did not know if a woman with dark hair, somewhere very far away, had felt a knot come undone in the night and known what it meant. He did not know if she would come back. He did not know if Kvothe would look for her, or let her find him.

He did not know if the door would hold.

He did not know anything.

But he knew the story. All of it, from the beginning to here. He knew it the way you know a song you have heard so many times that you can sing it in your sleep. He knew every piece of it, in its place. The brave parts and the terrible parts and the parts that make you laugh when you should not.

And he knew that he would tell it. True. The whole of it.

Because someone had to. Because it had been told to him, and a told story is a trust, and a trust must be kept. Because there was a man walking north on the road in the dawn, with a sword and a lute and a Fae prince leaning on his shoulder, and his name was Kvothe, and his name had been lost and was found again, and someone should remember.

So that no one would forget.

---

He stood up. He packed the pages carefully into his satchel, every one. He went downstairs, through the silent common room, past the upturned chairs and the empty cup and the blank sheet of paper weighted down with a salt cellar. He stopped at the door and looked back.

At the fire, burning low. At the long bar of dark wood. At the plaque on the wall, with its two empty pegs.

*FOLLY*, it said.

He looked at it for a long time.

Then he went out into the grey morning, and closed the door behind him, and took the road south.

---

And behind him, in the Waystone Inn, the three silences settled.

The silence of the house, warm and waiting.

The silence of the town, resting before the telling.

And the silence after the song, holding it, not yet ready to let it go.

It was a good silence. A patient one. The silence of a place where something has ended, and something else has begun.

It was the silence of a man who is no longer waiting to die.

Because he has remembered, at last, what it was he was waiting for.

And gone to find it.

---

*Here ends* The Doors of Stone.

*The story is not over.*

*Stories never are.*
