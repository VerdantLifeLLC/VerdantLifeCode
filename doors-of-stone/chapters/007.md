# Chapter Seven: Denna

I did not sleep that night. I sat on my bed with the blue book in my lap and read it again and again, until the candle burned low and the words swam before my eyes. I read the names of my grandfather and my grandmother, and their parents, and their parents before them. I traced the line of my blood back through the centuries, from Lackless to Lockless to Loeclos, until it disappeared into a darkness so old that the names were written in a script I could not read.

I had always thought of myself as Edema Ruh. That was my family, the only family I had ever known. My father's people, the troupers and the tinkers and the traveling players, who belonged to no land and no lord and carried their homes on their backs. I had been proud of that. I still was.

But now I knew that I was something else, too. I was the grandson of a lord. I was the nephew of the Maer's wife. I was the last of an old line that stretched back further than any history I had ever read.

And I was, perhaps, the reason my parents had died.

I did not know that yet. Not for certain. But the thought had lodged itself in my mind like a splinter, and I could not work it free. *The door holds while the line holds. The line holds while the name is kept.*

What door? What line? What name?

I did not know. But I knew that the Chandrian had come for my parents because of the songs they sang. And I knew, now, that my mother had come from a family that guarded something. Something old. Something that someone had tried very hard to hide.

What if it had not been the songs at all? Or not only the songs?

What if they had come for her?

---

I did not tell anyone what I had found. Not Sim, not Wil, not Devi. I did not know how. It was too large, too strange, too close to the bone. I carried it with me the way you carry a stone in your shoe, feeling it with every step, unable to set it down.

I went to class. I went to the Fishery and worked on Kilvin's candle, trying to understand the strange knotted binding at its heart. I went to the Archives and searched for more books on the Lackless family, and found nothing. I went to the Eolian and played, and the crowd applauded, and I smiled and bowed and felt nothing at all.

And then, on a cold grey afternoon a span after I had found the blue book, I came around the corner of the Eolian and nearly walked into Denna.

---

She was standing outside the door with her back to me, talking to Deoch. She wore a dress of deep green and a cloak the color of autumn leaves, and her dark hair was braided in an intricate pattern that wound around her head like a crown. She was laughing at something Deoch had said, her head thrown back, and the sound of it went through me like a knife.

I stopped. I could not help it. My feet simply stopped moving, and I stood there in the middle of the street like a fool, staring at her.

Deoch saw me first. His eyes flicked over her shoulder and met mine, and something passed across his face. Sympathy, maybe. Or amusement. With Deoch, it was hard to tell.

"Well," he said. "Look who's here."

Denna turned.

I have tried, many times, to describe what it was like to see Denna. I have never succeeded. I have written songs about it. I have written poems. I have lain awake at night searching for the words. And I have never found them.

The best I can say is this: when Denna looked at you, you felt seen. Not the way Elodin saw you, digging into the depths of you to find what was hidden there. Denna saw you the way the sun sees the earth. She shone on you, and you grew.

She looked at me now. Her eyes went wide. Her lips parted. And for a moment, just a moment, I saw something in her face that made my heart turn over in my chest. It was joy. Pure and simple and bright, like a child's.

Then it was gone, smoothed away behind the careful, practiced smile she wore for strangers.

"Kvothe," she said.

"Denna."

We stood there looking at each other. Deoch looked from one of us to the other. Then he cleared his throat.

"I'll just," he said, and gestured vaguely at the door. "Inside. Things to do." And he went.

Denna and I stood alone in the street.

"You're back," she said.

"I'm back."

"When?"

"A little over a month ago."

"A month." She tilted her head. "And you didn't send word?"

"I didn't know where to send it."

She laughed, a small soft sound. "No," she said. "I suppose you didn't." She looked at me for a long moment. "You look different."

"So I've been told."

"You've got a scar." She reached out and touched my cheek, very lightly, with the tips of her fingers. Her fingers were cold. "Who did that?"

"A teacher."

"A teacher cut your face?"

"She was a very dedicated teacher."

Denna laughed again, and this time it was a real laugh, bright and surprised. "Of course she was," she said. "Only you would find a teacher like that." She let her hand drop. "Walk with me?"

"Where?"

"Anywhere." She smiled. "It doesn't matter. I just want to walk."

---

We walked. We went down to the river and along the bank, past the docks and the warehouses and the little shacks where the fishermen mended their nets. The wind was cold and the sky was grey and it smelled like rain, but I did not care. I would have walked through a blizzard beside her. I would have walked through fire.

She asked me where I had been, and I told her. Not everything, but more than I had told Sim and Wil. I told her about the Maer, and his court, and his marriage. I did not tell her that I had written the love letters that won him his bride. I told her about the Adem, and she listened with bright eyes, asking questions about their women and their customs and their strange silent language of hands. I told her about Felurian, a little, and she laughed at me and said she did not believe a word of it, and I did not try to convince her.

And then, because I could not stop myself, I asked her where she had been.

She was quiet for a while. We had come to the end of the docks, where the bank rose up into a low grassy bluff overlooking the river. She sat down on the grass and wrapped her arms around her knees and looked out at the water.

"Here and there," she said at last. "The usual places."

"With your patron?"

She glanced at me. There was something wary in her eyes. "Sometimes."

"Is he still—" I hesitated. "Are you still working on the song?"

"Yes." She looked back at the river. "It's nearly done. I've been working on it for so long. Longer than I've ever worked on anything." She smiled faintly. "He says it's the most important thing I'll ever write."

"Your patron says that."

"Yes."

I sat down beside her on the grass. The wind blew her hair across her face, and she pushed it back with one hand. And as she did, the sleeve of her dress fell back from her wrist, and I saw the bruise.

It was fading. Yellow-green at the edges, the color of old grass. But it had been a bad one. I could see the marks where fingers had gripped her, four on one side and one on the other, dark against her pale skin.

She saw me looking. She pulled her sleeve down quickly.

"It's nothing," she said.

"Denna."

"It's nothing, Kvothe. I fell."

"You fell onto someone's hand?"

She turned to look at me, and her eyes were hard. "I said it's nothing. Leave it."

I wanted to leave it. I wanted to, more than anything. I wanted to let the moment pass and go back to sitting on the grass beside her, watching the river, pretending that the world was a simple place where nothing bad ever happened to anyone. But I could not. Because I remembered a voice from a tree in the Fae, a voice like honey poured over broken glass, telling me things I had not wanted to hear.

*When he beats her, it's not out of anger. He does it because he knows she will come back. He enjoys that.*

"Who is he?" I said. My voice was very quiet. "Your patron. What's his name?"

"You know I can't tell you that."

"Why not?"

"Because I promised." She looked away. "Because he'd know. He always knows. And he'd leave, and I'd have nothing." Her voice cracked slightly. "I've had nothing before, Kvothe. I've been nothing. I won't go back to that."

"You wouldn't have nothing. You'd have—"

I stopped. I had been about to say *me*. But I knew how that would sound. I knew what she would say. I had nothing to offer her. A room at an inn. A few talents in my purse. A patron of my own who would forget me the moment I stopped being useful. It was more than I had ever had before. But it was not enough. It was not nearly enough.

"You'd have your music," I said instead. "You'd have your voice. You don't need him for that."

She laughed, bitterly. "Don't I? Who do you think pays for my dresses? My rooms? My lessons? Who do you think opens the doors for me, so I can sing in the halls of lords instead of the back rooms of taverns?" She shook her head. "You don't understand. You've never had to understand. You've got your pipes and your talent and your University. You've got people who look at you and see something worth investing in." She looked down at her hands. "When people look at me, they see a pretty girl. That's all. And pretty girls are a copper a dozen."

"That's not true."

"It is true. You just don't want to see it." She stood up abruptly, brushing the grass from her skirt. "I should go."

"Denna—"

"I should go." She did not look at me. "I'm sorry. I'm glad you're back. I really am. But I have to go."

She walked away along the bluff. I watched her go, and I did not follow her. I wanted to. Every part of me wanted to run after her and take her hand and tell her that I would find him, her patron, whoever he was, and I would make him pay for every bruise he had ever left on her skin.

But I did not. Because I knew that if I did, she would never forgive me. And because I knew, deep down, that she was right. I did not understand. Not really. Not yet.

---

I sat on the bluff for a long time after she had gone. The rain came, finally, soft and cold, and I let it soak me through.

I thought about the Cthaeh. I had tried very hard, in the months since I had left the Fae, not to think about it. About its voice, its words, the terrible gleeful certainty with which it had told me things. I had told myself that it was a liar. That it had wanted to hurt me. That nothing it said could be trusted.

But I had seen the bruise. And I could not stop thinking about it.

I thought about the song, too. Denna's song. The one her patron had commissioned. I had heard part of it once, months ago, before I left for Vintas. It was a song about Lanre. But not the Lanre I knew, the Lanre of Skarpi's story, the hero who fell and became a monster. In Denna's song, Lanre was a hero still. A man wronged. A man betrayed by those he had loved and served, who had turned against them only because they had turned against him first.

I had been angry when I heard it. I had told her it was wrong, that the story was not like that, that Lanre was a traitor and a murderer. She had been angry too. She had said I did not know. She had said nobody knew. She had said that history was written by the winners, and that perhaps the losers had a story too.

We had not spoken for a long time after that.

I thought about it now, sitting in the rain. About why anyone would want to tell that story. About why a rich and secretive patron would pay a young singer to spend years of her life writing a song that made a monster into a hero.

I did not know the answer. But I knew, the way you sometimes know things, that it mattered. That it mattered more than almost anything.

---

When I finally went back to Anker's, soaked and shivering, there was a letter waiting for me.

It was folded small and sealed with a drop of plain wax. My name was written on the outside in a hand I knew. I opened it with cold, clumsy fingers.

*I'm sorry,* it said. *I didn't mean to be cruel. I'm staying at the Grey Man in Imre until the end of the span. If you want to find me, you know where I'll be. If you don't, I understand.*

*D.*

And beneath that, drawn very small in the corner of the page, there was a mark. A tiny knot, looped and twisted in a pattern that made my eyes ache to look at. I had seen it before. I had seen it in the margins of the blue book, beside the names of my mother's family. I had seen it in the heart of Kilvin's candle.

I sat on my bed with the letter in my hands and stared at that knot for a long, long time.

I did not know what it meant. But I knew, now, that I would have to find out.
