# Interlude: A Knock

Someone was pounding on the door of the Waystone Inn.

Chronicler nearly dropped his pen. He had been so deep in the story that the sound, when it came, seemed to come from another world. Three heavy blows, loud as hammer strokes, rattling the barred door in its frame. Then three more.

The innkeeper stopped speaking. He did not move. He sat very still at the table with his hands folded around his cold cup of tea.

Bast was on his feet. He had moved so fast Chronicler had not seen him rise. He stood between the table and the door with his hands at his sides and his face white, and his eyes, in the heatless firelight, had gone very dark. Almost black.

"*Kote!*" shouted a voice outside. "*Kote, are you in there? Open up! For God's sake, open up!*"

The innkeeper let out a breath. Something in his shoulders loosened.

"It's Aaron," he said. "The smith's boy." He stood up. "Let him in, Bast."

"Reshi—"

"Let him in. He sounds frightened."

Bast looked at him for a long moment. Then he went to the door and lifted the heavy bar and pulled it open.

---

Aaron stumbled in out of the dark. His face was red with cold and white with fear, and his breath came in great white clouds. He had a lantern in one hand, and the flame inside it was guttering, very small and very blue.

He saw Chronicler at the table and Bast at the door and the innkeeper standing beside the hearth, and he stopped. He looked around the common room with wide eyes.

"It's cold in here," he said. "It's cold as a tomb. Why's it so cold? You've got the fire going."

"It's cold everywhere tonight," said the innkeeper. His voice was mild. "What's wrong, Aaron?"

Aaron looked at him. His mouth worked for a moment before any words came out.

"Carter's cows," he said. "His whole herd. Eleven of them. In the field by the woods. I went out to help him bring them in, because it's so cold, and they were—" He stopped. He swallowed. "They were frozen, Kote. Standing up. In the middle of the field. Frozen solid, like statues. With frost all over them. And their eyes—" He shuddered. "Their eyes were open."

The common room was silent.

"And there's a light," said Aaron. His voice was shaking. "In the woods. Past Carter's field. A blue light. It's been there all day, Carter says. Just sitting there. Not moving. And the smoke, the smoke that's been hanging over the woods since morning, it's—" He looked at the window. "It's *leaning*. Toward town. Toward here."

Nobody said anything.

"Carter says it's a demon," said Aaron. "He says it's the end of the world. He's got his whole family in the church with the priest, praying. Half the town's there. Graham's there. Old Cob." He looked at the innkeeper. "They sent me to get you. They said you should come. They said it's safer, in the church. With everyone."

---

The innkeeper looked at Aaron for a long moment. His face was very calm.

"Thank you, Aaron," he said. "That was very brave, coming out here in the dark to tell me."

"You'll come?"

"No."

Aaron stared at him. "But—"

"I have a guest," said the innkeeper, nodding at Chronicler. "And an inn. I can't leave them." He smiled faintly. "Besides. If it really is the end of the world, I'd rather be here than in a church. I've never been much for churches."

"Kote, you don't understand. Carter's cows—"

"I understand." The innkeeper's voice was very gentle. He came across the room and put his hand on Aaron's shoulder. "Go back to the church, Aaron. Stay with the others. Bar the doors. Keep the fire high, even if it doesn't warm you. And whatever you hear tonight, whatever you see, don't go outside until the sun comes up." He paused. "Can you do that?"

Aaron looked up at him. His young face was pale and frightened.

"What is it?" he whispered. "Out there? You know. I can see it on you. You know what it is."

The innkeeper was quiet for a long moment.

"It's something old," he said at last. "Something that's been looking for something for a long time. And it's very close to finding it." He squeezed Aaron's shoulder. "But it's not looking for you. Or Carter. Or anyone in the church. You'll be safe there. I promise."

"How do you know?"

The innkeeper smiled. It was a small, crooked, rueful smile.

"Because," he said, "I know what it's looking for."

---

Aaron did not want to go. He stood in the doorway with his guttering blue lantern and looked at the innkeeper for a long time. His face was full of something Chronicler could not read. Fear, and confusion, and something else. Something that looked almost like recognition.

"Kote," he said slowly. "That song. The one I sang this evening. *Tariniel*." He hesitated. "When I sang it, you went all still. Like you'd been hit. And your face—" He stopped.

"What about my face?"

Aaron looked at him. At his red hair, true red, the color of a flame that has been burning for a long time. At his green eyes. At the bruises on his jaw, and the scar on his cheek.

"Nothing," Aaron said at last. "Nothing. It's just—" He shook his head. "The man in Rannish. The traveler. He said Kvothe had red hair. Real red. And green eyes. And a scar on his cheek, from an Adem sword." He swallowed. "And he said Kvothe was waiting. For something. And when it came, he'd come back."

The innkeeper did not move. He stood very still, with his hand on the boy's shoulder, and his face showed nothing at all.

"Go to the church, Aaron," he said quietly. "Go now. Please."

Aaron looked at him one more moment. Then he nodded. He turned and went out into the dark, with his little blue lantern bobbing, and was gone.

Bast closed the door behind him and dropped the bar back into its brackets.

---

The innkeeper stood by the hearth for a long time without moving.

"He knows," said Bast quietly. "Reshi. He knows."

"He suspects. It's not the same thing."

"He knows. I saw his face." Bast came away from the door. "They'll all know, soon. Everyone in town. After tonight."

"After tonight," said the innkeeper, "it won't matter."

He turned from the hearth. He came back to the table and sat down. He looked at Chronicler, who had not moved, who was still sitting with his pen poised over the page and his face very pale.

"You've stopped writing," said the innkeeper.

Chronicler looked down at the page. At the half-finished line of cipher. At the ink, thick and slow with cold.

"Eleven cows," he said. "Frozen standing up."

"Yes."

"That's him. Cinder. Coming here."

"Yes."

"And you're going to keep telling the story."

"Yes."

Chronicler looked up at him.

"Why?" he said. His voice cracked. "Why? Why not run? You could run. Right now. Out the back, into the dark, and he'd never find you. You've been hiding for years. You could hide again."

The innkeeper looked at him for a long moment.

"Because I'm tired of hiding," he said at last. "Because the door is cracked, and things are coming through, and people are dying. Carter's cows, and Shep, and a caravan at Farrel's Cut. Because the scrael are in the Commonwealth now, and the skin dancers, and worse things. Because every year it gets a little worse, and every year I sit here and polish the bar and pretend I don't know why." He was quiet for a moment. "Because a boy came to my door tonight and sang a song I wrote, and a woman wept at it, and I sat behind the bar and said nothing."

He looked down at his hands.

"And because I've been waiting," he said softly. "Like the Sleeper. For a long time. For something that might never come." He looked up. "And it's come. At last. And I find that I'm glad."

---

Nobody spoke for a long time.

Then Bast came back to the table. He did not sit in his backward chair. He knelt on the floor beside the innkeeper's chair, the way a child kneels beside a parent, and he put his head on the innkeeper's knee.

The innkeeper laid his hand on Bast's dark hair.

"Finish it, Reshi," Bast whispered. "Finish the story. Please. I want to hear the end." His voice cracked. "I want to hear you tell it. In your own voice. The whole thing. Before he comes."

The innkeeper looked down at him for a long moment.

Then he looked at Chronicler.

"Get your pen," he said.

Chronicler got his pen. He dipped it in the thick cold ink.

"They tried me," said Kvothe, "on the first day of summer. Exactly one year after Ambrose was crowned. In the great hall of the palace at Renere. Before all the lords of Vintas, and the Penitent King on his throne, and half the city crowded in at the doors."

He smiled faintly.

"I don't remember much of it," he said. "I was very grey, that day. But I remember the end. I remember when they asked me if I had anything to say."

"What did you say?"

The innkeeper looked at the sword above the hearth.

"The truth," he said.
