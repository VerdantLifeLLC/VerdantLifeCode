# Interlude: A Game of Tak

"Do you play?" said the innkeeper.

Chronicler looked up from the page he had been blotting. The innkeeper had stopped speaking some time ago, at the end of the first game in the Maer's small garden, and had gone behind the bar, and had come back with a flat wooden box under his arm. He set it on the table and opened it. Inside, nested in green felt, were two bowls of smooth river stones, black and white, and a board of dark wood with lines inlaid in pale ash.

"Tak?" said Chronicler. "A little. Badly. My uncle taught me, when I was a boy." He looked at the board. "I haven't played in years."

"Good," said the innkeeper. "Neither have I."

Bast, in his backward chair, made a small strangled sound.

The innkeeper looked at him mildly. "Something wrong, Bast?"

"No, Reshi." Bast's face was perfectly innocent. "Nothing at all. Only that you've played every night for six years. With Graham. In the kitchen. After closing."

"That's not playing," said the innkeeper. "Graham doesn't play tak. Graham plays *at* tak. There's a difference." He began setting out the board. "Our scribe is tired. His hand is cramping; I've been watching him flex it for the last hour. He needs a rest, and so do I. And I find myself wanting to play." He looked at Chronicler. "Unless you'd rather not?"

Chronicler looked at the board. At the smooth black and white stones in their bowls.

"I'd be honored," he said.

---

The innkeeper gave him white. White moves first.

Chronicler hesitated over the board for a long moment. Then he placed a stone near the center. It was the only opening his uncle had ever taught him. He was aware, as he did it, that it was a very ordinary move. A beginner's move.

The innkeeper looked at it. He did not smile. He picked up a black stone and set it down, apparently at random, at the far edge of the board.

They played.

---

It was not a long game. Chronicler had known it would not be. He played carefully, cautiously, the way his uncle had taught him: guarding his corners, building slowly, never overreaching. And the innkeeper played—Chronicler did not know how to describe how the innkeeper played. His stones seemed to go down without any thought at all, quickly, almost carelessly, scattered across the board with no pattern Chronicler could see.

And then, somewhere around the twentieth stone, the pattern was suddenly there. All at once. Chronicler looked down at the board and saw that every one of those careless scattered black stones had been placed exactly where it needed to be, and that his own careful white groups were surrounded, cut off, every one of them, and that there was nothing he could do about it. Nothing at all.

He sat back in his chair and looked at the board for a long moment.

"I've lost," he said.

"Yes."

"When? When did I lose?"

The innkeeper looked at the board. He reached out and touched a single black stone, near the edge. The second stone he had played.

"There," he said.

Chronicler stared at it. "That was the second move."

"Yes."

"You knew. From the second move."

"No." The innkeeper shook his head. "I didn't know. I hoped. There's a difference." He began gathering up the stones, black into one bowl and white into the other. "Your first move told me how you think. Careful. Honest. You open in the center because your uncle told you the center is strongest, and you believed him, and you've never tested it." He smiled faintly. "It was a good opening. It was a good opening for someone who wants to be safe. But I didn't want to be safe."

---

"Play again," said Bast. "Play him again, Reshi. Properly."

The innkeeper looked at him. "That was properly."

"No it wasn't." Bast leaned forward in his chair, his eyes bright. "You were playing Chronicler. Play the other way. The way you played the old man." He looked at Chronicler, with a sly, wicked grin. "Make it beautiful."

The innkeeper was quiet for a long moment. His hands, gathering the stones, had stopped.

"I don't remember how," he said.

"Yes you do." Bast's grin had gone. His voice was very soft. "You're remembering everything else. You can remember that."

The innkeeper looked down at the board. At the empty lines, pale ash on dark wood.

Then he looked at Chronicler.

"Would you mind?" he said. "It's not a fair game. I should tell you that. It's not really a game at all. Not the way you mean." He paused. "But I'd like to try. If you don't mind losing again."

Chronicler looked at him. Then he picked up a white stone.

"I'm used to it," he said.

---

The second game was different.

Chronicler played the same way, at first. Careful. Center. Corners. But the innkeeper's stones did not scatter this time. They went down slowly. Deliberately. Each one placed after a long pause, with a kind of attention Chronicler had seen in very old craftsmen, bent over their work. Each one placed not to win, Chronicler realized after a while, but to *answer*.

When Chronicler built a wall, the innkeeper's stones curved around it, not to break it but to echo it, like a second voice in a song. When Chronicler made a mistake, left an opening he should not have left, the innkeeper did not take it. He played beside it instead, and the shape the two stones made together was strange and lovely, and Chronicler found himself staring at it, forgetting the game entirely.

"Oh," he said, without meaning to.

The innkeeper did not look up. He placed another stone.

And slowly, stone by stone, the board began to fill with something Chronicler had never seen on a tak board before. Not a battle. Not a siege. Something more like a conversation. Two lines of stones, white and black, weaving around each other in long spirals, like the grain in a piece of wood, like the patterns of frost on a window, like two people dancing who had been dancing together for a very long time.

Chronicler forgot that he was losing. He forgot that he was playing. He placed his stones where they seemed to want to go, and the innkeeper answered them, and the pattern grew, and it was the most beautiful thing Chronicler had ever seen.

---

He did not know how long they played. The fire burned down. Bast got up, at some point, and put more wood on it, and sat down again, and did not say a word. The light in the windows changed.

And then, at last, the innkeeper set down a stone, and sat back, and folded his hands.

Chronicler looked at the board.

He had lost. He could see that. His white stones were surrounded, every one. But they were surrounded the way a garden is surrounded by a wall. Held. Kept. The black stones did not crush them. They cradled them.

And in the very center of the board, where he had placed his first careful beginner's stone, the black and white together made a shape. He could see it now, clear as ink on paper. A circle. With seven lines running out from it, like the spokes of a wheel, or the rays of the sun.

Or the doors of a house.

---

Nobody said anything for a long time.

"Where did you learn that?" said Chronicler at last. His voice was hoarse.

"From an old man," said the innkeeper, "in a garden in Vintas. Who taught me that the point of tak isn't to win. It's to make something beautiful, with someone else, even if one of you is going to lose." He looked at the board. "He told me that a beautiful game tells you everything about the person you're playing. Their fears and their hopes and the way they think. Everything they are." He was quiet. "He told me that the most dangerous man in the world is the one who can play a beautiful game and still win."

"Bredon," said Chronicler.

"Yes."

"The old man. From the story. Who was really—" Chronicler stopped.

"Yes," said the innkeeper. "I didn't know it then. That's why the story's the way it is." He reached out and touched a black stone, near the center of the circle. "He played like this. Every game. Every time. So beautifully that you forgot he was winning. So beautifully that you forgot to ask what he was winning *for*."

He began gathering the stones. Black into one bowl, white into the other. The pattern came apart under his hands, stone by stone, until the board was empty again. Pale ash on dark wood.

"I learned to play from him," he said quietly. "And I learned too well. And that was part of the trouble." He closed the lid of the box. "When you learn to play a beautiful game, you start to see the whole world as a board. Everyone in it as stones. And you start to think that if you just place them carefully enough, beautifully enough, it will all come out right in the end."

"Doesn't it?"

The innkeeper looked at him. His green eyes were very old.

"Sometimes," he said. "And sometimes the man across the board has been playing a different game the whole time. And you only find out at the very end, when you look down, and see the shape your stones have made. And realize you've drawn a door."

---

He took the box back behind the bar. He put it on the shelf, beside the bottles. He came back and sat down and picked up his cup.

Chronicler looked down at his hand. It was not cramping anymore. He flexed it. He picked up his pen.

"The garden," he said. "Bredon had just asked you to follow him."

"Yes." The innkeeper looked at the fire. "He wanted to show me something. He said it would explain a great deal." He was quiet for a moment. "It did. Though not in the way he meant."

He began to speak again. And Chronicler wrote.

But Bast, sitting in his backward chair with his arms folded across its back, did not listen to the story. He was looking at the box on the shelf behind the bar. And there was something in his face, something bright and fierce and fragile, that Chronicler would not have a name for until much later.

Hope.
