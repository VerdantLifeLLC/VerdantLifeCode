# The Lackless House

The king's army reached the ridge the next afternoon, and by nightfall there were fires all around the Lackless house like a ring of fallen stars.

We watched from a high rocky outcrop half a mile to the north, lying flat among the heather with Bast's glamour thin over us. We watched the long black column wind up the road from Levinshir, rank on rank of soldiers and horses and wagons, with the black and silver banner at its head. We watched it spread out across the slopes below the house and settle there like a flock of crows, pitching tents, digging latrines, lighting fires.

We watched a smaller group ride up to the house itself. Twenty or thirty riders, with the banner of Jakis and a covered wagon in their midst. They went in through the gate in the wall around the house, and the gate closed behind them.

"She's in the wagon," said Vashet softly. "The lady. I saw her face at the window."

"And the king?"

"On the black horse. At the front."

"And the old man?"

Vashet was quiet for a moment. "On the donkey," she said. "Behind the wagon. With the girl."

I lay in the heather and looked at the house. At its dark stone walls and its four towers and its windows with no light in them. It was very old. Older than Severen. Older, perhaps, than Renere. It looked less like a house than like something that had grown out of the ridge, the way a stone grows out of the earth when the soil around it washes away.

"Tomorrow night," I said. "The dark of the moon."

"Tomorrow night," said Vashet. "And tonight, we find a way in."

---

Fela found it.

She did not tell us how. She lay in the heather on the high outcrop with her palms pressed flat against the rock, and her eyes closed, and her lips moving, for most of an hour. When she opened her eyes, her face was pale and her hands were shaking. But her voice was steady.

"There's a way," she said. "Under. The ridge is hollow. There are caves, very old, running all through it. Some of them go up under the house." She pointed down the slope to the north, away from the army, toward a steep gully choked with brambles and loose stone. "One of them comes out there. In that gully. It's very narrow. Very old. It hasn't been used in a long time." She paused. "But the stone remembers people walking through it. Long ago. People carrying things. Going up, and coming down."

"Where does it go?" I asked.

Fela looked at me with her dark serious eyes.

"Down," she said. "It goes down. To the same place the stairs in the house go." She shivered. "To the door."

---

We went at midnight.

There were nineteen of us. Nine Adem, with Vashet at their head. Sim and Wil and Devi and Fela. Bast. Me. And four of the Maer's soldiers, the best he had left: Dagon, with his hatchet face, and three others whose names I have never forgotten, though I will not tell them here. They have families still. They would not want to be remembered for this.

The Maer had wanted to come. He had argued for a long time. I had told him no. I had told him that someone needed to stay with the rest of his people. That someone needed to be alive, afterward, to lead them. That his daughter needed a father, even if she would never know him.

In the end, he had agreed. But when we left, at midnight, he stood at the mouth of his cave and watched us go, and his face in the firelight was the face of a man watching his whole life walk away into the dark.

---

The gully was steep and treacherous, choked with brambles and loose scree. We went down it slowly, in single file, with the Adem leading and Bast's glamour stretched thin over all of us like a cloak that was too small. At the bottom, half hidden under a fall of rock and a tangle of blackthorn, there was a hole.

It was very small. Barely wide enough for a man to squeeze through on his hands and knees. Beyond it, there was darkness.

Fela knelt beside it and put her hand on the stone at its edge. She was quiet for a moment.

"This is it," she said.

We went in.

---

I will not tell you much about that passage. It was long and narrow and very dark. For the first hundred yards we crawled, on hands and knees, over sharp stones, with the rock pressing down on our backs. Then it opened out, a little, and we could walk bent over. Then a little more, and we could walk upright.

The walls were rough and unworked, the natural stone of the ridge. But here and there, as we went deeper, there were marks on them. Carvings. Very old, worn almost smooth. I held my sympathy lamp close and looked at them.

They were knots.

Yllish knots, carved into the stone. Long lines of them, running along the walls like words on a page. Some of them I could read. *Keeping*. *Door*. *Line*. *Name*. The same knots I had seen on the Dellers' cloth, and in the blue book, and in the heart of Kilvin's candle.

"The keepers," I said softly. "They came this way. To tend the door."

"They did," said Fela. Her hand was trailing along the wall. "For a long time. Hundreds of years. Thousands. The stone remembers their footsteps." She was quiet for a moment. "And then they stopped coming. A long time ago. Before your grandmother's time. Before her grandmother's." She looked at me. "Something happened. Something made them stop."

"What?"

Fela shook her head. "The stone doesn't know. It only knows they stopped." She looked down the passage, into the dark. "And that since then, the door has been alone. For a very long time. And very lonely."

---

We went down.

The passage sloped steadily downward, winding through the heart of the ridge. The air grew colder as we went. Colder than it should have been, even so deep underground. Our breath began to smoke in the light of our lamps.

And I began to hear it.

At first I thought it was the wind. A faint, low sound, somewhere far ahead, rising and falling. But there was no wind this deep. And the sound was too regular. Too steady. In, and out. In, and out.

Breathing.

The same breathing I had heard in the Chamber of Watches, beneath the Archives. Slow and deep and patient. The breath of something very large, sleeping very deeply.

But it was different here. Faster. Shallower. Less deep. The breath of something that was not quite asleep anymore. Something that was dreaming, and stirring in its dreams, and very close to waking.

Beside me, Fela had gone very pale.

"It knows," she whispered. "It knows we're coming. It knows *you're* coming."

---

We stopped to rest in a place where the passage widened into a small round cave. There was a trickle of water running down one wall, and a flat stone floor, and a low ceiling that dripped.

The Adem spread out around the edges of the cave, silent and watchful. Dagon and his men sat against the wall with their swords across their knees. Sim sat down heavily on the floor and put his head between his knees. Wil sat beside him and put a scarred hand on his back.

Devi sat down across from me and began to take things out of her satchel. Small clay pots. Glass vials. A coil of copper wire. A handful of what looked like iron nails.

"What are those?" I asked.

"More Surprises," she said. "Smaller ones. I made them on the way. And these—" she held up the nails, "—are for the scrael. If there are any. Iron. Heated. With a little something extra." She smiled her spider's smile. "They'll go through a scrael's shell like a hot knife through butter."

"Devi," I said. "Thank you. For coming."

She looked up at me. For a moment, her face was very serious.

"You still owe me a page," she said. "From the Book of Watches. You said you'd give it back someday." She went back to sorting her pots. "I'm not letting you die before you pay your debts."

---

Bast came and sat beside me. He did not say anything for a while. He was holding something in his hands, turning it over and over.

"What's that?" I asked.

He held it up. It was a small knife. Very plain, with a wooden handle and a short blade.

"It's iron," he said. His voice was tight. "Devi gave it to me. For the scrael. She said I'd need it." He looked down at it. His fingers were red where they touched the handle, as if he had been holding something hot. "It burns. It burns so much."

"Then don't hold it."

"I have to." He closed his fingers around the handle. His face tightened. "If there are scrael. If they come. I have to be able to fight them." He looked at me. "I can't just glamour everything, Reshi. Not down here. Not with *that* so close." He nodded down the passage, toward the breathing. "It's too loud. It's drowning me out."

I looked at him. At his fine-boned face, tight with pain. At his hand, closed around the iron knife, the skin going red and blistered.

"Put it away," I said. "In your boot. Only take it out if you need it."

He looked at me for a long moment. Then he nodded, and slid the knife into his boot, and flexed his burned fingers.

"Reshi," he said quietly. "When we get there. When you do what you're going to do. With your name." He hesitated. "Will you still be you? Afterward?"

I did not answer right away.

"I don't know," I said at last. "Selitos said I'd be less. Not empty. But less."

"Less how?"

"I don't know. I've never done it." I looked at him. "I suppose we'll find out."

Bast was quiet for a long time.

"I'll stay with you," he said at last. "Afterward. Whatever you are. However much less. I'll stay." He looked at me. "I owe you a life. I'll pay it. I'll stay until you're you again."

"What if I'm never me again?"

Bast looked at me with his deep blue eyes.

"Then I'll stay forever," he said simply.

---

We went on.

The passage wound down and down. The breathing grew louder. The air grew colder. Frost began to form on the walls, glittering in the light of our lamps. Our breath came out in great white plumes.

And then, ahead of us, there was light.

Not the light of lamps. Not the light of torches. A pale, cold, silvery light, like moonlight on snow. The same light I had seen in the Cellarage, beneath the University, when the Sleeper woke.

We stopped. We stood in the passage, all nineteen of us, and looked at the light.

"That's it," said Fela. Her voice was barely a whisper. "The chamber. The door."

I looked at the light. I listened to the breathing.

And beneath the breathing, faint and far away, I heard something else. Voices. Human voices. Someone talking, low and steady. Someone else answering. And beneath them, very soft, someone singing.

A woman's voice. Singing a song I knew.

Denna.

She was singing her Lanre song. The false one. The beautiful one.

And the breathing, behind the door, was keeping time.
