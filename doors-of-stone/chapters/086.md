@@PART PART FIVE: THE WAYSTONE

# The Road West

We traveled west with Hallow's Company for the rest of that summer.

I do not remember it well. I remember it the way you remember a journey you took as a very small child, sleeping in the back of a wagon: in pieces, out of order, without meaning. The creak of wheels. The smell of horses and woodsmoke. The sound of a fiddle played badly, every third note flat. Voices around a fire at night, talking and laughing, in a language I understood the words of but not the sense.

And the grey. Always the grey.

I rode in the back of the third wagon, among the sacks and the costumes and the painted canvas backdrops. Denna rode with me. She sat beside me in the straw with her harp in her lap and did not play it. She held my hand. Sometimes she talked, softly, about nothing in particular. The weather. The road. The towns we passed. Sometimes she did not talk at all.

I did not talk much either. There was nothing in me to talk with.

---

The troupe did not play in the towns we passed. Tessit said it was too dangerous. There were soldiers on the roads, and bounty hunters, and people who had heard that the kingkiller was not really dead. Word had spread, the way word always spreads. A red-haired man, traveling with a Ruh troupe. It would not take much for someone to put it together.

So we did not stop. We went west along the back roads, the small ones, through hill country and forest, avoiding the towns. We camped in fields and woods, beside streams, under the stars. We ate what we could find, and what the villages sold us, and what Kellen and Wat could snare.

And Mirra cut my hair.

She did it on the second night, sitting me down on a stool beside the fire with a blanket around my shoulders. She did not ask. She simply took a pair of shears out of her sewing box and began to cut. Short, very short, so short that the red of it hardly showed. When she was done, she rubbed something into what was left, a paste of walnut hulls and ash that stained my scalp and my fingers brown.

"There," she said, when it was done. She stood back and looked at me with her round face very grave. "Now you're just a boy with brown hair. There's a thousand of those on the roads."

I looked at my hands, stained brown. I did not know what I was supposed to feel.

"It'll grow out," said Mirra. "In a month or two. We'll do it again then. And again. As long as we need to." She paused. "Your mother used to do it too, you know. Dye her hair. She had the loveliest dark hair, but it had a little red in it, in the sun. She said it was too memorable." Mirra smiled faintly. "She used walnut hulls too. I learned it from her."

"I don't remember her," I said.

Mirra looked at me for a long moment. Her round face was very sad.

"I know, love," she said. "I know."

---

There was a baby in the first wagon.

I did not know whose it was. I did not know anything. But I heard it at night, sometimes, crying, and I heard Mirra singing to it, a soft low wordless tune that made something in my chest ache, though I did not know why. And sometimes, during the day, when the wagons stopped to rest, I saw a girl carrying it. A girl of seven or eight, with enormous eyes and a red dress, holding the baby very carefully against her shoulder.

The girl came to see me, one afternoon, when we had stopped beside a stream to water the horses. She came and stood beside the tailboard of the third wagon, where I was sitting, and looked at me with her enormous eyes.

"You don't remember me," she said.

"No."

"I'm Nell. You pulled me out of a well." She shifted the baby on her shoulder. "Denna says you gave your name away. To close a door."

"Yes."

Nell considered this. Then she held up the baby.

"This is Lark," she said. "She's my sister now. Mirra says. She's very small and she cries a lot, but she's nice." She looked at me. "Do you want to hold her?"

I did not know what to say. I looked at the baby. A tiny thing, wrapped in a blanket, with a small red crumpled face and a fuzz of dark hair. She was awake. She was looking at me with dark solemn eyes.

I held out my hands. I did not know why.

Nell put the baby in them. Very carefully. She showed me how to hold her, one hand under the head, one under the body. The baby was warm and light and she smelled of milk and woodsmoke.

She looked up at me. I looked down at her.

And the cord on my wrist hummed.

It was very faint. Barely there. But I felt it. The way a string hums when another string nearby is plucked. As if something in the cord recognized something in her. As if there were a thread running between us, very thin, very old.

"She likes you," said Nell. "She doesn't usually like people. She cries at Brin all the time."

I held the baby for a long time. She did not cry. She lay in my hands and looked up at me with her dark solemn eyes, and after a while, she fell asleep.

---

I tried to play the lute.

It was Denna who suggested it. We were sitting by the fire one night, late, after the others had gone to sleep. My lute was lying in its case beside me; someone had brought it out of Renere, I never learned who. Denna looked at it for a long time.

"Will you try?" she said.

"Try what?"

"To play." She picked up the case and opened it and took out the lute and held it out to me. "Just try. Just a little."

I took it. It was a beautiful thing, dark wood and ivory pegs and a soundboard of pale spruce. It was very old, and very well made, and someone had taken very good care of it. I did not know who.

I held it the way you hold a lute. My hands knew how. Even the left one, the cold one, with the white print of a dead man's fingers on its wrist. They settled onto the neck and the strings the way birds settle onto a branch.

I played a chord.

It was a good chord. Clean and clear, every note in its place. My fingers knew exactly where to go.

I played another. And another. A simple progression, the kind of thing you play to warm up. My hands moved on their own. They knew the shapes. They had known them for a very long time.

And it was nothing.

I do not know how else to say it. The notes were right. The chords were right. The rhythm was right. It was music, in every way that music can be measured.

But it was nothing. It was grey. It was like a painting of a fire, hung on a wall. It had the shape of warmth and none of the heat.

I stopped playing. I sat very still with the lute in my lap, and I looked at it.

Denna was watching me. Her face was very pale in the firelight.

"It's gone," she whispered. "Isn't it? The—the thing. The fire. Whatever it was that made it yours."

I did not answer. I did not know how.

She reached out and took the lute out of my hands, very gently. She put it back in its case. She closed the lid.

Then she put her face in her hands, and wept.

---

I watched her weep. I did not know why she was weeping. I did not know what I should do.

But I remembered something. Very faint. From very far down. A voice, in a lighthouse, by the sea. *You sing it so that it will be true. So that it will be remembered.*

And I understood, a little. Not with my mind. Somewhere else. I understood that I had lost something, and that she was weeping for it, because I could not.

I reached out and put my hand on her back. The way you put a hand on a frightened animal. I did not say anything.

After a while, she stopped weeping. She sat up. She wiped her eyes.

"I'm sorry," she said. "I'm sorry. It's just—I heard you, you know. At the Eolian. That night. With the song." Her voice cracked. "It was the most beautiful thing I ever heard. It was like the whole world was singing through you." She looked at the closed lute case. "And now it's just—a lute."

"It's still a lute," I said.

She looked at me.

"It's still a lute," I said again. "And my hands still know it. And maybe someday—" I stopped. I did not know what I was going to say.

"Someday what?"

I looked down at the cord on my wrist. At the knots, glowing very faintly.

"Someday it won't just be a lute," I said.

Denna looked at me for a long time. Then she laughed. A small, broken, astonished laugh.

"You're still in there," she said. "Aren't you? Somewhere. Under all the grey. You're still in there."

"I don't know."

"You are." She took my hand. My left hand, with the cord. "I can feel it. In the knots. You're in there. You're just asleep."

---

We came to the edge of Vintas at the end of summer.

It was a river. A wide slow brown river, running north to south, with the hills of Vintas on one side and the hills of the Commonwealth on the other. There was a ford, and a ferry, and a small town on the eastern bank with a toll house and a garrison of soldiers in the grey of the Penitent King.

We did not go to the ford. Tessit knew another way. A place upstream, a day's travel, where the river widened and grew shallow over a bed of stones, and a wagon could cross if the driver knew exactly where to put the wheels.

We crossed at dawn. Tessit led the first horse, wading chest-deep in the brown water, feeling for the stones with his feet. The wagons followed, one by one, lurching and swaying, with the water foaming around their wheels.

I sat in the back of the third wagon and watched the eastern bank fall away behind us. The hills of Vintas, green and brown and grey. The last of the country where I had been Kvothe, and the Maer's Ruh, and the kingkiller.

I did not feel anything. There was nothing in me to feel with.

But Denna, sitting beside me, reached over and took my hand. And held it very tight, all the way across the river.

---

On the western bank, when the wagons had all come up out of the water and the horses were shaking themselves and the troupe was laughing with relief, a woman was waiting for us.

She was small and slim, in blood-red clothes bound with a hundred cords. She was sitting on a stone beside the road, with a sword across her knees, and she was very still.

I did not know her. But my hands knew her. I felt them move, at my sides, before I had decided anything. A small gesture. Clumsy. Badly made.

The woman in red looked at my hands. Something moved in her face.

"Kvothe," she said.

I did not answer. The name meant nothing to me.

She stood up. She came across the road toward the third wagon. She stopped beside the tailboard and looked up at me.

"I have come," she said, "about the sword."
