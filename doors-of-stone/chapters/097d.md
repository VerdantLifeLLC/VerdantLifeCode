# Graham's Barn

Graham built a new barn in my fourth summer, and the whole town came to raise it.

He had been complaining about the old one for years. It was a sagging, weather-grey, drafty thing, older than Graham himself, with a roof that leaked and a floor that flooded and a north wall that leaned so far out of true that Old Cob said you could use it to tell which way the wind was blowing. Graham had patched it and propped it and cursed it for thirty years. And that spring, after a wet winter, the north wall finally gave up and lay down in the mud, very gently, like an old dog settling for a nap.

"That's it," said Graham, standing in the Waystone that evening with his mug of beer and his face like a thundercloud. "That's the last straw. I'm building a new one. A proper one. Oak frame. Slate roof. Stone footings." He glared around the room. "And every man jack of you is going to help me, or I'll know the reason why."

Everyone laughed. But they came.

---

A barn raising in a small town is not a job. It is a festival. Everyone knows it. The men do the heavy work, the lifting and hauling and pegging, and the women bring food, great quantities of it, and the children run around underfoot and get shouted at, and by the end of the day there is a barn where there was not one before, and everyone is sunburned and exhausted and a little drunk, and there is a dance in the new barn by lantern light.

I had not meant to go. I had meant to stay behind the bar. The inn would be empty all day, of course, with the whole town at Graham's; but I had meant to stay anyway, and polish things, and be small and quiet.

Bast would not let me.

"You're going," he said.

"There's work to do here."

"There's no work to do here. There's never any work to do here. You invent it." He took the rag out of my hands. "You're going to Graham's, and you're going to lift things, and you're going to eat Rena's pie and drink Jake's terrible cider, and you're going to stand around in the sun like a normal person for one day." He looked at me. "Please, Reshi."

I went.

---

It was a hot day, bright and clear, with a light wind from the south. Graham's farm lay a mile east of town, on a gentle slope above the river, and when Bast and I came up the lane, the yard was already full. Carts and wagons and horses, and tables set out under the apple trees, loaded with food. Children everywhere. Dogs everywhere. The smell of fresh-cut timber and crushed grass and bread.

The frame of the barn was laid out on the ground, in sections. Great squared oak beams, pegged together into walls, lying flat on the grass like the pieces of a giant's puzzle. Graham had spent the whole spring cutting and fitting them, with his sons, and they were beautiful. Even I could see that. Every joint was tight. Every peg was smooth. He had carved his initials, very small, into the foot of the center post.

"Right," said Graham, standing in the middle of it all with his sleeves rolled up. "East wall first. I want twenty men on the ropes and ten on the pikes. And nobody lets go until I say. Understood?"

They raised the east wall. Then the west. Then the south. It was slow, heavy, careful work: the walls lifted inch by inch on ropes and long pike poles, swaying, creaking, while Graham shouted directions and the men strained and sweated and swore. And then, when each wall was upright, men swarming up the frame like ants to drive the pegs home, with the whole thing groaning around them.

I worked the ropes. I pulled when Graham said pull and held when he said hold. It was good to have something to do with my hands. It was good to be one of twenty, sweating in the sun, pulling together, and not have to be anything else.

---

It was during the north wall that it happened.

The north wall was the biggest. It had the great double doors in it, and the hayloft door above, and it was heavier than all the others. It took thirty men to lift it. It went up slowly, very slowly, swaying on the ropes, groaning. Graham's face was red and running with sweat.

"Steady," he was shouting. "Steady! Hold it there! Hold!"

It was upright. Almost. Leaning slightly outward, held by the ropes and the pikes. And Graham's grandson, Tobin, a boy of fourteen, was already swarming up the frame with a mallet in his belt, to drive the first peg that would lock it to the corner post.

I was on the rope nearest the corner. I was looking up. I saw him climb.

And I saw the peg-hole he was reaching for. And the rope that held the top of the wall. And the way the rope was fraying, just slightly, where it ran over the edge of the beam.

---

I do not know how I saw it. I was not looking for it. I was not looking for anything. I was holding my rope and staring up at the sky, grey inside and empty, the way I always was.

But something in me saw it. Something old. Something that had spent years looking at the world the way Bredon had taught me to look at a tak board: seeing where every stone was, and where it would be, three moves on.

The rope was going to break. Not now. In a moment. When the boy leaned his weight on the frame to swing the mallet. The wall would lurch, and the rope would part, and the wall would swing outward, and the boy would fall. Twenty feet, onto the stone footings Graham had laid so carefully.

I let go of my rope.

---

I remember Graham shouting. I remember the man beside me swearing as the weight shifted onto him. I remember my feet, running, across the flattened grass, toward the corner post.

And I remember the sound of the rope breaking. A sharp, flat crack, like a whip.

The wall lurched. The boy, halfway up the corner post, with his mallet raised, lost his grip. His feet swung free. He hung for a moment by one hand, his face white with shock, and then his hand slipped, and he fell.

I caught him.

I do not know how. I was not close enough. I should not have been close enough. I had been twenty feet away when the rope broke. But I was there, under the corner post, with my arms out, when he came down. And he landed in my arms, all fourteen years of him, gangly and heavy, and the weight of him drove me down onto one knee on the stone footings. I felt something in my knee crack.

I did not let go.

---

Behind us, the wall swung. Thirty men shouted. Pikes scraped. The great oak frame groaned and swayed and leaned outward, further, further—

And stopped.

Graham had thrown himself on the broken rope. Him and both his sons and Baxter and Aaron and a dozen others. They had caught the frayed end and wrapped it around their arms and dug their heels into the grass, and they were holding the whole north wall of the barn by main strength, their faces purple, their arms shaking.

"Pikes!" roared Graham. "*Pikes!*"

The pike men scrambled. The poles went up. The wall steadied. Slowly, slowly, inch by inch, it swung back upright.

And held.

---

Nobody moved for a long moment. The whole yard was silent. Thirty men, standing on the flattened grass, staring at the barn wall. Women by the tables with their hands over their mouths. Children frozen mid-run.

Then Graham let go of the rope and turned around and looked at me.

I was still kneeling on the stone footing with his grandson in my arms. Tobin was staring up at me, his face white, his mouth open. He did not seem to be hurt. My knee was hurt. I could feel it now, a hot deep throbbing. I did not care.

"Kote," said Graham. His voice was hoarse. "Kote. How did you—"

I did not answer. I set the boy down, very carefully, on his feet. He stood there, swaying, and then his mother was there, and she had him in her arms, and she was weeping.

Graham came across the grass. He stood over me. He looked at me for a long time with his big slow face.

"You let go of your rope," he said. "Before it broke. I saw you. You let go and ran *before it broke.*"

I did not say anything.

"How did you know?"

---

I could not tell him. I could not tell him about Bredon, and the beautiful game, and the way you learn to see three moves ahead. I could not tell him about the Adem, and the Lethani, and the way your body learns to move before your mind has finished thinking. I could not tell him about any of it. It was in the chest, upstairs at the Waystone. It was in the cord.

But my body remembered. That was the thing. My body had always remembered. It had seen the rope, and known, and run.

"I saw it fraying," I said. "That's all. I was looking up."

Graham looked at me for a long moment. He did not believe me. I could see that. Everyone in that yard had been looking up. Nobody else had seen it.

But he did not ask again. He reached down one big hand, and I took it, and he pulled me up. My knee buckled. He caught me. And he held me there, for a moment, with one hand gripping my arm, and looked me in the face.

"I don't know who you are, Kote," he said quietly. So quietly no one else could hear. "I never asked. I figured it was your business." He glanced at his grandson, in his mother's arms. "And I won't ask now. But whoever you are—" His voice cracked. "Thank you."

---

They finished the barn that afternoon. With a new rope. Graham checked every inch of it himself, twice. I sat on a bench under the apple trees with my knee propped up on a stool, and Rena brought me pie, and Jake brought me cider, which was terrible, and Tobin sat beside me for an hour without saying anything, and then went away.

The barn was beautiful. Oak and stone and slate, square and solid and true. It stands there still, on the slope above the river, a mile east of Newarre. If you go and look at the corner post of the north wall, the one I caught Tobin under, you will find two sets of initials carved into the foot of it. Graham's. And below them, very small, very neat, carved later, when no one was looking:

*K.*

Graham did that. I never asked him to. I only found it the next spring, when I went up to help him with the hay.

I did not say anything about it. Neither did he.

---

There was a dance in the new barn that night, by lantern light. Everyone came. Old Cob played the fiddle, very badly. Bast danced with every girl in Newarre, one after another, until their mothers started glaring. I sat on a hay bale at the back with my knee wrapped in a strip of cloth and a mug of Jake's cider in my hand, and watched.

I did not dance. I did not feel anything. But I stayed until the very end, until the lanterns burned low and the last of the dancers went home. And when Bast came to find me, flushed and grinning, to help me limp back to the Waystone, he looked at me for a long moment and said:

"You caught him, Reshi."

"Yes."

"Before the rope broke. You knew."

"Yes."

Bast was quiet for a moment. Then he put my arm over his shoulder, and we started down the lane together in the dark, slowly, toward the lights of the town.

"You're still in there," he said softly. So softly I almost did not hear. "I knew it. I knew you were still in there."

I did not answer. My knee hurt. The stars were very bright.

But I did not tell him he was wrong.
