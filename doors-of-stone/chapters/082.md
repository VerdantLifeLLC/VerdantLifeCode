# The Field of Three Rivers

The war came to Renere in the spring.

I did not see it. I was in the Tower of Thorns, in my grey cell, with my grey bread and my grey water and the grey knife of light that came in at midday through the slit of a window. I did not see the armies. I did not see the banners, or the cavalry charges, or the smoke. I did not hear the drums or the trumpets or the screams.

But I heard Bast tell it. Every night, pressed into the window slit, with his deep blue eyes glinting in the dark. He told it to me the way a Ruh tells a story around a fire. With all the parts in their places. With the brave parts and the terrible parts and the parts that make you laugh when you should not.

I will tell it to you the way he told it to me. It is not my story. But it is part of mine.

---

The Maer came north with the spring rains.

He had spent the winter in the southern hills, gathering. Lord by lord, town by town, sword by sword. The lords who had never trusted Duke Jakis. The lords who had loved old King Roderic. The lords who had heard the rumors about the four dead heirs and the old man with the stick, and did not like what they heard. And the ones who simply wanted to be on the winning side, and had begun to suspect that the winning side might not be the one in Renere.

By the time the snows melted, he had an army. Not as large as the duke's. Four thousand to the duke's seven. But it was a different kind of army.

"It had the Adem," said Bast. "Twenty of them, now. More came, over the winter, from Haert. Vashet sent for them. They say Shehyn sent every sword she could spare." He laughed softly. "Twenty Adem. The duke's men were terrified of them. They'd heard stories. About what happened in the cellar. About Cinder's face."

"And the others?"

"Your friends." Bast's voice softened. "Wil has the arcanists. Thirty of them, from the University and from Imre and from the lords' households. Not very good ones, most of them. But he's taught them things. Things they don't teach at the University. How to make fire jump from one field to another. How to break a bridge with sympathy. How to make a man's sword so heavy he can't lift it." He paused. "He's very good at it. Better than he knew. He says his hands can't do fine work anymore, so he's had to learn to do big work instead."

"And Devi?"

"Devi has a wagon." Bast's eyes glinted with amusement. "A whole wagon, full of Surprises. Big ones. Little ones. Ones that make smoke and ones that make fire and ones that make a noise so loud it knocks men off their horses. The Maer's captains won't go near it. They call it the Devil's Cart."

"And Sim?"

Bast was quiet for a moment.

"Sim writes songs," he said. "About the war. About the men who die in it. He sings them at the fires at night, in the camp, and the soldiers cry." He paused. "He wears a patch over his eye. Black. With a white star sewn on it. Devi made it for him. He says it makes him look dashing." A soft laugh. "It doesn't. It makes him look like a very sad pirate. But nobody tells him."

---

They met on the Field of Three Rivers, a day's march south of Renere, where the three rivers that meet in the city first come near each other.

It is a wide flat plain, green in the spring, with low hills on either side and the three rivers winding across it like the strands of a braid. It is a good place for a battle. Everyone says so. There have been seven great battles fought there, in the history of Vintas. Eight, now.

The duke came out of Renere with seven thousand men and drew them up on the plain in the old way. Pikes in the center, horse on the wings, archers behind. Black and silver banners snapping in the wind. He had more men. He had more horses. He had the high ground, a low rise at the northern end of the plain.

The Maer came up from the south with four thousand and drew them up facing.

"Everyone thought the duke would win," said Bast. "Even the Maer's own captains. They told him not to fight. They said wait, gather more men, draw the duke out into the hills. But the Maer said no." A pause. "He said his daughter was growing up somewhere without knowing her name. And his wife had gone white-haired in a cellar. And a boy who had saved them both was sitting in a tower in Renere waiting to hang. And he was not going to wait anymore."

---

The battle began at dawn.

The duke's pikes advanced first. A great slow wall of steel, rolling down off the low rise toward the Maer's lines. The Maer's men held. They held for an hour, pike against pike, in the center of the plain, and neither side gave ground.

Then the duke's horse charged. Both wings at once, two thousand horsemen in black and silver, thundering around the ends of the Maer's line to take him in the flanks.

And Wil's arcanists broke the rivers.

"I didn't see it," said Bast. "I was on the hill behind. But I heard it. Everyone heard it." His voice was full of wonder. "They'd been bound for days. Wil's people. Bound to the riverbanks. Every stone, every clod of earth, every root. They'd been sitting in the mud for three days, holding their bindings. And when the horse came around the flanks, Wil gave the signal."

"And?"

"And the banks gave way. All at once. Both rivers, on both wings. The ground just fell out from under the horsemen. They went into the water, hundreds of them, horses and men together." Bast was quiet for a moment. "It was terrible. I'm glad I didn't see it. But it broke the charge. Both charges. The duke's horse was gone."

---

The duke's pikes were still coming. Still pushing, in the center of the plain. And the Maer's line was bending. Bending back, slowly, under the weight of it.

That was when Devi's cart came out.

"She drove it herself," said Bast, and I could hear the grin in his voice. "The Devil's Cart. Right out in front of the Maer's line, with two mules pulling it and her standing on the seat with her spectacles on and her hair flying. Nobody could stop her. The Maer's captains tried. She drove right past them."

"What did she do?"

"She threw things. Everything in the cart. All of it, one after another, right into the middle of the duke's pikes. White fire and smoke and noise and something that made the ground shake." He laughed. "The pikemen broke. They'd never seen anything like it. They thought the sky was falling. They threw down their pikes and ran."

"And Devi?"

"Devi's fine. She lost her eyebrows. She's very upset about it." Bast's voice softened. "The soldiers carried her on their shoulders, afterward. All around the camp. She pretended to hate it. She didn't."

---

The Adem went in after that. Into the gap, where the duke's pikes had broken. Twenty of them, in their blood-red clothes, moving like a single thing. Like a knife.

"I've never seen anything like it," said Bast. "I don't think anyone has. They didn't fight the way soldiers fight. They didn't shout, or charge, or form lines. They just—*went*. Into the duke's army. Through it. The way water goes through a sieve." His voice was very quiet. "Vashet was at the front. She was looking for someone."

"Who?"

"Cinder."

I went very still.

"Was he there?"

"No." Bast's voice was even quieter. "She looked all day. Through the whole battle. She didn't find him." A pause. "I don't think he's anywhere anymore. Not anywhere near. I think he ran. After the cellar. After what she did to his face." Another pause. "I think he's hiding. Somewhere very far away. Waiting for it to heal."

"Does it? Heal?"

"I don't know," said Bast. "Things like him—I don't know how they work. Maybe it heals in a day. Maybe it never heals." He was quiet. "I hope it never heals."

---

The duke died at noon.

Not in battle. Not by a sword, or an arrow, or an Adem's hand. He was sitting on his black horse on the low rise at the northern end of the plain, watching his army break, watching his pikes throw down their weapons and run, watching his banners fall one by one.

And he simply fell off his horse.

"His heart," said Bast. "The physicians said it was his heart. It just stopped. He was a big man, and old, and he'd been grieving all winter." He was quiet for a moment. "They found him lying in the grass on the rise, on his back, with his eyes open. Looking at the sky."

I did not say anything. I sat in the grey dark of my cell, and I thought about a heavy grey man kneeling on the stones of a courtyard with his hand on his son's chest. His shoulders shaking.

I did not know him. I did not know why I felt what I felt.

But I felt it.

---

"When the duke fell, it was over," said Bast. "His lords surrendered. His men threw down their weapons. The Maer rode up onto the low rise where the duke had been, and he got off his horse and knelt beside the duke's body, and closed his eyes." A pause. "He said something. I was close enough to hear."

"What?"

"He said, *I'm sorry. We were both fools. But you lost your son, and I only lost my city.*" Bast's voice was very quiet. "And then he stood up, and he turned to face his army, and they cheered him. Four thousand of them. On the Field of Three Rivers. They cheered him until the hills rang."

"And then?"

"And then he rode to Renere," said Bast. "And they opened the gates for him."

---

Bast came to the window the next night, and the night after, and the night after that. And every night he had more to tell.

The Maer had entered Renere. The city had opened its gates without a fight; the duke's garrison had fled in the night when word of the battle came. The people had lined the streets to see him pass. Some of them had cheered. Some of them had thrown flowers. Most of them had only watched, silent and wary, the way people watch a storm passing on the horizon.

The Maer had gone to the palace. He had gone to the great hall, where the throne of Vintas sat on its dais beneath the black and silver banners of the house of Jakis. He had had the banners taken down. He had not sat on the throne.

The lords had come to him. All of them. The ones who had fought for him and the ones who had fought against him. They had knelt before him in the great hall and offered him the crown.

"And he said no," said Bast. "He refused it. In front of all of them."

"Why?"

"He said he wasn't worthy. He said he'd brought war to the kingdom, and death, and burned cities. He said he'd let a man like Bredon live in his house for eleven years and never seen him for what he was. He said he'd sent a boy to Renere to do what he should have done himself, and the boy had been blamed for it, and was sitting in the Tower of Thorns waiting to hang." Bast paused. "He said a man like that shouldn't be king."

"And the lords?"

"They offered it again. The next day. And he refused again." Bast's voice was full of wonder. "And the third day, they came again. All of them. And they knelt, and they offered it a third time. And one of them—an old one, very old, from the east—stood up and said, *Your grace, there is no one else. There is no heir. There is no one else the kingdom will follow. If you refuse, there will be war again. And again. And again. Until there is nothing left.*"

"And?"

"And the Maer was quiet for a long time," said Bast. "And then he said yes. On one condition."

"What condition?"

"That he be allowed to do penance," said Bast. "Before he was crowned. For the war. For the dead. For everything. He said he would walk from the gates of the city to the doors of the cathedral, barefoot, in a shirt of sackcloth, with ashes on his head. In front of everyone. So that they would all see. So that they would all know that he knew what he had done."

---

He did it. On a grey morning at the end of spring, the Maer of Severen walked barefoot through the streets of Renere in a shirt of rough brown sackcloth, with grey ashes smeared on his forehead and in his hair. From the north gate to the cathedral. Two miles, over cobblestones.

The whole city came out to watch.

"They didn't cheer," said Bast. "Not at first. They didn't know what to do. They'd never seen a king do anything like it. They just stood there and watched him walk." A pause. "His feet were bleeding by the end. He didn't stop. He didn't look at anyone. He just walked."

"And at the end?"

"At the end, he went into the cathedral, and they crowned him. And he came out on the steps, in the crown, with his feet still bleeding and the ashes still on his head. And he looked out at the crowd." Bast's voice was very soft. "And someone, somewhere in the crowd, called out a name. And then someone else. And then everyone."

"What name?"

"The Penitent King," said Bast.

---

I sat in the grey dark of my cell, and I thought about that for a long time after Bast had gone.

The Penitent King. The Maer. Alveron. A man I did not remember, who had walked barefoot through a city in sackcloth and ashes, for a war I did not remember, for the deaths of people I did not remember.

For me.

I did not know him. But I held the cord on my wrist, and I ran my fingers over the knots, and I felt something. Very faint. Like a voice in another room.

It was not a memory. It was not a name.

But it was warm.

---

The Penitent King came to see me three days later.

He came alone. No guards, no servants, no courtiers. He came up the long winding stair of the Tower of Thorns by himself, in plain grey clothes, with a crown on his head and bandages on his feet. He stood at the grate in my door and looked in at me for a long time.

I was sitting on my pallet, with my back against the wall and my knees drawn up. I looked back at him.

He was older than I had expected. Hollow-cheeked and grey-haired, with deep lines around his eyes and mouth. He did not look like a king. He looked like a man who had been very ill for a long time, and had only just begun to recover.

"Kvothe," he said.

I did not answer. The name meant nothing to me.

"Do you know who I am?"

"The Penitent King."

Something moved in his face. It might have been pain. "Yes," he said. "I suppose I am, now." He was quiet for a moment. "Before that, I was the Maer of Severen. And before that, I was a sick old man in a palace on a cliff, being poisoned by his own physician. And a red-haired boy came, and saved my life."

I looked at him. I did not remember.

"You don't remember," he said.

"No."

"Meluan said you wouldn't." He was quiet. "She said something happened to you in the cellar. Something with your name. She said you gave it away, to close the door."

"Yes."

"Why?"

I looked at him. At his hollow face and his grey hair and the crown on his head. I tried to think of an answer. There was nothing in me to think with.

"Because it was the right thing," I said. "I don't remember why. But I know it was."

The Penitent King looked at me for a very long time.

Then he put his hand on the bars of the grate. His knuckles were white.

"They want you dead," he said quietly. "The lords. All of them. Even the ones who fought for me. They don't care that you saved my wife. They don't care that you closed the door. They don't even believe in the door, most of them." His voice was very low. "They only care that you killed a king. A king of Vintas. With your own hand. And if a Ruh can kill a king and live, then no king is safe."

I did not say anything.

"They'll try you," he said. "And they'll find you guilty. And they'll want you hanged. And I—" He stopped. His hand tightened on the bars. "I'm the king now. I can pardon you. I have that power. But if I do, they'll rise against me. All of them. And there'll be war again. And again. And again. Until there's nothing left."

"Then don't pardon me."

He stared at me.

"Don't pardon me," I said again. My voice was flat. "Let them hang me. It doesn't matter."

"It matters to me."

"Why?"

He looked at me for a long moment. And then something in his face broke, just for a moment. Like ice cracking on a pond.

"Because you're family," said the Penitent King. "And I do not abandon family."

He took his hand from the bars. He stood very straight.

"I'll find a way," he said. "I don't know how. But I'll find a way."

And he turned and went back down the long winding stair, alone, on his bandaged feet.
