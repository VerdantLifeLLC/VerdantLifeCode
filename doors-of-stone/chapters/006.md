# Chapter Six: The Missing Pages

It was Wil who first put the idea of the knife into words.

We were sitting in the Tomes, the great reading room of the Archives, late on a Cendling evening. The room was nearly empty. A few scattered students bent over their books at the long tables, and the red light of the sympathy lamps pooled around them like small warm islands in a dark sea. Wil was working his shift at the desk, but it was a slow night, and he had come over to my table to see what I was doing.

What I was doing was staring at a book with a hole in it.

Not a literal hole. The book was intact. Its binding was whole, its spine uncracked. But when I turned to the chapter I wanted, a chapter titled "On the Seven and Their Works," I found that the chapter was three pages long. The first page began with a paragraph of introduction. The third page ended with a list of sources. And in between, where the meat of the chapter should have been, there was only one page, and that page was about the tax policies of the Aturan provinces.

"That's strange," Wil said, looking over my shoulder.

"It's more than strange." I turned the page back and forth. "Look at the numbering. Page two hundred and eleven. Then two hundred and twelve. Then two hundred and thirteen. They're consecutive. Nothing's missing. But the text doesn't connect. It goes from the introduction of one chapter straight into the middle of another one, and then back again."

Wil leaned closer. He studied the pages for a long time with his dark eyes narrowed.

"Rebound," he said at last.

"What?"

"The book's been rebound." He pointed to the gutter, the narrow space between the pages where they met the spine. "See the stitching? It's different in this section. Newer thread. Someone took the book apart, removed some pages, replaced them with pages from another book, renumbered them, and sewed it back together." He straightened. "It's very good work. I've never seen better."

"Who would do that?"

Wil shrugged. "A scriv, maybe. Sometimes when a book is damaged, the scrivs will repair it with pages from a duplicate. But they're supposed to note it in the catalog." He frowned. "And they don't usually renumber."

I sat back in my chair. I felt cold.

"Wil," I said. "How many books have you seen like this?"

"Like what?"

"Books where the pages don't connect. Where it feels like something's missing."

He thought about it. "A few," he said slowly. "Not many. You notice it, sometimes, if you're reading closely. But most people don't read closely. They skim. They look for what they need and move on."

"Do you remember which books?"

He looked at me for a long moment. "Why?"

I told him. I told him about the years I had spent searching for information on the Chandrian and the Amyr, and how every trail seemed to lead to a dead end. I told him about the books with chapters that were too short, and the histories that skipped over entire decades, and the indexes that referred to pages that did not seem to exist. I had always thought it was simple bad luck, or bad scholarship, or the ordinary loss and decay that comes to any collection of old books.

But now I was not so sure.

"You think someone's been cutting out pages about the Chandrian," Wil said. His voice was carefully neutral.

"I think someone's been cutting out pages about something."

He was quiet for a long time. Then he said, "That would take a very long time. Years. Decades. There are millions of books in the Archives."

"I know."

"And they'd have to know exactly which pages to take. They'd have to read everything. Every book, every scroll, every scrap of paper."

"I know."

"And they'd have to do it without anyone noticing." He shook his head. "It's impossible."

"Is it?"

He did not answer.

---

I went to see Lorren the next morning.

His office was on the top floor of the Archives, a long narrow room lined with books from floor to ceiling. He was sitting at his desk, writing in a ledger, when I knocked. He looked up at me with his calm, expressionless face, and for a long moment he did not say anything.

"Re'lar Kvothe," he said at last. "What can I do for you?"

"I have a question, Master Lorren. About the books in the Archives."

"Ask."

"Has anyone ever been caught removing pages from books?"

He set down his pen. It was a small gesture, but from Lorren it was the equivalent of a shout.

"Why do you ask?"

I told him. I told him about the book with the rebound section, and about the other books I had found over the years with gaps in them. I did not tell him what the gaps were about. I had learned, long ago, that Lorren did not like to hear about the Chandrian.

He listened in silence. When I was done, he was quiet for a long time.

"Show me," he said at last.

I showed him. I took him down to the Tomes and pulled the book from the shelf and opened it to the rebound section. He looked at it for a long time. He ran his long fingers over the stitching. He held the pages up to the light.

Then he closed the book and put it back on the shelf and turned to me.

"Thank you, Re'lar," he said. "I will look into this."

"Master Lorren—"

"I will look into this," he repeated. His voice was very calm. It was always very calm. But there was something beneath it now, something hard and cold, like the edge of a knife under a silk cloth. "You may go."

I went.

But as I left the Tomes, I looked back, and I saw him standing there beside the shelf, his hand resting on the spine of the book, his head bowed as if in prayer. He stood like that for a long time. And I remember thinking, as I walked away, that he did not look like a man who had just learned something new. He looked like a man who had just been reminded of something old.

---

It was three days before I saw Lorren again. And when I did, it was not in the Archives. It was on the steps of the Mains, early in the morning, where I had stopped to buy a hot bun from a vendor before class.

He came down the steps toward me and stopped. He looked at me for a long moment with his calm, colorless eyes.

"Re'lar Kvothe," he said. "Walk with me."

I walked with him. We crossed the courtyard in silence and went out through the gates and along the road toward the river. It was a cold, bright morning. The trees along the road had lost most of their leaves, and the ones that remained were brown and brittle and rattled in the wind.

"The book you showed me," Lorren said at last. "I have examined it. You were correct. It has been rebound. Twenty-six pages were removed and replaced with pages from another volume."

"Do you know who did it?"

"No." He walked on in silence for a while. "I have examined other books. Books on similar subjects. I have found eleven more with similar alterations. The work is very old. Some of it is very, very old. Older than my tenure as Master Archivist. Older than my predecessor's." He paused. "Some of it is older than the University itself."

I stopped walking. Lorren stopped too, and turned to face me.

"That's impossible," I said. "The Archives are part of the University."

"The Archives," Lorren said, "are older than the University. The University was built around the Archives. Not the other way around." He looked at me. "Did you not know?"

"I'd heard. But I thought it was just a story."

"Most true things are, eventually." He began walking again, and I hurried to catch up. "The core of the Archives, the oldest part, was here before anyone came to this place. Before the bridge, before the town. There are records, very old records, that speak of a great stone building standing alone on the bank of the river. Nobody knew who had built it or why. The first scholars who came here found it empty. They filled it with books."

"Who built it?"

"I do not know." He was silent for a moment. "There are those who say it was built by the Amyr."

I stopped walking again.

This time Lorren did not stop. He walked on, his hands clasped behind his back, his long black robe fluttering in the wind. After a moment I ran to catch up.

"Master Lorren—"

"I know what you are going to ask," he said. "And I am going to tell you something. Once. I will not repeat it, and if you ever tell anyone that I said it, I will deny it, and I will bar you from the Archives for the rest of your life. Do you understand?"

"I understand."

He walked on for a while in silence. Then he said, "When I was a young man, younger than you are now, I was a student here. And like many students, I was curious about the Amyr. I read everything I could find about them. And I found, as you have found, that there was very little to read."

"The pages were missing."

"Some of them. Others were never written at all." He glanced at me. "The Amyr did not like to be written about. They were an order of great secrecy, even at the height of their power. Their records were kept in a cipher that has never been broken. Their members were known to one another by names that were not their own. And when the Church disbanded them, they did not go quietly into the night. They went somewhere."

"Where?"

"Nobody knows. But there were rumors, when I was a boy, that some of them had come here. That they had taken refuge in the Archives, in the old stone rooms beneath the Stacks. That they had hidden themselves among the books, the way a leaf hides among other leaves." He stopped walking and turned to face me. "I searched for them. For years. I read every book in the Archives that might have held a clue. I explored every room I could get into. I became Master Archivist, in part, because I thought it would give me the access I needed to find them."

"Did you?"

He looked at me for a long time. His face was as calm as ever, but his eyes were very tired.

"No," he said. "I found the Four-plate door. And I found that I could not open it. And I found, eventually, that I did not want to." He turned away, looking out at the river. "There are some questions, Re'lar Kvothe, that are better left unanswered. There are some doors that are better left closed. I learned that lesson a long time ago. I hope, for your sake, that you learn it too."

He began to walk back toward the University. Then he stopped and looked back at me.

"The pages were cut by someone who knew exactly what they were looking for," he said. "Someone patient. Someone who has been doing this for a very long time. Longer than any man could live." He paused. "If you go looking for them, they will know. And they will come looking for you."

He walked away. I stood alone on the road beside the river, watching him go.

The wind rattled the dead leaves on the trees. The river rushed past, cold and brown and swift.

*Longer than any man could live*, he had said.

I thought about the Chandrian. About Cinder, with his black eyes and his cold smile, who had not aged a day in all the stories I had ever heard about him. About Haliax, who could not die. About the seven who had been walking the world since before the fall of the Aturan Empire, since before there was an Aturan Empire.

If anyone could spend centuries in the Archives, cutting pages from books, it was them.

But the Chandrian could not have done it. Not alone. They could not have walked into the Archives without anyone noticing. Their signs would have given them away. The blue flame, the rot, the rust, the cold. Someone would have seen.

Which meant that someone else had done it for them.

Someone human. Someone patient. Someone who had been walking among us, perhaps for a very long time, cutting away the truth one page at a time.

---

I went back to the Archives that afternoon and spent the rest of the day in the Stacks, searching.

I did not find the Amyr. I did not find the Chandrian. But in a dusty corner of the lower Stacks, in a section devoted to the genealogies of minor Vintish nobility, I found a thin book bound in faded blue leather. Its title was stamped on the spine in flaking gold letters: *The Lines of Loeclos, with Some Account of Their Holdings and Obligations*.

I almost did not take it down. I was looking for something else. But the name caught my eye, and I remembered a rhyme that children in Vintas sang, a rhyme about a lady and seven things she kept. I remembered the Maer's new wife, Meluan, and her family, and the strange box she had shown me, the box with no lid and no lock that had seemed to hum under my fingers.

I took the book down. I opened it.

It was a genealogy. Names and dates, marriages and deaths, stretching back hundreds of years. The family had been called Loeclos, once. Then Lockless. Then Lackless. The names changed, but the line ran on unbroken, from mother to daughter, father to son, like a thread through the long cloth of history.

And beside some of the names, written in a tiny cramped hand in the margins, there was a mark. A small symbol, like a knot. I did not recognize it. But I had seen something like it before. I had seen it in the heart of Kilvin's candle, woven into the wax.

I turned the pages slowly, my heart pounding.

And near the end of the book, on a page that was otherwise blank, I found a single line of writing in that same cramped hand. It was in a script I did not know. But beneath it, someone had written a translation, in faded brown ink that might once have been blood:

*The door holds while the line holds. The line holds while the name is kept.*

I read it three times. Then I closed the book, and sat for a long time with my hands flat on its cover, feeling the cool leather beneath my palms.

I did not know what it meant. Not yet.

But I noticed something else, as I sat there. Something that made my skin prickle and the hair stand up on the back of my neck.

In the genealogy, there was a gap. Near the end, in the last few generations. A page had been removed. Carefully, cleanly, with a very sharp knife. The edge of it was still visible, a thin strip of paper along the spine.

The page before the gap listed the children of a lord named Lackless who had died some twenty years before. Two daughters. The younger was named Meluan.

The elder had no name at all. Where her name should have been, there was only a small black mark, as if someone had pressed the tip of a pen against the page and held it there until the ink soaked through.

I stared at that black mark for a long time. And as I stared, a memory rose up in me, unbidden, the way a fish rises in a still pond.

I was very young. We were camped somewhere in the hills, and I had learned a rhyme from a boy in a village we had played in. A rude rhyme, about Lady Lackless and the things she kept under her dress. I was proud of it. I sang it at the fire that night, loud and bright, the way children sing the things they know they should not.

My mother had not laughed. My mother, who laughed at everything. She had gone very still, and very pale, and she had taken me by the arm and walked me away from the fire and told me, in a voice I had never heard her use before or since, that it was not a kind song, and that I was never to sing it again. And then, strangest of all, she had held me tight, much too tight, and I had felt her shaking.

I had not understood. I had been a child. Children do not understand their parents; they only remember them.

But I remembered other things now, sitting there in the dusty light of the Stacks. The way she never spoke of her family. The way she would change the subject, smoothly and completely, whenever anyone asked where she had come from. The way my father had written a song for her once, a song about a woman who had left a great house and a great name behind her for love, and how she had asked him never to play it anywhere but around our own fire. The way she held a fork, and a teacup, and herself, as if she had been taught to do so by someone who expected her to one day sit at the tables of lords.

An elder daughter, whose name had been blotted from the book. A girl who had run away.

My mother's name had been Laurian. That was what my father called her. That was what she called herself.

But I knew, sitting there with the blue book open in my hands and the black mark staring up at me like an eye, that it had not always been her name.

Her name had been Netalia. Netalia Lackless. She had run away from her family to marry a trouper, one of the Edema Ruh. She had changed her name, and given up her house, and gone out onto the roads with a red-haired man who made songs. She had sung with him, about the Chandrian, songs they had pieced together from old men in taverns and from old books in forgotten libraries.

And one night, beneath a sky full of stars, she had died for it.

She was my mother. And her sister was married to the Maer.
