# Rike

In my third year at the Waystone, a boy tried to rob me.

He was not a very good thief. I knew that from the first moment, the way I would have known a bad lute player from the first chord. He came in on a slow afternoon in autumn, when the common room was empty except for me, and he stood by the door for a long time with his hands in his pockets, pretending to look at the sword over the bar.

He was about eleven. Thin, with a narrow sharp face and dirty blond hair cut short with a knife, and a bruise on his cheekbone that was going yellow at the edges. His shirt was too small for him and his shoes were too big. He had the look of a boy who had been hungry for a long time and had gotten used to it.

I knew that look. I had worn it for three years, in Tarbean.

"Can I help you?" I said.

"Just looking," he said.

"At the sword?"

"Yeah." He did not look at the sword. He looked at the till, the small wooden drawer under the bar where I kept the day's coin. Then at me. Then at the door to the kitchen. Then at the till again. He was not subtle.

"It's a good sword," I said. "Old. Came from a long way away."

"Yeah?" He edged toward the bar. "What's its name?"

"Folly," I said. "It says so on the plaque."

He looked at the plaque then. His lips moved slightly. He could not read, I realized. He was trying to make out the letters and he could not.

"Folly," he repeated. "Funny name for a sword."

"It was a funny sword."

---

I went into the kitchen. I did not need to. There was nothing in the kitchen that needed doing. But I went, and I left the door ajar, and I stood just behind it and waited.

It took him less than a minute. I heard him slip behind the bar. I heard the soft scrape of the till drawer. I heard coins, very quiet, very careful, sliding into a pocket.

I came back out.

He froze. He was standing behind the bar with one hand in the till and the other in his pocket, and his face went white under the yellowing bruise, and he looked at me with the flat, blank, hopeless eyes of a trapped animal.

I did not say anything. I went and sat down at the bar, on the customer's side, and folded my hands on the wood.

The boy did not move.

"It's a bad till to rob," I said. "There's never much in it. Most of my custom comes in the evening. You'd have done better to wait till closing."

He stared at me.

"And the kitchen door's a trick," I said. "You should always wonder why someone's leaving you alone. Nobody leaves a stranger alone with the till. Not unless they want to see what he'll do."

His hand came slowly out of the till. It was empty. The other hand stayed in his pocket.

"You going to call the constable?" he said. His voice was very steady. I was impressed. I remembered how hard it was to keep your voice steady at that age, when you were caught.

"There isn't a constable in Newarre," I said. "There's a sheriff in Baedn-Bryt, but he doesn't come out this way unless someone's been murdered. And even then he takes his time."

"Then what?"

I looked at him for a long moment.

"Are you hungry?" I said.

---

His name was Rike. He did not tell me that; I had to find it out later, from Graham. He did not tell me anything, that first afternoon. He sat at the bar where I put him and ate the bowl of stew I set in front of him, very fast, hunched over it with one arm curled around the bowl, the way you eat when you have learned that someone might take your food away. He ate three bowls. He ate half a loaf of bread. He did not say a word.

When he was done, he pushed the bowl away and looked at me with narrow, suspicious eyes.

"What do you want?" he said.

"Nothing."

"Everyone wants something."

I thought of a barn in the western hills, and an old man with a pack, and Mirra in a grey dress saying *I don't want anything. It's easier.*

"I want my money back," I said. "The coins in your pocket. Then you can go."

He looked at me for a long time. Then, slowly, he took his hand out of his pocket and set the coins on the bar. Four jots and a handful of iron drabs. I had not known there was that much in the till.

"You could've just called for help," he said. "Grabbed me. I'm not big."

"I could have."

"Why didn't you?"

I did not answer. I picked up the coins and put them back in the till. When I turned around, he was still sitting there, watching me.

"Come back tomorrow," I said. "Around noon. The woodpile needs splitting. I'll pay you a jot for it. And lunch."

He stared at me.

"Why?"

"Because the woodpile needs splitting," I said. "And I'm lazy."

He did not believe me. I could see that. But he slid off the stool and went to the door, and at the door he stopped, and looked back at me one more time with those flat suspicious eyes.

Then he was gone.

---

Graham told me about him that evening.

"Rike?" He shook his head slowly. "That's Jessom's boy. Lives out past the old quarry, in that shack with the fallen chimney. You'll have seen it." He took a long pull of his beer. "Jessom drinks. Has since his wife left. He's not a bad man, when he's sober. But he's not sober much."

"The bruise?"

Graham did not answer for a moment. He looked at his beer.

"There's been talk," he said at last. "About going out there. Some of us. Having a word with Jessom. But it's not our place, is it? A man's family is his own business." He did not sound as if he believed it. "And the boy never complains. Never says anything. Just goes about with his face all over colors and his mouth shut."

I did not say anything. I polished a glass.

"He's a thief, you know," said Graham. "Rike. Steals from everyone. Eggs out of Jake's henhouse. Apples. Once a whole ham out of the smokehouse behind Rena's. Everyone knows it's him. Nobody does anything." He shrugged. "What would we do? Put him in the stocks? He's eleven."

"No," I said. "Nothing like that."

---

Rike came back the next day. At noon. He did not say anything. He stood in the yard and looked at the woodpile, and I gave him the axe, and he split the wood.

He was not very good at it. He was too small and too thin and the axe was too heavy for him. But he did not give up. He split the whole pile, badly, over three hours, and when he was done he stacked the split wood against the wall of the stable, very neatly, and came into the kitchen and stood by the door.

I gave him a jot. I gave him lunch. He ate it the way he had eaten the stew, hunched over, one arm curled around the bowl.

"Come back tomorrow," I said.

He came back.

---

He came back every day, that autumn. Sometimes I had work for him and sometimes I did not, and when I did not, I made some up. He split wood and swept the yard and carried water from the well and mucked out the stable and scrubbed pots. He did not talk. I did not make him.

He stopped stealing. From me, at least. I do not know about Jake's henhouse. But the till was never short again, and I made a point of leaving it unlocked, and of leaving the room while he was there, so that he would know I trusted him. And he did not take anything.

Once, in the second month, I came back into the common room and found him standing in front of the sword again, looking up at the plaque.

"What does it say?" he said, without turning around. "Really. Not just the name. There's more. I can see the letters."

There was not more. The plaque said only *Folly*, burned deep into the oak. But I understood what he was asking.

"Would you like to learn?" I said. "To read?"

He turned around. His narrow face was very still.

"Why would you teach me?"

"Because someone taught me," I said. "A long time ago. A man named Abenthy. He didn't have to. He did it anyway."

Rike looked at me for a long time. Then he looked back at the plaque.

"F," he said. "That's the first one. I know that one. It's in *Fall*. On the sign at the mill."

"Yes," I said. "That's right."

---

I taught him to read that winter. At the corner table, in the afternoons, when the common room was empty. I wrote out letters on a slate and he copied them, frowning, with his tongue caught between his teeth. He was quick. Much quicker than he let anyone know. By midwinter he could read the sign over the door, and by spring he could read the whole of the Tehlin prayer book the priest had given the inn years ago, which sat on a shelf by the door gathering dust.

He did not thank me. He did not have to.

But one day in spring, he came in with a new bruise on his jaw, purple and swollen, and he sat down at the corner table and opened the prayer book and began to read, and he did not look at me, and I did not ask. And after a while, without looking up, he said:

"I'm going to leave. When I'm older. I'm going to go somewhere and be someone."

"Where?"

"I don't know." He turned a page. "Somewhere with books. Maybe the University." He glanced up at me, quick, and then down again. "You ever been there?"

I polished the glass in my hands. I looked at the sword over the bar.

"Once," I said. "A long time ago."

"What was it like?"

I was quiet for a long moment.

"It was the best place I ever knew," I said. "And I made a terrible mess of it."

Rike looked at me. For a moment, something moved behind his narrow, suspicious eyes. Something that was almost curiosity. Almost the beginning of a question.

Then he looked back down at the prayer book.

"I won't," he said. "Make a mess of it. When I go."

"No," I said. "I don't think you will."

---

He did go. Years later. But that is his story, not mine, and I will not tell it here.

I will only say that on the day he left, he came into the Waystone early in the morning with a pack on his back and new boots on his feet, boots that fit, and stood in front of the bar for a long moment without saying anything.

Then he reached into his pocket and set four jots and a handful of iron drabs on the bar.

"I owed you," he said.

I looked at the coins. Then at him.

"No," I said. "You didn't."

"I know," said Rike. He did not smile. He never smiled. But something in his narrow face was different. "That's why I have to give it to you."

He went out the door. He did not look back. And I stood behind the bar with four jots and a handful of iron drabs in my hand, and for a moment, just a moment, the grey in me cracked, and something came through.

It was only a little. Just enough.
