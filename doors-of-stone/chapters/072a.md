# The Last Keeper

We rested twice in the keepers' tunnel before we came to the silver light. I have told you about the first time, in the small round cave with the trickle of water, where Devi sorted her Surprises and Bast held an iron knife until his fingers blistered. I have not told you about the second.

It was deeper. Much deeper. The passage had been winding down through the heart of the ridge for what felt like hours, and the air had grown so cold that our breath hung in front of our faces like smoke, and the carvings on the walls had grown thicker. Knots. Lines and lines of them, spiraling along the stone, so dense in places that the wall seemed to be made of nothing else.

And then the passage opened into a small chamber, no bigger than a cottage kitchen, and in the chamber there was a chair.

---

It was carved from the living rock. A stone chair, plain and square, facing the passage that led onward and down. Beside it, a stone shelf. On the shelf, a lamp. Not a sympathy lamp; an old clay oil lamp, the kind you see in very old paintings, dry and cracked. And beside the lamp, a spindle, and a bundle of dark cord, and a small clay cup.

Fela stopped in the doorway. She put her hand flat against the wall.

"Someone sat here," she said softly. "For a long time. Years. Every day."

"A keeper," I said.

"The last one." Fela closed her eyes. "The stone remembers her. She came down every day for forty years. She sat in the chair and she tied knots. All day. Every day." Fela's voice was very quiet. "The stone remembers the feel of her hands. Small. Quick. Always moving."

"What was she tying?"

Fela was quiet for a long time, with her hand on the wall and her eyes closed.

"Names," she said at last. "The names of the keepers. All of them. Back and back and back. She was tying them into cords, so they wouldn't be forgotten." She opened her eyes. "The stone says she was afraid. Toward the end. Afraid that she was the last. That no one would come after her."

I looked at the bundle of dark cord on the shelf beside the lamp. I did not touch it.

"Was she?" I said. "The last?"

Fela did not answer right away. She moved her hand along the wall, slowly, the way a blind woman reads a face.

"Something happened," she said. "Near the end. Someone came down the tunnel who wasn't a keeper. A man. Old. With a stick." She stopped. Her face had gone very pale. "The stone remembers the stick. Tapping. On the floor. Coming down."

Everyone in the small chamber went very still.

"He talked to her," said Fela. "For a long time. She was sitting in the chair, and he was standing in the doorway. Where I am." She stepped out of the doorway, quickly, as if it were hot. "He was very kind. The stone remembers his voice was kind. And she listened. And then—" Fela stopped.

"And then?"

Fela looked at me. Her dark eyes were wet.

"And then she got up," she said. "And went with him. Up the tunnel. Out into the world. And she never came back." She was quiet for a moment. "The stone waited for her. For a long time. It didn't understand. Nobody ever came again."

---

I looked at the chair. At the lamp, and the spindle, and the bundle of cord.

"When?" I said. "When did this happen?"

Fela put her hand on the wall again. She was quiet for a long time.

"Stone doesn't count years the way we do," she said at last. "But not very long ago. For stone." She hesitated. "Sixty years? Seventy? A little more?"

I did the arithmetic in my head. The blue book in the Archives. *The Lines of Loeclos.* The long genealogy running back into darkness, and the last few generations before the gap where a page had been cut. My grandfather. My grandfather's mother.

"She had a name," I said. "The stone would know it. If she sat here forty years."

Fela closed her eyes. Her lips moved.

"*Liss*," she said at last. "Or something like it. The stone doesn't hear names the way we do. But her hands, when she tied—" She stopped. She opened her eyes. "Kvothe. She was tying a cord for herself, near the end. With her own name in it. The stone remembers."

"Where is it?"

We all looked at the shelf. At the bundle of dark cord beside the dry lamp.

I reached out. I did not decide to. I took up the bundle, very carefully. It was very old and very dry and it crackled in my hands. It was not one cord but many, tied together at one end. Dozens of them. Each one knotted along its length, dense and intricate.

And at the end of the bundle, tied on last, a short cord. Shorter than the rest. Newer. With only a few knots in it, and then nothing. As if the hands tying it had stopped in the middle.

I looked at it for a long time in the red light of my lamp. I could read some of it. Not much. The knot for *keeping*. The knot for *door*. The knot for *line*.

And a name. A small name. Not a true name, not the kind Denna could tie. Just a word, knotted in a cord, the way you would carve it on a stone.

*Liss*.

---

"My great-grandmother," I said.

I did not know it for certain. I have never known it for certain. But I knew it the way you sometimes know things. Standing in that cold small chamber with the keepers' cords in my hands, I knew it.

*Liss Lackless.* She had been in the blue book. I remembered the name now, in the long list of the last generations. *Liss, daughter of Harrow, keeper.* And beside it, in the margin, the small knot-mark I had seen beside so many of the names. And then a date. And nothing else.

No death recorded. Only the date. As if she had simply stopped.

"He took her," I said. "Bredon. He came down here and he talked to her and he took her away. The last keeper." I looked at the chair, empty for seventy years. "And after her, no one came. My grandfather never came. My mother never came. They didn't know. No one told them."

"Why?" said Sim softly. He was standing just behind Fela, with his thin face very pale in the lamplight. "Why take her? Why not just—"

"Kill her?" I shook my head. "He doesn't kill, if he can help it. He says it's inelegant. He moves you. He talks to you, and he's kind, and he shows you another road, and you walk down it yourself." I looked at the bundle of cords in my hands. "He didn't need her dead. He needed her gone. He needed the keeping to stop. So the line would forget. So that when it came time, whoever came to the door wouldn't know how to tend it. Only how to open it."

The small chamber was very quiet.

"The beautiful game," I said. "He's been playing it for seventy years. Longer. He took the keeper away, and the line forgot, and my mother ran away from a house full of secrets she was never taught to understand." My voice was very steady. "And then he found her. And then he found me."

---

Vashet had not come into the chamber. She was standing in the passage beyond, with the other Adem, watching the way ahead. But she turned her head now and looked at me.

"Kvothe," she said. "We cannot stay."

"I know."

"The cords. Will you take them?"

I looked down at them. At the dozens of dark knotted cords, dry and crackling, with the names of all the keepers back and back and back to a woman in the hills who loved a shaper for a little while. And the short one at the end, stopped in the middle.

"No," I said. "They belong here."

I laid them back on the shelf. Very carefully. Beside the dry lamp and the spindle and the clay cup. Exactly where she had left them, seventy years ago, when she got up and walked away with a kind old man and never came back.

And then I did something I had not planned. I took the thread from around my neck, the one with Denna's knot on it. *Road. Promise. A story told many times.* I did not take the knot off. I only held it, for a moment, over the short cord at the end of the bundle. Over *Liss*.

"I'm here," I said. Very quietly. To the chair, or the cords, or the stone. "I came. Someone came. You weren't the last."

Fela made a small sound behind me.

"It heard you," she whispered. "The stone. It heard you, Kvothe. It's—" She stopped. She pressed her hand flat against the wall. Her face was wet. "It's been waiting seventy years for someone to say that."

I put the thread back around my neck. I picked up my lamp.

And we went on, down the passage, toward the silver light.

---

I have thought about Liss Lackless a great deal since. About the forty years she sat in that chair, tying the names of the dead so they would not be forgotten. About the day a kind old man came down the tunnel, tapping his stick, and talked to her, and she got up and went with him.

I have wondered what he said. What story he told her. What other road he showed her, that was more beautiful than the chair and the cords and the cold.

I have never found out. There is no one left to ask. But I think I know the shape of it.

I think he told her she was free.

I think he told her that the keeping was a cage, and she had been in it too long, and she deserved to see the sun. I think he told her it was love.

And I think she believed him. Because it was half true. The Cthaeh only ever tells you half. So did Bredon.

And the half that hurts is always the half you do not hear until it is too late.
