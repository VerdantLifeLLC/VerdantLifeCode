# Chapter Two: Admissions

Admissions came three days after my return, and I went to it the way a man goes to a dentist he has paid in advance. There was still some dread in me, the old reflex of a boy who had once stood before the masters with nothing in his pockets but nerve. But there was something else, too. A kind of sly eagerness. For the first time, I wanted to see what they would do.

The line for admissions stretched out of Hollows and down the steps and halfway across the courtyard. It was a cold morning, clear and bright, and the students waiting in line stamped their feet and blew into their hands and complained to one another about the questions they expected to be asked. I saw a few faces I recognized. Fenton, who had once beaten me in a sympathy duel and never let me forget it. Mola, the pretty Medica student with a sharp tongue and sharper eyes. A pair of Ambrose's hangers-on, who saw me and stopped talking and watched me with the flat, measuring look of dogs who remember a kick.

Ambrose himself was not in line. I noticed that. I noticed it the way you notice that a sound you have grown used to has stopped.

When I finally stepped inside the hall, it was just as it always was. The long crescent table, the nine masters seated behind it, the chancellor in the center with his hands folded. The dust in the air. The hush. A single chair in front of the table, set there for the student to stand beside like a man at the gallows.

"Kvothe," said the Chancellor. His name was Herma, and he had been Master Linguist before he took the chair; his voice still carried the precise, slightly amused cadence of a man who had spent a lifetime correcting other people's grammar. "Welcome back. We were not certain we would see you this term."

"Neither was I, Chancellor."

A ripple of something went through the table. Not laughter exactly. The masters were not given to laughter during admissions. But Arwyl smiled, and Elodin, lounging at the far end with his feet up on the table, made a small noise that might have been a snort.

"You have been away for some time," Herma said. "Nearly a year, according to the Bursar's records. We understand you were in Vintas."

"I was, sir. In the service of Maer Alveron."

"So your letter said." Herma lifted a sheet of paper from the table in front of him. I recognized the heavy cream stock and the seal pressed into the wax at the bottom. The Maer had been generous with his letters. "The Maer speaks highly of you. He says you have done him great service, and that you enjoy his continued patronage. He asks that the University extend to you whatever consideration it might see fit."

"That's very kind of him."

"Mm." Herma set the letter down. "It is not the University's practice to take the opinions of nobles into account when setting tuition. You understand that, I hope."

"Of course, sir."

"Good." He folded his hands again. "Then let us begin. Master Brandeur?"

Brandeur, the Master Arithmetician, leaned forward. He was a narrow man with a narrow mouth and an unpleasant habit of asking questions to which there was no answer. He asked me three of them, and I answered each as best I could. Then Mandrag asked me about the rendering of pure stibnium from its ore, and I answered that with rather more confidence. Kilvin asked me about the properties of heat-sympathetic alloys, and I talked for a solid quarter of an hour, and when I was done he nodded his great shaggy head and said, "Good. This is good. Why have you not been in my shop?"

"I've been in Vintas, Master Kilvin."

"Vintas has forges." He frowned at me. "A man should not neglect his craft simply because he is traveling. The hands forget."

"Yes, Master Kilvin."

"You will come to the Fishery. Tomorrow. I have a project that will interest you."

"Yes, Master Kilvin."

Arwyl asked me three questions about the circulatory system, and I got two of them right. Lorren asked me about the Tak-Diawl schism in the early Aturan church, and I told him more than he wanted to know, and his expression did not change at all, which was the best I could hope for from Lorren. Hemme asked me a question about the legal status of sympathists under Modegan law that was deliberately obscure, and I answered it anyway, because I had spent two spans in the Maer's library doing little else but reading legal histories while waiting to be summoned. Hemme's lips went thin. He did not ask a second question.

That left only Elodin.

He had not moved throughout any of it. He sat with his feet on the table and his hands folded over his stomach and his eyes half closed, like a cat in a sunbeam. When Herma looked at him, he did not seem to notice.

"Master Elodin," said the Chancellor, with the patience of long practice. "Do you have a question for the student?"

Elodin opened his eyes. He looked at me.

It is difficult to explain what it felt like, being looked at by Elodin. Most people look at you. Elodin looked *into* you, the way you might look into a well to see how deep it goes. And he did not always seem to like what he saw at the bottom.

"Yes," he said. "I have a question."

He swung his feet down from the table and leaned forward, his elbows on his knees. The rest of the masters shifted slightly. Elodin was not supposed to ask questions at admissions, not really. Everyone knew he gave the same tuition to every student: he took whatever sum the others had arrived at and added or subtracted a random amount, apparently based on nothing more than his mood. But he had not asked a question in a very long time. I knew that. Everybody knew that.

"Where," Elodin said, "did you get the scar?"

I blinked. "Sir?"

"On your cheek. Where did you get it?"

I touched the scar. "In Ademre, sir. From a sword."

"Whose sword?"

"A woman named Vashet. She was my teacher."

"Your teacher cut you."

"Yes, sir."

"Why?"

I thought about that. The honest answer was that she had cut me because I had been careless, and because she had wanted me to remember that carelessness has a cost. But that was not the whole of it. There was a deeper answer, one I had only begun to understand in the long months since.

"Because I was learning the wrong thing," I said slowly. "I thought I was learning how to fight. She wanted me to learn how to listen. The cut was a way of saying so."

Elodin looked at me for a long moment. Then he smiled. It was not a pleasant smile. It was the smile of a man who has seen someone step on a rake and is waiting, with interest, to see how long it will take them to notice.

"Good," he said. "That's a very good answer." He leaned back in his chair and put his feet up on the table again. "I have no further questions."

Herma looked at him with an expression of carefully controlled exasperation. Then he turned back to me.

"Thank you, Kvothe," he said. "Please wait outside while we deliberate."

I went outside. I did not have to wait long.

When I came back, Herma held up a slip of paper with a number written on it. "Two talents, three jots," he said.

I stood very still. It was the lowest tuition I had ever been given. It was, if I was not mistaken, less than half of what I had paid the term before I left.

"Thank you, sir," I said.

"Don't thank me. Thank Master Elodin." Herma's voice was dry. "He subtracted rather a great deal."

I looked at Elodin. He was examining the ceiling as if it held the secrets of the universe. He did not look at me. But the corner of his mouth was twitching.

---

I paid my tuition at the Bursar's office with coins from the Maer's purse. The clerk counted them twice and wrote me out a receipt, and I folded it up and put it in my pocket and walked out into the bright cold morning feeling like a king.

I went to find Elodin.

He was not in his office. He was not in the Rookery. He was not in any of the lecture halls. It took me the better part of two hours to find him, and when I did, he was sitting on the roof of the Mains, his legs dangling over the edge, eating an apple.

"You found me," he said, without turning around. "Congratulations. Most students give up after an hour."

"I'm not most students."

"That," he said, "is either the truest or the most arrogant thing you've ever said. Possibly both." He took a bite of apple. "Sit down. You're making the roof nervous."

I sat down beside him, carefully. The roof of the Mains was a steep slope of old slate, and the edge was a long way above the cobbles of the courtyard below. Elodin did not seem to notice. He swung his legs back and forth like a child on a wall.

"Thank you," I said. "For the tuition."

"It wasn't a gift. It was a bribe." He tossed the apple core out into the air. We both watched it fall. It struck the cobbles below with a small wet sound. "I want you back in my class. And I want you to actually pay attention this time."

"I always pay attention."

"You always *listen*. It's not the same thing." He turned to look at me for the first time. His eyes were very dark. "You went away, and you came back different. I can see it. You carry yourself differently. You breathe differently. Someone has been teaching you how to be still."

"The Adem."

"The Adem." He said it slowly, tasting the word. "Interesting. I knew an Adem once. She could split a candle flame with her little finger and never burn herself. She told me it was because she knew the name of the flame, and I told her that was nonsense, and she hit me so hard I couldn't hear out of my left ear for a span." He smiled faintly. "She was right, of course. I was just too young to know it."

"What happened to her?"

"She went home. They always go home." He looked away again, out over the rooftops. "Tell me. Did your Vashet teach you the name of the wind?"

"No," I said. "But she taught me something about it. She said the wind was like the Lethani. You can't hold it. You can only move with it."

Elodin was quiet for a long time. The wind stirred his hair. Far below us, students hurried across the courtyard, small as ants.

"The Lethani," he said at last. "The Adem are very wise about some things. They are very foolish about others. But on that point, I think they are right." He turned to me again. "You called the wind once. Do you remember?"

"Of course I remember."

"Do you remember how it felt?"

I thought about that day. About Ambrose, and the cold, and the sudden terrible clarity that had come over me like a sheet of ice. About the name that had risen out of me without my calling it, and the wind that had come howling at its call.

"It felt," I said slowly, "like being very awake. Like I had been asleep my whole life, and suddenly I woke up."

Elodin nodded. "Yes. That's how it feels. That's how it always feels, the first time." He reached into his coat and pulled out another apple and bit into it. "And then you fall back asleep. And you spend the rest of your life trying to wake up again."

"Is that what happened to you?"

He did not answer. He chewed his apple and looked out over the rooftops and did not say anything at all, for so long that I began to think he had forgotten I was there.

Then he said, "I want you to learn the name of stone."

I stared at him. "Stone?"

"Stone." He gestured at the roof beneath us, at the great grey walls of the Mains, at the Archives rising in the distance. "It's all around you. It's the bones of the world. It's patient and it's old and it's very, very stubborn. It is not like wind. Wind wants to be called. Stone wants to be left alone." He smiled that unpleasant smile again. "You'll hate it. That's why you need to learn it."

"Why?"

"Because," he said, "something is coming, and when it comes, you're going to need to know how to be patient." He stood up, all at once, balancing on the steep slate as if it were a level floor. "Class is on Cendling. Don't be late. Or do. I don't really care." And he walked away along the ridge of the roof with his hands in his pockets, whistling, and did not look back.

I sat there for a long time after he had gone, looking at the stone beneath my hands.

It did not say anything to me. But then, stone rarely does.

---

On my way back across the courtyard, I heard the news about Ambrose.

It came to me the way news always comes at the University, which is to say sideways. I was passing a knot of students near the Rookery, and one of them was saying, "—second one in a year. That's what I heard, anyway. Fell off his horse and broke his neck."

"Horses," said another, with the wisdom of the very young. "You can't trust them. My uncle had a horse that—"

"Not the point. The point is, that's two of them dead. Two of the ones ahead of him."

"Ahead of who?"

"Ambrose, you idiot. Who do you think?"

I stopped walking.

The students did not notice me. They were too wrapped up in their gossip. I stood a little distance off, pretending to look for something in my satchel, and I listened.

It went like this. Ambrose Jakis was the son of Baron Jakis, one of the most powerful noblemen in Vintas. Everyone knew that. What everyone also knew, because Ambrose had never let anyone forget it, was that he stood somewhere in the line of succession for the throne of Vintas itself. Not close to the front. Not even particularly close to the middle. Somewhere around thirteenth, the last I'd heard, which was near enough to brag about and far enough that nobody took the bragging seriously.

But now, it seemed, two of the men who stood between Ambrose and the throne were dead.

The first had died a year ago, in the spring. A cousin of the king's, a young lord with a weak chest who had caught a fever in the damp season and never recovered. Nobody had thought much of it at the time. Fevers were common, and weak chests were weak.

The second had died a month ago. A distant uncle of the king's, an older man with a fondness for hunting. He had been riding in the hills near Renere when his horse had stumbled on a slope. He had been thrown. His neck had broken.

"Two deaths in a year," said one of the students. "It happens. It's not like anyone's killing them."

"I didn't say anyone was killing them. I just said it's interesting, that's all. Ambrose left last span. Went back to Vintas. His father sent for him."

"Maybe his father's dying too."

"Maybe. Or maybe his father wants him close by. Just in case."

They laughed at that, the easy laughter of people for whom the deaths of nobles are a kind of theater, something to be discussed and speculated upon and then forgotten. I did not laugh. I stood there with my hand in my satchel, and I felt something cold settle into the pit of my stomach.

Two deaths in a year. It happens.

But I had been in Vintas. I had spent months at the Maer's court. I had seen the way the nobles there played their games of power, the smiling knives and the poisoned cups. I had caught the Maer's own physician slowly killing him for gold. I knew, better than any student lounging in the courtyard, that in Vintas nothing simply happened.

I did not think Ambrose was killing anyone. He did not have the patience for it, nor the subtlety. Ambrose was a man who liked to break things with his own hands and watch them bleed.

But someone might be killing them on his behalf.

---

That night I lay awake in my narrow bed under the eaves, listening to the wind move across the rooftops, and I thought about many things.

I thought about Ambrose, and the two dead men, and the long cold line of the Vintish succession stretching away like a road into darkness.

I thought about Elodin, and the name of stone, and the strange grim look on his face when he said *something is coming*.

I thought about Auri, and the doors that were sleeping but not sleepy.

And I thought about Denna, because I always thought about Denna, the way a tongue always goes back to a sore tooth.

It was very late when I finally fell asleep. And when I did, I dreamed of a door. It was made of grey stone, and it had no handle, and no hinges, and no lock. It stood alone in a great dark space, and it was very old. Older than the University. Older than the stone bridge. Older, perhaps, than the world.

In my dream I walked up to the door and laid my hand against it. It was cold. Colder than stone ought to be.

And from the other side, very faintly, I heard something breathing.
