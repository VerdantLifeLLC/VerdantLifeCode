# What Lorren Knew

The note came that night, under my door at Anker's. A single folded sheet of heavy paper, sealed with plain grey wax, with no mark pressed into it.

Inside, in a narrow, precise hand I recognized at once:

*The east door of the Archives. Midnight. Come alone. Burn this.*

It was not signed. It did not need to be.

I burned it in the candle flame and watched the ashes curl. Then I sat on my bed and waited for midnight, and did not think about anything, because there was nothing I could think about that did not hurt.

---

The east door of the Archives is a small door. Most students do not know it exists. It is set deep in the grey windowless wall on the side facing the Mains, behind a buttress, in a place where the shadows are always thick. It is used by the scrivs, sometimes, when they are carrying crates of books in and out, and by the masters, when they want to come and go without being seen.

It was open when I got there. Only a crack. A thin line of red light from a sympathy lamp, lying across the flagstones.

I pushed it open and went in.

Lorren was standing in the corridor beyond, very tall and very still, in his long black master's robe, with the red lamp in his hand. His face, lit from below, was all hollows and planes, like a carving.

"Re'lar Kvothe," he said. Then he paused. "No. Not Re'lar. Not anymore." He looked at me for a long moment with his colorless eyes. "Kvothe."

"Master Lorren."

"Come with me."

---

He led me through the Archives. Through corridors I knew and corridors I did not. Down a narrow stair I had never seen, into a part of the Stacks so old that the shelves were stone, not wood, and the books on them were bound in leather gone black with age. Our footsteps were very loud. The red light of his lamp moved over the spines of the books like blood.

He did not speak. I did not ask. I had learned, over the years, that Lorren did not answer questions he had not decided to answer, and that asking only made him decide not to.

At last he stopped in front of a door. A small iron door, set into the stone at the end of a dead-end passage. There was no handle. There was no keyhole. There was only a small plate of dark metal in the center of it, about the size of a palm.

Lorren set his hand flat against the plate. He said something. A single word, very soft, in a language I did not know.

The door opened.

---

It was a small room. A cell, almost. Stone walls and a stone floor and a stone ceiling, very low. A single table and a single chair. A single shelf, holding a single row of books.

And on the table, laid out neatly side by side, the six ledgers Wil and I had brought him two months before. With the six lines of small, neat, slanting script, each one signed *—B.*

"Sit," said Lorren.

I sat in the chair. He did not. He stood by the door with his lamp, tall and dark, and looked at me.

"You are angry with me," he said.

It was not a question. I did not answer.

"You are angry because I voted for your expulsion," said Lorren. "You think I did it because of the Cellarage. Because you went where you were forbidden to go, and broke the rules of the Archives, and embarrassed me before the other masters." He was quiet for a moment. "Or you think I did it because I have always disliked you. Because you were careless with books, once, when you were very young, and I have never forgiven you."

"Didn't you?"

"No," said Lorren. "I did not."

---

He set the lamp down on the table, beside the ledgers. The red light fell on the six neat lines of script.

"Beredon," he said. "Twenty-two years ago. A scriv at my desk. Kind. Well-spoken. Very careful with the books." He touched the nearest ledger with one long pale finger. "When you brought me these, I went back through the records. All of them. Every book he touched, in the year he was here. Every shelf he reorganized. Every stack he catalogued." He was silent for a moment. "It took me six spans. I did not sleep much."

"What did you find?"

"Holes," said Lorren.

I looked at him.

"Not missing books," he said. "Those I had already found. Eleven of them, as you know. Twelve, now. No. Holes in the *catalogue*. Places where the record itself had been changed. Very subtly. Very carefully. So that a book that should have been shelved in one place was listed in another. So that a cross-reference that should have led from one volume to the next led instead to nothing." He looked at me. "Do you understand what that means?"

I thought about it.

"He wasn't just removing books," I said slowly. "He was hiding them. From the catalogue. So that even if they were still here, nobody could find them."

"Yes."

"Which books?"

Lorren did not answer at once. He turned and looked at the single shelf on the wall, with its single row of old black-bound books.

"These," he said.

---

There were nine of them. I counted. Nine books, bound in black leather, with no titles on their spines. Lorren took one down and set it on the table in front of me and opened it.

I could not read it. It was written in a script I had never seen. Not Tema, not Eld Vintic, not Yllish knot-script. Something older. Something angular and strange, with marks above and below the lines like the marks in music.

"The Amyr cipher," said Lorren quietly. "Their records. What is left of them. Beredon hid them in the catalogue, so deep that no one would ever find them. Not by accident. Not by searching." He touched the page. "I found them because I went looking for the holes. Six spans of looking. And at the bottom of the deepest hole, in a room I did not know existed, in the oldest part of the Stacks, there they were."

"Why hide them? If he's—" I stopped. I did not know how to finish the sentence. I did not know, then, what Bredon was. I only knew that the name Beredon and the name Bredon were very close, and that the old man in the Maer's garden walked with a stick of dark wood with a silver head.

"I do not know," said Lorren. "I have wondered. Perhaps he hid them to keep them safe. Perhaps he hid them to keep them from someone. Perhaps he hid them to keep them for himself, so that he could come back one day and find them where he left them." He closed the book. "Perhaps all three."

---

"Master Lorren," I said. "Why are you showing me this?"

He did not answer for a long time. He stood by the table with his hand resting on the closed black book, and he looked at me with his colorless eyes.

"Because he came back," he said at last.

My skin went cold.

"Last spring," said Lorren. "While you were in Vintas. He came to the Archives. Not as a scriv. As a visitor. A gentleman scholar from the south, with a letter of introduction from a lord in Renere. He asked for a reading pass. He was very polite. Very pleasant. He had a stick of dark wood with a silver head." Lorren's voice was very flat. "I did not see him. I was away, in Anilin, at a conference. The scriv at the desk gave him a pass. He spent three days in the Tomes. He read nothing of consequence. Histories. Genealogies. Books about the noble houses of Vintas." He paused. "And then he asked about a student."

I did not have to ask which one.

"He asked if you were enrolled," said Lorren. "He asked what you were studying. He asked who your friends were, and where you lodged, and whether you had ever been disciplined for entering restricted parts of the Archives." He looked at me. "The scriv told him. The scriv did not know any better. He was a gentleman with a letter from Renere, and he was very pleasant."

---

The red lamp flickered. The shadows on the stone walls leaned and steadied.

"When you came back," said Lorren, "I watched you. I watched you go into the Underthing, night after night. I watched you come back with dust on your clothes and stone in your pockets. I knew where you were going. I knew there was something down there, under the Archives. Something old." He was quiet for a moment. "And I knew that a man with a silver-headed stick had come here, a year before, asking whether you had ever been disciplined for entering restricted places."

"You think he wanted me to go down there."

"I think," said Lorren carefully, "that he wanted to know if you *would*. And now he knows. And if he knows, then you are in more danger here, at the University, sitting on top of whatever is down there, than anywhere else in the world."

I stared at him.

"You voted to expel me," I said slowly. "To get me away from here."

Lorren did not answer. His face showed nothing at all.

---

"I could not tell the others," he said at last. "Kilvin would have wanted to protect you here, in the Fishery, behind his wards. Elodin would have wanted to see what you would do. Herma would have wanted a committee." He looked at the black books on the shelf. "And any of them might have spoken of it to someone. And someone might have spoken of it to a pleasant gentleman with a letter from Renere." He paused. "It was simpler to vote. And to let you believe I despised you. And to let you go."

"You could have told *me*."

"Could I?" Lorren looked at me for a long moment. "If I had told you that a man who might be one of the Amyr—or something worse, something that has been hunting the Amyr for a very long time—had come to the University asking about you, what would you have done?"

I opened my mouth. Then I closed it.

"You would have gone looking for him," said Lorren quietly. "Of course you would. You have been looking for the Chandrian since you were twelve years old. You would have gone looking for him, and you would have found him, because he wants to be found by you. And then he would have had you. In whatever way he wants you." He shook his head. "No. It was better that you leave. Angry. Believing the University had cast you out. Believing I hated you. Going somewhere, anywhere, that was not here."

---

I did not say anything for a long time. I sat in the stone chair in the stone room with the nine black books on the shelf above me and the six ledgers on the table in front of me, and I did not know what to feel.

"Why tell me now?" I said at last. "If it was better that I leave angry?"

Lorren looked at me. And something happened to his face. Something I had never seen on it before. It was very small, and it was gone almost at once. But for a moment, just a moment, his long cold pale face looked tired. And old. And sad.

"Because I was a student once," he said. "And I was curious about the Amyr. And I went looking. And no one told me anything. No one warned me. No one said *be careful, there are things in the dark that are looking back*." He was quiet for a moment. "I was lucky. I found nothing. And I stopped looking, and became a master, and kept the books. But I have always wished that someone had told me."

He picked up the lamp.

"So I am telling you," he said. "Once. I will not repeat it. And if you ever tell anyone that I said it—"

"You'll deny it," I said. "And bar me from the Archives for the rest of my life."

The corner of his mouth moved. It was not a smile. Lorren did not smile. But it was close.

"You are already barred," he said. "I will have to think of something worse."

---

He led me back through the Stacks. Up the narrow stair, through the corridors I knew and the corridors I did not, to the small east door behind the buttress. He opened it. The night air came in, cold and clean.

"Your sword," he said, as I stepped out. "I confiscated it, at the hearing. It is the rule. Expelled students forfeit their weapons." He paused. "I will give it to Kilvin. He will see that it reaches you. I would prefer that you not know how."

"Thank you, Master Lorren."

"Do not thank me." His face was a pale shape in the red light, above the black of his robe. "Go far away. Stay away from old men with silver-headed sticks. And if you find the Amyr—" He stopped.

"Yes?"

Lorren was quiet for a long moment.

"If you find them," he said at last, "do not assume they are what the stories say. The stories were written by people who wanted to be remembered well. The Amyr were very good at that." He looked past me, into the dark. "Perhaps too good."

He closed the door. I heard the lock turn. And I stood alone in the cold beside the grey windowless wall of the Archives, and looked up at the stars, and felt, for the first time since the hearing, something that was not anger.

I did not know what it was. It felt a little like gratitude. It felt a little like fear.

It felt, I think, like being warned.

---

I did not listen, of course. I went south, three days later, to Severen. To the Maer's small garden, and a tak board, and an old man with a silver-headed stick who smiled at me across the stones as if I were his favorite grandson.

I have thought, since, about how differently things might have gone, if I had listened.

I have thought that Lorren must have thought about it too. Sitting in his long narrow office, with his long pale hands folded on his desk. Reading the news from Vintas, as it came up the roads. The murder of a king. A trial. A hanging in Renere.

I hope he did not blame himself. It was not his fault. He did everything he could.

He told me once. He did not repeat it.

It was enough. I just did not listen.
