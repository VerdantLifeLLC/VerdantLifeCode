# Interlude: The Question

"Can I ask you something?" said Chronicler. "About Denna?"

The innkeeper had stopped speaking at the edge of the river, with the grey dawn coming up behind him and a letter in knots in his pocket. He had been sitting quietly for some time, turning his cup in his hands. He looked up.

"You can ask," he said. "I may not answer."

Chronicler hesitated. He glanced at Bast, who was sitting in his backward chair with his chin on his folded arms, watching them both with bright, wary eyes.

"She betrayed you," said Chronicler. "Didn't she? Over and over. She worked for Bredon. She tied the knots in the old king's hair. She lured you to Renere, and then to the garden, and then to the door. Everything he wanted from you, he got through her." He paused. "And you keep forgiving her. Every time. You keep running back."

The innkeeper did not say anything. His face did not change.

"I'm not judging," said Chronicler quickly. "I'm only trying to understand. For the book. People will read this, and they'll ask. They'll say you were a fool."

"I was a fool," said the innkeeper mildly. "That's not in question."

"Then why?"

---

The innkeeper was quiet for a long time. He set down his cup. He folded his hands on the table. He looked at the fire.

"Have you ever been in love, scribe?" he said.

Chronicler opened his mouth. Then he closed it.

"Once," he said at last. "A long time ago."

"What was her name?"

"Tessa." Chronicler looked down at his pen. "She was a copyist, in Tinuë. In the same counting-house as my father. She had the most beautiful hand I ever saw. Every letter like a little bird." He was quiet for a moment. "She married a wine merchant."

"Did she betray you?"

"No. She just—" Chronicler shrugged. "Chose someone else."

"And do you hate her for it?"

Chronicler thought about it. It was a long time before he answered.

"No," he said. "I think about her sometimes. When I see a good hand. I hope she's happy."

The innkeeper nodded slowly.

"That's the difference," he said. "Between you and most people. And between me and most people. Most people, when they're hurt, want to hate. It's easier. It gives the hurt somewhere to go." He looked at the fire. "I tried to hate her. I want you to know that. After Renere. After the garden. After I found out about the knots in the old king's hair. I sat in my rooms at the Seven Swans and I tried as hard as I have ever tried to do anything. I tried to hate Denna."

"And?"

"And I couldn't find anything to hate." He almost smiled. "I kept looking. I kept turning it over, the way you turn a stone over looking for the crack. And every time, underneath, I found the same thing. A girl alone. A girl with no family and no name and no one in the world who wanted her for herself. A girl who'd been used by every man who ever looked at her, from the time she was fourteen. And one man, finally, who didn't seem to want anything from her at all. Who was kind. Who taught her to tie beautiful things. Who told her she was clever and gifted and important." He was quiet. "Who gave her a *purpose*."

"Bredon."

"Bredon." The innkeeper looked at his hands. "He found her the way he found everyone. He found the hole in her, the empty place where she wanted something, and he filled it. With kindness. With lessons. With a story about a hero named Lanre who was wronged by the world." He shook his head slowly. "And she believed him. Of course she did. It was the first time anyone had ever given her a story to believe in."

---

"But she knew," said Chronicler. "Didn't she? At some point. She must have known what he was."

"Yes." The innkeeper's voice was very quiet. "At some point. Not at first. But at some point." He was silent for a long moment. "Do you know what she did, when she found out?"

Chronicler shook his head.

"She didn't run," said the innkeeper. "She didn't tell anyone. She didn't come to me and weep and ask for help." He looked up. "She stayed. She kept working for him. She kept tying his knots. And she learned. Everything he knew. Every knot, every binding, every trick of tying a name into a cord. She let him teach her, knowing what he was, knowing what he wanted, so that she would know how to undo it." He was quiet. "She was playing his game. His beautiful game. Against him. For years. Alone."

The fire crackled. Nobody spoke.

"And when the time came," said the innkeeper, "when the door opened, and my name was hanging in the air, and the Thief was pulling at it, and I couldn't hold it—she was there. With a cord of her own hair in her sleeve. Ready." He looked at Chronicler. "She'd been ready for a year. She told me that, later. She'd been carrying that cord in her sleeve for a year, waiting for the moment. Because she knew. She'd seen the shape of his game. She knew it would come to that. Me, at the door, with my name in the air. And she wanted to be the one who caught it."

"Why?"

"Because she'd helped put me there." The innkeeper's voice did not change. "And she knew it. And she couldn't undo it. So she did the next best thing. She made sure that when I fell, someone would catch me."

---

Chronicler did not say anything for a long time. He looked down at his page. At the lines he had written, in his careful hand, about a girl with dark hair walking away into the dark by a river, and a letter in knots.

"You make her sound like a hero," he said at last.

"She was." The innkeeper looked at him. "She was the bravest person I ever knew. Braver than me. Braver than Tempi or Vashet or any of the Adem. I fought monsters with a sword, and a song, and all my friends around me. She fought the most dangerous man in the world with a piece of string. Alone. For years. And she never told anyone. And she never asked for thanks." He was quiet for a moment. "And when it was over, she gave me back. That's the part that matters most. She could have kept my name. She could have held it forever, the way Bredon held hers. And she gave it back."

"Held hers?"

The innkeeper looked at him. And something passed across his face, very quickly. Something old and sad and angry.

"That's later," he said. "That's in the story. You'll see."

---

Bast had not said anything all this time. But now he stirred in his chair, and lifted his chin from his arms, and looked at the innkeeper with an expression Chronicler could not read.

"You're leaving something out, Reshi," he said.

The innkeeper looked at him.

"The scribe asked why you keep forgiving her," said Bast. "And you've told him why she's brave. And why she's good. And why she did what she did." He tilted his head. "But that's not what he asked. He asked why *you* forgive her. And that's not the same thing."

The innkeeper did not answer for a long time.

"No," he said at last. "It isn't."

"So?"

The innkeeper looked at the fire. When he spoke, his voice was very quiet. So quiet Chronicler had to lean forward to hear it.

"Because I did the same thing," he said. "To everyone I loved. Over and over. I lied to Sim and Wil. I used Devi's help and never paid her back. I took Auri's trust and walked into the Cellarage and nearly got her killed. I let Fela hold up a burning building so I could sing a song. I let Stanchion die. I let the Maer use me, and I used him back, and a city burned." He looked down at his hands. "I did it all for good reasons. Or I thought they were good reasons. I did it to find the Chandrian. To close the door. To save the world." He was quiet. "Denna did the same thing. For the same reasons. And if I can't forgive her—"

He stopped.

"Then you can't forgive yourself," said Bast softly.

The innkeeper did not answer. He did not have to.

---

Chronicler sat very still. After a while, he picked up his pen. He did not write anything. He only held it.

"I'll put it in," he said. "What you said. If you want."

"I want." The innkeeper picked up his cup again. "Put it all in. The foolishness and the forgiving. People will say I was a fool. And they'll be right." He drank. "But maybe someone will read it, someday, who's done something terrible for a good reason. And they'll think: *if he could forgive her, maybe someone could forgive me.*"

He set the cup down.

"That's worth being a fool for," he said. "Don't you think?"

Chronicler looked at him for a long moment.

"Yes," he said. "I think it is."

He dipped his pen, and wrote.
