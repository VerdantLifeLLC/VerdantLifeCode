# The Book of Singers

I did not leave Myr Tariniel the morning after Selitos told me the price. I have told it as if I did, because that is the shape the story wants. But the truth is that we stayed three more days in the round white tower at the center of the dead city, and I spent most of them reading.

Selitos did not object. He seemed, if anything, pleased. He moved about the tower in his bare feet, making tea and feeding the fire and sitting for long hours by the narrow window with his one good eye closed and his stone eye open, looking at things I could not see. And every so often he would come and stand beside the table where I sat, among the books, and look down at what I was reading, and say nothing, and go away again.

Bast did not read. Bast went out into the ruins and wandered among them, and came back each evening with his face very still and his eyes very bright, and did not say where he had been or what he had seen. Selitos looked at him, once, on the second evening, for a long time. And then said, very quietly, "Yes. They are still there. Some of them. The ones who would not leave." And Bast nodded, and did not ask anything else, and neither did I.

---

The books in the tower were not like the books in the Archives. They were not like the Countess Laverne's books, either, or the Maer's. Most of them were not books at all, in the way I understood the word. Some were scrolls. Some were tablets of clay, or wax, or thin sheets of beaten silver. Some were cords of knots. Some were things I did not have names for: stones with patterns in them that shifted when you looked at them, and small boxes that hummed when you held them close to your ear.

Selitos had gathered them over ten thousand years. From every corner of a world that had changed its shape a dozen times since he began. He had read them all. More than once. Some of them, he said, he had read so many times that the words had worn off the page, and he read them now from memory.

"Looking for the other way," he said, when I asked him why. "The way to end the curse without opening the door." He smiled faintly. "It's in here somewhere. I'm sure of it. I simply haven't found it yet."

"Ten thousand years."

"Ten thousand years." He shrugged. "I'm very slow. And very stubborn. Like stone."

---

On the second day, he brought me a book I had not asked for.

It was very large and very plain, bound in grey leather that had gone soft and pale with age. There was no title on the cover. He set it down on the table in front of me and opened it, and stood back.

It was a list. Names, written in a hundred different hands, in a hundred different inks, in languages I knew and languages I did not. Page after page after page of them. Beside each name, a date. Or what I thought was a date, in some reckoning I did not recognize. And beside the date, a place.

"The Singers," said Selitos quietly. "All of them. Everyone who has ever carried the true song, since the night I dug a boy out of a fountain and asked him what he wanted to be."

I looked down at the list. At the thousands of names.

"And the dates?"

"When they died." Selitos's voice did not change. "And where. And how, when I know it."

I turned the pages. Slowly. The names went on and on. Some of them had notes beside them, in Selitos's small precise hand. *Burned. Frozen. Taken by rot. Found in a well. Found in a field with frost on her eyelashes. Found.*

There were so many. Thousands. Every one of them someone who had learned the true story of Lanre and sung it, somewhere, to someone. Every one of them hunted and found and killed.

"You write them all down," I said.

"Every one." Selitos looked out the narrow window at the ruins. "It is the least I can do. I made the Singers. I sent them out into the world with a song that I knew would get them killed. I told myself it was necessary. For the greater good." His voice was very quiet. "The Amyr say that. *For the greater good.* I taught them to say it." He was silent for a moment. "The least I can do is remember."

---

I found my parents on the last page.

I had not known they would be there. I had not known anyone kept such a list. But I turned the last page of the grey book, and there, near the bottom, in Selitos's small precise hand, I found them.

*Arliden. Of the Edema Ruh. A maker of songs.*

*Laurian. Of the Edema Ruh. Born Netalia, of the line. A singer.*

And beside them, a date. And a place. A forest near the edge of the Commonwealth. And a single word.

*Burned.*

And below them, other names. Eleven of them. Trip, and Shandi, and Teren. Marie, who played the fiddle. Old Hetch. Kari, who taught me to whistle through my fingers, and whose sister sat across from me at the Maer's table. All of them. Every person in my father's troupe. Every one.

*Burned. Burned. Burned.*

I sat very still at the table in the round white tower, with the grey book open in front of me, and I did not move for a long time.

---

Selitos came and stood beside me. He looked down at the page. He did not say anything.

"You knew," I said at last. My voice sounded very far away. "When I came. You knew who I was."

"I knew." He was quiet for a moment. "I heard your father's song, the night they died. Very faint. Very far. The last true song sung before yours." He looked at the page. "I wrote their names that same night. And I sat by this window until dawn, and I wept, as I weep for all of them."

"There's a space," I said. "Below them. After Kari. Before the bottom of the page."

"Yes."

"Is that for me?"

Selitos looked at me for a long moment with his one good eye.

"It was," he said. "For a long time. I kept it there. I thought you would be the next. The boy who survived the burning, who would grow up and sing the true song and be found." He was quiet. "I have left spaces for many people. Most of them, I have filled."

"But not mine."

"Not yet." He reached down and turned the page. The next was blank. "You sang the whole song, and you lived. You sang it in front of the Seven and the dark one himself, and you lived." His one good eye was very steady. "No Singer has ever done that. Not in ten thousand years."

"They can't kill me. Because I'm the key."

"Yes. That is why." Selitos closed the book. "But it is not the only reason. You live because you choose to keep living, in spite of everything. That is rarer than you think." He laid his long hand on the grey cover. "When you give up your name, at the door, you will not be a Singer anymore. You will not be anything. And I will not have to fill your space."

"Is that a comfort?"

Selitos looked at me.

"No," he said. "But it is true."

---

I asked him for a pen, that evening. He gave me one, without asking why. And ink, very old, in a small stone pot.

I opened the grey book to the last page. To my parents, and the eleven names below them, and the space.

And I wrote in the space. Not my own name. Another one.

*Stanchion. Of Imre. Who could not play, and built a room for those who could.*

*Burned. But his walls stood.*

Selitos read it over my shoulder. He was silent for a long time.

"He was not a Singer," he said at last.

"He let one sing," I said. "In his house. Knowing what would come. He stood on the stair between the rot and a girl holding up his walls, and he told a Chandrian she couldn't have them." I set down the pen. "That's a kind of singing."

Selitos looked at me for a long moment. Then he nodded, slowly.

"Yes," he said. "Yes. I suppose it is."

He took the pen from my hand. And below Stanchion's name, in his small precise hand, he wrote another.

*Tempi. Of the Adem. Who taught the singer to be still.*

I stared at him.

"He has not died yet," said Selitos quietly. "Not by your reckoning. But I see names, singer. And his is very bright, and very close to its end." He closed the book. "I thought you should know. So that when it comes, you will not be surprised."

---

I did not believe him. Or I did not let myself. I went down out of the Stormwal and through the twilight with Bast and came to the Maer's camp in the hills, and Tempi was there, and I took his hands in mine, and he said *you are my student, you are in trouble, where else would I be*. And I did not think of the grey book at all.

And then he died, in the burning camp by the river, with Cinder's pale sword in him, making the sign for *go*.

And I remembered. Standing on the hill above the Maer's valley, beside his cairn, with Vashet moving my hands into the shape of grief. I remembered Selitos's small precise hand, writing his name in a book of the dead before he was dead.

I have never decided whether that was cruel or kind.

I think, like most things Selitos did, it was both. I think he had been alive so long that he could no longer tell the difference.

I think, if I live long enough, I will be the same.
