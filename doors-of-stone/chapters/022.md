# Three Days

We made them count.

I have thought about those three days many times since. I have taken them out and turned them over in my mind the way you might turn over a smooth stone found on a beach, feeling its weight, its shape, the way the light catches it. They were among the saddest days of my life. They were among the happiest. I did not know, then, that a thing could be both. I know it now.

---

The first day, I went to see Devi.

I climbed the treacherous wooden stairs to her door and knocked, and she opened it before my knuckles had finished their second rap. She looked terrible. Her eyes were red and swollen, and her face was blotchy, and her strawberry-blond hair hung loose and uncombed around her shoulders. She looked at me for a long moment without saying anything. Then she stepped aside and let me in.

The room was a wreck. Books lay scattered across the floor. Papers were strewn everywhere. The alchemical bench in the corner had been swept clean, its glassware smashed and scattered. The cat was hiding under the bed, and it hissed at me as I came in.

"I heard," Devi said. Her voice was hoarse. "About the hearing."

"I thought you might have."

"You didn't tell them. About me. About Fela."

"No."

"Why not?"

I looked at her. "Because it wouldn't have changed anything. They'd still have expelled me. And they'd have done worse to you."

"They couldn't do worse to me. I'm already expelled." She laughed, a harsh, ugly sound. "They'd have done worse to Fela, though. She'd have lost everything." She sat down heavily in her chair. "She came to see me, you know. Yesterday. She wanted to go to Herma and confess. I talked her out of it."

"Thank you."

"Don't thank me." She put her face in her hands. "It's my fault. All of it. If I hadn't taken that page—"

"Devi."

"If I hadn't taken it, the seal wouldn't have rung. We'd have gotten out. Nobody would have known. You'd still be—" Her voice broke. "You'd still be here."

I sat down on the edge of her bed. The cat hissed at me again from beneath it. I ignored it.

"Maybe," I said. "Or maybe not. Maybe it would have rung anyway. Maybe it would have heard me. It knew I was there, Devi. It spoke to me." I shook my head. "I don't think we were ever going to get out of there clean."

She looked up at me through her fingers. "It spoke to you?"

"Yes."

"What did it say?"

"My name."

She stared at me for a long time. Then she took her hands away from her face and wiped her eyes with the back of her wrist and sat up straight.

"All right," she said. "All right." She took a deep breath. "Then I'm going to give you something. And you're going to take it. And you're not going to argue."

She went to her desk. She pulled open a drawer, and then another drawer beneath it, and then pressed on something at the back of the second drawer, and a small panel slid open with a click. From behind the panel she took a folded piece of paper.

It was the page. The one she had taken from the Book of Watches. I knew it the moment I saw it: the same pale, faintly translucent material as the book's cover, the same old Aturan script, faded brown with age.

"I read it," she said. "The night we got back. I sat up all night reading it." She held it out to me. "I think it's yours."

I took it. It was lighter than it looked, and cold to the touch.

"What does it say?"

"Read it yourself." She sat back down. "I'll tell you this much. It's not a record. Not like the rest of the book. It's a warning. Someone wrote it at the very end, after the last entry. After the watchers stopped coming." She was quiet for a moment. "I think it was the last thing any of them ever wrote."

I unfolded the page. I read it.

It was short. Only a few lines, in a hand that was hurried and cramped, as if the writer had been afraid of running out of time.

*The watch is ended. We are too few. The Knife has found us.*

*He comes as a friend. He comes as a teacher. He is patient beyond any patience. He has been cutting the truth from the world for longer than the Empire has stood, one page at a time, one name at a time. He serves the Seven, but he is not of them. He is only a man. That is what makes him terrible.*

*He carries a staff of dark wood crowned in silver. He plays at games. He smiles.*

*If any come after us, beware him. He wants the line. He wants the key. He wants the door opened, and he will wait a thousand years to see it done.*

*The door holds. God help whoever holds it after us.*

---

I read it three times. Then I folded it carefully and put it in the inside pocket of my cloak, next to my father's song and Denna's knot.

I thought of Bredon.

I want to be honest about that. I read those words, *a staff of dark wood crowned in silver*, *he plays at games*, *he smiles*, and for a single heartbeat I saw him as clearly as if he were standing in front of me. The steps of the Mains in the snow. Lorren's long pale hands on the scrivs' ledgers. *He always won.*

And then I pushed it away. The way you push away a thought of something shameful. I told myself that it was a coincidence. That the page was very old, and the Amyr who wrote it had been dead for centuries. That there were a thousand old men with sticks. I thought of the Chandrian instead. I thought of Cinder, and Haliax. I thought of anything and everything except a kind old man who had taught me to play tak in the Maer's court.

It is a strange thing, how blind we can be to what is right in front of us. I have thought about it a great deal. I think it is because we do not want to see. Because some part of us knows that if we see, we will have to act. And acting is hard, and seeing is easy to avoid.

I did not want to see. And so I did not.

---

"What will you do?" Devi asked.

"Go to Vintas. To the Maer."

"And then?"

I thought about it. "I don't know. Find out more. About the doors. About the line." I paused. "About the Knife."

"Be careful," said Devi. She looked at me, and her eyes were very serious behind her spectacles. "Kvothe. I mean it. Be careful. Whoever wrote that page, they were afraid. They were Amyr, and they were afraid. That's not something you should take lightly."

"I won't."

"You will," she said. "You always do. That's the trouble with you." She smiled faintly. "But try, at least. For me."

"For you," I said.

She stood up. She came over and stood in front of me, and for a long moment she did not say anything. Then she leaned down and kissed me on the cheek, very quickly, the way you might kiss a brother goodbye at a dock.

"You still owe me," she said. "For the page. I want it back someday."

"Someday," I said.

"I'll hold you to that."

---

The second day, I went to see Fela.

She was in the Fishery, at her bench, working on a piece of stone. She was carving it, very slowly and carefully, with a small steel chisel. When she saw me come in, she put down the chisel and stood up and came over to me, and her face was so full of guilt and grief that I could hardly bear to look at it.

"Kvothe," she said. "I'm so sorry. I'm so, so sorry."

"You don't have to be."

"I do. It was me. I asked the stone. I made it promise. And then when it rang—" Her voice broke. "I should have been stronger. I should have held it."

"You held it longer than anyone could have." I took her hands. They were cold and rough with stone dust. "Fela, listen to me. You did something no one has done in two hundred years. You asked the stone for something, and it listened. That's not failure. That's the opposite of failure."

She looked at me with wet eyes. "Then why do I feel like this?"

"Because you're a good person. And good people always feel guilty, even when they haven't done anything wrong." I squeezed her hands. "Keep studying with Elodin. Learn everything you can about stone. Someday you might need it."

"Why?"

I hesitated. Then I told her. Not everything. But I told her about the line, and the doors, and the key. I told her what I had read in the book.

She listened in silence. When I was done, she was pale as milk.

"Seven doors," she whispered. "Seven doors, all made of stone."

"Yes."

"And you're—" She could not finish.

"Yes."

She looked at me for a long, long time. Then she took her hands out of mine and went back to her bench and picked up the piece of stone she had been carving. She held it out to me.

It was a small thing, about the size of a walnut. She had carved it into the shape of a closed eye. It was smooth and cool and perfectly made, every line precise.

"I was making it for you," she said. "I was going to give it to you for your birthday. I didn't know what it was for. I just knew I had to make it." She pressed it into my hand. "Now I think I know."

"What is it?"

"It's stone," she said simply. "It says no. Keep it with you. When the door calls your name, hold it. Maybe it will help you say no too."

I held the little stone eye in my palm. I could feel it, very faintly. The long slow patient no.

"Thank you, Fela."

She smiled through her tears. "Come back someday," she said. "When it's safe. Come back and tell me you didn't need it."

---

The third day, I spent with Sim and Wil.

We did not do anything special. We ate breakfast at Anker's, the way we always did. We walked down to the river and sat on the bank and threw stones into the water. We went to the Eolian in the afternoon, when it was nearly empty, and I played for them. Just for them. Every song they asked for, and some they did not.

Stanchion came out from behind the bar and listened. Deoch came in from the street and stood by the door. By the time I finished, there were a dozen people in the room, and they applauded when I was done, and Stanchion wiped his eyes with his sleeve and said something gruff about the smoke from the fire.

In the evening, we went back to Anker's and got very drunk.

I do not remember most of that night. I remember Sim reciting poetry, very badly, standing on a table. I remember Wil laughing, really laughing, which I had seen perhaps three times in all the years I had known him. I remember Anker bringing out a bottle of something very old and very strong from behind the bar and pouring it for us without charging. I remember singing. I remember crying, though I do not remember why.

And I remember, very late, sitting on the roof of Anker's with Sim and Wil on either side of me, looking up at the stars.

"I'll write," Sim said. His voice was slurred. "Every span. I promise."

"I know you will."

"And you have to write back. You never write back. You're terrible at writing back."

"I'll write back."

"Promise?"

"I promise."

Wil was quiet for a long time. Then he said, "Kvothe."

"Yes?"

"Whatever you're going to do. Whatever it is you found down there." He was looking at the stars, not at me. "If you need help, you'll ask. Won't you?"

I did not answer right away. I thought about the page in my pocket, and the Knife, and the doors of stone. I thought about how many people had died because of the songs my parents sang.

"I'll ask," I said.

It was a lie. We all knew it. But they let me tell it anyway, because they were my friends, and because sometimes a lie is the only gift you have to give.

---

I left at dawn on the fourth day.

Kilvin met me at the gates. He had my sword. He did not say how he had got it back from Lorren, and I did not ask. He had Kilvin's candle, too, in its wooden box, and he pressed it into my hands.

"You will need this more than I," he said. "Where you are going."

"Master Kilvin, I can't—"

"You can. You will." He looked down at me with his dark shaggy brows drawn together. "And you will write to me. When it burns blue. Every time. You will tell me where and when, and I will keep the records." He paused. "Someday, perhaps, we will understand it together."

I took the candle. I could not speak.

Kilvin nodded, once, as if I had said something. Then he turned and walked back toward the Fishery, his great shoulders hunched against the cold.

I did not see Elodin before I left. I did not see Lorren, or Herma, or any of the other masters. I did not see Auri.

But as I crossed the stone bridge in the grey dawn light, with my lute on my back and my sword at my hip and my whole life packed into a single bag over my shoulder, I stopped at the top of the arch and looked back.

And there, on the roof of the Mains, I saw a small figure standing against the pale sky. A girl, with a cloud of hair floating around her head like a dandelion's ghost. She was holding something up. A candle. Its flame was small and steady and yellow in the dawn.

She did not wave. She simply stood there, holding the candle, and watched me go.

I watched her back until the road turned and the trees closed in and I could not see her anymore.
