# The Wind's Daughter

The ship was called the *Wind's Daughter*, and she was the ugliest vessel in the harbor of Tarbean.

She was a coastal trader, broad in the beam and low in the water, with patched sails and a hull so thickly crusted with barnacles that she seemed to have grown a second skin. Her paint had worn away years ago. Her rigging was a tangle of old rope and older knots. She listed slightly to port, even at anchor, like a drunk leaning against a wall.

Her captain was a woman named Hessa Tern, and she was the second ugliest thing on the *Wind's Daughter*. She was short and broad and weathered, with a face like a clenched fist and a voice like a gull with a grievance. When I came up the gangplank and asked if she needed a hand for the voyage east, she looked at me the way you might look at a fish that has flopped onto your deck uninvited.

"Can you sail?"

"No."

"Can you cook?"

"Badly."

"Can you fight?"

"Yes."

She looked at my sword. Then at my lute. Then at me.

"And what's that for?" she said, jerking her chin at the lute case.

"Music."

"Music." She snorted. "I've got no use for music. Music doesn't haul line or bail bilge or keep the pirates off." She turned away. "Go find another ship."

"I can do something else," I said. "Something useful."

She looked back at me over her shoulder. "What?"

I looked up at the patched sails, hanging limp in the still harbor air. Then I looked at her.

"I can call the wind," I said.

---

It was not entirely true. I want to be honest about that.

I had called the wind once, at the University, in a moment of cold fury, and it had come roaring at my call and nearly killed a man. Since then, I had never managed it again. Not on purpose. I had heard it, sometimes, at the edges of my mind. I had felt it moving in me, the way you feel a word on the tip of your tongue. But I had never been able to reach out and take hold of it and make it come.

But I had spent nine days with Skarpi, singing the true song. And somewhere in those nine days, something had shifted in me. Something had opened. When I stood on the rocks outside the lighthouse and watched the sea, I could hear the wind the way I had learned to hear stone. Not its name, not quite. But its shape. Its voice. The long restless *yes* that it said to everything, the way stone said *no*.

I thought, standing on the deck of the *Wind's Daughter* in the still air of the harbor, that I might be able to call it. If I was very quiet. If I did not want it too much.

Hessa Tern looked at me for a long moment. Then she laughed, a harsh barking laugh like a seal.

"Call the wind," she said. "All right, pretty boy. Call it. If you can put wind in my sails, right now, in this dead calm, I'll carry you to Imre for free and feed you on the way. If you can't, you'll swim off my ship and I'll throw your pretty lute after you."

The crew had gathered to watch. A dozen hard-faced men and women, leaning on the rails and grinning. They had heard this sort of thing before, I suspected. Every harbor has its share of charlatans who claim to command the weather.

I set down my lute case. I walked to the bow of the ship and stood there, looking out over the still grey water of the harbor.

I closed my eyes.

I did not reach for the wind. I did not call it. I did not want it. I thought about Skarpi, sitting on the rocks, watching the sea. I thought about the way the wind had moved over the water, restless and endless, going everywhere and nowhere, touching everything and holding nothing.

And after a while, I heard it.

It was far away. Out beyond the harbor mouth, beyond the headland, out on the open sea. It was moving there, the way it always moved. Restless. Curious. Saying *yes* to everything it touched.

I did not call it. I simply listened to it. And then, very softly, I began to hum.

It was not a song. It was barely a tune. It was the wind's own voice, its long restless *yes*, hummed back to it the way I had hummed the stone's *no* to the Sleeper. I hummed it the way you would hum to a friend you had not seen in a long time. *Hello. I hear you. I've missed you. Come and see.*

The wind heard me.

---

I felt it turn. Far out on the open sea, beyond the headland, I felt it pause in its restless wandering and turn its attention toward the harbor. Toward the ugly ship at anchor, with its limp patched sails. Toward me.

It came.

It came slowly at first. A breath of air across the water, ruffling the grey surface into little cat's-paws. Then stronger. A breeze that lifted my hair and tugged at my cloak. Then stronger still, a steady wind blowing in from the sea, cold and clean and smelling of salt.

The sails of the *Wind's Daughter* stirred. They lifted. They filled.

Behind me, I heard the crew go silent.

I kept humming. I did not open my eyes. The wind was all around me now, moving over me and through me, playing with my hair and my cloak and the loose ends of the rigging. It was curious about me. It wanted to know who I was, who had called it so politely. It swirled around me like a cat around a stranger's legs, sniffing, testing.

I let it. I did not try to hold it or command it. I only hummed, softly, and let it come and go as it pleased.

And then, very gently, I stopped humming.

The wind did not stop. It kept blowing, steady and strong, filling the sails of the *Wind's Daughter* until they cracked and bellied out against their ropes. But it was not blowing because I told it to. It was blowing because it wanted to. Because it had come to see who was calling, and liked what it found, and decided to stay a while.

I opened my eyes. I turned around.

Hessa Tern was standing on the deck behind me, staring at the sails. Her mouth was open. The crew were staring too, every one of them, at the sails and then at me and then back at the sails.

"Well," said Hessa Tern at last. Her voice was hoarse. "Well, well. Well, God's bloody teeth."

"Do I get my passage?" I said.

She looked at me. Then she began to laugh. She laughed and laughed, bent over with her hands on her knees, until tears ran down her fist of a face.

"Get my ship out of this harbor," she said, when she could speak. "And then you can have anything you want."

---

The *Wind's Daughter* sailed east along the coast for eleven days. It should have taken twenty. The wind stayed with us the whole way, steady and strong, filling our sails from dawn to dusk and dusk to dawn. The crew said they had never seen anything like it. Hessa Tern said she had been sailing for thirty years and never had a voyage so fast or so easy.

I did not call the wind again. I did not have to. It seemed content to stay with us, playing in the rigging and whistling through the shrouds. Sometimes, at night, when I was sitting on the deck with my lute, it would come and swirl around me, lifting my hair and tugging at my sleeves, and I would play for it. Soft songs, restless songs, songs about roads and journeys and places far away. It seemed to like those best.

I spent those eleven days writing.

I had my father's fragments, and the pieces Skarpi had given me, and my own memories of the story. I had the true song in my bones, the way Skarpi had sung it to me in the broken lighthouse. But that song was old. It was in languages no one spoke anymore, in forms no one understood. If I sang it at the Eolian, the way Skarpi had sung it, no one would understand a word.

So I had to make it new. I had to take the old true song and put it into words that people could hear. Into Aturan, and into melodies they would recognize, and into a shape that would hold them in their seats and make them listen. Without losing any of the truth.

It was the hardest thing I have ever done. Harder than learning sympathy, harder than the Ketan, harder than hearing stone. It was like trying to pour the sea into a cup without spilling a drop.

I worked at it every day, from dawn until my eyes gave out. I sat in the bow with my lute across my knees and a sheaf of paper on the deck beside me, weighted down with a belaying pin against the wind. I wrote and crossed out and wrote again. I sang the verses to myself, over and over, until the crew began to hum them as they worked.

And slowly, verse by verse, it began to take shape.

---

On the ninth day, we met the pirates.

They came out of the dawn mist off the coast of the Small Kingdoms, three long low ships with black sails and many oars. They came fast, the oars flashing in the grey light, and they came straight for us.

Hessa Tern saw them first. She was standing at the wheel, and she went very pale, and then she began to shout orders in her gull's voice. The crew ran to the rails with pikes and crossbows and belaying pins. Someone rang the ship's bell, a harsh clanging sound that echoed out over the water.

I stood in the bow with my lute in my hands and watched them come.

"Kvothe!" Hessa Tern shouted from the wheel. "Can you give us more wind? Enough to outrun them?"

I thought about it. The wind was still with us, playing in the rigging. But the pirates had oars. They did not need the wind. And they were close, very close, closing fast.

"No," I shouted back. "Not enough."

"Then what the bloody hell can you do?"

I looked at the three black ships, cutting through the grey water toward us. At the oars flashing in the dawn light. At the men crowding the bows, with swords and axes and grappling hooks.

I looked at the wind, playing in our rigging.

And then I looked at the pirates' sails. Black and furled, bound tight to their yards, because they did not need them.

I put down my lute. I walked to the very tip of the bow, where the bowsprit jutted out over the water. I stood there with the spray in my face and the wind in my hair, and I closed my eyes.

*Hello*, I hummed to the wind. *Hello, friend. Would you like to play a game?*

---

It was not a very kind thing I did. I am not proud of it.

I asked the wind to go and look at the pirates' sails. That was all. I did not tell it what to do when it got there. I only asked it to go and look, and to be curious, the way it was always curious. And the wind, which had been bored for nine days playing in our rigging, was delighted to have something new to look at.

It went. It swept across the grey water toward the three black ships, and it found their furled sails, bound tight to their yards. And it was curious. It wanted to know what was inside them. It tugged at them, gently at first, then harder. It worked its fingers into the bindings and pried at them. It pulled and teased and worried at them, the way a cat worries at a ball of string.

The bindings gave way.

All at once, on all three ships, the black sails burst free of their yards. They billowed out, huge and flapping, catching the wind full on. And the wind, delighted, filled them to bursting. It pushed them. It shoved them. It sent the three black ships lurching and heeling over, their oars tangling, their men falling and shouting, their bows swinging wildly away from the *Wind's Daughter* and toward the rocky coast.

I heard the crash of oars snapping. I heard men screaming. I heard the grinding shriek of a hull striking rock.

I opened my eyes.

The three black ships were in chaos. One of them had run aground on a reef near the shore, its hull cracked open, men leaping into the water. The other two were heeled over at crazy angles, their sails flapping wildly, their oars trailing broken in the water. They were drifting away from us, fast, pushed by a wind that had found a new game and did not want to stop.

On the deck of the *Wind's Daughter*, no one spoke.

I turned around. The crew were staring at me. Hessa Tern was staring at me from the wheel. Her face was white.

I walked back along the deck to where I had left my lute. I picked it up. My hands were shaking.

"Go," I said. "While they're busy. Go."

Hessa Tern stared at me for one more moment. Then she began to shout orders again, and the crew ran to obey, and the *Wind's Daughter* swung her ugly bow toward the east and fled.

---

No one on the ship spoke to me for the rest of that day. They did not look at me, either, except in quick sidelong glances when they thought I would not notice. They gave me a wide berth when they passed me on the deck. At supper, they left an empty space around me on the bench.

I understood. I had seen the same thing at the University, after I called the wind at Ambrose. People are afraid of what they do not understand. And they are more afraid of it when it has just saved their lives.

That night, Hessa Tern came and found me in the bow. I was sitting with my lute, not playing it, looking out at the dark water.

She stood beside me for a long time without speaking. Then she said, "Who are you?"

"Nobody. A musician."

"Musicians don't sink pirate ships."

"I didn't sink them. The wind did."

"Because you asked it to."

"I asked it to look. It decided what to do on its own."

She was quiet for a long time.

"My mother used to tell me stories," she said at last. "When I was a girl. About the old namers, who could speak to the wind and the sea and the stone. She said they were all gone now. Killed in the old wars, or died out, or went away." She looked at me. "She said if you ever met one, you should be very polite to them. And very careful."

"That's good advice."

"Is it?" She looked out at the dark water. "Should I be careful of you, pretty boy?"

I thought about it. I thought about the three black ships, and the men screaming in the water, and the way the wind had laughed as it pushed them onto the rocks.

"Yes," I said. "I think you should."

She nodded slowly. Then, to my surprise, she sat down beside me on the deck.

"Play something," she said. "Something that'll make me forget I'm afraid."

I looked at her. Then I picked up my lute, and played "Tinker Tanner," with all the silly verses, and after a while, she began to laugh. And after a while longer, some of the crew drifted over, and sat down on the deck around us, and listened. And by the end of the night, they were singing along.

The wind stayed with us all the way to the mouth of the Omethi, and then, as if it could not bear to leave off a good game, up the long brown reaches of the river, pushing the ugly old ship against the current until the stone bridge rose out of the morning mist ahead of us, and I was home.
