# The Lantern-Bearer

On the first day past Felurian's spring, among the black trees, I met a child carrying a lantern.

I say *child*. I do not know what it was. It was the size of a child, perhaps eight or nine years old, and shaped like one, with thin arms and bare feet and a mop of dark hair falling into its eyes. It wore a shift of grey cloth that came down to its knees. And it carried, in both hands, held out in front of it at arm's length, a lantern of old green glass, with a light inside it that was not a flame.

It was standing in the middle of the path when I came around a bend between the black trees. It did not move. It watched me come with large dark eyes, very solemn, and held its lantern out, and waited.

I stopped a few paces away. I had learned a little, by then, about the Fae. I had learned that the things that look most harmless are often the most dangerous, and that the things that look most dangerous are often only lonely. I had learned to be polite. And I had learned never, ever, to take anything without knowing what it would cost.

"Hello," I said.

"Hello," said the child. Its voice was very soft and very clear, like water falling into a still pool. "You're mortal."

"Yes."

"You smell of her." It wrinkled its small nose. "The one by the spring. The one who makes cloaks out of shadows. You were with her."

"Yes."

"And she let you go." The child tilted its head. "She doesn't let anyone go. Not usually. You must have given her something very good."

"A song," I said.

The child's eyes widened. It looked at me with new interest.

"A song," it said. "Oh. Oh, that's very good. Songs are the best thing." It held out the lantern a little further. "Would you like to trade?"

---

I looked at the lantern. It was very old. The green glass was clouded and cracked, and the iron frame was black with age, and the handle was worn smooth by many hands. And the light inside it—

I looked closer. And my breath caught in my throat.

It was not a flame. It was a room. A tiny room, no bigger than my thumb, glowing inside the green glass. A wagon. The inside of a wagon, painted in bright colors, red and gold and green. With a small window, and a curtain, and a lamp hanging from a hook. And a woman sitting on a bench beneath the lamp, with a child in her lap, singing.

I could not hear the song. The glass was too thick. But I could see her lips move. I could see her hand, stroking the child's red hair, slowly, in time with the music. I could see her face.

My mother's face.

---

I do not know how long I stood there. The child did not move. It held the lantern out, very patiently, and watched me with its solemn dark eyes.

"What is it?" I said at last. My voice sounded very far away.

"A memory," said the child. "One of yours. From when you were very small. So small you don't remember it anymore." It looked at the lantern fondly. "I found it lying on the road. A long way from here. A long time ago. You dropped it, I think, when you were a boy. When something bad happened and you had to forget a lot of things all at once." It shrugged its thin shoulders. "People drop them all the time. When it hurts too much to carry them. They just fall out. I pick them up."

"Why?"

The child looked surprised.

"Because they're pretty," it said. "Because someone should keep them. It's a shame to leave them lying in the road." It held the lantern up, so the light fell on its face. "I have lots. Hundreds. But this is one of my favorites. Look how bright it is. She loved you very much, that woman. You can tell. The ones with love in them are always the brightest."

---

I could not look away. My mother, singing. Her hand in my hair. The lamp swaying gently on its hook as the wagon rolled along the road. I could almost smell it. Woodsmoke and horses and the lavender she kept in a little bag beside her bed.

I did not remember this. I had tried, many times, to remember my mother's face. In Tarbean, in the dark, curled up on a rooftop, I had tried so hard that my head ached. And I could never quite do it. I could remember her voice, a little. Her laugh. The way she sang. But her face had faded, over the years, like a painting left out in the sun.

And here it was. Bright and clear and perfect, in a green glass lantern, held out to me by a child in the Fae.

"What do you want for it?" I said.

---

The child smiled. It had very small, very white teeth.

"A song," it said. "The song you gave her. The first time, long ago. The one that let you leave her." It tilted its head. "I heard it. Through the trees. Everyone did. It was very beautiful." Its eyes were bright. "I want it. For my collection. I'll keep it in a lantern, like this one, and I'll look at it whenever I'm sad."

I did not understand. "I'll sing it for you. Right now. As many times as you like."

"No," said the child patiently. "Not *sing* it. *Give* it. The song. The memory of it. Where it came from, and how it felt to make it, and what her face looked like when she heard it." It held up the lantern. "One for one. Your song for your mother. That's fair. That's a good trade."

I went very still.

"If I give it to you," I said slowly, "I won't remember it."

"No. It'll be mine." The child nodded. "And this will be yours. You'll remember her face. Always. Every time you close your eyes." It looked at me with its large dark eyes. "Isn't that better? A song you made once, long ago, for a woman who isn't even yours? Or your mother's face, forever?"

---

I stood in the path between the black trees for a long time.

I wanted it. I want to be honest about that. I wanted that lantern more than I had ever wanted anything. More than the Archives, more than the name of the wind, more than Denna, even. My mother's face. Singing. Her hand in my hair.

And the song for Felurian—what was it? A song. I had made hundreds of songs. I would make hundreds more. What was one song, against my mother's face?

I reached out my hand.

And stopped.

Because I remembered something. Not a thought. A feeling. Down in the bottom of me, where the truth lives. The feeling of sitting in Felurian's clearing, by the spring, with my lute in my lap, and finding the song. Not making it. Finding it. The way you find a path in the dark, one step at a time, by feel. And the moment when it was finished, and I looked up, and saw her face.

Her face, when she heard it. The most beautiful and dangerous creature in the Fae, weeping. And then letting me go.

That song was why I was alive. That song was why I was walking on this path, and not lying in her clearing forever, wasting away, the way the stories said. If I gave it to the child, I would still be alive. The trade would not change that. But I would not know *why*.

I would not know how I had done it. I would not know that I could.

---

"No," I said.

The child's face fell.

"No?"

"No." I lowered my hand. "I'm sorry. I can't."

"But it's your *mother*." The child held the lantern out further, pleading. "Look at her. Look how bright she is. Don't you want her?"

"More than anything," I said. My voice was not steady. "But the song isn't just a song. It's how I got out. It's how I know I can get out." I looked at the lantern, at my mother's tiny glowing face, and my eyes stung. "If I trade it, I'll have her face. But I won't have the part of me that made the song. And I think I'm going to need that part. Where I'm going." I swallowed. "I think she would want me to keep it."

The child looked at me for a long time.

"That's very sad," it said at last.

"Yes."

"And very brave."

"I don't know about that."

"I do," said the child. It looked down at the lantern, and then back up at me. "I've been doing this a very long time. Collecting. Trading. And almost everyone says yes. Almost everyone would rather have something they lost than something they made." It tilted its head. "You're very strange."

"So I'm told."

---

The child was quiet for a moment. Then it did something I did not expect.

It held the lantern up to its face, and it looked at the tiny glowing room inside. At the painted wagon, and the lamp on its hook, and the woman singing. It looked for a long time.

Then it opened the little glass door on the side of the lantern.

"Here," it said. "You can look. Just once. For as long as you want. And then I'll close it again, and keep it safe." It held the open lantern out to me. "That's free. That's not a trade. That's just—" It frowned, searching for the word. "Kindness. I think. I've heard of it. I've never tried it before."

---

I looked.

I cannot tell you what I saw. I have tried, many times since. I have tried to write it down, and to sing it, and to tell it. And it does not come. I think that is part of the bargain, even though the child said it was free. You can look, in the Fae. But you cannot take it with you.

I remember that she was singing. I remember that the light was warm and gold. I remember her hand in my hair, and the smell of lavender, and the sway of the wagon on the road. I remember that she was smiling. I remember that she looked down at me, at the very small red-haired child in her lap, and her face was full of such love that it hurt to look at.

I remember that I was crying.

And I remember that after a long time, a very long time, I stepped back, and nodded to the child, and it closed the little glass door very gently. And the room inside the lantern went on glowing, small and bright and far away.

---

"Thank you," I said.

"You're welcome." The child looked pleased and a little shy, as if it had done something it was not sure it was allowed to do. "I'll keep her safe. I promise. In my collection. With all the others." It hesitated. "And if you ever want to trade, later—if you ever change your mind—"

"I won't."

"No," said the child. "I didn't think so." It stepped aside, off the path, into the shadow of the black trees. "Go on, then. Strange mortal. The birches are that way. The Sithe will find you there." It looked at me with its solemn dark eyes. "Be careful of them. They're not kind. Not like me."

I walked on. When I looked back, a few paces later, the child was gone. There was only the path, and the black trees, and far back among them, very faint, a small green light, bobbing away into the dark.

---

I did not remember my mother's face after that. Not clearly. It faded again, the way it always had, like a painting left out in the sun.

But I remembered that I had seen it. That for a little while, on a path in the Fae, I had looked at her, and she had been smiling. And that was something. That was more than I had had before.

And years later, when a woman with dark hair took my name and tied it in a cord, and I lost everything—my songs and my stories and my friends and my self—I found, when it came back, that one thing had been waiting for me at the very bottom. Under all the rest. The first thing I found, when I went looking.

Not my mother's face. The child had kept that.

But the song for Felurian. Every note of it. Where it came from, and how it felt to make it, and what her face looked like when she heard it.

The part of me that could get out.

I had kept it. And it had kept me.
