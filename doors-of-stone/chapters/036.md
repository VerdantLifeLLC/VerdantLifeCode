# The Unknotting

The door behind the yew hedge was still unlocked.

I had not truly expected it to be. Andrian had left it open for me, but Andrian was dead, and the dead do not keep their appointments, and someone in the palace must surely have noticed an unlocked door in the garden wall and bolted it. But when I came through the dark streets of Renere two nights after the prince's death, with my cloak drawn close and my face hidden in its hood, and slipped down the narrow alley that ran along the eastern wall of the palace garden, I found the door behind its screen of black yew exactly as he had said. Closed, but not locked. It swung open at my touch without a sound. Someone had oiled the hinges.

I stood in the doorway for a long moment, looking into the dark garden. I thought about a young man with a weak chin, walking out here in the afternoon while his father lay dying, and quietly oiling the hinges of a door so that a stranger could come in the night and try to save him.

Then I stepped through and closed it behind me.

---

Kelm was waiting for me by the fountain. He was wrapped in a dark cloak, and his spectacles glinted in the thin moonlight, and he was shaking.

"You came," he whispered.

"I said I would."

"They're saying things about you. In the palace. Terrible things." He clutched at my arm. "They're saying you killed the prince. That you bewitched his horse. Duke Jakis has been talking to the captain of the guard all day. They're going to arrest you, Kvothe. Tomorrow, I think. Maybe tonight."

"Then we'd better be quick."

He stared at me. Then he laughed, a thin shaky sound. "You're mad," he said. "The Maer said you were clever. He didn't say you were mad."

"The Maer is very diplomatic."

Kelm shook his head. But he let go of my arm and turned and led me through the garden, along a gravel path between dark hedges, to a small door set low in the palace wall. He had a key. He unlocked it with shaking fingers and we slipped inside.

---

We went up through the servants' passages, narrow dark corridors that ran behind the walls of the palace like the veins beneath skin. Kelm knew them well. He had been the king's physician for eleven years, and in that time, he told me in a whisper, he had learned every hidden stair and back corridor in the royal apartments.

"The chief physician goes to bed at midnight," he murmured as we climbed. "The priests leave at one. After that, there's only the night nurse and the guards at the door. The nurse is a friend. She'll let us in by the dressing room and say nothing."

"And the guards?"

"The guards stand outside the main door. They don't come in unless someone calls." He glanced back at me. "Don't make anyone call."

We came out at last into a small dim room hung with the king's robes and smelling of cedar and camphor. A stout grey woman in a white apron was waiting there. She looked at me with sharp suspicious eyes, then at Kelm, and then she nodded once, curtly, and opened the door into the bedchamber.

It was just as I remembered it. The great red bed. The roaring fire. The heat and the smell of sickness. But it was empty of people now, the physicians and priests and courtiers gone to their beds, and in the dim firelight it looked less like a king's chamber than like a tomb.

Roderic lay in the center of the bed, exactly as he had lain before. His eyes were closed. His breathing was very shallow. His hair spread out across the pillows around him, long and grey and knotted.

It was longer than before. Much longer. It had grown in two days more than it should have grown in a year. It spilled off the pillows and down the sides of the bed and onto the floor, a great grey tangled mass, full of knots.

"It's worse," Kelm whispered. "Since the prince died. It's been growing so fast. The valet cut it this morning, and by noon it had grown back." He looked at me. "What is it? What's happening to him?"

"His name is being written," I said. "In his own hair. And the words are saying *end*."

---

I knelt beside the bed. I pushed back my sleeves. I took a candle from the bedside table and held it close, and I began to search through the king's hair for the three knots I had found before.

It took a long time. There were so many knots now. Hundreds of them. Most were empty, decoys, meaningless tangles meant to hide the true ones. But the true ones had grown too. When I found them at last, deep in the hair near the king's scalp, they were larger than before. More complex. The knot that said *ending* had spread out into a great sprawling tangle, looping around itself again and again, like a vine strangling a tree.

And the name. The name was there, at the center of it all. Woven so tight and so deep that it seemed to be part of his skull.

I looked at it for a long time. I tried to read it. I could not. It was too complex, too intricate, too much. It was like trying to read a whole book written on the head of a pin.

"Can you untie it?" Kelm whispered.

"I don't know."

"You have to try. Please. He's dying. He'll be dead by morning."

I looked at the knots. At the tight, neat, precise loops, doubling back on themselves in that particular way.

I thought about Denna, in the Grey Man, her fingers moving through a cord. *It feels like the knot is holding on to something. Like it's reaching out and catching hold of something and not letting go.*

I thought about what I had told her, sitting on the wall at the edge of the cliff in Severen. *Everything that's tied can be untied.*

I did not know if that was true. I had said it because I wanted it to be true. Because I could not bear the thought that it was not.

But I reached out anyway. And I took hold of the knot that said *ending*. And I began to try.

---

I cannot tell you how long I worked. Hours. Kelm said later it was nearly four hours, but it did not feel like hours. It felt like no time at all, and it felt like years.

I did not try to untie the knot with my fingers. I tried that first, and it was useless; the hairs were too fine, the loops too tight, and every time I thought I had found an end, it slipped away from me and vanished back into the tangle. I tried cutting a single strand, very carefully, with the point of my knife, and the knife slid off the hair as if it were made of steel.

So I stopped trying to untie it. I stopped trying to do anything to it at all. I let my mind go still, the way I had in the round room at the top of the Rookery, the way I had in the Cellarage when I sang the Sleeper to sleep. I did not reach for the knot. I did not want anything from it. I simply held it, the way you might hold a bird, and listened.

And after a while, I heard it.

It was not like hearing stone. Stone says no. This was something else. This was a voice saying *yes*. Over and over, very softly, very insistently. *Yes. End. Yes. End. Yes. Slowly. Yes.*

It was a voice that had been told what to say, and was saying it, and could not stop.

I listened to it for a long time. I listened until I understood it. Not the words, but the shape. The way it was bent. The way it had been made to say something it did not want to say.

Because it did not want to. I could feel that. The hair did not want to kill its king. It was his hair. It had grown from his body. It had been part of him since he was born. It had been bent and twisted and knotted into a shape that said *end*, and it was saying it, because it had to. But it did not want to.

I opened my mouth. I did not know what I was going to say. I had no name for this, no word for it. I did not know the name of hair, or of knots, or of ending.

But I knew what it felt like to be bound. To be tied into a shape you did not choose. To be made to say *yes* when every part of you wanted to say *no*.

I began to hum.

---

It was not a song. It was barely a tune. It was the stone's no, the long slow patient no that I had learned in the round room and sung to the Sleeper in the dark. But it was softer than that, gentler. It was not no to the whole world. It was no to one thing. One small thing. One word, knotted into a king's hair by a girl who had not known what she was doing.

*No,* I hummed. *No, you don't have to. No, you're not what they made you. No, you can let go.*

I felt the knot shift under my fingers.

It was very small. The barest movement, like a sleeping animal stirring. But I felt it. One loop, one tiny loop, deep in the heart of the tangle, loosened. Just a little.

I kept humming. I held the knot in my fingers and hummed to it, softly, the way you would hum to a frightened child. *No. No. You can let go now. It's all right. You can let go.*

Another loop loosened. And another.

And then, slowly, like ice melting in spring, the knot that said *ending* began to come apart.

---

It did not come apart all at once. It came apart loop by loop, strand by strand, over what felt like hours. I kept humming the whole time. My voice grew hoarse, and then it cracked, and then it was barely a whisper. But I kept humming. I did not dare stop.

At last, the knot was gone. There was only hair in my hand. Long grey strands, smooth and straight, lying loose across my palm.

I let go. I sat back on my heels. My whole body was shaking. My hands were numb. My throat felt as if I had swallowed a handful of gravel.

"Did it work?" Kelm whispered.

I looked at the king.

He was still lying in the center of the great red bed, with his eyes closed. He was still thin and yellow and terrible. But his breathing had changed. It was deeper. Slower. Easier.

And as I watched, his eyes opened.

---

He looked at me. His eyes were pale grey and very clear. They were not the eyes of a dying man. They were the eyes of a king.

"Who are you?" he said. His voice was a thread, barely audible. But it was steady.

"A musician, your majesty. Sent by the Maer of Severen."

"Alveron." Something moved in his face. It might have been a smile. "Clever Alveron. Always sending me clever things." He closed his eyes, then opened them again. "I've been dreaming. A long time. Dreaming of being tied. Tied up in ropes, very tight. Couldn't move. Couldn't speak." He looked at me. "Were you the one who untied me?"

"Only one of the ropes, your majesty. There are others."

"Yes. I can feel them." He was quiet for a moment. "I'm still dying. Aren't I?"

I did not answer. I did not have to.

"Yes," said Roderic. "I thought so. But it's different now. Before, it was like being drowned. Slowly. Dragged down into the dark by something I couldn't see." He breathed in, slowly. "Now it's just dying. Just an old man, dying. That's—" He smiled faintly. "That's better. Thank you."

"Your majesty," I said. "Who did this to you? Do you know?"

He looked at me for a long moment. His pale eyes were very steady.

"My son is dead," he said. "Isn't he? Andrian."

I hesitated. Then I nodded.

"I dreamed it," said the king. "I dreamed him falling." He closed his eyes. A tear slid out from beneath one lid and ran down into the grey hair at his temple. "He was a foolish boy. Weak. I was always hard on him. Too hard." He opened his eyes again. "Who did this? You ask me who did this?"

"Yes, your majesty."

"The same one who did it to my son. And my cousins. And my uncle. And my nephew." His voice was growing fainter. "An old man. With a stick. He came to court years ago. Played tak with my father. Everyone loved him." His lips twisted. "I loved him. He taught me to play when I was a boy. He used to sit with me in the garden and tell me stories about the old kings."

"Bredon," I said.

"Is that what he calls himself now?" Roderic's eyes were drifting closed. "He had another name, when I was a boy. Something else. I can't remember." He was quiet for a long moment. Then his eyes opened again, suddenly, and fixed on mine. "He wants the throne. Not for himself. For the Jakis boy. For Ambrose. Because Ambrose is—"

He stopped. His breath caught.

"Because Ambrose is what, your majesty?"

Roderic looked at me. His pale grey eyes were very wide.

"Because Ambrose is of the line," he whispered. "The old line. On his mother's side. A very distant branch. Very thin. Very weak." He gripped my wrist with his clawed hand, and his grip was surprisingly strong. "Not strong enough to open it. Not alone. But strong enough to hold the key. Strong enough to carry it to the door. And a king can go anywhere. A king can command anyone." His grip tightened. "A king can make the true heir come."

I stared at him.

"Ambrose is a Lackless?"

"A drop. A single drop. From four hundred years ago." Roderic's voice was fading. "But a drop is enough. For a key." His grip loosened. His eyes began to close. "Stop him, musician. Whoever you are. Stop the old man. Stop the Jakis boy. Don't let them—"

He did not finish. His hand fell away from my wrist. His eyes closed.

He did not open them again.

---

Kelm was weeping. The night nurse was weeping. I was not weeping. I was sitting on my heels beside the king's bed, with a handful of loose grey hair in my lap, staring at the dead man's face.

*Ambrose is of the line.*

And then the door of the bedchamber burst open.

I heard the crash of it before I saw it. I turned my head, very slowly, as if I were moving through water. The main door of the king's chamber had been flung back so hard it struck the wall. And in the doorway, lit from behind by the lanterns in the corridor, there were men. Guards, in the livery of the royal house. A dozen of them. With swords drawn.

And at their head, dressed all in black, with a smile on his face that I will remember until the day I die, Ambrose Jakis.

"There he is," said Ambrose. His voice was very loud in the silent room. "There's the Ruh. Kneeling at the king's bedside, with the king's hair in his hands." He looked at the bed. At the still yellow face on the pillow. His smile widened. "Oh," he said. "Oh, dear. It looks as though we're too late."

He looked at me.

"Arrest him," he said. "For the murder of the king."
