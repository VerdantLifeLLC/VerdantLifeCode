# The Half-Mast

The Tehlins had not forgotten Skarpi. Neither had I.

I remember the day they took him. I was twelve years old, sitting on the floor of a tavern in Waterside called the Half-Mast, listening to an old man with bright eyes tell the story of Lanre. He had told it over several days, a piece at a time, and I had come back every day to hear the next part. It was the first story I had heard in years that made me feel anything. It made me feel as though the world was bigger than the gutters of Tarbean. As though there were mysteries in it, and wonders, and things worth knowing.

And then, in the middle of a telling, the door had burst open and the Tehlins had come in. Priests in grey robes, and men of the Justice behind them with their iron-shod staves. They had taken Skarpi by the arms and dragged him from his stool, and they had accused him of heresy, of telling lies about Tehlu and the angels. And Skarpi had laughed at them, and called one of them by a name I did not recognize, and said something about Tehlu's fat, and let them take him.

I had never seen him again. I had always assumed he was dead. Burned, perhaps, or hanged, or rotting in some Tehlin dungeon.

But Chronicler—

---

The innkeeper stopped. He looked across the table at Chronicler.

"You knew him," he said. "Didn't you? Skarpi."

Chronicler went very still. His pen stopped moving.

"Yes," he said quietly. "I knew him."

"You told me, the first night. When I asked you who had told you where to find me." The innkeeper's green eyes were very steady. "You said a friend. An old storyteller. You didn't say his name."

"No."

"But it was Skarpi."

Chronicler was silent for a long moment. Then he nodded.

"It was Skarpi," he said. "He came to me in Tinuë, last autumn. I'd known him for years; he used to come by my rooms sometimes to argue about history. He told me that a man I'd been looking for was alive, and running an inn in a little town called Newarre, and that if I wanted the true story, I should go and ask for it." He paused. "He said you'd give it to me. He said you'd been waiting for someone to ask."

The innkeeper looked at him for a long time. Then he smiled. It was a small, strange smile, sad and wry and not at all surprised.

"Of course he did," he said. "Of course." He looked down at the table. "He always did like to arrange things."

At the window, Bast had turned around. He was looking at Chronicler with an expression Chronicler could not read.

"You didn't tell us that," Bast said. "That he sent you."

"You didn't ask."

"We *should* have asked." Bast's voice was sharp. "Reshi—"

"It doesn't matter, Bast." The innkeeper did not look up. "It doesn't change anything. If anything, it makes more sense." He was quiet for a moment. "Skarpi always knew more than he said. And he always said it at exactly the right time."

He looked up at Chronicler.

"Let me tell you how I found him," he said. "The second time."

---

The Tehlins, as it turned out, had not burned Skarpi. Nor hanged him, nor left him to rot. They had tried him before the Justice on Hillside, before a court of three priests and a magistrate, for the crime of heresy. And he had been found guilty. And he had been sentenced to death by burning.

And on the morning of his execution, when they came to his cell to take him to the pyre, they had found the cell empty. The door was locked. The bars were whole. The guards had seen nothing. Skarpi was simply gone, as if he had never been there at all.

I learned all this in a single afternoon, from an old Tehlin clerk in the records office of the Justice, who remembered the case very well and was only too happy to talk about it for the price of a mug of beer. He told me that the priests had searched the city for him for a month. That they had offered a reward. That they had never found a trace.

"Some said a demon took him," the old clerk said, wiping foam from his lips. "Some said the Duke of Gibea, the old heretic, came back from the dead to carry off his own. Some said he was an angel in disguise, sent to test us." He shrugged. "Me, I think somebody bribed a guard. But you didn't hear that from me."

"Did anyone ever see him again?"

The old clerk looked at me. He hesitated.

"There's talk," he said at last. "In Waterside. That he comes back, sometimes. To the Half-Mast. The old tavern where he used to tell his stories." He shook his head. "But that's just talk. People in Waterside'll say anything."

---

The Half-Mast was still there.

It had changed more than Waterside had. The sign had fallen down and been replaced with a new one, painted by someone who had clearly never seen a ship; the mast was bent at an impossible angle, and the sails were the wrong shape entirely. The old bar had been torn out and replaced with a new one, cheap and badly made. The floor had been covered in sawdust. The windows had been boarded up.

But the room was the same. Long and low and dark, with a great smoky hearth at one end and a scatter of rough tables and benches. And beside the hearth, in the corner, there was a stool.

It was empty. But it was there.

I went in and sat down at a table near the hearth. I ordered a mug of beer I could not afford, with the last copper I had found in the bottom of my pack. It was terrible beer, thin and sour. I drank it slowly and watched the door.

I waited all afternoon. People came and went. Sailors, dockhands, fishwives, beggars. A pair of whores who looked at me and laughed and went away. A drunk who tried to pick a fight with me and then fell asleep on the floor. None of them was Skarpi.

I waited all evening. The tavern grew crowded and loud. The fire roared in the hearth. Someone began to play a fiddle, badly. I sat at my table with my empty mug and watched the door and the stool, and I did not move.

It was near midnight when the fiddle stopped.

I did not notice at first. The tavern was so loud, and I was so tired, and my eyes had begun to close in spite of myself. But then I realized that the room had gone quiet. Not silent. There were still people talking, still mugs clinking. But quieter. As if someone had come in and everyone had turned, without meaning to, to look.

I opened my eyes.

There was an old man sitting on the stool by the hearth.

---

He had not come in through the door. I had been watching the door all day and all evening, and I would have seen him. And he had not been there a moment before. I was sure of that. But he was there now, sitting on the stool with his hands folded on his knees, as if he had been sitting there all along.

He was just as I remembered him. Tall and thin, with a long white beard and hair the color of old snow. His face was lined and weathered, the face of a man who has spent his life outdoors. His clothes were ragged. And his eyes were very bright, bright and dark at the same time, like the eyes of a bird.

He was looking at me.

"Well," he said. His voice was just as I remembered it too. Deep and rich and warm, with a faint lilt to it, like the voice of a man who has spent his life telling stories. "Well, well. Look who's come back to hear the ending."

The tavern had gone very quiet now. Everyone was looking at him. Or rather, everyone was looking at the stool by the hearth, and at the old man sitting on it, with the vague uneasy expression of people who have just seen something they cannot quite explain.

"Hello, Skarpi," I said.

"Hello, Kvothe." He smiled. His teeth were very white. "You've grown."

"Everyone keeps saying that."

"Everyone keeps being right." He looked around the tavern. At the staring faces, the half-raised mugs, the fiddler frozen with his bow in the air. "It's a bit crowded in here. Shall we find somewhere quieter?"

"Where?"

He stood up. He did not seem to move so much as unfold, like a heron rising from a pond.

"I know a place," he said.

---

He took me out of the Half-Mast and through the dark streets of Waterside. Down toward the harbor, and then along it, past the piers and the wharves and the moored ships creaking at their ropes. The moon was up, three-quarters full, silver on the black water.

He did not speak as we walked. Neither did I. I had a thousand questions, but I did not ask them. I had learned, over the years, that some people will tell you things only when they are ready, and that asking before then is worse than useless.

At last we came to the end of the harbor, where the piers gave out and the city gave out and there was only a long, low spit of land running out into the sea. It was rocky and bare, with a few stunted trees clinging to it, and at the very end of it, there was a ruin.

It had been a lighthouse once. You could see that from the shape of it: a round stone tower, tall and narrow, rising out of the rocks. But the top of it was gone, fallen in long ago, and the stones were black with age and slick with sea spray. There was a doorway at the bottom, with no door in it.

Skarpi led me through the doorway. Inside, the tower was hollow, a tall round shaft of stone open to the sky. Moonlight came down through the broken top and fell on the floor in a pale silver circle. There was a fire burning in the center of the circle, small and bright, with no wood to feed it. And beside the fire, there were two stones, flat-topped, like stools.

Skarpi sat down on one of them. He gestured to the other.

"Sit," he said. "You've come a long way."

I sat. The fire was warm. It crackled softly, though there was nothing in it to crackle. I looked at it, and then at Skarpi.

"Who are you?" I said.

He smiled. "That's an interesting question. Most people start with *what*."

"All right. What are you?"

"A storyteller." He spread his hands. "That's all I've ever been. I tell stories. True ones, mostly. Which is why the Tehlins didn't care for me." He chuckled. "They prefer their stories with the edges filed off."

"You escaped from a locked cell."

"I did."

"How?"

"I asked the door to let me out, and it did." He shrugged. "Doors are very reasonable, if you ask them properly."

I stared at him. "You're a namer."

"I know some names. Not many. Door is one. Fire is another." He nodded at the small bright flame between us. "Stone, a little. Wind, when it's in a good mood." He looked at me. "You know some names too. I can hear them on you. Wind. Stone. Something else, something newer. Something tangled." He tilted his head. "Knots, is it? You've been learning knots?"

"A little."

"Hm." He looked at me for a long moment with his bright bird's eyes. "Dangerous thing, knots. Much more dangerous than names. A name is a thing that is. A knot is a thing that's made to be." He shook his head. "The Yllish knew that. Most of them forgot. The ones who didn't forget—" He stopped. "Well. That's another story."

"I didn't come for another story," I said. "I came for the end of the one you started. The one about Lanre."

Skarpi smiled. It was a slow, warm, delighted smile.

"Yes," he said. "Yes, I thought you might have. You were always the one who came back." He settled himself on his stone, folding his long thin hands on his knees. "Where did I leave off?"

"Selitos," I said. "He'd cursed Lanre. At the ruins of Myr Tariniel. He'd put out his own eye with a stone, and he'd cursed Lanre with his own name, and he'd said something about a curse on all of Lanre's followers." I paused. "And then the Tehlins came."

"Ah. Yes." Skarpi nodded slowly. "Yes, that's where we were." He looked into the fire for a long moment. "That's where most people stop. Right there. At the curse. Selitos puts out his eye and curses Lanre, and Lanre becomes Haliax, and the Seven become the Chandrian, and Selitos founds the Amyr, and everyone goes home." He shook his head. "That's the story as it's usually told. That's the story the Amyr told, and the Tehlins told, and your parents were trying to tell."

"Is it wrong?"

"No. It's not wrong. It's just not finished." He looked up at me. His eyes were very bright in the firelight. "The story doesn't end with the curse, Kvothe. The curse is where the story *begins*."

He leaned forward.

"Listen," he said. "And I'll tell you the rest."
