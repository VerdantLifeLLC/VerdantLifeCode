# Interlude: Dawn

The people of Newarre came out of the little stone church at dawn.

They came slowly, cautiously, the way people come out after a storm. Carter first, with his wife and his children behind him, blinking in the grey light. Then Graham, with a cudgel in his hand. Then Old Cob, leaning on his stick. Then Jake, carrying Tam, who was nearly too big to carry now and was asleep on his father's shoulder anyway. Then the rest of them. The miller and his wife. Baxter the smith. Rena the widow. Shep's widow, and her sister. Aaron, the smith's apprentice, with an iron bar in his hand and his young face set and pale.

They stood in the churchyard among the graves and looked around them. At the grey sky, paling in the east. At the grey fields. At the grey road.

There was no frost.

That was the first thing they noticed. The whole night, the frost had crept. Across the fields, across the road, up the walls of the church. They had watched it through the windows, white and feathery, spreading. They had heard things, in the dark, beyond the walls. Footsteps on frozen grass. A voice, very soft, very cold, saying words they did not understand.

And now it was gone. The grass in the churchyard was wet and green. The road was mud. The air was cool and damp and smelled of rain.

"It's gone," said Carter. His voice was hoarse. "Whatever it was. It's gone."

"The cows," said his wife. "Go and look at the cows."

Carter went. He came back an hour later, walking slowly, with his face very strange.

"They're dead," he said. "All eleven. Lying in the field. But they're not frozen anymore." He shook his head. "They're just dead. Like they died of old age. In their sleep."

Nobody said anything.

"The Waystone," said Aaron suddenly. "Kote. I went there last night, to tell him. He wouldn't come. He said he knew what it was. He said it was looking for something." He looked at the others. "He said it wasn't looking for us."

They looked at each other.

Then, without anyone saying anything, they began to walk toward the Waystone Inn.

---

The door was open.

That was the first thing they saw, coming up the road in the grey dawn light. The door of the Waystone, standing open. And inside, a fire burning in the great stone hearth. Yellow. Warm. They could feel the warmth of it from the yard.

And they could hear something.

Music.

It was coming from inside the inn. Very faint. A lute. Someone was playing a lute.

They stopped in the yard. All of them. They stood among the puddles in the grey light and listened.

It was a slow song. A sad song. The melody rose and fell like wind in trees. It was the most beautiful thing any of them had ever heard. More beautiful than the bard who had come through, years ago, with his bright red cloak and his confidence. More beautiful than the priest's choir on Mourning Day. More beautiful than anything.

Shep's widow began to weep.

She did not know why. She stood in the yard of the Waystone Inn, in the grey dawn light, with her sister's arm around her, and she wept. The music went into her. Through her. Down and down, into the place where Shep had been, where nothing had been since the night the stranger came. And it found something there. Not Shep. Something else. Something like him. The shape of him. The warmth.

"That's it," she whispered. "That's the song. The one Aaron sang. *Tariniel*." She was shaking. "That's the whole of it."

---

They went in.

Slowly. Quietly. The way you go into a church. They came through the open door into the common room of the Waystone Inn and stopped just inside, in a crowd, and stared.

The floor was wet. Puddled everywhere, as if someone had spilled a great deal of water. There were dark stains on the boards near the center of the room. There were broken bottles behind the bar.

At a table by the hearth, a man with a neat beard and ink-stained fingers was sitting very still, with a great untidy stack of paper in front of him. His face was pale and his eyes were red. He did not look up when they came in.

On a bench against the wall, a young man with dark hair was lying on his back, asleep, with a blanket over him. His face was very pale. His shirt was dark with dried blood. But he was breathing, slow and even.

And on a stool beside the great stone hearth, in the warm yellow light of the fire, the innkeeper was sitting with a lute in his lap.

He was playing.

---

They did not know him.

That was what they said afterward, when they told the story. And they did tell it, all of them, for the rest of their lives. In the Waystone and in the church and in their own houses, around their own fires, to their children and their grandchildren. They told it a hundred times, and every time they told it, they said the same thing.

They did not know him.

It was Kote. It was the innkeeper. The same red hair and green eyes. The same bruises on the jaw. The same scar on the cheek. They had seen that face every night for seven years, behind the bar, pouring drinks and polishing glasses and saying the right thing.

But it was not Kote.

It was like the difference between a painting of a fire and a fire. The same shape. The same colors. But one of them was only paint on a wall, and the other was burning.

He was burning.

He sat on the stool beside the hearth with the lute in his lap and his head bowed over it and his eyes closed, and he played. His fingers moved on the strings like water, like wind, like flame. And the music came out of him and filled the room, filled the whole inn, and poured out through the open door into the grey morning.

He was playing *Tariniel*.

---

He did not look up when they came in. He did not stop playing. He played the white city on the last day of its life. The towers, and the gardens, and the fountains full of children. He played Lanre, and Lyra, and the Thief's whisper in the dark. He played Myr Tariniel burning. He played Selitos weeping among the ruins.

And he played the child. The boy in the fountain. Asking to be a singer.

He played it slowly. Much slower than the bard had played it, years before. Like a child playing. Not like a march.

And when he came to the last line, he did not sing it like an ending. He sang it like a beginning.

*A singer. Like my mother. So that no one will forget.*

---

The last note hung in the air for a long moment. Trembling. Then it faded.

The common room of the Waystone Inn was silent.

Nobody moved. Nobody spoke. They stood just inside the door, the people of Newarre, in a crowd, with their faces wet and their hands still. The fire crackled in the hearth. Somewhere outside, a bird was singing.

The innkeeper lifted his head. He opened his eyes. He looked at them.

His eyes were green. Very green. Burning.

"Good morning," he said.

His voice was different too. Not the flat, mild voice of the innkeeper. Something deeper. Something that filled the room.

Nobody answered.

Then Shep's widow came forward. Slowly. Through the crowd. She stopped in front of the innkeeper's stool and looked at him. Her face was wet with tears.

"You told me," she said. "Last night. I asked you. If you ever heard the whole of it. The song. You said you'd tell me." Her voice cracked. "You said *if*."

"I did."

"You knew it. All along. You knew the whole of it."

The innkeeper looked at her for a long moment.

"No," he said gently. "Not all along. I forgot it, for a while. A long while." He looked down at the lute in his lap. "But I remember now."

She looked at him. At his red hair, and his green eyes, and the lute in his lap.

"Who are you?" she whispered.

The innkeeper was quiet for a long time.

Then he stood up. He set the lute down very carefully on the stool. He looked around the room at the people of Newarre, crowded just inside the door. At Graham, with his cudgel. At Old Cob, leaning on his stick. At Jake, with Tam asleep on his shoulder. At Carter, and his wife, and his children. At Aaron, the smith's apprentice, with his iron bar and his young face pale and set.

At Aaron, most of all.

Aaron was staring at him. His face was very white. His mouth was open.

"You're him," Aaron whispered. "Aren't you? You're really him."

The innkeeper looked at him for a long moment. Then he smiled. It was a small smile, crooked, a little sad. But it was real.

"My name is Kvothe," he said.

---

Nobody spoke.

It was the silence that comes after something so large that no one knows how to answer it. The silence of a crowd that has just been told the sky is a lid, or the ground is the back of a sleeping beast. It went on and on.

Then Old Cob made a sound. A sort of wheezing cough. And said, into the silence, with the air of a man playing a trump card:

"I *knew* it. I said so. Didn't I say so? Years ago. I said he had red hair."

Graham turned and looked at him. "You said he was a demon. With horns."

"I said he *might* have horns. I never said he definitely had horns."

"You said he killed the king with a word."

"Well," said Old Cob, with great dignity, "didn't he?"

Everyone turned and looked at the innkeeper.

He looked back at them. At Old Cob, and Graham, and all the rest. At their faces, pale and astonished and a little afraid.

And he laughed.

It was a real laugh. Sudden and bright and startled, like a bird taking flight. It burst out of him the way the music had, from somewhere very far down. He laughed until his eyes were wet, standing in the middle of the common room of the Waystone Inn, with the fire behind him and the sword on the wall above his head.

And after a moment, very uncertainly, the people of Newarre began to laugh with him.

---

He told them. Not all of it. Not the way he had told Chronicler, over three days, with every piece in its place. That would have taken too long, and there were things in it that were not for farmers and millers and smiths.

But he told them enough. He told them who he was, and what he had done, and why he had come to Newarre. He told them about the chest, and the stone at the crossroads, and the little name a girl had given him to wear. He told them about the thing that had come in the night, with its cold and its frost and its crooked scarred face, and what he had done to send it away.

He told them about the door. And the crack. And the scrael, and the skin dancer, and the things that were coming through.

And he told them he had to leave.

---

"Leave?" said Graham. His big slow face was stricken. "Why? You just—you just got back. Whoever you are."

"Because they'll come," said Kvothe. "The others. The ones like the thing that came last night. They'll come looking for me. They can hear me now. I'm very loud." He looked around the room. "If I stay here, they'll come here. To Newarre. To you."

"We'll fight them," said Aaron fiercely. "We've got iron. I can make more. Swords. Spears. Whatever you need."

Kvothe looked at him. His face was very gentle.

"I know you would," he said. "You already have. You saved my life once, Aaron. With an iron firedog, against a thing that had taken Shep." He looked at the boy for a long moment. "I never thanked you."

Aaron went red to the roots of his hair.

"But I won't let you do it again," said Kvothe. "Not for me. Not here." He looked around at all of them. "This was a quiet place. A small place. That's why I came. And I want it to stay that way. For you. For your children." He looked at Tam, asleep on Jake's shoulder. "It's a good thing, to be small and quiet. I didn't understand that, before. I do now."

"Where will you go?" said Shep's widow.

Kvothe was quiet for a long moment.

"To the door," he said at last. "The one I closed. It's cracked. Things are coming through. I need to find a way to close it all the way." He paused. "And there's an old man in a tower in the mountains, who's been waiting a very long time for someone to come back and sing him a song. I told him I'd come, if I ever found the other way."

"The other way?"

"To end the curse. Without opening the door." Kvothe smiled faintly. "I haven't found it yet. But I think I might know where to start looking."

---

He gave the inn to Aaron.

He did it that afternoon, in front of everyone. He took a sheet of paper from Chronicler's satchel and a pen, and he wrote it out in a clear strong hand. *The Waystone Inn, with all its furniture and fixtures, its stock and its stores, its stable and its yard, to Aaron, apprentice smith of Newarre, freely given.* And he signed it. Not *Kote*. His other name. His real one.

Aaron stared at the paper. Then at him.

"I can't," he said. "I'm a smith. I don't know anything about running an inn."

"Neither did I," said Kvothe. "When I came here. I learned." He smiled. "Graham will help you. And Old Cob will tell you everything you're doing wrong. And the regulars will come every night whether the stew is good or not, because there's nowhere else to go." He put his hand on Aaron's shoulder. "You'll be fine."

"But why me?"

Kvothe looked at him for a long moment.

"Because you picked up the iron," he said. "When everyone else ran, or froze, you picked up the iron and you used it. You didn't know what the thing was. You didn't know if it would work. You just did it, because someone had to." He squeezed Aaron's shoulder. "That's the kind of person who should keep a waystone. Someone who knows how to say no."

---

He did not take the chest.

He took what was in it. The lute. The folded paper with Sim's terrible poem on it, which he read, sitting by the fire in the afternoon, and laughed at until he wept. A second folded page, torn from a moneylender's ledger, which he read after the poem, and laughed at, and wept over, and held for a long time. The small box of dark wood with no lid and no lock, which hummed in his hands, and which he wrapped in an oiled cloth and put in the bottom of his pack.

But he left the chest where it was. At the foot of the bed in the room at the top of the stairs. Empty. With its two open locks and the empty place between them where a knot had been.

"It's done its work," he said to Chronicler, when Chronicler asked. "It held me. For seven years. It held the thing I couldn't carry." He was quiet for a moment. "Let it rest now. It's earned it."

---

And he took down the sword.

He did it last, in the evening, when the regulars had come in for their supper and were crowded around the bar, drinking Aaron's first beer and telling Aaron everything he was doing wrong. He climbed up on a chair in front of the great stone hearth and reached up and lifted the sword off its two iron pegs.

The common room went quiet. Everyone watched.

He held it in his hands for a long time. A long straight blade, grey and plain, with a simple hilt wrapped in leather. He looked at it the way a man looks at a letter he has read so many times that the paper has gone soft at the folds.

Then he climbed down off the chair. He looked at the plaque on the wall, beneath the empty pegs. Dark oak, with a single word burned into it.

*FOLLY*.

"Leave that," said Graham quietly, from the bar. "Please. Leave the plaque. I made it."

Kvothe looked at him. Then he looked at the plaque again.

"All right," he said. "I'll leave it."

"What'll you call it now?" said Old Cob. "The sword. It's not on the wall anymore. Is it still Folly?"

Kvothe looked down at the blade in his hands for a long moment.

"Yes," he said at last. "It's still Folly. It always will be." He slid it into its sheath. "But folly isn't always a bad thing. Sometimes it's the only thing that gets anything done."

He buckled it on.

---

Bast woke that evening. He opened his eyes on the bench against the wall, with the blanket over him, and saw Kvothe standing in the middle of the common room with the sword at his hip and the lute on his back and his pack over his shoulder.

He stared at him for a long time.

"Reshi," he said at last. His voice was very weak. "You're going."

"We're going," said Kvothe. "When you can walk."

Bast looked at him. His deep blue eyes were very bright.

"Where?"

"North, first. To the Stormwal. There's an old man in a tower who told me he'd like to hear a song." Kvothe smiled. "And then the door. And then—" He shrugged. "Wherever the road goes."

Bast was quiet for a long moment. Then he sat up, very slowly, wincing, with his hand pressed to his side.

"I can walk," he said.

"You can't."

"I can. Watch me." He swung his legs off the bench. He stood up. He swayed, and caught himself on the table, and stood there, white-faced and grinning. "See? Walking."

"That's standing."

"It's the first half of walking." Bast grinned wider. "The rest will come."

Kvothe looked at him. Then he laughed, and crossed the room, and put his arm around Bast's shoulders to hold him up.

"Tomorrow," he said. "At dawn."

"Everything always happens at dawn with you," said Bast. "Have you noticed that? You always leave places at dawn."

"I've noticed," said Kvothe.
