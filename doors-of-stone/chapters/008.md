# Interlude: Knots

Kvothe stopped speaking, and the Waystone Inn came back into itself.

Chronicler blinked. For a moment he had forgotten where he was. He had been sitting on a grassy bluff above a cold river in the rain, his clothes soaked through, a girl's footprints fading in the wet grass beside him. Now he was sitting at a scarred table in an empty common room, and the fire was crackling in the hearth, and there was ink drying on his fingers.

He flexed his hand. It ached. He looked down at the page in front of him and saw that he had filled it to the very bottom with his tiny, crabbed cipher, and had begun, without noticing, to write sideways along the margin.

"You didn't know," he said. "About your mother. Before that."

"I suspected." The innkeeper was looking at the fire. "I had pieces. I had a rhyme she scolded me for singing, and a way she held her cup, and the look on the Maer's wife's face when she spoke of her lost sister. I had all the pieces, lying there in front of me. I simply hadn't put them together." He smiled faintly. "It's a strange thing. You can be very clever about everything in the world except yourself."

Bast came out of the kitchen with a fresh pot of tea. He set it on the table and did not leave. Instead he pulled out the third chair, the one that had been sitting upside down on the tabletop, and set it on its feet and sat in it backward, his arms folded across its back.

"The knot," he said. "On her letter."

The innkeeper glanced at him. "Yes."

"You never told me about the knot."

"There are a great many things I've never told you, Bast."

"But that one." Bast's voice was strange. Tight. "Reshi, that's—you know what that is. You must have known, even then. That's not a lover's mark. That's not something a girl draws at the bottom of a letter because she thinks it looks pretty."

"I didn't know. Not then." The innkeeper poured himself a cup of tea. His hands were steady. "I thought it was Yllish. A story knot. You've seen them. The Yllish used to tie stories into their hair, into cords, into the fringes of their clothes. A knot for a birth, a knot for a death, a knot for a promise kept or broken. Denna wore them in her braids. I'd noticed that long before. I thought it was a fashion."

"It's not a fashion," Bast said.

"No," the innkeeper agreed. "It's not."

Chronicler looked from one of them to the other. "What is it, then?"

The innkeeper was quiet for a long moment. He turned his cup a quarter turn on the table, the way he had that morning.

"Do you know how writing began?" he asked at last.

Chronicler hesitated. "I've read some theories. Most scholars agree that it began with merchants. Tallies. Records of trade. A mark for a sheep, a mark for a bushel of grain. Over time the marks became more complicated, and—"

"That's how *our* writing began," said the innkeeper. "Tema. Aturan. Siaru. The writing you use every day. It's a way of keeping track of things. It's a way of remembering." He lifted his cup and did not drink from it. "But there's an older kind of writing. It doesn't remember things. It *makes* them."

Chronicler felt a small chill go down his spine. He did not say anything.

"The Yllish knew it," the innkeeper went on. "Or some of them did, once, a long time ago. Before they forgot. A true knot, tied in the true way, doesn't record a story. It binds it. It fixes it in place, the way a nail fixes a board. Tie a man's death in a cord, the old way, and he'll stay dead. Tie a promise, and it will be kept, whether the one who made it wants to keep it or not." He set down his cup. "Tie a name—"

He stopped.

The fire popped. A log shifted in the grate, sending up a shower of sparks. Bast was very still in his backward chair, and his eyes were fixed on the innkeeper's face.

"Tie a name," said Chronicler quietly, "and what?"

The innkeeper looked at him. His green eyes were very dark.

"We'll get to that," he said. "It's later in the story."

Chronicler wanted to press. Every instinct he had, every habit of a lifetime spent pulling stories out of reluctant mouths, told him to press. But there was something in the innkeeper's face that stopped him. It was not anger, and it was not fear. It was something older and quieter than either. It was the look of a man standing at the edge of a very deep hole, looking down.

"All right," Chronicler said. He dipped his pen. "Later."

"Later," the innkeeper agreed.

Bast made a small sound, low in his throat. The innkeeper glanced at him.

"You don't like it," he said.

"I don't like any of it." Bast's voice was low and fierce. "I don't like that she drew it. I don't like that she drew it *to you*. I don't like that you're telling it now, like this, out loud, with him writing it down." He jerked his chin at Chronicler. "Some things shouldn't be said, Reshi. Some things shouldn't be written."

"And some things," the innkeeper said mildly, "have to be."

They looked at each other for a long moment. Something passed between them that Chronicler did not understand. Then Bast looked away. He stood up, abruptly, his chair scraping on the floor, and went to the window and stood looking out with his back to them and his arms folded tight across his chest.

The innkeeper watched him for a while. Then he sighed and turned back to Chronicler.

"He's worried about me," he said. "He's always worried about me. It's a very tiring habit. I've tried to break him of it, but he's stubborn."

"I can hear you," said Bast, without turning around.

"I know. That's why I said it." The innkeeper smiled faintly. "Where was I?"

"The Grey Man," said Chronicler. "In Imre. She said she'd be there until the end of the span."

"Yes." The innkeeper nodded slowly. "Yes. I went, of course. How could I not? I went the very next day." He paused, looking down into his cup. "And that was the beginning of it. The real beginning. Everything I'd done up to then—the Maer, the Adem, even Felurian—that was all just prologue. That was the stage being set. The real story started the day I walked into the Grey Man and asked her to teach me how to read her knots."

"Did she?"

The innkeeper looked up. For a moment, just a moment, his face changed. The bruises were still there, and the swollen eye, and the careful weariness of a man who has been waiting a long time. But something else showed through underneath, bright and sharp and hungry, like a blade catching the light.

"She did," said Kvothe. "God help us both. She did."

---

Outside, a cart rattled by on the road. Someone laughed. The noon bell rang faintly from the little church at the far end of the town, a thin cracked sound that the people of Newarre had long since stopped hearing.

At the window, Bast stood very still. He was watching the tree line at the edge of the fields, beyond the last of the houses. The smoke Chronicler had seen that morning was still there, a thin grey thread against the pale sky. It still leaned. Bast watched it for a long time, and his lips moved silently, as if he were counting.

Then he turned from the window and went back to the table and sat down in his backward chair, and when the innkeeper began to speak again, he listened without interrupting.

But his hands, folded on the back of the chair, were clenched so tight that the knuckles had gone white.
