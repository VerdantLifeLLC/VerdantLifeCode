# The Sithe

They came for me on the second day past Felurian's spring, in a place where the black trees gave way to birches.

I had seen the change coming for some time. The black trees had been growing thinner, and further apart, and the twilight had been growing brighter between them. Then, quite suddenly, there were no more black trees. There were only birches, white and slender and graceful, rising up into a sky that was paler and clearer than it had been in the Deep Wood. The ground beneath them was covered in soft grey moss, and the air smelled clean and cold and faintly sweet, like snow on the way.

It was beautiful. It was the most beautiful place I had seen in the Fae. And I knew, the moment I stepped into it, that I was being watched.

I did not see them. I did not hear them. But I felt them. The way you feel eyes on the back of your neck in a crowded room. The way you feel the stillness of a forest when a hawk is overhead.

I did not stop walking. I did not look around. I followed the silver string, and I kept my hands where anyone watching could see them, and I did not reach for my sword.

And I thought about the Cthaeh.

It was close. I could feel it. Somewhere to the east, off to my right, beyond the white birches. A kind of pressure on the air, like the pressure before a storm. A kind of weight. A kind of *attention*.

It knew I was here.

---

The first arrow struck the ground six inches in front of my left foot.

It was very fast, and very quiet. I did not hear it coming. I only heard the soft *thock* as it sank into the moss, and I looked down and saw it standing there, quivering. A long slender arrow, fletched with white feathers, with a shaft of pale grey wood.

I stopped.

I stood very still. I did not look around. I kept my hands at my sides, open and empty.

"I'm not here to go to the Tree," I said. My voice sounded very loud in the silent birch wood. "I'm only passing through. On the Moon's Road. North, to the mountains."

Nothing happened for a long time. Then a second arrow struck the ground six inches in front of my right foot.

"I know you can smell it on me," I said. "I know I've been there before. I know what that means to you." I took a breath. "I'm not going to fight you. I'm going to stand here. And if you want to shoot me, you can. But I'd like the chance to tell you why I'm here first."

Silence. The birches stood white and still around me. The silver string floated in the air ahead of me, pointing north.

Then they came.

---

There were five of them. They came out of the birches without a sound, one from each direction, and they stopped in a circle around me, ten paces away, with arrows nocked and bows half drawn.

They were tall. Taller than any man I had ever seen, taller than Felurian, taller even than the Sleeper. They were slender and graceful, with long limbs and long fingers and long pale faces. Their skin was the grey-white of birch bark. Their hair was silver, and hung down their backs in long braids. Their eyes were the color of the twilight sky.

They wore armor of some grey metal that shimmered like water, fitted so close to their bodies that it seemed to be part of them. Their bows were long and pale and elegant. Their arrows were white-fletched.

They did not look like warriors. They looked like statues. Like carvings of something too beautiful and too terrible to be real.

The one in front of me lowered her bow slightly. She was the tallest of the five, and her silver braids were bound with bands of grey metal, and there was something in her face that made me think she was very old. Older than Felurian. Older than the forest.

"You stink of the Tree," she said. Her voice was cold and clear, like water over stone. "We smelled you the moment you crossed the border. We have been following you since."

"I know. I felt you."

"Did you." She looked at me with her twilight eyes. "Then you know what we must do."

"I know what you think you must do."

A ripple went through the circle. The other four Sithe shifted, very slightly. Their bows came up a little.

The tall one did not move.

"Speak," she said. "Quickly. You have the time it takes me to draw this bow."

---

I told them.

I told them everything. I had never told anyone all of it before, not all at once, not in a single breath. But I told the Sithe. I stood in the center of their circle, in the white birch wood, with five arrows pointed at my heart, and I told them.

I told them about the Cthaeh, and what it had said to me, the first time. I told them about Denna and her patron. I told them about the Archives and the door, and the line, and what I was. I told them about Bredon and Ambrose and the king's hair. I told them about the Eolian, and the song, and Haliax. I told them about Selitos, and the mountains, and what I hoped to learn there.

I told them the truth. All of it. As fast as I could, as clearly as I could, holding nothing back.

The tall one listened without moving. Her bow stayed half drawn. Her eyes stayed on my face. When I finished, she was silent for a long time.

"The Thief's own blood," she said at last. "A namer. Walking the Moon's Road. Past the Grove."

"Yes."

"Do you know what you are asking?"

"I'm asking you to let me pass."

"You are asking us to let the Tree's poison walk free through the twilight. Toward the one place in all the world where that poison could do the most harm." Her voice was very cold. "You are asking us to let the key walk past the one who wants the door opened most."

"The Cthaeh doesn't want the door opened. It wants—"

"You do not know what it wants." She raised her bow, very slightly. "No one knows what it wants. No one has ever known. It sees every path the world might take, and it chooses, every time, the one that ends worst. That is all we know. That is all anyone knows." She paused. "And you have spoken to it. You carry its words in you. Every step you take, every choice you make, may be a step on its path. You cannot know. You will never know."

I looked at her. I did not say anything.

"That is why we kill those who have spoken to it," she said. "Not because they are evil. Not because they are wicked. Because they are *poisoned*. Because they carry its will inside them, like a seed, and they do not know it. And wherever they go, whatever they do, the seed grows."

"Then kill me," I said.

The circle went very still.

"If that's what you believe," I said. "If you think I'm poisoned, and that everything I do is the Cthaeh's will. Then kill me. Right now. And the door stays closed, and the key stays in Ambrose's tent, and Haliax never gets his wish." I looked at her. "But if you do—if you kill me—are you sure that's not what it wants?"

---

The tall one did not move. Her bow stayed where it was, half drawn, pointed at my heart. But something changed in her face. Something very small.

"Explain," she said.

"It told me about Denna's patron," I said. "It told me exactly what it knew would send me running back to the mortal world, looking for him. It knew I'd find Bredon. It knew I'd lose the box. It knew I'd end up here, on the Moon's Road, walking past its grove." I took a breath. "It knew you'd be waiting."

"And?"

"And it knew you'd want to kill me." I looked around the circle, at the five tall pale faces, the five white-fletched arrows. "What if that's the path? What if the worst ending is the one where the only person who can close the door for good dies here, in the birch wood, with a Sithe arrow in his heart? What if the Cthaeh brought me here so that *you* would kill me?"

Silence. Long and deep and cold.

"Or," said the tall one slowly, "what if that is exactly what it wants me to think?"

"It could be," I said. "I don't know. You don't know. No one knows." I spread my empty hands. "That's the whole problem, isn't it? Once it's spoken, you can never know which choice is yours and which is its. Every path might be the wrong one." I paused. "So all you can do is choose. And bear what comes after."

The tall one looked at me for a very long time.

"You speak like an Adem," she said at last.

"I was taught by one."

"Hm." Her twilight eyes were very sharp. "The Adem have a word for what you describe. For choosing without knowing."

"The Lethani."

"The Lethani." She was silent for a moment. "We have a word too. An older one. It means much the same." She lowered her bow. Not all the way. But a little. "I will think on this."

---

That was when we heard the screaming.

It came from the east. From beyond the birches, off to our right. From the direction of the Grove. It was high and thin and desperate, the scream of something caught and terrified, and it went on and on.

The five Sithe turned their heads toward it, all at once, like hounds.

"Another," said one of them. A younger one, by the look of him, with shorter braids. "Near the Grove. Close. Too close."

The tall one looked at me. Then at the east. Then at me again.

"Stay," she said. "Do not move. If you move, we will kill you."

And the five of them were gone, into the birches, toward the screaming.

I stood there alone in the white wood. The silver string floated in the air ahead of me, pointing north. The screaming went on and on.

I did not stay.

---

I have thought about that choice a great deal, over the years. Whether I should have stayed. Whether it was my choice at all, or the Cthaeh's. Whether the screaming was a trap, set by the Tree to pull me toward its grove, and I walked into it like a fool.

I do not know. I will never know. That is the nature of the thing.

But I heard someone screaming, and I went. Because I am who I am, and I could not have done otherwise. And because I have heard a great many people scream in my life, and I have come too late to almost all of them.

I did not want to come too late again.

---

I ran east through the birches, following the sound. I ran with the shaed billowing around me and my good hand on Caesura's hilt and the silver string tugging at my wrist, trying to pull me back north. The screaming grew louder. So did the pressure on the air, the weight, the attention. The closer I came, the harder it pressed on me, until it was like running into a strong wind.

I did not look toward the Grove. I did not let myself. I kept my eyes on the ground in front of me, and I held Fela's stone so tight in my fist that its edge cut into my palm, and I repeated its long slow *no* over and over in my head. *No. No. No.*

And then I came over a low rise and saw them.

The five Sithe were standing in a half-circle at the bottom of a shallow dell, with their bows drawn. In front of them, at the bottom of the dell, there was a net.

It was a strange net. Silver and fine as spider silk, and it glowed faintly, like the string on my wrist. It was stretched between two birch trees, and it was full of something that was thrashing and screaming.

A young man. Or something that looked like a young man. Slender and dark-haired, with a fine-boned face twisted in terror. He was tangled in the silver net so thoroughly that he could barely move, and every time he struggled, the net tightened, and he screamed again.

His eyes were blue. A very deep blue, like the sky just after sunset.

And his feet, where they stuck out of the net, were not feet. They were hooves. Small and cloven and delicate, like the hooves of a deer.

"He was going to the Grove," said the tall Sithe. Her voice was very cold. "We have been watching him for three days. He came from the south, from the Twilight Court. He came to ask the Tree a question." She drew her bow a little tighter. "He did not reach it. We caught him first." She paused. "But he was very close. Close enough to hear it."

"Did he?" I said. "Hear it?"

The tall Sithe looked at the young man in the net. He had stopped screaming. He was hanging there, breathing hard, staring at the five arrows pointed at him with his deep blue eyes.

"I don't know," she said. "I cannot tell. He was very close." She raised her bow. "It does not matter. He came to the Grove to ask it something. That is enough."

"Wait," I said.

---

She did not wait. None of them did. I saw their fingers tighten on their bowstrings. I saw the arrows come back to their full draw.

I did not think. I did not decide. My body moved on its own, the way it had in the garden with Bredon, and in the Cellarage with the Sleeper.

I stepped in front of the net.

Five arrows, pointed at my heart. Five pairs of twilight eyes, staring at me in astonishment.

"If you're going to shoot him," I said, "you'll have to shoot me first."

The tall Sithe stared at me. For the first time since I had met her, something showed on her face. It was not anger. It was not surprise. It was something else. Something that looked almost like fascination.

"You would die for him?" she said. "A stranger? A Fae princeling you have never seen before?"

"I'd rather not die at all," I said. "But yes. If you make me choose."

"Why?"

I looked at her. I thought about it.

"Because he's screaming," I said. "And I've heard too many people scream. And I'm tired of being too late."

The birch wood was silent. Behind me, in the net, the young man with the deer's hooves had gone very still.

The tall Sithe looked at me for a long, long time.

Then, slowly, she lowered her bow.
