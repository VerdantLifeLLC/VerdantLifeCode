# Haliax

It was not a shadow the way a shadow is cast by a candle. It was not the absence of light. It was something else. It was a place where light went to be forgotten.

It rose up out of the floor in the center of the Eolian, among the overturned tables and the splintered chairs and the pools of blue fire. It rose slowly, like smoke, like mist, like the dark that pools in the corners of a room at dusk. It was the shape of a man. Tall and broad-shouldered and very still. But there was no man inside it. Or if there was, he was so far away, so deep inside the dark, that he could not be seen.

The blue candles dimmed around it. The frost on the floor crept back from it, as if even the cold were afraid.

The Chandrian went still.

All of them, at once. Cyphus, with his dancing fire. Stercus, with his hands full of rust. Grey Mardou, a smudge of mist by the east wall. Pale Alenta, beside the withered flowers. Usnea, at the top of the cellar stairs, beside Stanchion's body. Cinder, at the edge of the stage, with his cold hand around my wrist.

They went still the way hounds go still when their master walks into the room.

"Ferule," said the shadow.

It was a voice. It came from the shadow, or from everywhere, or from nowhere. It was deep and quiet and very tired. It was the voice of a man who has been awake for ten thousand years.

Cinder let go of my wrist.

He did not want to. I saw that. His fingers loosened slowly, reluctantly, one by one. But they loosened. And he stepped back from the edge of the stage and bowed his head.

"Lord," he said.

---

The shadow turned toward me.

I cannot tell you how I knew it turned. There was no face to see, no eyes, nothing but the shape of a man made of forgetting. But I felt it. I felt its attention fall on me like a weight. Like the weight of the whole sky.

"So," said Haliax. "You are the one who sings."

I did not answer. I could not. My voice was gone, burned out of me by the song. My left arm hung numb and useless at my side. My whole body was shaking with cold and exhaustion and something else, something I did not have a name for.

"It was a good song," said Haliax. "The best I have heard in a very long time. Better than your father's. Better than any of the Singers'." He paused. "You put in the child. The boy in the fountain. No one has ever put in the child before."

I looked at the shadow. I made myself look at it, though every part of me wanted to look away.

"Why not?" I whispered. My voice was barely a breath.

"Because no one knew," said Haliax. "Only Selitos, and the child himself. And Selitos never told." The shadow seemed to shift, very slightly. "So the old storyteller is still alive. The last of them. I wondered."

I did not say anything.

"You should not have put him in," said Haliax quietly. "It makes the song truer. And I have had quite enough of truth."

---

On the first balcony, someone moved.

I looked up. Denna was standing at the rail, with her hands gripping it so tightly that her knuckles had gone white. She was staring down at the shadow in the center of the room. Her face was the color of chalk.

Beside her, in the shadows, Bredon stood leaning on his stick. He was not looking at the shadow. He was looking at me. And he was smiling.

The shadow did not look up. But it said, "Is that the girl? The one who sings the other song?"

"Yes, my lord," said Bredon. His voice carried clearly across the silent room. "That is Denna."

"Hm." The shadow was quiet for a moment. "Her song is very beautiful."

"Thank you, my lord."

"It is not true."

"No, my lord. That is rather the point."

"Yes." The shadow sighed. It was a sound like wind through a ruined city. "Yes. I suppose it is." It turned back to me. "And yet. I find I prefer his."

Bredon's smile faltered. Just slightly. Just for a moment.

"My lord?"

"His is true," said Haliax. "It hurts. I had forgotten what it was like, to hear it sung true. To hear Lyra's name in a voice that did not lie." The shadow was very still. "It hurts very much."

No one spoke. No one moved. The blue candles burned without flickering.

"Every time the true song is sung," said Haliax, "I remember. Every time. It is the curse. Selitos made it so. I cannot forget. I cannot rest. I cannot die. And every time someone sings it true, I remember it all again, as if it were yesterday." The voice was very quiet. "Your parents sang it. Your father's song was very good. Very close. I remembered, when I heard it. I remembered everything."

He was silent for a long moment.

"That is why they died," said Haliax. "Not because the song was dangerous. Not because it made the curse stronger. Because it hurt. And I wanted it to stop."

---

I stared at the shadow. I could not speak. I could not move.

"You understand now," said Haliax. "Don't you? What I am. What I want." The shadow seemed to lean toward me. "I want it to stop. All of it. The remembering. The grief. The endless weight of what I did and what was done to me. I want to rest." A pause. "I want to die."

"And for that," I whispered, "you'd end the world."

"The world ended for me a very long time ago," said Haliax. "In a white city. In the arms of a woman who would not wake." The voice was very gentle. "What is one more ending?"

---

On the balcony, Denna made a sound.

It was a small sound. A gasp. I looked up. She was clutching her wrist, the one with the knot, and her face was twisted in pain. But not the pain I had seen in the garden. Something else.

The knot was glowing. Not red, this time. A pale, cold, silvery light, like the light Kilvin's candle had shown once, in my room at Anker's. Like moonlight on snow.

And it was loosening.

I saw it. Even from the stage, across the ruined room, I saw it. One loop of the knot slipping, just slightly. Just a little. The way the king's hair had loosened when I hummed to it.

Denna stared down at her wrist. Then she looked at me. Her eyes were enormous.

Bredon had seen it too. He turned toward her, and for the first time since I had known him, his face was not mild. It was not kind. It was something else entirely. Something old and cold and furious.

He reached for her wrist.

"Leave her," said Haliax.

Bredon stopped. His hand hung in the air, an inch from Denna's arm.

"My lord," he said. His voice was very controlled. "The song. His song. It has weakened the binding. If I do not—"

"I said leave her." The shadow did not raise its voice. It did not need to. "The binding was tied in a false story. The true story loosens it. That is the way of things." A pause. "You knew that when you tied it."

"I did not think he would sing it so well."

"No," said Haliax. "Neither did I." The shadow turned back toward me. "He is very good, your little key. Better than you told me."

Bredon lowered his hand. He looked down at me from the balcony, and his face was calm again, mild again. But his eyes were not.

"Yes, my lord," he said. "He is."

---

"Ferule," said Haliax.

Cinder stepped forward. "Lord."

"We are finished here. Gather the others."

Cinder looked at me. At my numb arm, and my shaking body, and my face wet with tears. He looked at me for a long moment with his black eyes.

"Lord," he said. "He sang the true song. He sang it here, in front of hundreds. It will spread. It will undo years of work." He paused. "Let me take his hands, at least. Let me take his voice. He does not need either to open the door."

"No."

"Lord—"

"He is needed," said Haliax. And the words fell into the silent room like stones into deep water. "Whole. With his hands and his voice and his name. He is needed, Ferule. As he is." The shadow was very still. "You will not touch him again. None of you will. Not until he stands before the door."

Cinder was silent. Then he bowed his head.

"As you say, lord."

---

They left the way they had come. Not through the doors. Simply gone, one by one, like candles snuffed. Cyphus first, his fire guttering out. Then Stercus, and Grey Mardou, and Pale Alenta. Usnea stood a moment longer at the top of the cellar stairs, looking down at Stanchion's body. Then she was gone too.

Cinder was the last. He stood at the edge of the stage and looked up at me.

"You think you've won something," he said softly. "You haven't. You've only made him sad. And when he's sad, he's patient. And when he's patient—" He smiled. "Well. You'll see."

He was gone.

Only the shadow remained. It stood in the center of the ruined room, among the wreckage, and it looked at me.

"You will come to the door," it said. "You know that. Whatever you tell yourself. You will come." A pause. "And when you do, I will be waiting. And I will thank you."

"Why?" I whispered. "Why thank me?"

"Because," said Haliax, "you will be giving me the only gift anyone has ever given me that I truly wanted."

And then it was gone too. The shadow sank back into the floor like water into sand. The darkness drained out of the air. The blue candles flickered, and guttered, and went out.

And in the sudden dark, I heard the roof beginning to burn.

---

It had caught somewhere near the top of the north wall, where one of the chandeliers had fallen. Ordinary fire, now. Yellow and orange and red. It licked along the old dry rafters, crackling, spreading. Smoke began to pour down into the room.

I tried to stand. My legs would not hold me. I fell off the stool onto the stage, and my lute fell beside me with a discordant jangle of strings.

I lay there on the stone, in the dark, with the fire crackling above me and the smoke thickening around me. I could not move. I was empty. Completely, utterly empty. The song had gone through me like fire through dry grass, and there was nothing left. Not my voice. Not my strength. Not my thoughts.

Not my name.

I lay there on the stage and I tried to remember who I was, and I could not. I knew there was a word for it. A name. The shape of me. But it was gone. The song had pushed it out. There was only a thin empty shell, lying on a stone floor in a burning building, with the smoke coming down.

*Hold onto something*, someone had said. Someone. I could not remember who. *Something small.*

My right hand moved. It moved on its own, slowly, the way a sleeping man's hand moves. It found my chest. It found something there, under my shirt. Something small and light, on a thread around my neck.

A knot.

I held it. I did not know what it was. I did not know why it mattered. But I held it, and I felt it. The faint tightening in the air. The sense of something reaching out and catching hold.

*Road*, it said. *Promise. A story told many times, and changes in the telling.*

And beneath that, woven into it so fine I had never noticed, one more knot. A very small one. It said a name.

Not my name. Hers.

I held it, and I remembered her. Dark hair, and a laugh like a bell, and hands that knew how to tie a story into a cord. I remembered sitting with her in the Grey Man, learning her secret language. I remembered her face, looking at me across a crowded room.

And remembering her, I remembered the one who had loved her.

*Kvothe*, I thought. *My name is Kvothe.*

It came back. Slowly. Like water filling a cup. My name, and my self, and everything I was. It came back into the empty shell, and filled it, and I was myself again.

---

Hands found me in the smoke. Strong hands, pulling at my arms. Voices shouting. I heard Sim, and Wil, and Devi. I heard Deoch, roaring orders. I felt myself lifted, carried. My lute banged against my legs; someone had picked it up.

We went out through the north door, into the night, into cold clean air. They laid me down on the cobblestones of the street. People were everywhere. Crowds, pressed back against the buildings across the street, staring at the Eolian as it burned.

I turned my head and looked.

The roof was a sheet of flame. It roared up into the night sky, orange and red and gold, throwing sparks high into the air. The smoke billowed up black against the stars. The heat of it was like a hand pressing against my face, even from across the street.

But the walls stood.

The stone walls of the Eolian stood firm and straight, with the fire raging above them. They did not crack. They did not fall. They held, stubborn and patient, saying their long slow *no* to the fire.

And down in the cellar, I knew, a girl with dark hair was still kneeling in the dark with her hands pressed flat against the foundations, asking them to stand.

"Fela," I croaked. "Fela's still inside."

Wil was kneeling beside me. He looked at me, and then at the burning building, and his face went grey.

He stood up and ran back into the fire.
