# Breaking

We left the garden the way I had come in. There was no other way; the green door in the lane had no handle on the outside, and it was locked from the inside, and I did not want to go through the door Bredon had used. So I made a stirrup of my hands and boosted Denna up the ivy to the top of the wall, and then I climbed up after her, and we dropped down together into the dark lane on the other side.

She landed badly. She had been cradling her wrist, and she could not catch herself properly, and she stumbled and went down on one knee on the cobblestones. I helped her up. She did not thank me. She did not say anything at all.

We walked. Neither of us said where we were going. We simply walked, through the dark streets of the high city, past the great houses behind their walls, past the gardens and the fountains and the statues. The lamplighters had finished their rounds, and the streets were lit with soft gold pools of light, and between the pools there was darkness.

At last we came to the edge of the cliff. There was a low wall there, and beyond it nothing: only the long drop down to the low city, and the lights of the low city spread out below like a scattering of coals, and beyond them the black ribbon of the river and the dark plain stretching away to the horizon.

Denna sat down on the wall. She did not seem to care how close she was to the edge. I sat down beside her.

For a long time we sat there in silence, looking out over the dark.

---

"Let me see your wrist," I said at last.

She hesitated. Then she held it out.

The cord had stopped glowing. It lay against her skin, thin and dark and almost invisible, tied in its small complicated knot. Beneath it, her skin was red and angry, as if it had been burned. I touched the cord very lightly with one finger. It was warm.

I tried to read the knot. I had learned a great deal from Denna, in those days at the Grey Man, and I had learned more on my own since, puzzling over the Dellers' cloth. But this knot was beyond me. It was so small and so tight and so intricately woven that I could not even see where it began and ended. It was like trying to read a word written in a language made of shadows.

But I could feel it. The tightening in the air. The sense of something reaching out and catching hold.

It held. It held very, very hard.

"Can you take it off?" I asked.

"No. I've tried." Her voice was flat and tired. "I've tried everything. I tried to untie it, and I couldn't find the ends. I tried to cut it, and the knife slid off as if the cord were made of iron. I tried to burn it, once. The candle went out." She took her wrist back and held it against her chest. "It doesn't come off. It never will. Not unless he takes it off. Or unless I die."

"There has to be a way."

"There isn't."

"There's always a way. Everything that's tied can be untied." I thought about it. "Elodin might know. The Master Namer. He knows things about bindings that nobody else does. If I could get you to the University—"

"You've been expelled."

"Then I'll bring him here. I'll write to him. I'll—"

"Kvothe." She put her good hand on my arm. "Stop. Please."

I stopped.

"You heard what he said," she said quietly. "I'll finish the song. I have no choice. If I try to stop, if I try to run, if I try to tell anyone—" She held up her wrist. "You saw what happened. That was just a warning. A little squeeze. He can do worse. Much worse." She shivered. "He told me once, when he first tied it, that it could stop my heart. Just like that. If I ever betrayed him." She looked at me. "I thought he was joking. He was smiling when he said it. He's always smiling."

I did not say anything. I did not know what to say.

"So I'll finish the song," Denna went on. "And I'll sing it. And the lie will grow weaker, and the curse will grow weaker, and one day, maybe, the Thief will come out from behind his door and break it." She laughed, a small broken sound. "And the world will end. And it will be my fault."

"It won't be your fault."

"Whose fault will it be, then?"

"His."

"He'll say it was mine. He'll say I chose it. He'll say I could have stopped at any time, if I'd been willing to die." She looked out over the dark plain. "And he'll be right. I could have. I could still."

I went very cold.

"Denna," I said. "Don't."

"I'm not going to." She smiled, faintly, without looking at me. "I'm too much of a coward. I've always been a coward. I've spent my whole life running away from things, because running is easier than standing still and letting them hit you." She was quiet for a moment. "But I thought about it. Just now, in the garden, when he let go of my wrist. I thought about how easy it would be. Just stop the song. Just stop, and let the knot do what it does, and be done."

"Please don't."

"I said I'm not going to." She turned and looked at me. Her eyes were wet. "You'd never forgive me. And I couldn't stand that. I couldn't stand you not forgiving me."

---

We sat on the wall at the edge of the cliff for a long time. The moon rose, a thin crescent, pale and sharp as a sickle. It cast very little light. The city below us was dark and silent except for the distant slow thunder of the mill wheels.

At some point, Denna leaned against me. I put my arm around her. She fit there, against my side, as if she had been made to fit there. Her hair smelled of pear blossoms.

"Tell me something," she said. "Something true. Something that isn't about any of this."

I thought about it. Then I told her about the Adem. Not the fighting, not the Ketan or the Lethani. The small things. The way they eat in silence, and talk with their hands, and laugh with their whole bodies when they laugh at all, which is not often. The way the children run wild on the hillsides in summer, brown as nuts, and the grown-ups pretend not to see them. The way Vashet had scolded me for singing, and then later, when she thought no one was listening, had hummed one of my songs to herself while she sharpened her sword.

Denna laughed at that. A real laugh, soft and surprised.

"You made a warrior woman hum your song," she said.

"I'm very persuasive."

"You're very *something*." She tilted her head up and looked at me. Her face was very close to mine in the dark. "Tell me another."

I told her about Auri. Not her name, and not the ring. Just the little things. The way she named everything she saw, and gave everything a personality. The way she called cheese *brave* and apples *patient*. The way she had stood on the roof of the Mains with a candle, the morning I left, and watched me go.

"She sounds lovely," said Denna softly.

"She is."

"Do you love her?"

"Not like that." I thought about it. "Like a sister. Like someone who needs to be kept safe."

"Everyone you love needs to be kept safe," said Denna. "Have you noticed that? You collect us. Lost things. Broken things." She was quiet for a moment. "Is that what I am to you? A lost thing?"

"No."

"Then what?"

I did not answer right away. I looked down at her face in the dark. At her eyes, and her mouth, and the small crease between her brows that appeared when she was worried.

"You're the only thing I've never been able to find," I said. "Even when you're right beside me."

She looked at me for a long time. Then she reached up, with her good hand, and touched my face. Very lightly. The way she had touched my scar, all those months ago, outside the Eolian.

And then she kissed me.

---

I will not tell you about that night. Some things are not for stories. Some things belong only to the people who lived them, and to no one else.

I will tell you only that it was the first time, and the only time, and that afterward we lay together in my bed in the Maer's palace, in the dark, with the thin moonlight coming through the window and falling across the floor, and she slept with her head on my chest and her wounded wrist cradled between us. I did not sleep. I lay awake and listened to her breathing and felt her heart beating against my ribs, and I thought that I had never been so happy, and I had never been so afraid.

I thought about Bredon. About his mild kind face and his flat cold eyes. About the tak board, and the shape of the door.

I thought about the box, beneath my pillow. I could feel it there, a hard warm lump beneath my head, humming its long slow no.

I thought about the knot on Denna's wrist, and what it could do.

And sometime near dawn, I think, despite everything, I slept.

---

When I woke, she was gone.

I knew it before I opened my eyes. I knew it the way you know, waking, that it has snowed in the night: by the particular quality of the silence. The bed beside me was cold. The room was empty. The grey light of early morning was coming through the window, and it fell across the floor where her clothes had been, and there was nothing there now.

I lay there for a long moment, not moving. Not letting myself think.

Then I reached under my pillow.

The box was gone.

---

I sat up. I looked under the pillow, and under the other pillow, and under the bed. I looked in my pack, and my trunk, and the wardrobe, and behind the curtains. I looked in every corner of every room. I knew it was useless. I did it anyway.

The box was gone.

And on the table by the window, where I would be sure to see it, there was a small length of dark cord, tied in a knot.

I picked it up with shaking fingers. I held it up to the light.

It was a complex knot, looped and twisted in a pattern that made my eyes ache. But I could read it. She had made sure I could read it. She had tied it slowly and carefully, using only the knots she had taught me herself, in the Grey Man, on those long afternoons.

*Forgive.* That was the first knot. *Keep.* That was the second. *Safe.* The third. And at the heart of it, holding it all together, a knot I had only seen once before: in the center of the cord she wore in her hair, and in the little knot she had left me when she went away from Imre.

*A story that is told many times and changes in the telling.*

And beneath it, very small, one more knot. One I had never seen. It took me a long time to puzzle it out. It was made of pieces I knew, put together in a way I did not. *Door*, and *no*, and *hand*, twisted around each other so tightly that they became something new.

I sat on the edge of the bed with the cord in my hands and turned it over and over, and at last I understood.

*Without the key, the hand cannot open the door.*

---

She had taken it to protect me.

I sat there in the grey light and understood that, all at once, and the understanding was like a knife in my chest. She had listened to Bredon in the garden. *You'll go to the door. You won't mean to. But you'll go.* She had lain awake beside me in the dark, the way I had, and she had thought about it. About the box under my pillow. About what I was, and what I could do, and what Bredon wanted.

And she had decided that the only way to keep me from opening the door was to make sure I could not.

She had taken the key. Not to give it to him. I knew that, the way I knew her face. She had taken it to hide it. From him, and from the Seven, and from me. She had taken it somewhere she thought none of us would ever find it.

She had done it because she loved me.

And she had not asked. She had not trusted me to make the choice myself. She had made it for me, the way Bredon made choices for her, and she had gone away in the night, the way she always went away, and left me a knot to say she was sorry.

I sat there on the edge of my bed and held the cord in my hands and I did not know whether to laugh or weep or scream. So I did nothing. I sat very still, the way Vashet had taught me, and I let it all rise and pass.

It did not pass. It rose and rose and did not stop rising.

---

I went down to the Mill Race. Of course I did. Orla the landlady met me at the door with a face like thunder.

"She's gone," she said. "Paid her bill in the middle of the night and went off with her harp and her bags. Didn't say where." She looked at me with narrowed eyes. "What did you do to her?"

"Nothing."

"Nothing. She came in last night looking like a ghost, and went out again, and came back an hour before dawn looking worse, and packed up everything she owned, and was gone before the cock crowed." Orla folded her arms. "If that's nothing, I'd hate to see something."

I did not answer. I turned away and walked out into the street.

I went to the garden with the green door. I climbed the wall again, in broad daylight, not caring who saw. The garden was empty. The table beside the black pool was bare. The tak board was gone. The lantern hung dark on its branch. There was no sign that anyone had ever been there at all.

I went back to the palace and asked Stapes where Bredon's rooms were. He told me. I went there. They were empty too. Swept clean. The bed stripped. The wardrobe bare. A servant I did not know was polishing the floor, and when I asked her where the gentleman who had lived here had gone, she looked at me blankly and said that the rooms had been empty for a month.

I stood in the empty room, in the late morning light, and I understood.

He had gone. They had both gone. He had played his stones and made his moves and set his shape upon the board, and then he had simply stepped away, the way he always did, leaving me to stare at the pattern and try to understand what I had lost.

And the box was gone too. The key. Somewhere out there in the world, in Denna's hands, being carried to a hiding place I did not know.

And Bredon had a cord around her wrist that could stop her heart.

I stood in that empty room and I realized, slowly, with a horror that crept up my spine like cold water, that Denna had not hidden the key from Bredon at all.

She had only moved it from a place he could not reach, to a place he could.

It was the most beautiful move I had ever seen. And I had not even seen him make it.
