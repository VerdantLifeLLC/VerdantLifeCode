# Tarbean

I smelled Tarbean before I saw it.

It came to me across the water on the third morning out from Hallow's End, while I was standing at the rail watching the grey coast slide by. A thin sour thread of it on the wind. Smoke and sewage and rotting fish, tar and brine and the hot rank breath of a hundred thousand people packed too close together. It was faint at first, so faint I thought I had imagined it. Then it grew stronger. And then, all at once, it was everywhere, filling my nose and my mouth and the back of my throat, and I had to grip the rail with both hands to keep from being sick.

It was not the smell that did it. I had smelled worse. I had smelled the Deller farm, and the king's bedchamber, and the bottom of a ravine in the Hollow Hills. It was the memory. Every breath of that smell was a door opening, and behind every door there was a boy of twelve, starving, shivering, alone, curled up in the rain on a rooftop with his arms wrapped around a broken lute.

I had spent three years in Tarbean. Three years that I do not like to talk about, even now. I have told you some of it. I have told you about Trapis and his cellar full of lost children, and about the Tehlin priests and their bread, and about Pike and his friends and what they did to my lute. I have told you about the sea of rooftops where I slept, and the gutters where I ate, and the long cold nights when I did not think I would live to see morning.

I have not told you all of it. There are things about Tarbean that I have never told anyone. Things I did, and things that were done to me. Things I have locked away so deep that even I do not go looking for them.

And now I was going back.

---

The ship came into harbor a little after noon. Tarbean's harbor is a great sprawling mess of piers and wharves and jetties, built out into the water without plan or order over hundreds of years, so that the whole waterfront looks like a mouthful of broken teeth. Ships of every size crowd the piers, from tiny fishing smacks to great three-masted merchantmen from Yll and Atur and the Small Kingdoms. The noise is incredible. Gulls screaming, men shouting, chains rattling, the thud and creak of cargo being loaded and unloaded. It never stops. Not day or night. Not ever.

I walked down the gangplank onto the pier with my lute on my back and my sword at my hip and my pack over my shoulder. My legs were unsteady after three days at sea. The pier seemed to sway beneath me. I stood for a moment at the foot of the gangplank, letting the noise and the smell wash over me, and I waited to feel something.

I did not feel anything. That was the strange thing. I had braced myself for fear, or grief, or anger. I had expected to be overwhelmed. But I stood there on the pier in Tarbean, in the middle of the noise and the stink and the crowds, and I felt nothing at all. Only a kind of distant, hollow calm, like the calm at the center of a storm.

I did not trust it. But I was grateful for it.

---

I went to Waterside first.

I do not know why. It was not where I needed to go. Skarpi, if he was still alive, if he was still in Tarbean at all, would not be in Waterside. But my feet took me there anyway, through the crowded streets and the stinking alleys, down toward the part of the city where the poor lived and died.

It had not changed. That was the first thing I noticed. It had been six years since I left, and in six years, Waterside had not changed at all. The same crooked streets. The same leaning houses. The same beggars on the same corners, or ones so like them that there was no difference. The same children, thin and dirty and hollow-eyed, darting through the crowds like fish, their small quick hands reaching for purses.

I saw one of them reach for mine. A boy of about ten, with a filthy face and a shock of pale hair. He came at me from the side, quick and quiet, the way I had been taught to come at marks when I was his age. I felt his fingers brush the strings of my purse.

I caught his wrist.

He froze. He looked up at me, and his eyes were enormous in his thin face, and I saw the terror in them. The terror of a child who knows exactly what happens to pickpockets who get caught.

I did not hurt him. I held his wrist, very gently, and I looked at him.

"You're too slow," I said. "You came at me from the left, which is good, but you looked at my purse before you touched it. Don't look. Never look. Look at their face, or at something past them. Your hand knows where the purse is. Let it find it on its own."

He stared at me. His mouth fell open.

I let go of his wrist. Then I reached into my purse and took out a silver talent and pressed it into his hand.

"Go to the Tehlins on Hillside," I said. "The big church with the iron doors. Tell them you want bread, and give them this, and they'll feed you for a month."

He looked at the coin in his hand. He looked at me. Then he turned and ran, and was gone into the crowd before I could say anything else.

I stood there for a long moment, looking at the place where he had been. Then I went on.

---

Trapis's cellar was still there.

I found it at the end of a narrow dead-end alley, down a short flight of stone steps, behind a door that was just as battered and ill-fitting as it had been when I was a child. I stood at the top of the steps for a long time, looking at that door, not moving.

Then I went down and knocked.

For a long time, nothing happened. Then I heard footsteps, slow and shuffling, and the door creaked open, and a face looked out at me.

It was not Trapis. It was a young woman, perhaps twenty, thin and plain, with lank brown hair and tired eyes. She was wearing a stained apron over a patched dress. There was a baby on her hip, and it was crying.

"Yes?" she said.

"I'm looking for Trapis."

Her face changed. Something went out of it. "Trapis," she said. "Oh." She looked at me more closely. "Did you know him?"

*Did.* I felt something cold settle in my chest.

"Yes," I said. "A long time ago. When I was a boy."

She looked at me for a long moment. Then she stepped aside.

"Come in," she said. "He's in the back."

---

He was dying.

It was the second time in a month that I had sat at the bedside of a dying man, and I remember thinking how strange that was. How the world seemed to keep putting me in the same place, again and again, as if it were trying to teach me something I refused to learn.

But Trapis was not a king. He lay on a narrow cot in the back of the cellar, beneath a thin grey blanket, in the same room where he had always kept the sickest of the children. There was a candle burning on a stool beside him. The room smelled of sickness and of something else, something clean and sharp, like vinegar.

He was very small. Smaller than I remembered. He had always been a small man, stooped and thin, with a round bald head and gentle hands. But now he was tiny, shrunken, a little bundle of bones under the blanket. His skin was grey and papery. His breathing was shallow and slow.

But his eyes were open. And when I came into the room and knelt beside his cot, they found my face, and they knew me.

"Well," he said. His voice was very faint. "Well, well. Look who's come back."

"Hello, Trapis."

"Hello, lad." He smiled. It was the same smile. Exactly the same. Gentle and tired and utterly without judgment. "You've grown."

"A little."

"More than a little." He looked at me, his eyes moving slowly over my face, my clothes, my sword. "You look well. You look—" He paused, searching for the word. "Strong. You look strong."

"I'm not, really."

"You are. I can see it." He reached out one thin hand from beneath the blanket and laid it on my arm. His fingers were cold and very light, like the touch of a moth. "I always knew you would be. Even when you were small. Even when you were sick. There was something in you. Something that wouldn't give up."

I did not trust myself to speak. I nodded.

"Where have you been?" he asked. "All this time?"

So I told him. I sat beside his cot in that dim room with his hand on my arm, and I told him everything. The University, and the Archives, and Elodin. Denna. The Maer and Meluan. The Adem, and the Fae, and the Chandrian. The king's bedchamber, and the knots, and Harl dead in the laundry door.

I had not told anyone all of it before. Not all at once, not like that. But I told Trapis. And he listened, the way he had always listened, with his head tilted a little to one side and his gentle eyes fixed on my face. He did not interrupt. He did not ask questions. He simply listened, the way you would listen to a child telling you about a bad dream.

When I was done, he was quiet for a long time.

"That's a great deal," he said at last. "A great deal for one young man to carry."

"Yes."

"Are you tired?"

The question took me by surprise. I thought about it.

"Yes," I said. "Very."

"I thought so." He patted my arm gently. "It's all right to be tired, you know. Even heroes get tired. Especially heroes." He smiled faintly. "Especially the ones who don't think they're heroes at all."

"I'm not a hero, Trapis."

"No? What are you, then?"

I did not know how to answer that. I sat beside his cot and looked at his thin grey face, and I did not know what to say.

"You're a boy who came back," said Trapis softly. "That's what you are. You went out into the world and you did all these great and terrible things, and then you came back here. To this cellar. To a dying old man who fed you soup when you were small." He closed his eyes. "That's not nothing. That's not nothing at all."

---

I stayed with him that night. I sat beside his cot and held his hand, and the young woman, whose name was Mae, brought me a bowl of thin soup and a heel of bread, and I ate it sitting on the floor. It tasted exactly the way it had tasted when I was twelve. Salty and weak and faintly of onions. It was the best thing I had eaten in months.

The children came to look at me. There were a dozen of them in the cellar, maybe more. Thin, pale, silent children, some of them sick, some of them simply lost. They stood in the doorway and stared at me with their enormous eyes, and when I smiled at them, they did not smile back. They only stared.

I took out my lute and played for them. Softly, so as not to disturb Trapis. I played "Tinker Tanner" and "Jenny Nettle" and a silly song about a pig who wanted to be a king. And slowly, one by one, the children came into the room and sat down on the floor around me and listened. And by the end, one or two of them were smiling. And one very small girl, with a wild tangle of black hair and a bandage around her head, had crept into my lap and fallen asleep there.

Trapis woke once, near midnight, and looked at me sitting on the floor with my lute and the sleeping child and the ring of children around me.

"There," he said softly. "You see? That's what you are."

Then he closed his eyes again.

He died a little before dawn. Very quietly. I was holding his hand, and I felt it go still, and I looked up and saw that he had stopped breathing. His face was peaceful. He looked like he was sleeping.

Mae came in and saw, and she sat down on the floor beside me and wept. The children woke and saw, and some of them wept too, and some of them only stared. The little girl in my lap did not wake. I held her and let her sleep.

I did not weep. Not then. I sat there with Trapis's hand in mine until the grey light of dawn came down the stairs and through the door, and then I laid his hand on his chest and stood up and gave the sleeping girl to Mae.

"What will you do?" I asked her. "Now that he's gone."

She looked at me with red eyes. "Keep going," she said. "What else? The children still need feeding. The sick ones still need looking after." She shook her head. "He'd have wanted that. He always said it didn't matter who did the work, so long as someone did it."

I took out my purse. The Maer's purse. I opened it and poured everything that was in it into her hands. Silver and gold, talents and marks and royals, more money than she had probably ever seen in her life.

She stared at it. Then at me.

"This is too much," she said.

"It's not enough," I said. "It'll never be enough. But it's what I have."

She looked at me for a long time. Then she closed her hands around the coins and held them against her chest.

"Thank you," she whispered.

"Don't thank me. Thank him." I looked at Trapis, lying still on his cot. "He saved my life. More than once. I never paid him back."

"You just did," said Mae.

---

I left the cellar as the sun was rising over Tarbean. I climbed the stone steps into the narrow alley and stood there for a moment, blinking in the grey morning light.

I had no money now. Not a penny. I had given it all away. I had my lute, and my sword, and the clothes on my back, and the few things in my pack. Kilvin's candle. The scrap of the Dellers' cloth. Fela's stone eye. Denna's knot. The page from the Book of Watches.

It was very little. But it was more than I had had the last time I walked these streets.

I turned and began to walk toward Hillside. Toward the part of the city where the Tehlins kept their great churches, and the courts of justice, and the prisons where they kept men who told the wrong sort of stories.

I was going to find Skarpi.

And this time, I promised myself, I would listen to the end.
