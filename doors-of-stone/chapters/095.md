# Newarre

It took us two days to reach it, north and east through the rolling wheat country, along roads that grew smaller and emptier with every mile.

Newarre, when we came to it, was exactly as Skarpi had said. A town so small and so quiet that no one had ever heard of it. A few dozen houses clustered around a crossroads, with a smithy and a mill and a little stone church with a cracked bell. A handful of shops. A green, with a well in the middle of it. Fields of wheat and barley running away on every side, gold in the late summer sun, to a line of dark woods on the horizon.

And at the edge of the town, where the two roads crossed, there was a stone.

It was grey. Tall and grey and very old, taller than a man, standing alone at the corner where the roads met. It was worn smooth by the wind and the rain and the years, so smooth that it looked less like a stone than like something that had grown there, the way a tree grows. There was lichen on it, grey-green and orange, in patches. There were marks on it, very faint, that might once have been carvings. They were too worn to read.

I stopped the cart in the road beside it. I looked at it.

I did not hear anything. I did not know how to listen. But I felt something. Standing there, in the road, in the sun. A kind of stillness. A kind of quiet. As if the whole world had taken a breath and was holding it.

Beside me, on the seat of the cart, Bast had gone pale.

"Oh," he said softly. "Oh, Reshi. It's so *loud*."

"I don't hear anything."

"No. You wouldn't." He was staring at the stone. His deep blue eyes were very wide. "It's saying no. Like the stone in your pocket. Like the mountain. But so much louder. So much older." He put his hands over his ears, as if that would help. "I can barely think. It's like standing next to a waterfall."

"Is that good?"

Bast looked at me. Then he looked back at the stone. Then he turned around on the seat and looked at the cart behind us. At the chest, under its canvas cover.

He was quiet for a long moment.

"I can't hear it," he said at last. His voice was full of wonder. "The chest. The name. I've been hearing it every night for a year. And now I can't hear it at all." He looked at me. "The stone's drowning it out."

---

The inn was on the other side of the crossroads, across the road from the waystone.

It was a long, low building of grey fieldstone, two stories tall, with a slate roof and a stable behind and a yard with a well in it. It had been a good inn, once. You could see that in the bones of it: the solid walls, the deep-set windows, the great stone chimney at one end. But it had been empty for three years, and it showed. The windows were shuttered and the shutters were cracked and peeling. The yard was overgrown with weeds. The stable door hung off one hinge. There was a sign over the door, but it had faded so badly that you could not read it.

I got down from the cart. I walked across the yard to the door. I pushed it open.

It creaked. Of course it creaked. Inside, it was dark and dusty and smelled of damp and mice and old smoke. There was a common room, long and low, with a great stone hearth at one end and a bar along one wall. There were tables and chairs, stacked in corners, covered in dust. There were bottles behind the bar, empty, covered in cobwebs. There was a staircase at the back, leading up.

I stood in the doorway and looked at it for a long time.

It was grey. Everything was grey.

But it was quiet. So quiet. The quietest place I had been since the chamber beneath the Lackless house. Quieter even than that. There, the quiet had been the silence after a scream. Here, it was the silence of a place where nothing had ever screamed at all.

Bast came up behind me. He stood in the doorway beside me and looked at the dusty common room.

"It's awful," he said.

"Yes."

"It's falling apart."

"Yes."

"There are *mice*, Reshi. I can hear them. In the walls."

"Yes."

Bast looked at me for a long moment.

"You want it," he said. "Don't you?"

I looked at the common room. At the dusty tables and the empty bottles and the cold hearth.

"Yes," I said.

---

I bought it the next day.

The widow's brother-in-law handled the sale. He was a big slow farmer named Graham, with a carpenter's hands and a face like a well-worn saddle. He came up from his farm on the east road when word got round that a stranger was asking about the old inn, and he stood in the dusty yard and looked at me and Bast and the cart and the mule, and he scratched his head.

"You want to buy it?" he said. "The inn?"

"Yes."

"Why?"

I thought about it. I did not have a good answer. So I gave him the only one I had.

"It's quiet," I said.

Graham looked at me for a long moment. Then he looked at the inn, with its peeling shutters and its broken stable door. Then he looked back at me.

"It's that," he said. "Nobody's going to argue with you there." He scratched his head again. "It needs a lot of work."

"I know."

"The roof leaks. Over the back bedrooms. And the chimney's cracked. And there's rot in the stable floor." He looked at me. "You know anything about fixing things?"

"Some."

"Hm." Graham looked me up and down. At my brown-stained hair, and my plain clothes, and my hands. He looked at my hands for a long time. "You've got good hands. Working hands. I can see that." He nodded slowly. "All right. I'll tell Mary. She'll be glad to be shed of it. She's been worrying about it for three years."

We agreed on a price. It was very low. Much lower than Skarpi's purse. I paid it in gold, and Graham looked at the gold in his hand and then at me, and did not ask any questions.

"What'll you call it?" he said, as he was leaving.

"What?"

"The inn. It needs a name. The old one was the Crossroads. But the sign's gone. Faded right off." He looked up at the blank board over the door. "You'll want a new one. I can carve it for you, if you like. I'm good with wood."

I looked at the blank sign. Then I looked across the road, at the tall grey stone standing at the corner of the crossroads, in the late afternoon sun.

"The Waystone," I said.

Graham followed my gaze. He looked at the stone for a long moment.

"The Waystone Inn," he said slowly. "Hm. That's a good name. Simple. Easy to remember." He nodded. "I'll carve it for you. Give me a span."

---

He carved it. He brought it up from his farm on a cart, a span later, and hung it over the door himself, on two iron hooks. It was a good sign. Oak, very solid, with the letters carved deep and painted black. *The Waystone Inn.* And beneath the letters, a picture. A tall grey stone, standing alone at a crossroads, with the sun behind it.

I stood in the yard and looked up at it for a long time.

"You don't like it," said Graham. He sounded disappointed.

"No," I said. "I do."

"You've got a funny way of showing it."

I looked at him. I thought about Bast, on the road, saying *you don't like anything. You don't feel anything.*

"I'm sorry," I said. "I'm not very good at showing things."

Graham looked at me for a long moment. Then he shrugged.

"That's all right," he said. "Nor am I. My wife says I've got a face like a board." He grinned. "Suppose that makes two of us."

---

We moved in that same day. Bast and I. We unloaded the cart and carried everything inside. It was not much. Our packs, and a few blankets, and some pots and pans, and the lavender a girl had given me on a roof in Imre, which I hung from a nail in the kitchen.

And the chest.

It was very heavy. Roah is very heavy. It took both of us to lift it, and we staggered under it as we carried it through the door and across the common room and up the narrow stairs. There was a room at the top of the stairs, at the front of the inn, with a window that looked out over the crossroads. Toward the waystone.

We put the chest there. At the foot of the bed. Where I could see it every night before I slept, and every morning when I woke.

Bast stood in the doorway and looked at it for a long time.

"Can you hear it?" I asked.

He was quiet for a moment. He tilted his head, listening.

"No," he said at last. "Not a thing. The stone's too loud." He looked at me. "It's safe, Reshi. For now. Nobody's going to hear it here."

I looked at the chest. At its dark smooth wood, and its two locks, and the small dark knot sunk into the grain between them.

I did not feel anything. I did not know what I should feel.

But I put my hand on the lid. Just for a moment. The wood was cool and smooth under my palm.

And I found, when I took my hand away, that I had been holding my breath.

---

That night, I sat alone in the common room of the Waystone Inn.

Bast had gone to bed, in a little room at the back. The mice were quiet. The fire was burning in the great stone hearth, the first fire it had seen in three years. It crackled and popped and threw long shadows across the dusty floor.

I sat at a table beside it with a rag and a pot of grease, and I polished the brass rail of the bar. I had taken it off, and brought it to the table, and I was polishing it. Slowly. Carefully. In small even circles.

It was very tarnished. It took a long time. But slowly, under the rag, it began to gleam.

I did not think about anything. I did not remember anything. I polished the rail, and the fire burned, and the inn was very quiet around me.

And somewhere, very far down, at the bottom of the hole where my name had been, something settled. The way a stone settles at the bottom of a pond. The way dust settles in an empty room.

It was not peace. Not exactly. It was the shape of peace. The ghost of it.

But it was something.

---

Outside, across the road, the waystone stood in the dark. Tall and grey and silent, saying its long slow *no* to the world.

And in the room at the top of the stairs, in a chest of roah wood, a name lay folded in a cord of dark hair, beside a lute and a box with no lid and a very bad poem.

And it was quiet. For the first time in a very long time.

It was quiet.
