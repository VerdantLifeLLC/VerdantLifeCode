# Cold

It began with the candles on the tables nearest the stage.

I saw it from the corner of my eye. A flicker. A change in the color of the light. The warm yellow glow that lit the edge of the stage went pale, and then paler, and then it was blue. A deep, cold, winter-dusk blue, the color of a bruise, the color of the candle in Kilvin's box on the night of the Deller farm.

Then the candles on the next ring of tables. And the next. And the next. A ripple of blue spreading outward from the stage like a stone dropped in a pond. Up the walls. Along the balconies. Into the great iron chandeliers that hung from the ceiling, until every flame in the Eolian was burning blue.

And the cold came.

It came all at once, like a door opening onto winter. My breath smoked in the air. Frost bloomed on the glasses on the tables, on the brass rails of the balconies, on the strings of my lute beneath my fingers. The wine in people's cups went cloudy and thick. Someone's breath caught in a gasp that turned to a white plume.

The crowd noticed. Of course they noticed. There was a rustle, a stir, a murmur that grew rapidly toward something louder. People turned in their seats to look at the candles. People stood up. Someone laughed nervously, and then stopped laughing.

I did not stop singing.

*You keep singing*, Skarpi had said. *No matter what. No matter who comes.*

I kept singing.

---

I sang the founding of the Amyr. Selitos gathering the survivors of the war, the warriors and the namers and the scholars. Giving them their oath and their burden. *For the greater good.* I sang it the way it was, grim and necessary and terrible, and the words came out of me in clouds of white breath.

And the first of them appeared.

He was standing on the first balcony, at the rail, not far from where Denna had gone. I had not seen him come. No one had. He was simply there, where a moment before there had been a lady in a green gown. He was tall and pale and very still, with a face like a knife. His hair was the color of frost. His eyes were black. Not dark. Black. Pure and depthless, like holes cut in the world.

Cinder.

I had seen him last when I was eleven years old, crouching before me in the firelight among the bodies of my family. He had not changed at all. Not a line on his face, not a hair on his head. He looked exactly as he had looked that night. He would look exactly the same, I understood, a thousand years from now.

He looked down at me from the balcony. And he smiled.

My voice did not falter. I want that written down. I looked up at the man who had murdered my mother and my father and everyone I had ever loved, and he smiled at me, and my voice did not falter. I kept singing.

---

Then the others came.

A woman in grey, on the second balcony, with a face so pale it was almost translucent, and around her, where she stood, the wood of the balcony began to go soft and dark and crumbling. *Usnea*, I thought. *Rot.*

A man on the main floor, near the north door, stocky and dark, and around him the iron of the door's hinges went red and flaking, the iron bands on the water barrels bled rust down the wood. *Stercus.*

A shape by the east wall that was hard to look at, a smudge of grey mist where a man should have been, and the air around it thickened and dimmed until the blue candles there were barely visible. *Grey Mardou.*

A woman near the stage, young and lovely and terrible, and the flowers that Threpe had set on the edge of the stage in honor of the evening withered and blackened as she passed them. *Pale Alenta.*

And one more. A man standing in the very center of the room, among the tables, with blue fire dancing along his arms and in his hair and in his open mouth when he smiled. *Cyphus.*

Six of them. Six of the Seven.

The crowd saw them.

---

I have heard a great many screams in my life. I have heard men scream in battle, and women scream in childbirth, and children scream in the night. But I have never heard anything like the sound the Eolian made when the crowd saw the Chandrian.

It was not one scream. It was hundreds. It rose up from every corner of the room at once, a great wordless howl of terror, and it shook the walls and rattled the glasses and drowned out everything else.

And the crowd broke.

They ran. All of them, all at once, in every direction. They surged toward the doors like water toward a drain. Tables went over. Chairs splintered. People fell and were trampled. The balconies shook with the stampede of feet.

And Stanchion's men were ready.

They stood at every door, and they did not run. They held the doors open and shouted, and they pulled people through, and they shoved people out into the street, and they went back in and pulled out more. Deoch stood at the main door with his oak staff, and he did not use it on anyone. He used it to hold back the crowd so that it did not crush itself in the doorway. He shouted in a voice like a trumpet, *slow, slow, one at a time, everyone out, everyone out*, and somehow, impossibly, they listened.

I saw all this from the stage, in pieces, in flashes, the way you see things in a lightning storm. I did not stop singing.

---

Stercus moved first.

He raised his hands, and the iron in the room answered him. The great chandeliers groaned on their chains as the links rusted through. The iron nails in the balcony supports bled red. I heard wood cracking, and a shriek of tearing metal, and one of the chandeliers began to fall.

It did not fall. It stopped in mid-air, swinging wildly, twelve feet above a table full of screaming students. I looked up and saw Wil on the second balcony with his hand outstretched and his face twisted in concentration. He had bound it. Somehow, in the chaos, he had found something to bind it to, and he was holding it up by main force of will.

His face was grey with effort. His arm was shaking. He could not hold it for long.

Below him, Sim was pulling students out from under the chandelier. He was shouting. I could not hear what. He grabbed one boy by the collar and hauled him out, and then a girl, and then another boy. When the table was empty, he looked up and shouted something at Wil, and Wil let go, and the chandelier crashed down onto the empty table in an explosion of splinters and blue fire.

I kept singing. I sang the Singers. Skarpi's order. The keepers of the true story, who would go out into the world and sing it wherever they went. I sang their oath. I sang it so that the Chandrian could hear it.

Cyphus heard it. He turned toward the stage, and the blue fire on his arms leapt up, and he threw it.

---

It came at me like a wave. A great rolling wall of cold blue flame, washing across the floor toward the stage, devouring tables and chairs as it came. I felt the heat of it and the cold of it at the same time, burning and freezing, and I knew that if it reached me, I would die.

It did not reach me.

A cloud of white smoke exploded in front of the stage, thick and choking and smelling of lime and sulfur. The blue flame hit it and hissed and faltered. Where it touched the smoke, it went dim and grey and sputtered out.

I looked up. On the first balcony, directly above me, Devi was leaning over the rail with her satchel open and her face fierce. She had another clay ball in her hand, the size of an apple, wrapped in waxed cloth. She threw it. It hit the floor at the edge of the stage and burst, and another cloud of white smoke billowed up, and the blue flame retreated from it like a dog from a whip.

"Keep singing, you idiot!" Devi shouted. "*Keep singing!*"

I kept singing.

---

Then the balconies began to fall.

It was Usnea. She had been walking slowly along the second balcony, her pale hands trailing along the rail, and everywhere she touched, the wood went soft and black and rotten. The rail. The floor. The great beams that held the balcony to the wall. It rotted away beneath her fingers like old bread, and the whole balcony began to sag.

There were still people on it. A dozen, maybe more, trapped by the crush at the stairs. They screamed as they felt the floor tilt beneath them. They clung to the rail, and the rail crumbled in their hands.

And beneath my feet, I felt the stone of the stage begin to hum.

It was very faint at first. A vibration, deep and low, rising up from somewhere far below. From the cellar. From the foundations. It grew louder. It spread outward through the floor, through the walls, up into the stone pillars that held up the balconies.

Fela.

She was down there, in the dark, with her hands pressed flat against the foundations of the Eolian. And she was asking the stone to stand.

The stone pillars under the second balcony stopped trembling. They held. The rotting wood above them sagged and cracked and crumbled, but the pillars held. They held the weight of the balcony by themselves, by stubbornness, by the long slow patient *no* of stone, and the people on the balcony scrambled across the sagging floor to the stairs and down and out.

Usnea stopped. She turned her pale face downward, toward the floor, toward the cellar. She tilted her head, as if listening.

Then she smiled, and began to walk toward the cellar stairs.

---

I could not stop her. I could not stop singing. I could only watch, with the song pouring out of me, as she drifted along the balcony toward the stairs that would take her down to where Fela knelt alone in the dark.

And then Stanchion was there.

I do not know where he came from. I had not seen him since the Chandrian appeared. But he came up the stairs to the second balcony like a bull, with a bucket of water in each hand, and he stepped between Usnea and the cellar stairs.

"Not in my place," he said. His voice was a roar. "Not in *my place*, you rotten bitch."

And he threw the water at her.

It did nothing. Of course it did nothing. Water is not a weapon against something like Usnea. It splashed over her and ran down her pale grey dress and dripped onto the floor, and where it touched the rotting wood, the wood hissed and bubbled.

But she stopped. She looked at him. She looked at this big bearded man with his empty buckets, standing between her and the stairs, and I saw something move across her face. Surprise, perhaps. Or curiosity. As if she had not expected anyone to stand in her way.

Then she reached out, very gently, and laid her pale hand on his chest.

I saw Stanchion's face change. I saw it go grey. I saw the color drain out of it, the way color drains out of a leaf in autumn. I saw his beard go white. I saw his big strong body shrink and wither, as if all the years he had lived and all the years he had left were being drawn out of him at once.

He did not fall. Not right away. He stood there with her hand on his chest, and he looked at her, and he said something. I could not hear what. But his lips moved, and she tilted her head, and something in her face changed again.

Then she took her hand away, and he fell.

---

I did not stop singing.

I want to be very clear about that. I want it written down, in plain ink on plain paper. I watched Stanchion die. I watched a man who had been kind to me, who had given me a place to play and a place to belong, who had built the Eolian from a stable with his own two hands, fall on the rotting boards of his own balcony with his beard gone white and his face gone grey.

And I did not stop singing.

It was the hardest thing I have ever done. Harder than anything that came before, and harder than almost anything that came after. Every part of me wanted to stop. To throw down my lute and run to him. To scream. To call the wind and tear Usnea apart. To do anything, anything at all, except sit on that stool and keep singing.

But Skarpi had told me. *If you stop in the middle, the song is broken. And a broken song is a lie.*

And I would not let Stanchion die for a lie.

So I sang. I sang the sealing of the doors. I sang the seven doors in seven places, and the keepers set to watch them. I sang the line of the Thief's daughter, bound to the keeping. I sang it with tears running down my face and my voice cracking and breaking and the song pouring through me like fire through dry grass.

And Usnea turned away from Stanchion's body, and looked down at me on the stage, and her face was very strange.

She did not go down to the cellar. She stood at the top of the stairs, with her pale hands at her sides, and listened.

---

Cinder came down from the first balcony.

He did not use the stairs. He simply stepped off the rail and drifted down, slowly, like a leaf falling, and landed on the floor of the Eolian among the wreckage of the tables. He walked toward the stage. His boots made no sound. The frost spread out from his feet across the floor as he walked, white and crackling.

He stopped at the edge of the stage. He looked up at me.

"Stop," he said.

His voice was soft. Reasonable. Exactly as I remembered it. It was the voice of a man asking you to pass the salt.

I did not stop.

"Stop singing, boy," Cinder said. "You know I can't kill you. But I can hurt you. I can hurt you very much." He smiled. "I can take your hands."

I looked down at him. At his black eyes and his frost-colored hair and his smile. I looked at him the way I had looked at him when I was eleven years old, crouching in the firelight.

I kept singing.

Cinder's smile faded. Something moved in his black eyes. Anger, perhaps. Or something older and colder than anger.

He reached up and took hold of my left wrist.

---

His hand was cold. Colder than anything I have ever felt. Colder than ice, colder than the bottom of a winter lake. It was the cold of the space between the stars. It went into my wrist and up my arm and into my chest, and I felt my heart stutter and my breath catch.

My fingers went numb on the strings. They stopped moving. The lute went silent.

But I did not stop singing.

I sang without the lute. I sang with nothing but my voice, cracked and broken and shaking with cold. I sang the last verse. The one Skarpi had taught me in the broken lighthouse, on the ninth day. The one that was not in any book, or any song, or any story anyone had ever told.

It was a very short verse. Only a few lines. It was about a little boy, hiding under a slab of white marble, listening to his city burn. And an old man with one eye, digging him out with his bare hands.

And what the boy said, when the old man asked him what he wanted to be.

*A singer*, I sang. *Like my mother. So that no one will forget.*

The last note left me. It went out into the Eolian, into the frost and the blue fire and the wreckage and the smoke. It went out through the open doors into the streets of Imre. It went out, I think, much farther than that.

And then I stopped.

---

The Eolian was silent.

Not quiet. Silent. The kind of silence that has weight. The kind of silence that presses on your ears like deep water. The blue candles burned without flickering. The frost glittered on every surface. The smoke hung in the air without moving.

Cinder was still holding my wrist. He was looking up at me. And his face had changed.

He looked, for the first time since I had known him, uncertain.

"That," he said slowly, "was not in the old song."

"No," I said. My voice was a croak. "It wasn't."

He stared at me. And I saw something in his black eyes that I had never expected to see there. Something that looked almost like fear.

And then, from the center of the room, where Cyphus stood among the wreckage with blue fire dancing on his arms, a shadow began to grow.
