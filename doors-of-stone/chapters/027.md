# A Box with No Lid

Meluan Lackless received me in a small sitting room off her bedchamber. It was a beautiful room, all soft greens and golds, with a fire burning in a marble hearth and heavy curtains drawn across the windows. It was very warm. It smelled of lavender and woodsmoke and something else, something faint and bitter, like the smell of an apothecary's shop.

She was sitting in a high-backed chair by the fire. She had changed out of her dinner gown into something looser and simpler, a robe of pale grey wool that fell around her in soft folds. Her dark hair was unbound and fell over her shoulders. Her hands were folded on the swell of her belly.

And in her lap, beneath her folded hands, there was a box.

It was just as I remembered it. A small box of dark wood, about the size of a loaf of bread, smooth and polished and faintly glowing in the firelight. There was no lid. There was no lock. There were no seams, no hinges, no joins of any kind. It was as if it had been carved from a single piece of wood, and then someone had taken whatever was inside and sealed it in with nothing at all.

"Sit," said Meluan.

I sat, in a chair across from her. Stapes closed the door behind me and left us alone.

For a long time, she did not say anything. She sat looking at me across the firelit room, her dark eyes very steady, her hands resting on the box. I waited. I had learned, in the Maer's court, that there is a time to speak and a time to be silent, and that the second is usually longer than you think.

"My husband tells me," she said at last, "that you were expelled from the University."

"Yes, my lady."

"For going somewhere forbidden. In the Archives."

"Yes."

"And that while you were there, you found records. About my family."

"Yes."

She was quiet again. The fire crackled. Her fingers moved on the surface of the box, very slightly, the way you might stroke a sleeping cat.

"Tell me," she said, "what you found."

I told her. I told her about the blue book, *The Lines of Loeclos*, with its long genealogy stretching back into darkness. I told her about the knot-marks in the margins, and the line of writing at the end. *The door holds while the line holds. The line holds while the name is kept.* I told her about the Book of Watches, and the account at the front of it, and the seven doors, and the keeper's door beneath the house of Loeclos that only the line could open, with the key of stone.

I did not tell her about the Thief. I did not tell her that her line was his line. I did not know how.

She listened in silence. Her face did not change. But her hands had gone still on the box, and they were pressed down on it now, hard, as if she were afraid it might fly away.

When I finished, she was silent for a very long time.

"I have never heard any of that," she said at last. "Not a word. Not from my father, not from my grandmother, not from anyone." She looked down at the box. "They told me only that I was to keep this. That it was the most important thing our family possessed. That it had been passed down from eldest daughter to eldest daughter for longer than anyone could remember, and that it was never, ever to be opened." She laughed, a small bitter sound. "As if it could be. There's no lid. There's no lock. There's no way in at all."

"May I see it, my lady?"

She looked at me. For a moment I thought she would refuse. Then, slowly, she lifted the box from her lap and held it out.

I took it.

---

It was heavier than it looked. Much heavier. It was like holding a stone, not a box of wood. And it was warm. Not the warmth of the fire, or of Meluan's hands. A warmth of its own, deep and steady, like the warmth of a living thing.

And it was humming.

I had felt that the first time I held it, a year ago. A faint vibration, just at the edge of perception, like a sound too low to hear. I had thought, then, that it was my imagination. I knew better now.

I closed my eyes. I let my mind go still, the way I had in the round room, the way I had in the Cellarage. I did not reach for the box. I did not try to make it do anything. I simply held it, and listened.

It was stone.

Inside the box, beneath the wood, there was stone. I could hear it. Its long slow patient no. But it was different from other stone. It was not saying no to everything. It was saying no to one thing. One particular thing. Over and over, patiently, endlessly. *No. No. No.*

It was saying no to a door.

And beneath that, fainter, woven into the stone's voice like a thread through cloth, there was something else. A name. Not the stone's name. Another name. A long, winding, complicated name that I could not quite hear, the way you cannot quite hear a word spoken in another room.

But I knew it. I knew it the way I had known my own name when the Sleeper spoke it, and when the voice behind the door whispered it. I knew it the way you know your own face.

It was a name that belonged to my family.

---

I opened my eyes. Meluan was staring at me. Her face had gone white as milk.

"What did you do?" she whispered.

"Nothing. I only listened."

"It sang." Her voice was shaking. "It sang when you held it. I heard it. It's never done that. Never, not in all the years I've had it." She pressed her hands to her belly. "What are you?"

I looked at her. At her white face and her frightened eyes and her hands pressed against her unborn child. At the box in my lap, warm and humming.

I had not planned to tell her. I had thought about it a great deal on the road south, and I had decided that it would be better not to. She had no love for the Edema Ruh. I knew that. Her sister had run away with one, and her family had never recovered from the shame. She had told me once, the year before, that she hated the Ruh more than any people on earth.

But she was frightened. And she was my mother's sister. And the box had sung.

"My lady," I said. "Your sister. Netalia. The one who ran away."

She went very still.

"How do you know her name?" she said. Her voice was very quiet.

"Because it was in the book. The blue one. Her name had been blotted out, but I knew it anyway." I took a deep breath. "She ran away with a trouper. An Edema Ruh. A red-haired man named Arliden, who made songs."

Meluan did not move. She did not breathe.

"She changed her name," I said. "She called herself Laurian. She traveled the roads with him for twelve years. She sang. She was happy. And she had a son." I looked at her. "Red hair, like his father. Green eyes, like his mother."

"No," Meluan whispered.

"She died when he was eleven. They both did. Killed, along with everyone in their troupe, by people who didn't want the songs they were singing to be heard." I swallowed. "The boy lived. He lived in the woods for a while, and then on the streets of Tarbean. And then he went to the University. And then he came to Severen, and met a lady named Meluan Lackless, and helped the Maer win her hand."

"No," she said again. Her voice cracked.

"My lady," I said. "I'm sorry. I'm so sorry. But she was my mother."

---

For a long time, neither of us moved.

The fire crackled in the hearth. Somewhere in the palace, very faintly, a bell was ringing the hour. Meluan sat in her high-backed chair and stared at me, and her face was white as bone, and there were tears running down her cheeks. She did not seem to notice them.

"You're lying," she said.

"I'm not."

"You're lying. You're Ruh. Ruh lie. It's all they do." Her voice rose. "You've heard the story somewhere. You've pieced it together. You want something from me. Money, or power, or—"

"I want nothing from you, my lady."

"Everyone wants something!"

"I don't." I held out the box. "Take it back. I don't want it. I don't want anything. I only wanted you to know."

She stared at the box. Then she reached out, very slowly, and took it from my hands. She held it against her chest the way Auri had held her ring.

"She left," Meluan said. Her voice was very small. "She left me. I was fourteen. She was seventeen. We shared a room, we shared everything. We used to lie awake at night and whisper about the men we'd marry, the houses we'd have, the children." Her face crumpled. "And then one day she was gone. Just gone. She didn't say goodbye. She didn't leave a note. She just went off with some—some *tinker*—"

"He wasn't a tinker. He was a trouper. He was the best trouper on the roads."

"I don't care what he was!" She was crying openly now. "She left me. She left all of us. My father never spoke her name again. My mother died of grief. And I—" She stopped. She pressed her hand to her mouth. "I had to be the eldest daughter. I had to keep the box. It should have been hers. It should have been *hers*, and she just *left*—"

She broke off. She bent over the box in her lap and sobbed, great heaving sobs that shook her whole body.

I did not know what to do. I sat there in my chair and watched her weep, and I felt helpless and cruel and very, very young.

Then, slowly, I got up. I crossed the room and knelt beside her chair. I did not touch her. I only knelt there, close, so she would know I was there.

After a long time, her sobs quieted. She sat up. She wiped her face with the back of her hand, like a child.

"Was she happy?" she whispered. "With him. Was she happy?"

I thought about my mother. About her laugh, bright as a bell. About the way she sang with my father around the fire at night, her head on his shoulder. About the way she held me when I was small, and the way she smelled, of woodsmoke and lavender and the road.

"Yes," I said. "She was the happiest person I ever knew."

Meluan closed her eyes. More tears slid down her cheeks. But her face had changed. It had softened, somehow. Something in it had let go.

"Good," she said quietly. "Good. I'm glad."

---

We talked for a long time after that. Hours. The fire burned down to embers and Meluan did not call for more wood. She wanted to know everything. What my mother had been like, on the road. What songs she sang. What she laughed at. Whether she ever spoke of home.

I told her what I could. It was not easy. I had spent so many years not remembering, locking those memories away behind the doors of sleep. But I opened those doors now, for her. I let the memories out. And as I told her about my mother, I found that I was remembering things I had thought were lost forever. The way she hummed while she cooked. The little scar on her left thumb, from a knife that slipped when she was cutting bread. The way she said my name when she was angry, and the way she said it when she was not.

Meluan listened to all of it. Sometimes she laughed. Sometimes she cried. Once she reached out and took my hand and held it, very tightly, and did not let go for a long time.

It was near dawn when she finally asked the question I had been waiting for.

"The box," she said. "You said there was a door. Beneath our house. And the line could open it, with the key." She looked down at the box in her lap. "Is that what this is? The key?"

"I think so."

"And you're of the line."

"Yes."

"So you could open it." Her voice was very quiet. "The door. You could open it."

"I don't know," I said honestly. "Maybe. I don't know how. And I don't want to."

"Why not?"

I thought about the Book of Watches. About the account at the front of it. About the Thief, locked away beyond the world with his stolen moon. About the breathing on the other side of the door beneath the Archives, and the voice that had whispered my name.

"Because I think there's something behind it," I said. "Something that was put there for a very good reason. And I think if the door is ever opened, something terrible will happen."

Meluan looked at me for a long time.

"Then someone must make sure it never is," she said.

"Yes."

She looked down at the box. Then, slowly, she held it out to me.

"Take it," she said.

I stared at her. "My lady—"

"Take it. I don't want it. I never wanted it." Her voice was steady, but her eyes were wet. "It should have been hers. She was the eldest. It should have gone to her, and then to her child. To you." She pressed it into my hands. "Take it far away. Keep it safe. Keep it from whoever wants it."

"I can't. Your husband—"

"My husband will understand. Or he won't. It doesn't matter." She laid her hand on her belly. "I have a child coming. A child of the line, like you. And I don't want this child to grow up with this thing under their pillow, afraid of every sound in the night. I don't want them to be a keeper. I want them to be a child." She looked at me. "Please. Take it."

I looked down at the box in my hands. It was warm, and heavy, and humming. Its long slow no.

I thought about all the people who wanted it. The one who had come into Meluan's room at night. The Knife, with his staff of dark wood crowned in silver. The Chandrian.

I thought about how much safer it would be, here, in the Maer's palace, behind walls and guards and locked doors. And how much more dangerous, in my hands, on the roads, with the Chandrian already hunting me.

And then I thought about Meluan, lying awake every night with the box under her pillow. Listening for footsteps. Waiting.

"All right," I said. "I'll take it."

She closed her eyes. Her whole body seemed to sag, as if a great weight had been lifted from her shoulders.

"Thank you," she whispered.

---

I went back to my rooms as the sun was rising. The palace was quiet, the long galleries empty and grey in the early light. I carried the box under my cloak, pressed against my side. It was warm through the cloth. I could feel it humming against my ribs.

I did not know, then, what I had done. I did not know that the box had been safe in Meluan's hands precisely because she did not know what it was. I did not know that a key in the hands of the line is a very different thing from a key in the hands of anyone else.

I did not know that I had just done exactly what someone had been waiting, very patiently, for me to do.

I only knew that my aunt had wept in my arms, and called my mother by her name, and given me a box. And that for the first time since I was eleven years old, I had a family again.

It was a very small family. Just one frightened woman in a green room, and her unborn child. But it was mine.

I went to sleep that morning with the box under my pillow, the way she had. And for the first time in many months, I slept without dreaming.
