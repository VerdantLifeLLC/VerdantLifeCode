# The Medica

I had not meant to go back to the Medica that term. I had enough to do. Kilvin wanted me in the Fishery, Elodin wanted me listening to rocks, and the Maer's gold, while generous, would not last forever if I did not keep up my twice-a-span at Anker's and the Eolian. But Arwyl sent a note on the fourth day, written in his small tidy hand on a slip of paper that smelled faintly of camphor, and the note said only this:

*Re'lar Kvothe. We are short of hands. Third bell, if you please.*

You did not refuse Arwyl. It was not that he was frightening, the way Lorren was frightening, or unpredictable, the way Elodin was. He was a small, round, gentle old man with spectacles and a soft voice, who looked like someone's favorite grandfather and smelled like a pharmacy. But he had been Master Physicker for thirty years, and in that time he had saved the lives of half the masters, most of the Arcanum, and a good portion of the city of Imre. People did what Arwyl asked. It seemed the least they could do.

So at third bell I was standing in the long cool white hall of the Medica in a grey apron, scrubbing my hands in a basin of water so hot it turned my skin pink, while Mola stood beside me and told me everything I had forgotten in a year of wandering.

"You'll be on the ward," she said. "Not surgery. Not today. Arwyl wants to see if your hands still know anything before he lets you near a knife." She glanced at me sidelong. "What happened to your face?"

"A teacher."

"A teacher did that?"

"She was very dedicated."

Mola snorted. She was a sharp-faced, sharp-tongued young woman from Vintas who had been a Re'lar in the Medica for as long as I had known her and was, everyone agreed, going to be the best physicker in a generation, if she did not murder someone first. She had never liked me very much. I had never been quite sure why. I suspected it had something to do with the time I had fainted in her arms during my first dissection and she had had to carry me out.

"Well," she said, "try not to bleed on the patients. It upsets them."

---

The ward was a long room lined with beds, each one separated from its neighbors by a white linen curtain. It was very clean and very quiet, and it smelled of lye soap and boiled linen and the particular sweetish smell of sickness that no amount of soap can ever quite wash out. There were perhaps twenty patients that afternoon. Students with fevers. A carter with a broken leg. An old woman from Imre with a cough that sounded like a saw going through wet wood. A baby with the croup, and its mother sitting beside it on a stool, white-faced, rocking.

I went from bed to bed. I took pulses and felt foreheads and changed dressings and wrote things down in the ledger at the foot of each bed. My hands remembered, mostly. There is a rhythm to that kind of work, a way of touching people that is firm and gentle at the same time, and my hands had learned it once and did not seem to have forgotten.

It was near the end of the afternoon when I came to the last bed.

The curtain was drawn all the way around it, which was unusual. Beside it, on a stool, an E'lir I did not know was sitting with a book open on her knees, pretending to read. She looked up as I came near, and her face was pale.

"Arwyl said no one's to go in without him," she said.

"Arwyl sent me."

She hesitated. Then she nodded, and looked back down at her book, and I saw that her hands were not quite steady on the pages.

I pulled back the curtain.

---

The man on the bed was a trapper. You could see it in him: in the leathery weathered skin of his face and hands, in the thick shoulders and the thin legs, in the way his hair had been hacked short with a knife rather than cut. He was forty, perhaps, or fifty. It was hard to tell. He was lying on his back with the blanket drawn up to his chin, and he was awake, and he was looking at the ceiling.

And he was cold.

I felt it the moment I drew back the curtain. A draft of cold air, coming out from behind it, like the air that comes out of an icehouse when you open the door. It was late summer, and the ward was warm, and the windows were open to the sunshine. But inside the curtain, around that bed, it was winter.

I looked at the ledger at the foot of the bed. It was written in Arwyl's small tidy hand.

*Deran, trapper, of the Eld near Tilth. Brought in by his brother. Frost-burn to the left forearm, severe. Patient insists injury sustained in the forest, in the month of Caitelyn.* Caitelyn is high summer. *Patient will not say more. Keep warm. Keep watched. Do not let him sleep alone.*

I looked at the trapper. He was still looking at the ceiling.

"Deran," I said.

His eyes moved. Slowly. They found my face.

"You're new," he said. His voice was a hoarse whisper. "Haven't seen you."

"I've been away." I came around to the side of the bed. "May I see your arm?"

He looked at me for a long moment. Then, slowly, he drew his left arm out from under the blanket.

---

I have seen a great many wounds. I have seen men who were burned in fires and men who were cut with swords and men who had been crushed under carts. I have seen what the cold does to fingers and toes in a Tarbean winter, when a boy sleeps on a rooftop without shoes. I thought I knew what frost-burn looked like.

I had never seen anything like this.

His forearm was white. Not pale. White, like marble, like bone, like the belly of a fish. The skin was hard and shiny and cold to the touch; when I laid my fingers on it, very gently, it was like touching a stone that has been lying all night in the snow. And the whiteness had a shape.

It was a hand. The print of a hand, long-fingered and narrow, wrapped around his forearm just below the elbow. As if someone had taken hold of him there, very gently, the way you might take hold of a child's arm to lead him across a road. And where the fingers had touched, the flesh had died.

I did not say anything for a long time.

"It's a hand," said Deran. His voice was very quiet.

"Yes."

"Nobody else will say it. The old man, the physicker, he looked at it and he said *hm* and *interesting* and wrote things in his book. The girl out there won't look at it at all." He laughed, a thin cracked sound. "But it's a hand. Isn't it?"

"Yes."

He closed his eyes.

"I'm not mad," he said. "I want you to know that. They think I'm mad. My brother thinks I'm mad. He says I got drunk and fell asleep in a snowbank." He opened his eyes. "In Caitelyn."

"I don't think you're mad."

He looked at me for a long moment.

"Why not?" he said.

I did not answer right away. I looked at the print on his arm. At the long narrow fingers. I thought about a man with black eyes and frost-colored hair, crouching in front of me in the firelight, a long time ago.

"Because I've seen something like it," I said. "Once."

---

He told me. Not all at once. In pieces, over the next hour, while I sat on the stool beside his bed and the E'lir outside the curtain pretended to read and the long ward went quiet around us as the afternoon wore on toward evening.

He had been running his traplines in the Eld, in the deep part, where the old trees grew so thick that it was dusk at noon. It was a place he knew well. He had been trapping there for twenty years. He knew every stream and every hollow and every deadfall.

And one evening, coming back along a line he had run a thousand times, he had come around a bend in a stream and found a camp.

"Wagons," he said. "Three of them. Painted. Like players' wagons. Pulled up in a clearing by the water, in a half circle, with a fire in the middle." His face was very still. "I thought it was Ruh. A troupe, lost in the forest. I thought I'd go down and tell them the way out."

"Was it?"

"No." He was quiet for a long time. "It was their wagons. But not them."

I did not ask him what he meant. I did not have to. He told me anyway.

The wagons were empty. The fire was burning, but it was burning blue. And there was no one in the clearing, no one at all, except a man sitting on the step of the nearest wagon with his elbows on his knees, looking into the fire.

"He looked up," said Deran. "When I came down the bank. He looked up and smiled at me. Like he'd been waiting." He swallowed. "His eyes were black. All the way. No white in them at all."

"What did he say?"

Deran looked at me.

"He asked me if I'd seen a red-haired boy," he said. "A singer. About so high." He held his good hand up, level with his shoulder. "Travels with a lute. Might be calling himself something else."

The ward was very quiet.

"I said no," said Deran. "I said I hadn't seen anyone. Not in the forest. Not in months." He was shaking now, very slightly, under the blanket. "And he looked at me for a long time. And then he got up off the step and came across the clearing and took hold of my arm. Like this." He touched the white print, very lightly. "Gentle. Like he was helping me. And he said, *if you do, tell him Ferule was asking*." Deran's voice cracked. "And then he let go, and I ran, and I didn't stop running until I came out of the forest. Two days later. And my arm was like this."

---

I sat very still on the stool beside his bed.

*Tell him Ferule was asking.*

I did not know the name. Not then. I did not know, sitting in the Medica on a warm afternoon in late summer, that I would hear it again in a burning hall, and on a ridge above a burning camp, and in a chamber beneath an old house, spoken each time by a voice made of forgetting. I did not know that I would say it myself, one day, in a small cold room at the top of the stairs of an inn, and watch the man it belonged to flinch.

I only knew that someone had been in the Eld, in a clearing, beside a fire burning blue, asking after a red-haired boy with a lute.

And that the wagons had been painted like a troupe's.

---

"Do you know him?" said Deran. "The man with the black eyes?"

I looked at him. At his weathered face and his frightened eyes and his white arm lying on the blanket.

"No," I said.

It was not quite a lie. I did not know his name. I did not know what he was, or why he did what he did. I only knew his face.

Deran looked at me for a long time. He did not believe me. I could see it. But he was too tired to argue.

"Will it get better?" he said. "The arm?"

I looked at the white print. At the hard shiny dead skin, and the cold coming off it.

"I don't know," I said honestly. "I'll ask Master Arwyl."

---

I did ask. I found Arwyl in his office at the end of the ward, a small crowded room lined with jars and books and smelling powerfully of camphor. He was sitting at his desk writing in a ledger. He did not look up when I came in.

"You've seen the trapper," he said.

"Yes."

"And?"

"And I've never seen anything like it."

"Nor have I." He set down his pen. He took off his spectacles and rubbed his eyes. "Forty years a physicker, and I've never seen anything like it." He put his spectacles back on and looked at me. "I've read of it, though. Once. In a very old book. There's a copy in the Archives, in the Stacks, in the section on diseases of the northern marches. Very old. Very incomplete. Someone took a knife to it, long ago." He was quiet for a moment. "It describes a man with an arm frozen in the shape of a hand. In summer. And it says the man died, a month later. Not of the arm. Of the cold. It spread. Slowly. Up the arm, into the chest. Into the heart."

I stood very still.

"Is that going to happen to Deran?"

"I don't know." Arwyl looked at me over his spectacles. "I'm going to try to stop it. Heat. Poultices. Sympathy, if Master Kilvin will lend me someone who knows heat-binding." He paused. "You know heat-binding, don't you, Re'lar Kvothe?"

"Yes, Master Arwyl."

"Good." He picked up his pen again. "Then you'll come back tomorrow. And the day after. And every day until we know whether it's spreading."

---

I went back every day for a month.

I sat beside Deran's bed with a copper plate in one hand and a hot brick in the other, bound together with a sympathetic link, and I fed the heat of the brick into the copper and the copper into his arm. Slowly. Carefully. A little at a time. Arwyl said too much heat too fast would kill the flesh around the print, and then he would lose the arm.

It was tedious work. It was exhausting. I held the binding for hours at a time, with my mind split in two, and when I stood up at the end of each day my head ached and my hands shook and I was so cold myself, from giving all my heat away, that I had to go and sit by the fire in the Mews for an hour before I could stop shivering.

But the print did not spread.

It did not get better, either. The white skin stayed white and hard and cold. The long narrow fingers of it stayed wrapped around his arm, just below the elbow, as clear as the day he had come in. But it did not grow. It did not creep up his arm toward his chest, the way Arwyl's old book said it would.

"It's holding," said Arwyl, at the end of the month, bending over the arm with his spectacles on the end of his nose. "Whatever you're doing, it's holding." He looked up at me. "I don't know if it'll ever heal. But it's holding."

Deran went home to the Eld a span later, with his arm wrapped in wool and a jar of Arwyl's poultice in his pack. He shook my hand at the door of the Medica with his good hand, and looked at me for a long time.

"If I see him again," he said. "The man with the black eyes. What should I tell him?"

I thought about it.

"Tell him you didn't see anyone," I said. "Tell him the forest is empty."

Deran looked at me for a moment longer. Then he nodded and went away down the road, toward the bridge and the river and the long road home.

I never saw him again. But I heard, years later, from a traveler who had come through Tilth, that there was a trapper in that country with a white arm, who would not go into the deep part of the Eld anymore, and who told anyone who asked that the forest was empty.

---

I did not tell anyone what he had told me. Not Sim, not Wil, not Kilvin. Not Denna, when she came back to Imre that autumn.

But I lit Kilvin's candle that night, in my room under the eaves, and I sat and watched it until very late. It burned yellow. It burned yellow all night, and the next night, and the night after that.

I did not find that comforting.

Because I knew now that they did not always make the candle burn blue. They were not always doing something. Sometimes they were only sitting on the step of a stolen wagon in a clearing in the forest, looking into a fire, waiting.

Asking after a red-haired boy.
