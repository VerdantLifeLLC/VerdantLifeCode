# Interlude: The Chest

Chronicler did not remember getting up from the table.

He remembered the innkeeper's voice, very quiet, from the top of the stairs. *Scribe. Bring the story.* And then he was on his feet, and the pages were in his arms, all of them, three days of writing gathered up in a great untidy armful of paper, some of it frozen stiff, some of it smeared with black ice where the ink had frozen before it dried. And he was crossing the frost-covered floor of the common room toward the stairs.

He had to pass Cinder to get there.

He did not look at him. He could not. He kept his eyes on the stairs, on the innkeeper standing on the landing above, and he walked. Past the tall pale shape with the pale sword. Past Bast, lying on the floor with a boot on his chest and blood on the frost beside him. Past the cold, which came off Cinder in waves, like heat off a forge, only the opposite.

He felt Cinder's black eyes on him as he passed. He felt them the way you feel the edge of a knife laid against your skin.

Cinder did not stop him.

"Go on," said the soft, reasonable voice behind him. Amused. "Go on, scribe. Take him his story. I'd like to see what he does with it."

Chronicler climbed the stairs.

---

The innkeeper's room was at the front of the inn, at the top of the stairs. A small plain room with a narrow bed and a washstand and a window that looked out over the crossroads. The shutters on the window were covered in frost, thick and white. The air was so cold it hurt to breathe.

The chest was at the foot of the bed.

Chronicler had never seen it before. He had not been in this room. He stood in the doorway with his arms full of paper and looked at it.

It was a plain dark box, about the size of a small trunk. Smooth and heavy and without ornament. The wood was almost black, with a faint reddish tinge in the grain. There were two locks on the front of it: one copper, with a keyhole shaped like a small closed eye; one stone, grey and plain, with no keyhole at all. And between them, sunk into the wood so deep that it seemed part of the grain, a small dark knot.

The innkeeper was kneeling in front of it. He had his hands flat on the lid. His head was bowed.

"It's humming," he said, without looking up. "I can hear it. For the first time in seven years, I can hear it." His voice was very quiet. "The waystone's been drowning it out all this time. But I'm loud enough now to hear it over the stone." He paused. "And it's loud enough to hear me."

Chronicler did not know what to say. He stood in the doorway with his arms full of paper and his breath smoking in the frozen air.

The innkeeper looked up at him.

"Sit," he said. "On the bed. Where I can hear you."

Chronicler sat.

"Now," said the innkeeper. "Read it to me."

---

Chronicler stared at him.

"Read it?"

"The story. What you wrote." The innkeeper looked at the pages in Chronicler's arms. "All of it, if we had time. We don't. So read me the parts that matter." He looked back at the chest. "The copper lock is bound to me. To Kvothe. To the man with his name. It won't open for Kote. It doesn't know Kote." He laid his hand on the copper lock. "But it might know Kvothe's story. If it hears enough of it. If it's told true."

Chronicler looked down at the pages in his arms. At the tiny crabbed cipher, row after row, filling every page from top to bottom and up the margins and across the tops.

"I don't know which parts matter," he said.

The innkeeper looked at him. His green eyes were very steady.

"Yes, you do," he said. "You've been listening for three days. You know."

---

From downstairs, very faintly, Chronicler heard a sound. A small, choked sound. Bast.

And then Cinder's voice. Soft and reasonable. Carrying up the stairs as clearly as if he were standing in the room.

"*I'm waiting, singer,*" it said. "*I'm a very patient man. But not infinitely patient.*" A pause. Another small choked sound. "*Neither is the princeling. He's losing a great deal of blood.*"

The innkeeper closed his eyes.

"Read," he said. "Please."

---

Chronicler read.

His hands were shaking so badly he could barely hold the pages. His voice cracked on the first word. But he read.

He did not start at the beginning. There was no time for the beginning. He sorted through the pages, quickly, blindly, his frozen fingers fumbling at the stiff paper. He did not know what he was looking for. He only knew, the way you sometimes know things, that he would know it when he found it.

He found it.

"*Kilvin was in his office,*" he read. His voice was hoarse. "*He was always in his office, as far as I could tell. I sometimes wondered if he slept there, curled up on the floor beneath his desk like a great bear in a cave.*"

He read the Fishery. The candle. Kilvin's great scarred hands, and the ledgers, and the dates. He read the moment Kvothe realized what the candle was. He read Kilvin's face, full of compassion. *Maybe nobody else would have to lose their family.*

He read the Fishery at night. The roah planks. Kilvin with his plane, shaving long pale curls of wood. Wil at the bench with his scarred hands, making the copper lock. *It's a sympathy lock. The key has to be bound to the lock by the one who owns it.*

The innkeeper was kneeling in front of the chest with his hand on the copper lock and his eyes closed. His lips were moving. Very faintly. Saying something Chronicler could not hear.

Chronicler read.

He read the moment the cord came off Kvothe's wrist. *It felt like dying.* He read Denna, saying *Kote. Can you hear me?* He read Kvothe saying *no*.

He read the lid closing.

And the copper lock clicked.

---

It was a small sound. Barely a sound at all. A single soft click, like a key turning in a well-oiled lock.

The innkeeper opened his eyes. He looked down at the copper lock under his hand.

The small closed eye of its keyhole had opened.

"One," he said softly.

---

From downstairs, Cinder's voice. Closer, now. At the foot of the stairs.

"*I heard that,*" it said. "*How very clever.*" A pause. "*I'm coming up, singer. I'd like to watch.*"

Footsteps on the stairs. Slow. Unhurried. Each one leaving a small white bloom of frost on the wood.

"The stone," said the innkeeper. His voice was very steady. "Read me the stone. Fela's lock. It knows the Eolian. The night of the song. The stones in it held the building up while it burned." He looked at Chronicler. "Read me that night."

Chronicler's hands were shaking so badly he dropped half the pages. They scattered across the frozen floor. He fell to his knees and scrabbled for them. Found the one he needed. It was frozen stiff, the ink smeared with black ice. He could barely read it.

He read it anyway.

"*It began with the candles on the tables nearest the stage,*" he read.

---

He read the Eolian. The candles going blue. The cold. The Seven, appearing one by one. He read Wil holding up the chandelier with his face grey with effort. He read Devi at the balcony rail, throwing her Surprises. He read the balconies rotting under Usnea's hands.

He read the stone.

*And beneath my feet, I felt the stone of the stage begin to hum. It was very faint at first. A vibration, deep and low, rising up from somewhere far below. From the cellar. From the foundations.*

*Fela.*

*She was down there, in the dark, with her hands pressed flat against the foundations of the Eolian. And she was asking the stone to stand.*

The stone lock on the chest was humming.

Chronicler could hear it. Even he could hear it. A deep low vibration, rising up out of the grey stone, filling the small cold room. It was the most patient sound he had ever heard. The sound of something that had been saying *no* for a very long time, and was about to say something else.

The footsteps on the stairs had stopped.

Chronicler read on. He read Stanchion, with his empty buckets, standing between Usnea and the cellar stairs. *Not in my place, you rotten bitch.* He read Stanchion falling.

He read Kvothe, still singing. *And I did not stop singing.*

He read the walls, standing in the dawn, blackened and scorched and cracked. But standing.

*Never seen stone hold like that*, the old stonemason had said. *Never in my life.*

The stone lock stopped humming.

It did not click. It did not open. It simply went quiet. And then, very slowly, the grey stone of it split down the middle, cleanly, the way stone splits along a seam. And the two halves fell apart, and lay on the floor, and inside the hollow where they had been, there were two small flat stones. Worn smooth. With a single mark scratched into each one.

The innkeeper picked them up. He held them in his palm. He looked at them for a long time.

"Two," he said.

---

"*Three,*" said a voice from the doorway.

Cinder was standing there. Tall and pale and very still, with his pale sword in his hand and his crooked scarred smile. The frost bloomed out from his feet across the floor of the small room, white and crackling.

"There's a third lock," he said. "Isn't there? The girl's knot." He looked at the small dark knot sunk into the wood between the two open locks. "I can see it from here. Very neat. Very tight." He smiled. "You can't read that one open, singer. It needs the other half. The twin. And the twin is with her. Far away. Wherever she's hiding."

The innkeeper did not look at him. He was looking at the knot.

"You've done very well," said Cinder. "Two out of three. Very impressive. The old man would have been proud." He came into the room. One step. Two. "But it's over now. Pick up the chest. Come with me. We'll find the girl together. I'm very good at finding people." His crooked smile widened. "And when we find her, she'll untie it. Because she loves you. And I'll be holding a knife to your throat."

The innkeeper was quiet for a long moment.

Then he reached into the pocket of his apron.

---

He took out something small. Chronicler could not see what it was. The innkeeper held it cupped in his palm, and looked down at it, and his face did something that Chronicler would remember for the rest of his life.

It was the face of a man who has been handed something he lost a very long time ago, and never expected to see again.

"A tinker came by today," said the innkeeper quietly. "This afternoon. He sold needles and mended pots and gave the children sugared almonds. And before he left, he gave me this." He opened his hand. "He said it was something I'd lost. On the road. A long time ago. He said someone had asked him to carry it for me, until the time was right." He looked up at Cinder. "He said the time was right."

In his palm, there was a cord. Short. Barely as long as a finger. Braided from three strands of dark hair.

With a single knot tied in it.

Cinder stopped moving.

"No," he said softly.

"*Knots are very good at finding their way home*," said the innkeeper. "She told me that. On the night we made the chest. I didn't remember it, when the tinker gave me this. I didn't know what it was. I only knew that it made me want to weep." He looked down at the knot. "But I remember now. The story brought it back."

He leaned forward. He pressed the twin knot against the knot in the wood.

And it came undone.

---

It did not happen quickly. It happened the way ice melts in spring. The knot in the wood loosened, one loop at a time, slowly, as if it were waking up from a long sleep. The dark strands of hair unwound from the grain of the roah. They drifted up out of the wood like smoke, and wound themselves around the twin knot in the innkeeper's fingers, and the two knots became one, and then the one knot came undone, and there was only a short loose piece of dark hair, lying across the innkeeper's palm.

The lid of the chest shifted. Just slightly. The way a lid shifts when it is no longer held down.

"Three," said the innkeeper.

---

Cinder moved.

He came across the small room like a blade of ice, his pale sword raised. He moved faster than Chronicler could follow, faster than anything should move.

But the innkeeper was already moving too.

He did not stand up. He did not turn to face Cinder. He did not reach for a weapon. He did the only thing left for him to do.

He opened the chest.

The lid came up. The faint green glow of something lining its underside flickered and went dark, satisfied. Inside, in the dark, there were four things.

A folded sheet of paper, covered in cramped untidy writing.

A lute, dark wood and ivory pegs, lying on its back.

A small box of dark wood, smooth and polished, about the size of a loaf of bread. No lid. No lock. No seams. Humming.

And lying across all of them, glowing faintly silver in the blue light of Cinder's cold, a cord. Braided from three strands of dark hair. Knotted along its whole length. Hundreds of knots. Looping and twisting and doubling back.

The innkeeper reached in and took hold of it.

And Cinder's pale sword came down.
