# Two Songs

The Eolian was full by sundown.

I had never seen it so full. Not on the night I won my pipes, not on the busiest night of the year, not ever. People were packed shoulder to shoulder on the main floor, crowded around every table, standing along every wall. The balconies were so full that Stanchion had posted men at the top of every stair to keep anyone else from going up, for fear the old wood would give way. People were sitting on the edge of the stage. People were sitting on the bar. People were standing outside in the street, crowded around every open door and window, craning their necks to see in.

Stanchion had done everything I asked. Every door stood open, all the way around the great round room, and there was a man at every door. Barrels of water stood along the walls, with buckets hanging from their rims. Deoch stood at the main door with a stout oak staff in his hands and a look on his face that made even the drunkest students think twice about pushing past him.

My friends were in their places. Devi was on the first balcony, directly above the stage, with a satchel full of things she had spent four days making and would not tell me about. Wil was on the second balcony, across the room, near the north door. Sim was on the main floor, near the south door, with a candle in a lantern and a look of terrified determination on his face.

And Fela was in the cellar.

I had not asked her to go there. She had chosen it herself. "The foundations," she had said. "That's where the stone is strongest. That's where I can hear it best." She had gone down the cellar stairs an hour before sunset with a lantern and a blanket and nothing else, and she had not come up since.

I did not know if she could do what I had asked. I did not know if anyone could. But she had gone.

---

I waited in Stanchion's office behind the bar, with the door closed and my lute in my lap, and listened to the noise of the crowd through the wall. It was like the sea. A great restless roar, rising and falling.

I was not nervous. I want to be honest about that. I had played in the Eolian many times, before crowds almost as large. I had played before the Maer and his court. I had played for Felurian, which is a thing that would make any musician nervous, and I had survived it.

I was not nervous. I was something else. Something that did not have a name.

It was like standing at the top of a very high cliff, looking down at the sea far below, knowing that you are going to jump. Not afraid, exactly. Not excited, exactly. Just very, very aware. Of everything. Of every breath, every heartbeat, every tiny movement of the air.

I sat in Stanchion's office and tuned my lute. I tuned it three times. It did not need tuning. But it gave my hands something to do.

Then there was a knock on the door, and Stanchion put his head in. His face was very strange.

"She's here," he said.

I did not ask who. I did not have to.

---

She was on the stage when I came out.

I had not expected that. I had thought she would sit in the balconies, with the envoy from Vintas and his retinue. I had thought she would wait for me to sing, and then answer. But she was on the stage, sitting on a stool at the very center of it, with her harp in her lap.

She looked beautiful. She always looked beautiful. But there was something different about her now. She was dressed in black silk, very fine and very expensive, cut close to her body. Her hair was braided in an intricate crown around her head, and there were cords woven into it, dark cords knotted in patterns I could not read from where I stood. Her face was pale and composed and very still.

And on her wrist, the knot. I could see it from across the room. It was not glowing. But it was there.

The crowd had gone quiet when she came out. They had not expected her either. They were staring at her, and whispering, and some of them were pointing. I heard her name, and other names. Dianne. Dinael. The king's nightingale.

She looked up. Across the crowded room, over the heads of hundreds of people, her eyes found mine.

For a long moment, neither of us moved.

Then she looked away. She looked up at the first balcony, where a man in the rich clothes of a Vintish lord sat with a glass of wine in his hand. The envoy, I supposed. And beside him, in the shadows, a figure I could not quite see. Tall and white-haired, leaning on a stick.

Denna looked back down at her harp. She laid her fingers on the strings.

And she began to sing.

---

I will not tell you her song. It is not mine to tell. And even now, after everything, I cannot bring myself to repeat it. It was too beautiful, and too terrible, and too much a part of her.

But I will tell you what it was like.

It was like being in love. Truly in love, the first time, when you are young and everything is new. It was like the moment when you look at someone and realize that you would do anything for them, anything at all, and that the world has changed its shape around them without your noticing. It was warm and sweet and aching and full of longing.

It was about Lanre. Of course it was. It was about a great hero, the greatest who ever lived, who loved a woman more than light, more than breath. Who fought for his people and bled for them and died for them. Who was brought back from death by his wife's love, and then betrayed. Betrayed by the people he had saved, by the friends he had trusted, by a jealous old man with one eye who could not bear to see him happy.

It was about grief. About a man whose wife was taken from him by the cruelty of others, and who wept for her until his tears turned to ash. About a man who was cursed for his grief, punished for his love, damned forever for the crime of caring too much.

It was about injustice. About a lie told so long and so often that it had become the truth. About a hero turned into a monster by the stories of his enemies.

And it was beautiful. God help me, it was so beautiful.

I stood at the edge of the stage and listened, and I felt it working on me. Felt it slipping in under my defenses, the way water slips into cracks in stone. I felt myself begin to believe it. To feel sorry for Lanre. To feel angry at Selitos, and the Amyr, and everyone who had told the old story.

I looked around the Eolian. The crowd was silent, enraptured. People were weeping. Great strong men, with tears running down their faces. Old women, clutching each other's hands. Students, with their mouths open and their eyes shining. They were all feeling it. All believing it. All being changed by it, a little, in some small deep place they would never notice.

And I understood, standing there, what Skarpi had meant. About the song being a crack in the wall. Every person in this room who heard it, who wept at it, who went home and thought about poor wronged Lanre as they fell asleep—every one of them was a crack. A tiny weakness. A place where the truth had been worn away and replaced with something prettier.

And there were hundreds of them. Here, tonight. And thousands more, in all the towns and cities and halls where she had sung it.

The curse was bleeding away. I could almost feel it.

---

She finished. The last note hung in the air for a long moment, trembling, and then faded.

The Eolian was silent. Utterly, completely silent. Not a cough, not a whisper, not a creak of a chair. It was the silence that comes after something so beautiful that people do not know how to respond to it.

Then the applause came. It came like a storm breaking. It thundered down from the balconies and rolled across the floor and shook the walls. People were on their feet, shouting, stamping, weeping. It went on and on.

Denna did not smile. She did not bow. She sat on her stool with her harp in her lap and her head bowed, and she did not move.

When the applause finally began to fade, she stood up. She picked up her harp. And she walked off the stage, past me, very close.

As she passed, she turned her head, just slightly. Her eyes met mine. They were wet.

"I'm sorry," she whispered. So softly that only I could hear.

Then she was gone, up the stairs to the first balcony, where the envoy and the white-haired man were waiting.

---

I walked out onto the stage.

The crowd saw me and began to murmur. A ripple of sound went through the room, whispers and exclamations. *The Ruh. The kingkiller. That's him.* I heard someone laugh. I heard someone else hiss.

I did not look at them. I sat down on the stool Denna had left, still warm from her, and I laid my lute across my knees.

I closed my eyes. I thought about what Skarpi had said. *Don't sing against her. Sing for her.*

I thought about what Elodin had said. *Hold onto something. Something small.*

I touched the knot hanging around my neck, under my shirt. The one she had given me at the Grey Man, before everything. *Road. Promise. A story told many times, and changes in the telling.*

And then I opened my eyes, and I began to play.

---

I began with the white city.

Not with Lanre. Not with the war, or the betrayal, or the curse. With Myr Tariniel, on the last day of its life. With the white towers rising against a blue sky, and the gardens full of flowers, and the fountains full of children playing. With a woman singing in the great hall, her voice rising like a bird. With a little boy hiding under a slab of white marble, listening.

I sang it the way Skarpi had taught me. Not to be heard. Not to be applauded. For the city. For the children. So that they would be remembered.

And the Eolian went quiet.

I felt it happen. The murmuring stopped. The hissing stopped. The whole great round room went still, the way a forest goes still when the wind dies. I did not look up. I did not look at the crowd. I kept my eyes on my lute, on my fingers moving on the strings, and I sang the white city into being.

And then I sang Lanre.

Not the Lanre of Denna's song. Not the wronged hero. Not the monster of the Tehlin sermons either. The true Lanre. A man. A great man, a brave man, a man who loved his wife more than light and more than breath. A man who fought for his people and bled for them and died for them at Drossen Tor, and was brought back by his wife's voice speaking his name.

I sang his love for Lyra. I sang it so that everyone in the room could feel it, the way I had felt it in the broken lighthouse when Skarpi sang it to me. I sang their happiness, and their fear, and their grief.

I sang Lyra's death. How she was taken, in the war, not by Selitos or the Amyr or any of Lanre's friends, but by the war itself. By the shapers and the thing they had made of the world. How Lanre held her body in his arms and called her name, as she had called his, and she did not answer.

And I sang what came next.

I sang the Thief. The one who stole the moon. How he came to Lanre in his grief, in the dark, and whispered to him. *I can bring her back. I can shape a world where she lives. But first, this world must end.*

I sang Lanre's choice. How he believed the Thief. How he wanted to believe. How grief can make a man believe anything, if it promises to end the grief.

I sang Myr Tariniel burning.

---

The crowd was silent. I could hear people weeping, but it was different weeping than before. It was not the sweet ache of Denna's song. It was harder. Deeper. It was the weeping of people who have been shown something true and terrible and cannot look away.

I sang Selitos, standing among the ruins with his bleeding eye. I sang the curse. I sang Lanre's question, *why did you not kill me*, and Selitos's answer, and Lanre's laughter like a cracked bell.

And then I sang Selitos weeping.

I sang it softly. So softly that the people at the back of the room had to lean forward to hear. I sang an old man alone among the ashes of everything he loved, looking into himself and seeing what he had become. I sang his grief, and his rage, and his shame. I sang the little boy he dug out of the rubble, and the question he asked him, and the answer the boy gave.

*I want to be a singer. Like my mother.*

---

I was nearly at the end. The last verses. The founding of the Singers, and the Amyr, and the sealing of the doors. I could feel the song building in me, rising toward its close, like a wave rising toward the shore.

I could feel something else, too.

It had started, I think, somewhere around Lyra's death. A pressure. A fullness. As if the song were growing larger inside me than I was. As if it were filling me up, the way water fills a cup. Every verse I sang made it grow. Every note. Every word.

I remembered what Elodin had said. *It doesn't just go out into the world. It goes through you. It uses you.*

I felt it using me. I felt it pushing at the edges of me, pressing against the inside of my skin. I felt myself getting smaller, and the song getting larger, until I was only a thin shell around something vast and old and true.

I held onto the knot around my neck. I held onto it with everything I had. *Road. Promise. A story told many times.*

I kept singing.

And that was when the candles began to burn blue.
