# The Rest of the Story

*This is what Skarpi told me, in the broken lighthouse at the end of the world, with the sea breathing on the rocks outside and a fire burning between us that fed on nothing at all.*

---

When Selitos had spoken the curse, he stood for a long time among the ruins of Myr Tariniel, with the blood running down his face from his ruined eye and the stone still clenched in his fist.

The city was gone. The white towers had fallen. The great gardens had burned. The people, all the people, the singers and the scholars and the children playing in the fountains, were dead. And Lanre, who had done this, stood before him with his own name hung around his neck like a millstone, and could not die.

Selitos looked at him with his one good eye. And Lanre looked back. And for a long moment, neither of them spoke.

Then Lanre said, *Why?*

It was not the question Selitos had expected. He had expected rage. He had expected curses of his own, or pleading, or laughter. He had not expected a single word, spoken so quietly, like a child asking why the sky was dark.

*Why what?* said Selitos.

*Why did you not kill me?* said Lanre. *You could have. You know my name. You could have spoken it and unmade me. I would have let you.* He looked at the ruins around them. *I came here to be killed. I came here so that someone would finally end it. And you did not.*

Selitos was silent for a long time.

*Because death is too small for what you have done,* he said at last. *Because a thousand thousand souls died here tonight, and your death would pay for none of them. Because if I killed you, you would have what you want. And you do not deserve to have what you want.*

And Lanre laughed. It was a terrible sound, like the sound of a great bell cracking.

*Then you are no better than I am,* he said. *You have done this for the same reason I did what I did. Because you are angry. Because you are grieving. Because you want someone to suffer as you suffer.* He shook his head. *We are the same, you and I. We are both men who have lost everything, and who have decided that the world should pay.*

And he turned and walked away into the dark, with his six companions behind him, and Selitos let him go.

---

That is the end of the story, as it is usually told. But it is not the end.

Because Selitos did not sleep that night. He sat among the ruins of Myr Tariniel with his bleeding eye and the stone in his hand, and he thought about what Lanre had said. And the more he thought about it, the more he feared that it was true.

He had not cursed Lanre for justice. He had cursed him for grief. For rage. For the thousand thousand souls who had died, and for his own helplessness to save them. He had taken Lanre's name, his own true name, and twisted it into a shape of endless suffering, and bound it to him forever. And he had done it not because it was right, but because it was what he wanted.

And that is a very dangerous thing, for a namer to do. Because a name is a true thing. It is the shape of what a person is. And when you twist it, when you bend it to your will, you do not only change the one who bears it. You change yourself.

So Selitos looked into himself, that night, among the ruins. He looked with his one good eye, and with the eye he had put out, which could no longer see the world but could see other things. And he saw that Lanre was right. He had become something that he did not want to be. Something that took names and twisted them for vengeance.

And he wept.

---

*Skarpi stopped. He looked at me across the fire.*

*"That part isn't in any of the books," he said. "The Amyr didn't write it down. They didn't want anyone to know that their founder had wept, or why." He smiled faintly. "They preferred him stern and one-eyed and certain. It made a better symbol."*

*"How do you know it, then?"*

*Skarpi looked at me for a long moment. His bright bird's eyes were very old.*

*"Because I was there," he said. "I was a boy, then. A very young boy, younger than you were when your family died. I had lived in Myr Tariniel all my life. I was one of the children playing in the fountains." He looked into the fire. "I was the only one who lived."*

*I stared at him.*

*"That was—"*

*"A very long time ago. Yes." He nodded. "Longer than you can imagine. Longer than I like to think about." He smiled, a little wryly. "Selitos found me in the ruins, the morning after. I was hiding in a fallen fountain, under a slab of white marble. He dug me out with his own hands. And he looked at me with his one eye, and I looked at him, and neither of us said anything for a long time."*

*"And then?"*

*"And then he asked me what I wanted to be, when I grew up." Skarpi laughed softly. "Can you imagine? The city was burned. My parents were dead. Everyone I had ever known was dead. And this terrible bleeding man with one eye dug me out of the rubble and asked me what I wanted to be."*

*"What did you say?"*

*"I said I wanted to be a singer," said Skarpi. "Like my mother. She sang in the great hall of the city, and people came from all over the world to hear her." He was quiet for a moment. "Selitos looked at me for a long time. And then he said, 'Yes. Yes, I think that is exactly what the world will need.'"*

*He looked back into the fire, and took up the thread of the story again.*

---

Selitos went out from Myr Tariniel and gathered to himself those who had survived the war. Namers and warriors and scholars, and others who were not quite any of those things. And he made two orders.

The first was the Amyr. You know of them. They were to be the sword. They were to hunt the Seven wherever they went, and stop them wherever they could. They were to do whatever was necessary, however terrible, for the greater good. That was their oath and their curse. *For the greater good.* They were Selitos's anger, given form and purpose.

But the second order, fewer people know. Selitos called them the Singers. And they were not a sword. They were something else.

Selitos had understood, among the ruins, what his curse had done. He had bound Lanre to his name, and bound his name to a story. The story of what he had done at Myr Tariniel. And as long as that story was told truly, as long as people remembered it and sang it and wrote it down, the curse would hold. Lanre would remain Haliax. He would remain cursed, and unable to die, and unable to forget.

But if the story was forgotten, or changed, or told falsely, the curse would weaken. Because a curse spoken in a name is only as strong as the truth that holds it. And the truth is only as strong as the people who remember it.

So Selitos made the Singers. They were to be the keepers of the true story. They were to sing it, and tell it, and teach it, wherever they went. Not the pretty version. Not the heroic version, or the simple version with a clear villain and a clear hero. The true version. The whole of it. Lanre's love, and Lanre's fall, and Lanre's grief. Selitos's rage, and Selitos's tears. All of it.

Because only the true story would hold. A false story, however beautiful, would be a crack in the wall. And through that crack, over time, the curse would bleed away.

---

*"The Chandrian kill the Singers," I said slowly. "That's why they killed my parents. My father was writing the true song."*

*"Your father was writing a very good song," said Skarpi. "Not the true one, not all of it. He didn't have all the pieces. But he was close. Closer than anyone had come in a long time." He looked at me. "And your mother knew some things that she should not have known, from her own family. Things about the doors. She put them into the song, in little ways. Hints. Clues." He shook his head. "That made it doubly dangerous. It was a true song about Lanre, and a map to the keeper's door, both at once."*

*"So they came."*

*"So they came."*

*I was silent for a long time. The fire crackled. The sea breathed outside.*

*"And now someone's writing a false song," I said. "Denna. Bredon's making her write a song that makes Lanre a hero. To weaken the curse."*

*"Yes."*

*"And when the curse is weak enough—"*

*"Then he will open the door," said Skarpi quietly. "Or try to. And the Thief will come out. And the Thief, who can unmake anything, will unmake the curse." He paused. "And then, perhaps, many other things."*

*"The world."*

*"Perhaps. The Thief was never very fond of the world. He wanted a different one. One he had made himself." Skarpi looked into the fire. "That's what the war was about, in the end. Whether the world would be as it is, or as he wanted it to be."*

---

There is one more piece, *Skarpi said.* One more piece of the story, that I have never told anyone. Not in the Half-Mast, not in any tavern or hall. Because it is not a piece that should be told in public. It is a piece for the one it belongs to.

It is about the Thief. The one who stole the moon.

Before the war, before he became what he became, he was a man. A shaper, but a man. And like all men, he loved. Not only the moon. There was a woman, too, a mortal woman, who lived in the hills where the Lackless house now stands. He loved her for a little while, the way shapers love things, fiercely and briefly and without thought for what it would cost. And she bore him a child.

A daughter. The first of the line.

When the war was over and the Thief was sealed away beyond the doors, Selitos found that child, now grown, with children of her own. And he looked at her with his one eye, and he saw the Thief in her. In her blood, in her bones, in the shape of her name. He saw that she was bound to her father, as all children are bound to their parents, in ways that cannot be broken.

And he understood that this was the key.

Because the door that held the Thief was made to be opened by no one. But a door that cannot be opened by anyone is a door that cannot be kept. It will crack, over time. It will weaken. It will fail. A true lock needs a true key, so that it can be checked, and tended, and held.

So Selitos made the keeper's door. The first of the seven. And he made it so that it would open only to the Thief's own blood, with a key of stone, speaking the door's own name. And he gave the key to the Thief's daughter, and he bound her line to the keeping. *While the line holds, the door holds.* As long as the line lived, and kept the key, and kept the name close, the door would be tended and would not fail.

But it was also the greatest danger. Because the line that could keep the door could also open it. And if ever a child of that line came to the door with the key in their hand and the name on their lips, and chose to speak—

---

*Skarpi stopped. He looked at me across the fire, and his bright eyes were very sad.*

*"You know the rest," he said.*

*I did. I knew it the way you know the ending of a song you have heard a hundred times, even when the singer stops before the last verse.*

*"The Thief's daughter," I said. My voice sounded very far away. "She was the first Loeclos."*

*"Yes."*

*"And my mother was her descendant."*

*"Yes."*

*"And I—"*

*"And you are the Thief's own blood," said Skarpi gently. "Many, many times removed. Diluted by a thousand generations. But still there. Still bound." He paused. "And you are a namer. The first of the line in a thousand years who can speak true names. Who could, if you chose, speak the name of the door."*

*I sat very still. The fire was warm on my face. The sea breathed outside.*

*"That's why Haliax spared me," I said. "That's why Cinder didn't kill me. He is needed."*

*"Yes."*

*"They want me to open the door."*

*"They need you to open the door. No one else can. Not truly. Ambrose has a drop of the blood; he can carry the key, but he cannot turn it. Meluan has more of it, but she has no gift for names. Her child, when it is born, may have both, or neither. It will be many years before anyone knows." Skarpi looked at me. "You are the only one, Kvothe. Right now, in all the world, you are the only one who can open it."*

*"And I won't."*

*Skarpi smiled. It was a very old smile, and very tired.*

*"That's what your mother said," he said. "When she was your age. I told her the same story, in a tavern in the Lackless hills, long before she met your father. She said she would never go near the door. Never. And she kept that promise. She ran away from everything to keep it." He shook his head. "But you're not your mother. You're something else. You're a namer, and a Ruh, and you've never met a door you didn't want to see behind." He leaned forward. "So I'm going to ask you something. And I want you to think very carefully before you answer."*

*"What?"*

*"Will you sing?" said Skarpi. "Will you take your father's song, and your mother's hints, and all the pieces I've given you tonight, and make the true song? The whole of it? And will you sing it, where people can hear it, knowing what it will bring down on you?"*

*I looked at him across the fire.*

*"You're asking me to become a Singer."*

*"I'm asking you to become the last Singer," said Skarpi quietly. "Because I'm very old, Kvothe. Older than I have any right to be. And the others are all gone. The Chandrian found them, one by one, over the centuries. I'm the only one left." He looked into the fire. "And if the song isn't sung—truly, fully, well—then the girl's song will win. And the curse will break. And the door will open, whether you go to it or not." He looked up at me. "Because they'll make you. They'll find a way. They always do."*

*I sat there in the broken lighthouse for a long time, looking into the fire that burned on nothing.*

*Then I said yes.*

---

That is what Skarpi told me, and what I told him.

I have thought, since, that I should have said no. That if I had refused, if I had walked away from that lighthouse and taken ship for somewhere far away and never sung a note of the true song, perhaps things would have gone differently. Perhaps fewer people would have died.

But I do not think so. Not really. Not at the bottom, where the truth lives.

I think the board was already set. I think the stones were already placed. I think that Bredon, and Skarpi, and Selitos, and the Thief behind his door, had all been playing their games for so long that there was no move left to me that was truly mine.

Except one. There was always one.

But I did not know that yet.
