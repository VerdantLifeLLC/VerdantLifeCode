# A Council in the Rocks

They came the next morning, up the same path Hallow's Company had come up the night before. Four of them, on tired horses, with a mule carrying their packs.

I was sitting at the Maer's fire at the head of the valley, eating a heel of Mirra's bread, when I saw them. I did not recognize them at first. They were dusty and ragged and thin, like everyone else in the valley. They rode slowly, with their heads down.

Then the one in front looked up, and I saw a thatch of straw-colored hair and a thin eager face, and I dropped my bread.

"Sim," I said.

He saw me. His face went through several shapes very quickly. Then he slid off his horse and ran up the valley toward me, stumbling on the rocks, and I stood up and ran down to meet him, and we collided somewhere in the middle and nearly knocked each other over.

"You're alive," he said. "You're alive, you're alive, you're *alive*—"

"You say that every time."

"Because every time you're *alive*, Kvothe! Every time I think you're dead and you're not!" He was laughing and crying at the same time. "Five months! Five months, and not a word! We thought you were lost in the Fae forever!"

"I was. For a while."

Behind him, the others had dismounted. Wil, with his scarred hands held carefully at his sides. Devi, in a dusty travelling cloak with her spectacles pushed up on her forehead and her satchel over her shoulder. And Fela, pale and thin, with her dark hair braided and a look on her face that I could not read.

Wil came up and embraced me, briefly and hard, the way he always did.

"You owe me more drinks," he said. "I've lost count."

"I'll pay you back."

"You keep saying that."

Devi did not embrace me. She stood in front of me with her hands on her hips and glared at me.

"Five months," she said. "Do you know how much interest accrues in five months?"

"Hello, Devi."

"Don't you *hello Devi* me." Her voice cracked. Then she threw her arms around my neck and held on so tight I could not breathe. "You idiot," she said into my shoulder. "You stupid, stupid idiot."

Fela came last. She stood in front of me and looked at me for a long moment with her dark serious eyes.

"Do you have it?" she said.

I reached into my pocket and took out the flat grey stone from the Eolian's foundation. I held it out.

"I haven't given it back yet," I said. "I told you I would, when I came back."

She looked at it. Then she closed my fingers over it with her own.

"Keep it a little longer," she said. "You're not back yet. Not really."

---

They had come south from Imre when the news reached them that Severen had fallen. Threpe had paid for everything: the horses, the supplies, the bribes at the border. He had wanted to come himself, but his physician had forbidden it. So he had sent them instead, with a purse of gold and a letter for the Maer and a great deal of advice about the proper wines to serve at a war council.

"He said to tell you," said Sim, "that if you die, he'll never forgive you. And that he's commissioning a statue of you for the square in front of the Eolian, and he'll make them give you a very small nose out of spite."

"That's very like him."

"And Stanchion's—" Sim stopped. "Deoch's rebuilding the Eolian. He started a month ago. He's doing it himself, stone by stone. The way Stanchion did." He looked at me. "He said to tell you it'll be ready for you. When you come back. He said you owe him a song."

I did not say anything. I could not.

---

We held a council that afternoon, in the mouth of the Maer's cave.

It was the strangest council I have ever been part of. The Maer sat on a flat rock at the center, with his bandaged arm in his lap, looking like a king in exile, which he was. His captains stood around him, grim and hard-faced men in battered armor. Stapes stood at his elbow, calm and precise.

Tessit and Mirra were there, for the Ruh. Tessit had taken off his battered hat and was turning it in his hands.

Sim and Wil and Devi and Fela were there, sitting on the rocks in a row like students at a lecture.

Bast was there, sitting on a rock a little apart from everyone else, with his legs drawn up and his hooves hidden under his cloak. The Maer's captains kept glancing at him and then glancing away.

And I was there. Standing in the middle of it all, with Denna's cord inside my shirt and the door's name sitting in my bones like a stone.

"Four days," I said. "Maybe five. Until the dark of the moon. That's when they'll open the door."

"Where?" said one of the captains. A lean grey man named Dagon, with a face like a hatchet.

"Beneath the Lackless house. In the hills near Levinshir." I looked at the Maer. "Your grace knows it?"

"I know it." The Maer's voice was flat. "My wife's family seat. It has been empty for twenty years. Since her father died. She would never go there." He paused. "She said it was cold."

"The king's army is marching there now," I said. "They'll arrive in two or three days. They'll wait for the dark of the moon. And then they'll go down to the door."

"How many men?" said Dagon.

"Ten thousand, when they took Severen," said another captain. "Less now. They've left garrisons behind. Seven thousand, maybe. Eight."

"And we have three hundred," said Dagon. "And a handful of Ruh and refugees." He looked at me. "We cannot fight them."

"No," I said. "We can't."

"Then what—"

"We don't fight them. We go around them. Under them. Through them." I looked around the circle. "We don't need to beat their army. We only need to do two things. Get the Maer's wife and daughter out of the king's camp. And get me to the door."

The circle was silent.

"You want to go to the door," said the Maer slowly. "The door they want to open. The door they need you to open."

"Yes."

"Why?"

I looked at him. I looked at all of them. At Sim and Wil and Devi and Fela, at Tessit and Mirra, at Bast.

"Because I know how to close it," I said. "For good. So no one can ever open it again. Not them, not the baby, not anyone." I paused. "But I have to be there. At the door. With the key in my hand."

"The key," said the Maer. "The box. The king wears it around his neck."

"Yes."

"So you need to take it from him."

"Yes."

The circle was silent again. Longer, this time.

"That," said Dagon at last, "is the most insane plan I have ever heard."

"I know."

"You want to walk into the middle of an army of eight thousand men, steal the king's own necklace off his neck, walk out again, and go down into a hole in the ground to fight whatever's waiting for you there." He stared at me. "Alone?"

"Not alone," I said.

And that was when the Adem arrived.

---

They came up the lower path in single file, as silent as shadows. Twelve of them. Small and slim, in the blood-red clothes of the Adem mercenaries, close-fitting and bound with a hundred cords. They walked with the easy, unhurried grace of people who know exactly how dangerous they are and do not need to prove it to anyone.

The soldiers at the lower end of the valley saw them and reached for their weapons. The Adem did not stop. They did not even look at the soldiers. They walked straight up the valley, through the camp, past the fires and the refugees and the staring children, until they came to the mouth of the Maer's cave.

The one in front stopped. She was small and plain, with short dark hair and a face that showed nothing at all. There was a sword on her back.

"Vashet," I said.

"Kvothe." She looked at me. Her hands moved. *Relief. Anger. Something else.* "You are alive."

"Barely."

"Barely is enough." She looked around the circle. At the Maer, and his captains, and my friends, and the Ruh, and Bast. Her eyes lingered on Bast for a long moment. Then she looked back at me. "I told you I would come back. Before the end."

"You did."

"This is the end." It was not a question.

"Yes."

"Good." She turned to the Maer and bowed, a small precise Adem bow. "I am Vashet, of the school of Haert. These are my brothers and sisters. Shehyn has sent us." She paused. "We are yours. Until this is done."

The Maer stared at her. Then at the eleven others standing silent behind her. Then at me.

"Kvothe," he said. "What is this?"

"This," I said, "is how we get to the door."

---

Behind Vashet, one of the Adem stepped forward. A man, tall for an Adem, lean and quiet, with pale eyes and a face I knew.

"Tempi," I said.

He looked at me. His face did not change. But his hands moved. *Greeting. Joy. Family.*

I had not seen him since Haert. Since the day he brought me there, to be judged by his school, for the crime of letting a barbarian learn the Ketan. He had stood beside me through it all. He had been my first teacher among the Adem, and my friend.

I crossed the circle and took his hands in mine, the Adem way. He held them. His grip was very strong.

"You came," I said.

"Of course," he said. His voice was quiet and careful, the way it always was, in a language that was not his own. "You are my student. You are in trouble. Where else would I be?"

---

We planned all afternoon, and into the night.

Tessit had the first idea. He was sitting on his rock, turning his battered hat in his hands, listening to the captains argue, when he said, quite suddenly, "We played there. Five days ago. In the king's camp."

Everyone turned to look at him.

"They let us in," he said. "The soldiers. They like music. They're bored, marching all day. And the king likes it too, they said. He had us play in front of his pavilion. We got very close." He looked at me. "We could play there again. On the road to the Lackless house. They're camped every night. We could ask to play. They'd let us in."

"And I'd be with you," I said slowly.

"You'd be with us. You're Ruh. You'd fit right in." Tessit smiled faintly. "We'd have to do something about the hair, though."

"Bast can do that."

Everyone looked at Bast. He looked back at them, a little startled.

"I can," he said. "A glamour. I can make his hair brown. Or black. Or green, if you like." He grinned suddenly. "I could give him a big nose. Like on the posters."

"Brown will do," I said.

"And once you're in?" said Dagon. "Once you're in the camp, close to the king, playing your little songs. Then what?"

"Then we get to the Maer's wife and daughter," I said. "And we get them out."

"How?"

I looked at Vashet. She looked back at me, and her hands moved. *Leave it to us.*

"The Adem," I said. "While the Ruh are playing, and everyone's watching them, the Adem will be in the camp. They'll get to the lady's tent. They'll get her and the baby out."

"And the key? The box around the king's neck?"

I did not answer right away. I looked at Devi.

She looked back at me. Then she smiled her spider's smile and patted the satchel on her lap.

"I've been working on something," she said. "On the road down from Imre. Something for exactly this."

"What is it?"

"A surprise." Her smile widened. "You'll like it. It makes a lot of noise."

---

It was very late when the council finally broke up. People drifted away to their caves and tents and fires. The Maer went into his cave to sleep. His captains went to see to their men. Tessit and Mirra went back to their wagons.

I stayed by the fire. I sat on a flat rock with my lute in my lap and did not play it.

After a while, Bast came and sat beside me.

"It's a good plan," he said.

"It's a terrible plan."

"It's a terrible plan," Bast agreed. "But it's a good terrible plan. The best kind." He looked at me. "Are you afraid?"

"Yes."

"Good. Only fools aren't afraid." He was quiet for a moment. "Reshi. The door. What you're going to do there. To close it." He hesitated. "It's going to cost you something. Isn't it? Something big."

I did not answer.

"I heard some of what Selitos said," Bast went on quietly. "In the tower. I was pretending to sleep. I heard him say you'd have to give up your name." He looked at me. "Is that true?"

I looked at him for a long time.

"Yes," I said.

Bast was silent. He looked into the fire.

"And the girl," he said at last. "The one with the knots. She's going to hold it for you."

"If she's willing."

"She is. I saw your face when you read her letter. She said she would." Bast's voice was very quiet. "You're going to give your name to the girl who's bound to the man who wants it most."

"Yes."

"That's insane."

"I know."

"The Cthaeh said—"

"I know what the Cthaeh said." I looked at him. "Bast. There's no one else. There's no one else in the world who can do it. Not Elodin, not Fela, not Selitos. Only her. And she's willing." I paused. "And her knot's loosened. The song loosened it. She's not his, not all the way. Not anymore."

"And if she is? If he pulls the cord, and she gives it to him?"

I did not answer.

Bast looked at me for a long time. Then he looked away.

"Then I'll kill him," he said quietly. "I'll find him, and I'll kill him, and I'll take it back. And I'll keep it safe myself."

"Bast—"

"I mean it." He turned to look at me. His deep blue eyes were very bright in the firelight. "I owe you a life, Reshi. The old kind. I'll pay it. Whatever it costs." He paused. "Whatever it costs *me*."

I looked at him. At his fine-boned face and his fierce wet eyes.

*You will keep him in a cage of your own making*, the Cthaeh had said to him. *And you will call it love.*

I did not know that, then. He had not told me. But I think I felt it, somehow. The shape of it. The weight.

"Thank you, Bast," I said.

He looked at me for a long moment. Then he smiled, a small, fierce, crooked smile.

"Don't thank me," he said. "Just don't die."
