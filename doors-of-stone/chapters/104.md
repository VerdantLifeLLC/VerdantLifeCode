# Interlude: Ferule

It was not the name *Ferule*.

Chronicler would understand that later, much later, when he had time to think about it. *Ferule* was what Haliax called him. It was a name, the way *Cinder* was a name, the way *Kote* had been a name. Something to be called by. Something small.

What Kvothe spoke, standing in the small cold room at the top of the stairs of the Waystone Inn, was something else.

It was not a word. Chronicler could not have written it down, not in his cipher or in Aturan or in any language that has letters. It was a sound, but not only a sound. It was a shape. A long, winding, intricate shape, like a melody, like a knot, like the path a river takes through a valley. It came out of Kvothe's mouth and filled the room, and the room seemed to lean toward it, the way a flower leans toward the sun.

And it was beautiful.

That was the thing that Chronicler would never forget, and never be able to explain. Cinder's name was beautiful. It was the most beautiful thing he had ever heard. It was like the first line of a song he had loved as a child and forgotten. It was like the sound of someone singing in a great hall, a very long time ago, with people come from all over the world to listen.

Cinder heard it.

He went very still. His burned hands, raised in front of him, dropped to his sides. His black eyes went wide. His crooked scarred face went slack.

"*No*," he whispered. "*No. No. Not that one. Not that one. No one knows that one.*"

"I know it," said Kvothe quietly.

"*How?*"

"A woman in a forest told me," said Kvothe. "A long time ago. On the Moon's Road, beside a spring. She kissed my wrist where you'd held it, and she told me who you'd been." He was quiet for a moment. "She said you had the most beautiful voice. She said you sang at the Thief's court, before the war. She said people came from all over the world to hear you." He looked at Cinder. "I didn't understand, then. I didn't know what she was giving me. I only heard a story." He paused. "But stories are names too. The oldest kind."

---

He began to sing.

Not *Tariniel*. Not any song Chronicler had ever heard. It had no words, or none that Chronicler could understand. It was Cinder's name, the beautiful winding shape of it, sung. Slowly. Softly. The way a mother sings to a child who cannot sleep.

And as he sang, Chronicler saw things.

He did not know how. He was sitting on the narrow bed in the innkeeper's room, with the pages of the story scattered around his feet, and he was seeing things that were not in the room. Things that were not anywhere. Things that had not been anywhere for ten thousand years.

He saw a great hall. Bigger than any hall in the world. With a ceiling of stars. And in the center of it, on a stage of white stone, a young man was singing.

He was tall and pale and very beautiful, with hair the color of frost and eyes that were not black. They were grey. Grey like the sky before snow. And his voice—

Chronicler had no words for his voice.

He saw the young man singing. He saw the crowd listening. Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. Men and women and things that were not men or women, from every corner of a world that no longer existed. He saw them weeping. He saw them laughing. He saw them sitting very still, with their faces turned up toward the stage, the way people sit when they are hearing something they will remember for the rest of their lives.

And he saw, at the back of the hall, on a great dark throne, a figure watching. Tall and dark and terrible, with something behind its eyes that Chronicler could not look at. Something hungry. Something that wanted.

And he saw the figure lean forward, and whisper something to the young man with the frost-colored hair, after the song was done.

And he saw the young man's face change.

---

Kvothe sang.

He sang the young man's choice. He sang the war that followed. He sang the young man going with the Thief, and with Lanre, and with the others. Not because he was wicked. Not because he wanted to. Because he had been promised something. Because he had been told that if he helped, if he fought, if he did what was asked, he would have a hall of his own someday. A hall with a ceiling of stars. Where he could sing forever, and people would come from every corner of the world to hear him, and no one would ever tell him to stop.

He sang the curse.

He sang Selitos, standing among the ashes of Myr Tariniel with his bleeding eye, speaking the words that bound the seven. He sang the young man's voice going cold. Going silent. He sang the frost coming into him, from the inside, the way frost comes into a pond in winter. Freezing the song. Freezing the voice. Freezing everything he had ever been.

He sang the young man never singing again.

---

Cinder was on his knees.

Chronicler did not see him fall. He was simply there, on his knees on the wet floor of the innkeeper's room, with his burned hands pressed against his face. His shoulders were shaking.

He was weeping.

Chronicler had not known that the Chandrian could weep. He had not known that anything so cold could make a sound so broken. It was a thin high keening sound, like wind through a crack in a wall, like ice breaking on a winter lake.

Kvothe stopped singing.

The room was silent except for the sound of Cinder weeping.

"You remember," said Kvothe quietly.

"*Stop*," whispered Cinder through his hands. "*Stop it. Please. Please stop.*"

"You remember what you were. Before. What you wanted. What you gave up." Kvothe's voice was very gentle. "It hurts. Doesn't it? The way it hurts him, when someone sings his story true. That's the curse. That's what Selitos did to all of you. Not just the cold. Not just the signs. The remembering. Every time someone sings it true, you remember." He was quiet for a moment. "That's why you kill the singers. Not because the songs are dangerous. Because they hurt."

"*Please.*"

"I'm not going to kill you," said Kvothe.

Cinder went very still.

"I could," said Kvothe. "I know your name. I could speak it with anger, the way Selitos spoke Lanre's, and I could unmake you. Or try." He looked down at the weeping thing on the floor. "I've wanted to since I was eleven years old. Since I came back through the woods and found my family dead, and you crouching in front of me with your black eyes and your smile." His voice did not change. "I've wanted it more than I've ever wanted anything."

Cinder did not move.

"But Selitos did that," said Kvothe. "A long time ago. He spoke a name in anger. And he's been sitting in a tower in the ruins of a white city for ten thousand years, wondering if he was right." He was quiet for a moment. "I don't want to sit in a tower. I've already sat in an inn for seven years. That was enough."

---

He knelt down. On the wet floor, in front of Cinder. So that their faces were level.

Cinder took his hands away from his face. His black eyes were wet. His crooked scarred face was twisted with grief.

"Go," said Kvothe. "Go now. Out of this house. Out of this town. Take your cold with you." He looked into Cinder's black eyes. "And don't come back. Not here. Not to anyone I love. Not ever."

"*Or?*" Cinder's voice was a thread.

Kvothe looked at him for a long moment.

"Or I'll sing it again," he said quietly. "All of it. The whole of it. In a great hall, before a thousand people. Your song. The one about the young man with the frost-colored hair and the most beautiful voice in the world." He paused. "And I'll put in the part about the grey eyes. And the ceiling of stars. And the promise."

Cinder stared at him.

"And everyone who hears it," said Kvothe, "will remember you. The way you were. Before. They'll remember you singing. And they'll weep for you." His voice was very soft. "Every time. For the rest of your life. Which is to say, forever."

---

Cinder looked at him for a very long time.

Then he got up. Slowly. Unsteadily. The way a man gets up who has been very ill, or very drunk, or very badly hurt. He stood in the small wet room with his burned hands hanging at his sides and his black eyes wet and his scarred face slack.

He looked at Kvothe. He looked at Chronicler, sitting on the bed. He looked at the open chest, with the lute and the box and the folded paper inside it. He looked at the pale sword, lying broken on the floor.

He did not pick it up.

"He'll come," he said. His voice was very quiet. Not soft. Not reasonable. Just quiet. Tired. "The lord. He'll come for you himself, now. You know he will."

"I know."

"And the others. Usnea. Stercus. The rest." Cinder shook his head slowly. "They won't be as easy as me. They don't have—" He stopped. "They don't have songs. Not anymore. They've forgotten them."

"Then I'll find them," said Kvothe. "The songs. I'll find them, and I'll learn them, and I'll sing them." He paused. "Every one."

Cinder looked at him for a long moment. Something moved in his black eyes. It was there and gone so quickly that Chronicler almost missed it.

It might have been fear.

It might have been hope.

Then he turned and walked out of the room, and down the stairs, and out of the Waystone Inn. Chronicler heard his footsteps, slow and uneven, crossing the common room. He heard the door. And then nothing.

The last of the blue light guttered out.

And every candle in the Waystone Inn burned yellow.

---

Kvothe was already moving.

He was down the stairs before Chronicler could stand up from the bed. Chronicler followed, stumbling, his legs stiff with cold. He came down into the common room and stopped at the foot of the stairs.

The frost was gone. The floor was wet, puddled everywhere with melted ice. The fire in the great stone hearth was burning yellow, bright and warm. Real warmth. Chronicler felt it on his face like a hand.

The door was standing open. Beyond it, the night was very dark and very still. There was no frost on the porch.

And in the middle of the common room, in a pool of melted frost and blood, Bast was lying on his side.

Kvothe was kneeling beside him.

---

He had Bast's head in his lap. He had one hand pressed against the wound in Bast's side. There was blood everywhere. On the floor, on Bast's clothes, on Kvothe's hands. Very red. Very bright. Too much of it.

Bast's eyes were open. They were not black anymore. They were deep blue, the color of the sky just after sunset. They were looking up at Kvothe's face.

"Reshi," he whispered.

"I'm here."

"You're—" Bast's voice was very faint. "You're back. Aren't you? You're really back."

"I'm back."

Bast looked up at him for a long moment. Then he smiled. A small, weak, crooked smile.

"I knew it," he whispered. "I knew you would. I knew—"

His eyes began to close.

"No," said Kvothe. "No, Bast. Stay awake. Stay with me."

"I'm tired, Reshi."

"I know. I know you are. Stay with me anyway."

Bast's eyes fluttered. His breathing was very shallow.

Kvothe looked down at the wound in his side. At the blood coming out between his fingers. His face was very still.

Then he closed his eyes.

---

Chronicler did not know what he did. He would never know, not exactly. He knelt on the wet floor a few paces away and watched, and he did not understand.

He saw Kvothe's lips move. He saw his hand, pressed against the wound, begin to glow. Very faintly. A soft warm golden light, like candlelight through a window.

He felt the air change. Tighten. The way air tightens before a storm.

He heard Kvothe speak. Very softly. Words Chronicler did not know. Not names, he thought. Something else. Something smaller. The kind of words a sympathist uses, binding one thing to another, making them the same.

"*Blood to blood*," Chronicler heard. "*Mine to his. Skin to skin. Closed. Closed. Closed.*"

The golden light brightened. Just a little.

And Bast gasped.

It was a small sound. A sharp intake of breath. His eyes flew open. His back arched. His hand came up and clutched at Kvothe's wrist.

And then he went limp.

And then, very slowly, he began to breathe. Deeper. Steadier. The shallow fluttering breaths of a dying man giving way to the slow even breaths of a man asleep.

Kvothe took his hand away from the wound.

It was closed. Not healed. There was still blood, and a long angry red line where the pale sword had gone in. But it was closed. The skin had drawn together over it, the way skin draws together over a wound after many days of healing.

Kvothe looked down at it. His face was grey with effort. His hands were shaking.

But he was smiling.

"Sympathy," he said. His voice was hoarse. "It's been seven years. I didn't know if I still could." He looked down at his own left wrist. At the thin pale scar around it. At a fresh cut, just below the scar, bleeding slowly. "Blood to blood. The oldest binding there is. I gave him some of mine. To close the hole." He laughed, very weakly. "Arwyl would have my hide. It's not a thing they teach in the Medica."

"Will he live?" said Chronicler.

Kvothe looked down at Bast, sleeping in his lap with his dark hair across his face.

"Yes," he said. "He'll live. He's very stubborn. And he owes me a life. The old kind." He brushed the hair out of Bast's eyes. "He's not allowed to die until he's paid it."

---

They sat there for a long time, on the wet floor of the common room, in the warm yellow light of the fire. Kvothe, with Bast's head in his lap. Chronicler, a few paces away, with his back against the leg of a table and his hands, which could feel again, lying open in his lap.

Nobody said anything.

Outside, beyond the open door, the night was very dark and very still. Somewhere far off, a dog was barking. Somewhere closer, an owl called, and was answered.

And somewhere, very far away, beyond the fields and the woods and the dark, a thin grey line of smoke that had been leaning all day toward the Waystone Inn straightened, and thinned, and blew away on a wind that had not been there before.

It was a warm wind. It smelled of rain.
