@@PART PART ONE: THE NAME OF STONE

# Chapter One: Return

Money is a kind of weather. When you have none, it is all you can think about, the way a man lost in a snowstorm can think of nothing but the cold. When you have enough, you stop noticing it, the way you stop noticing a pleasant afternoon. But when you have more than enough, when you have suddenly and unexpectedly been handed more than you have ever held, it becomes a storm of a different sort. It gets into your head. It changes the way you walk.

I came back to the University with the Maer's gold in my purse and his letter of patronage folded in my shirt, and I walked like a man who owned the road.

I want to be clear. I was not rich, not the way the sons of barons were rich, the ones who lived in the Golden Pony and paid a man to carry their books. But I had enough to pay my tuition for a year. I had enough to settle my debt with Devi, which was the kind of debt that followed a man into his dreams. I had enough to buy new clothes and a good pair of boots and to eat meat every day of the week if I chose. I had enough, for the first time since I was a boy of twelve sleeping on the rooftops of Tarbean, to stop counting.

That was the danger. When you stop counting, you stop paying attention. And I have always been at my worst when I have stopped paying attention.

---

The road from Severen had been long and wet. By the time the stone bridge came into view, rising over the Omethi river in its great impossible arch, I had been traveling for the better part of a month, and I was tired in the particular way a man gets tired when he has been sleeping in inns where the sheets were changed last spring.

But when I saw the bridge, I felt something loosen in my chest.

It is hard to explain what the University was to me. It was not home, not the way the wagons of my troupe had been home. It was not safe; I had been nearly killed there more than once. But it was the place where I had first understood that the world was a thing that could be learned. It was the place where I had found friends who did not want anything from me but my company. It was the place where the Archives stood, windowless and grey, holding every answer I had ever wanted and a thousand more I had not yet thought to ask.

I crossed the bridge at a walk, though every part of me wanted to run. At the top of the arch I stopped, as I always did, and looked out over the river. The water was high and brown with the autumn rains. The trees along the banks had gone gold and red. And there, rising above the rooftops of the town, was the Archives, looking just as it always had: a great square block of stone with no windows at all, like a secret that had decided to stand up in the middle of the road and dare anyone to ask it questions.

I smiled. I could not help it.

Then I went down the far side of the bridge and into Imre, and the first thing I did was buy a pie.

---

It was a meat pie, and it was not a very good one. The crust was too thick and the gravy too thin, and there was something in the meat that might have been rabbit and might have been something with more legs. But I ate it standing in the street outside the baker's shop with gravy running down my wrist, and I have eaten meals at the tables of kings that did not taste half so fine.

When I had finished, I licked my fingers, wiped my hands on my new trousers, and went to find my friends.

I found Simmon first. It was not difficult. It was midafternoon, which meant that Sim would be in one of three places: in the Archives, pretending to study; in the Mews, actually sleeping; or at Anker's, drinking cider and arguing about poetry with anyone who would sit still long enough.

He was at Anker's. He was arguing about poetry. The person he was arguing with was a very bored-looking second-year I did not recognize, who had the glazed expression of a man who has made a terrible mistake and is only now beginning to understand its scope.

"—because meter is not a *cage*," Sim was saying, with the fierce earnestness he brought to everything. "It's a trellis. You grow the words along it. And if you just throw it away, the whole thing sprawls. You get—"

"Weeds," I said, dropping into the chair beside him.

Sim turned. He stared at me. Then his face split open in a smile so wide and so bright that I felt my own face answer it before I could stop myself.

"Kvothe!" He threw his arms around me, nearly upsetting the table. The second-year took this opportunity to flee, and I don't think either of us noticed. "Tehlu's balls, you're back! You're actually back! We thought—Wil said—" He pulled back and looked at me, still gripping my shoulders. "You look *terrible*."

"Thank you."

"No, I mean—you look older. And you've got—" He frowned. "Is that a scar?"

I touched my cheek. There was a thin line there, pale against the tan I had picked up on the road. A souvenir of Haert, and of a woman named Vashet who had very firm ideas about how swiftly a student ought to learn to get out of the way.

"It's a long story," I said.

"You always say that."

"They're always long stories. I don't know what to tell you. I lead a very complicated life." I signaled to Anker, who was behind the bar watching us with an expression of mild amusement. He nodded and reached for a mug. "Where's Wil?"

"Archives. He's been working the desk three shifts a week since you left. Lorren likes him. He says Wil has the soul of a filing cabinet." Sim grinned. "Wil took it as a compliment."

"It probably was."

"That's what worries me." Sim sat back, looking at me with bright eyes. "So. Tell me everything. Where have you been? What happened with the Maer? Did you find the Amyr? Did you—" He stopped. His face did something awkward. "Did you see Denna?"

The mug came. I drank from it to give myself time. It was cider, cold and sharp, and it tasted like home.

"I'll tell you," I said. "But not now, and not here. Let's find Wil first. It's a story I'd only like to tell once."

Sim looked at me for a moment longer. He was not the most perceptive of people. He was too kind for it, and too honest; he took people at their word because it would never have occurred to him to do otherwise. But he had known me for more than two years, and he was not a fool.

"All right," he said quietly. "Wil gets off shift at sixth bell. We'll go to the Eolian."

I nodded. Then, because I could not stop myself, I asked, "Has she been here?"

Sim did not have to ask who I meant.

"Once," he said. "About two months ago. She came to the Eolian and sang, and then she was gone again. She asked about you."

"What did she ask?"

"Whether you were back. Whether we'd heard from you. The usual things." He hesitated. "She looked well, Kvothe. She looked happy."

I nodded and drank my cider and did not say anything. Something in my chest twisted, and I could not have told you whether it was pleasure or pain. With Denna it was often both.

---

I spent the rest of the afternoon settling back into myself.

First I went to see Anker about my room. It was still there, the narrow little space under the eaves at the top of the stairs, with its window that opened onto the rooftops. Anker had kept it for me, though I had been gone for most of a year and had not paid a penny in rent. When I tried to pay him now, he waved me off.

"You played here," he said, "before you left. Twice a span. The place was full every time. I figure that's worth something." He scratched his chin. "Course, if you want to start playing again, I won't say no."

"Two nights a span," I said. "Same as before."

He smiled. "Same as before."

It is a small thing, to have a room waiting for you. But I had spent a great deal of my life without one, and I climbed the stairs feeling lighter than I had in months. I opened the door. The room was just as I had left it: the narrow bed, the small table, the window. Someone, probably Anker, had dusted. There was a faint smell of lavender, which was strange until I saw the small bundle of dried flowers tied with a bit of blue thread and set upon the windowsill.

I knew who had left it there.

I went to the window and looked out at the rooftops. The sun was going down, painting the slate and tile with gold. Somewhere out there, beneath the University, in the dark tunnels and forgotten rooms of the Underthing, a small girl with hair like a dandelion's ghost was going about her business.

I would go and see her tonight, I decided. Once the moon was up.

---

Wil was waiting for us outside the Eolian when Sim and I arrived. He stood with his hands in his pockets and his dark Cealdish face as unreadable as ever, but when he saw me his eyebrows went up a fraction of an inch, which for Wil was the equivalent of dancing in the street.

"You're alive," he said.

"Evidently."

He considered this. "Good," he said. And then, with no change of expression whatsoever, he stepped forward and embraced me, briefly and hard, and stepped back again. "You owe me three drinks."

"I owe you a great deal more than that."

"True. But three drinks is a place to begin." He gestured to the door. "Shall we?"

The Eolian was just as I remembered it. The great round room with its high ceiling. The stage in the center, raised like an altar. The balconies circling above, where the wealthy and the wellborn sat to look down on the rest of us. The smell of wine and smoke and wood polish, and beneath it, faint and sweet, the smell of music. That sounds foolish, I know. But any musician will tell you that a place where music is played well and often takes on a smell of its own, the way a church takes on the smell of incense even when no incense is burning.

Stanchion was behind the bar. He saw me as I came in and his face broke into a broad grin beneath his beard.

"Look what the cat dragged in!" he boomed. "Kvothe! We'd thought you'd run off to be a pirate."

"I considered it," I said. "But the hours were terrible."

He laughed and came around the bar to clap me on the shoulder. Stanchion was a big man, and his claps on the shoulder were not gentle things. I staggered a little and he laughed again. "Your pipes still good?"

I touched the silver pipes pinned to my cloak. "Still good."

"Then you'll play tonight."

"I don't have my lute. It's at Anker's."

"Then go get it." He grinned. "I'm serious. We've had nothing but fiddlers for two spans. Fiddlers and one very sad man with a hurdy-gurdy. The crowd is starving. You play, and your drinks are free."

I glanced at Sim and Wil. Sim was nodding enthusiastically. Wil shrugged, which from Wil was a ringing endorsement.

"All right," I said. "But I have to tell these two a story first."

---

We took a table in the corner, away from the stage. I bought the first round, and the second, and I told them everything.

Well. Not everything. There were things about Felurian I left out, for reasons of both discretion and self-preservation. And there were things about the Cthaeh I did not tell them, because I did not know how, and because some part of me was afraid that if I spoke of it aloud, it would become more real.

But I told them about the Maer, and his troubles with the poisoner Caudicus. I told them about the bandits in the Eld, and the Amyr, or rather, the lack of Amyr. I told them about the Adem, and Vashet, and Tempi, and the long hard days of learning the Ketan. I told them about my sword, which I had left in my room at Anker's because it did not seem polite to wear a sword to a tavern where people played music. And I told them about the Maer's marriage to Meluan Lackless, and how I had helped to arrange it with songs and letters, and how he had rewarded me.

When I finished, Sim was staring at me with his mouth open. Wil had not moved at all, except to drink.

"That's," Sim said. He shook his head. "That's the craziest thing I have ever heard."

"All of it's true."

"I know. That's what makes it crazy." He ran his hands through his hair. "You went into the Fae. You actually went into the Fae. And came out again."

"Keep your voice down."

"Why? Nobody here will believe it." He laughed, a little wildly. "I'm not sure I believe it, and I know you."

Wil set down his mug. "What did Felurian look like?"

"Wil!" Sim hit him on the arm.

"It is a scholarly question."

"It is not a scholarly question. It's a *prurient* question."

"The two are not mutually exclusive." Wil looked at me with his dark, patient eyes. "Well?"

I thought about how to answer that. Then I thought about how she would feel if she knew I had described her to a pair of students in a tavern, and I decided that I did not want to find out.

"She was beautiful," I said. "Beyond that, I'd rather keep my skin."

Wil nodded slowly, as if this confirmed something he had long suspected. "Sensible."

We sat in silence for a moment. On the stage, a fiddler was finishing a reel. The crowd applauded politely. Sim looked at me across the table, and his face was serious for once.

"And the Chandrian?" he asked. "Did you learn anything?"

I hesitated. Then I shook my head. "Not much. Not enough. The Maer's library had a few old books that mentioned them, but nothing I didn't already know. Stories. Hints. Nobody who's actually seen them and lived." I paused. "Except me."

"You'll find something," Sim said. "You always do."

I smiled at him, and I did not say what I was thinking, which was this: I had been at the University for more than two years. I had spent more hours in the Archives than I could count. I had read every book on the Chandrian I could find, and every book on the Amyr, and every book that mentioned either of them even in passing. And I had learned almost nothing.

It was as if someone had gone through the Archives before me with a sharp knife, cutting out every page that mattered.

At the time I thought that was only a turn of phrase.

---

I went back to Anker's and got my lute and came back to the Eolian and played until my fingers ached.

I played "Tinker Tanner" to warm up, because the crowd was restless and it's hard for any crowd to stay restless in the face of a tinker's song. I played "The Lay of Sir Savien Traliard" because I had not played it in a long time and wanted to know if I still could. I played two songs I had learned from the Adem, which are not really songs at all but more like patterns of breath and silence, and the Eolian went so quiet you could hear the candles burning. And I played a song I had written on the road back from Severen, a small, sad thing about a girl who never stayed in one place long enough to be found.

I did not look up at the balconies while I played that one. I did not look for a dark-haired girl with a ring on her finger. I did not look for her the way you do not look at the sun, because you know that if you do, you will be blind for a long time after.

When I finished, the room was silent. Then the applause came, rolling down from the balconies like thunder. I bowed. I smiled. I felt, for a moment, the old sweet hot joy of it, the joy of holding a roomful of people in the palm of your hand.

Then I went back to the table where Sim and Wil were waiting, and Stanchion sent over a bottle of wine, and the three of us drank it, and for a few hours I forgot to be anything but seventeen and glad to be home.

---

It was late when I left the Eolian. The streets of Imre were dark and mostly empty. The moon was up, three-quarters full, silver and serene. I crossed the bridge back to the University with my lute on my back and my head buzzing pleasantly with wine.

I did not go to Anker's. Instead I went to a small courtyard behind the Mains, where a grate in the ground led down into darkness. I had brought a few things with me in my pockets: a bit of cheese wrapped in cloth, a small sack of dried apples, a sprig of mint I had picked on the road that morning and kept in a twist of paper.

I knelt by the grate and whistled softly. Three notes, rising.

For a long moment nothing happened. Then, far below, I heard a faint answering sound, a patter of bare feet on stone. A small pale face appeared at the bottom of the shaft, looking up at me with enormous eyes.

"Kvothe," Auri breathed. "You came back."

"I did," I said. "I brought you some things."

She tilted her head. "What sort of things?"

"Good things. Come up and see."

She scrambled up through the grate with the quick, careful grace of a squirrel and sat back on her heels in the moonlight, looking at me. She was thinner than I remembered. Her hair floated around her head like a cloud of silk. She wore a simple dress that had once been white and was now the color of old bone, and her feet were bare and very clean.

"You were gone a long time," she said.

"I know. I'm sorry."

"The moon went round and round." She looked up at it, then back at me. "Seven times. I counted."

"That's right."

"I left you flowers." She said it shyly, looking down at her hands.

"I found them," I said. "They were lovely. They made the whole room smell like a meadow."

She beamed at that, a sudden brilliant smile like sunlight through a cloud. Then she looked at my pockets expectantly.

I laughed and gave her the cheese first. She unwrapped it with great solemnity, sniffed it, and pronounced it "brave." The dried apples were "patient." The mint made her eyes go wide and wondering, and she held the sprig up to the moonlight and turned it this way and that, as if it were a jewel.

"This is from very far away," she said.

"From the road near Tinuë. I picked it this morning."

"It remembers." She pressed it to her nose and closed her eyes. "It remembers the rain."

We sat on the stones in the moonlight and ate cheese and apples together, and she told me about the Underthing, about the new pipe that had begun to drip in Clinks and the place in Tumbrel where a whole family of little grey moths had come to live. She did not ask me where I had been, and I did not tell her. That was one of the things I loved about Auri. She never asked questions that were not hers to ask.

When the cheese was gone, she sat for a while in silence, looking up at the moon. Then she said, quite suddenly, "The doors are sleeping."

I looked at her. "What doors?"

"The deep ones." She did not look away from the moon. "They are sleeping, but they are not sleepy. There's a difference." She frowned, as if trying to make the words fit. "Something is sleeping on the other side of them. And sometimes, when it dreams, the doors dream too."

A chill went through me. I could not have said why.

"Auri," I said carefully. "Which doors are those?"

But she only shook her head. "That's not a proper question," she said, and there was a note of reproach in her voice. "You don't ask a door its business. You wait until it tells you."

Then she stood, all at once, and brushed off her dress. "Thank you for the brave cheese," she said formally. "And the patient apples. And the mint that remembers."

"You're welcome, Auri."

She smiled at me, and then she was gone, back down through the grate and into the dark, the patter of her feet fading away into silence.

I sat there alone for a long time, looking up at the moon.

The doors are sleeping, she had said. But they are not sleepy.

I did not know it then, but I had just been told the first true thing about the rest of my life.
