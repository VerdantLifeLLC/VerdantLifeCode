# Cracks

The first scrael came to Newarre in the autumn of the fifth year.

Carter found it. Or rather, it found Carter. He was coming back from Baedn along the north road with a load of beer and salt in his wagon, late in the evening, and something came out of the woods at the edge of the road and killed his horse.

He came into the Waystone an hour later, white as milk and shaking so hard he could not hold the cup I gave him. He had walked the last three miles in the dark. He had left the wagon where it stood. He had left the horse, what was left of it, lying in the road.

"Spider," he kept saying. "Big as a dog. Black. Shiny. Like a beetle. Legs like knives." His teeth were chattering. "Came out of the dark and just—*went* for her. For Nelly. Cut her to pieces. Right in front of me." He looked up at me, and his eyes were wild. "And I hit it. With my axe. I hit it right in the back, as hard as I could. And it bounced. The axe *bounced*, Kote."

The regulars were crowded around him. Graham and Old Cob and Jake and Shep. They were all very quiet.

"What did you do?" said Graham.

"Ran." Carter laughed, a high thin broken sound. "What else? I ran. And it didn't follow me. It just stayed there. With Nelly." He put his face in his hands. "Eleven years I had that horse. Eleven years."

I stood behind the bar with a cloth in my hand. I was not polishing anything. My hands had stopped.

*Big as a dog. Black. Shiny. Legs like knives.*

I did not remember. But something in me knew. Something far down, below the grey, in the hole where my name had been. It knew the shape of what Carter was describing, the way your tongue knows the shape of a missing tooth.

*Scrael.*

---

I went out that night.

I did not tell anyone. I waited until the regulars had gone home and Bast had gone to bed, and I went out into the yard and took an old iron poker from the woodpile and a lantern from the stable, and I walked north along the road in the dark.

I did not know why. I did not decide to. My feet simply took me.

It was a long walk. Three miles, in the dark, with only the lantern for light. The road was empty. The fields on either side were black and silent. The woods on the horizon were a darker black against the sky.

I found Carter's wagon where he had left it, standing in the road with its traces empty. I found what was left of his horse.

And I found the scrael.

---

It was sitting on the road beside the horse. Very still. Its black shell gleaming in the lantern light. Its legs folded around it like the ribs of an umbrella.

It did not move when I came up. It did not seem to notice me. It sat on the road beside the dead horse and was still.

I stopped a few paces away. I held up the lantern. I looked at it.

And it turned toward me.

It did not have a head. It did not have eyes. But I felt it turn. I felt its attention settle on me, the way you feel eyes on the back of your neck. It was very cold. Very old. Very wrong.

It knew me.

Not me. Not Kote, the innkeeper, with his poker and his lantern. The other one. The one in the chest. The one it had come through a door to find.

*Child of my child*, something whispered. Very faint. Very far away. From somewhere behind the scrael. From somewhere behind everything.

The scrael moved.

---

It came at me very fast. Faster than anything that size should be able to move. Its knife-legs flashed in the lantern light.

I did not think. My body moved. It moved the way it had been taught to move, once, a long time ago, on a hillside I did not remember, by a woman in red I did not know. *Tree Falling. Water Flows Around.*

I was not where the scrael's legs came down. I was beside it. And the iron poker was in my hand, and it was coming down, and it struck the scrael's shell with a sound like a bell.

The scrael shrieked. Where the iron touched it, the shell smoked. It reared back, its legs flailing.

I struck again. And again. And again.

It was not skill. Not really. I did not remember how to fight. But my body did. It knew where to put its feet, and how to turn its shoulders, and when to strike. And the iron did the rest.

The scrael shrieked one last time and collapsed. Its legs folded in on themselves. It lay on the road beside Carter's horse, smoking, and did not move again.

I stood over it with the poker in my hand and my chest heaving.

And then, from the woods at the edge of the road, I heard more of them.

---

I will not tell you all of it. It was a long night. There were five of them in the end, coming out of the woods one after another, and I killed them all. With the poker, and a second poker I had not known I had brought, and my hands, and my feet, and the wagon's iron-bound wheel, which I pried loose and swung like a club when the second poker broke.

I do not know how I did it. I did not know then, and I do not know now. I only know that when the sun came up, grey and pale over the fields, I was sitting on the road beside Carter's wagon with my back against the wheel and my hands bleeding and six dead scrael lying around me in a ring.

And I was not grey.

That was the strange thing. For the first time in five years, sitting on the road with the scrael dead around me, I was not grey. Not entirely. There was a color at the edge of things. Red. The red of the blood on my hands. The red of the dawn, coming up over the fields. The red of the fire I had not lit, but that I could feel, somehow, burning somewhere far down in the hole in my chest.

It did not last. By the time I got back to the Waystone, it was gone. The world was grey again.

But it had been there. For a little while. I had felt it.

---

Bast was waiting for me in the yard. He was standing by the pump with his arms folded, and his face was white.

He looked at me. At my bleeding hands and my torn clothes and the broken poker I was still carrying.

"Where have you been?" he said. His voice was very quiet.

"On the north road."

"Doing what?"

"Killing scrael."

Bast stared at me.

"Six of them," I said. "By Carter's wagon. I burned them. With oil from the wagon. So no one else would find them."

"You went out," said Bast slowly, "in the dark, alone, with a *poker*, and killed six scrael."

"Yes."

"Why?"

I thought about it.

"Because they were there," I said. "And they killed Carter's horse. And they would have killed Carter. And someone had to."

Bast looked at me for a long time. Then he came across the yard and took the broken poker out of my hand and looked at it.

"You're not supposed to do things like this," he said. His voice was very strange. "You're supposed to be small. Quiet. You're supposed to polish things." He looked up at me. "What if someone saw? What if someone *heard*?"

"No one heard."

"You don't know that." He threw the poker down on the ground. "They came here, Reshi. The scrael. They came *here*. To Newarre. To the one place in the whole world where you're supposed to be safe." His voice cracked. "Why do you think that is? Why do you think they came here, of all places?"

I did not answer.

"Because the door's cracked," said Bast. "Because you closed it, but not all the way. Because things are coming through. A little at a time. Seeping out, like water through a wall." He was shaking. "And they're looking for you. Even if they don't know it. Even if they can't hear you. They're looking for the thing that closed the door. The thing that's in the chest."

I looked at him.

"Then I'll kill them," I said. "When they come."

"With a *poker*?"

"With whatever I have."

Bast stared at me. Then he laughed. It was a broken, astonished, terrible laugh.

"You're still in there," he said. "Aren't you? Somewhere. Under all of it. You're still—" He stopped. He pressed his hands against his face. "God help us. You're still you."

---

After that, there were more of them. Not many. Not often. One here, one there. A shepherd's flock torn apart in the hills. A family's dog found in pieces in the road. A traveler who did not arrive in Baedn when he was expected, and whose horse came home alone.

I went out after them. Every time. At night, with iron. Bast came with me, sometimes, though he could not touch the iron and could do very little to help. He came anyway. He stood behind me in the dark with his hands at his sides and his face white, and watched me kill them.

I did not tell the regulars. They thought the scrael were wild beasts, come down from the mountains in a hard winter. They were frightened of them, and they talked about them, and they told each other to keep their doors barred at night. But they did not know what they were. And I did not tell them.

I only went out, in the dark, with iron. And came back, in the dawn, with blood on my hands. And for a little while afterward, the world was not quite so grey.

---

And then, in the spring of the sixth year, something else came.

It came into the Waystone on a Felling night, when the common room was full. A traveler. A man in a long dark coat, with a pack on his back and a hat pulled low over his eyes. He came in out of the rain and stood dripping in the doorway, and everyone turned to look at him, the way people in a small town always turn to look at a stranger.

He did not say anything. He crossed the room to the bar and sat down on a stool at the end, and he looked at me.

"Drink?" I said.

He did not answer. He only looked at me. His face was pale and strange under the brim of his hat. His eyes were—

I did not know what his eyes were. I could not see them clearly. They seemed to shift, when I looked at them. Now brown, now grey, now something else. Like water with something moving underneath it.

I felt the cold before I understood it. A small cold, at the base of my spine. The same cold I had felt on the north road, when the scrael turned toward me.

"Drink?" I said again.

The man smiled.

It was not a man's smile. It was a smile that had been put on, like a coat. It stretched his face in the wrong places. It did not reach his eyes, because his eyes were not his. And when he spoke, his voice was wrong too. It was a man's voice, but it was worn. The way a coat is worn.

"I'm looking for someone," it said. "Someone who closed a door."

---

The common room had gone quiet. I did not notice when. The regulars were looking at the stranger, and then at me, and then at each other, with the uneasy expressions of people who sense that something is wrong but do not know what.

Shep was sitting closest. He was a round, red-faced, cheerful man, and he had been drinking since sundown, and he was not afraid of anything.

"Well, you won't find any doors closed here, friend," he said cheerfully. "The Waystone's always open. Kote never turns anyone away." He clapped the stranger on the shoulder. "Sit, have a drink, dry yourself off. You look like a drowned rat."

The stranger turned and looked at him.

And I knew, all at once, what it was.

I did not know the name. I did not know anything. But the knowing came up out of the hole in my chest like water out of a well. The shape of it. A thing that takes men's skins and wears them. A thing that walks among other men, and no one knows.

A thing I had seen once before, in a chamber beneath an old house, taking a soldier's face.

"*Shep*," I said. "*Get away from him.*"

It was too late.

---

I will not tell you what happened to Shep. You have heard it, I think. Graham told you, or Old Cob, or someone. They all tell it, now, in the Waystone, on dark nights, in low voices. How the stranger took hold of Shep, and Shep screamed, and then he did not scream anymore.

I will tell you what I did.

I came over the bar. I did not decide to. I was simply over it, and then I was in front of the thing that was wearing the stranger's skin, and I opened my mouth, and I said something.

I do not know what I said. It was not a word I knew. It was not a word in any language I remembered. It came up out of the hole in my chest from somewhere very far down, from the place where names used to live. It was a command. A binding. The kind of word that a namer speaks, to make a thing stop.

It did not work.

It came out of my mouth, and it had the shape of power, and it had nothing inside it. It was a painting of a fire on a wall. It was a lute with no strings. The thing wearing the stranger's skin heard it, and paused, and tilted its borrowed head.

And laughed.

"*Oh*," it said, in its worn voice. "*Oh, it's you. It's really you. But there's nothing there.*" It laughed again. "*He'll be so pleased. He'll be so very pleased.*"

It let go of Shep. Shep fell to the floor. He did not move.

The thing reached for me.

---

It was Aaron who saved me.

He was fifteen that year, the smith's apprentice, tall and broad-shouldered and strong from the forge. He had been sitting at the back of the room with a mug of cider, and when the thing took hold of Shep, he had not run, as some of the others had. He had not frozen, as most of the others had. He had gone to the corner where I kept the iron firedogs for the hearth, and he had picked one up. A long iron bar, heavy and black.

He came up behind the thing as it reached for me. And he hit it across the back of the head with the iron bar, as hard as he could.

The thing screamed. The stranger's skin split open where the iron struck it, and something came out of the split, something grey and wet and wrong, and it screamed and screamed, and Aaron hit it again, and again, and again.

It fell. It lay on the floor of the Waystone beside Shep's body, twitching and smoking. The stranger's skin was sloughing off it like a snake's. Underneath, there was something I will not describe.

Aaron stood over it with the iron bar in his hands. His face was white. His hands were shaking.

"What is it?" he whispered. "Kote. What *is* it?"

I looked at it. At the thing on the floor, twitching and smoking. At Shep, lying beside it, very still.

"Something that came through a door," I said.

---

We buried Shep three days later, in the churchyard behind the little stone church. The whole town came. Graham made the coffin. Old Cob said a few words, and for once he did not wander off into digressions. The priest said a prayer. Shep's widow wept.

I stood at the back, behind everyone, and did not say anything.

Afterward, when everyone else had gone, Bast found me still standing there, beside the fresh grave.

"It wasn't your fault," he said.

"It came for me. It said so. It was looking for someone who closed a door."

"It wasn't your fault," said Bast again. "It was the door's fault. The crack's. The Thief's." He looked at me. "Not yours."

"I tried to stop it. I tried to name it. And there was nothing there." I looked down at my hands. "I opened my mouth, and I reached for the word, and there was *nothing there*, Bast. Nothing at all."

Bast was quiet for a long time.

"I know," he said at last. "I heard." He was quiet for a moment. "Reshi. You can't keep doing this. Going out at night with iron. Fighting things. You're not—" He stopped. "You're not strong enough. Not like this. Not without your name."

"Then what should I do?"

Bast did not answer. He looked at the fresh grave. At the raw earth, and the wooden marker Graham had carved, with Shep's name on it.

"I don't know," he said. "But something. We have to do something." He looked at me. "This can't go on, Reshi. You can't go on like this. Being small. Being quiet. Waiting." His voice cracked. "It's killing you. I can see it. Every year, a little more. You're fading. Like my mother faded, in the twilight."

I did not say anything.

"And they're coming," said Bast. "More of them. Every year. Scrael, and skin dancers, and worse. Through the crack. Looking for you." He looked at me with his deep blue eyes. "And one day, one of them is going to find you. And you won't be able to stop it. And then—"

He did not finish.

I looked at the grave for a long time.

"Skarpi said someone would come," I said at last. "A scribe. To ask for the story. He said I should wait."

"How long?"

"He didn't say."

Bast stared at me. Then he laughed, very softly, very bitterly.

"Of course he didn't," he said. "Of course he didn't."

He turned and walked away, back toward the Waystone. He did not look back.

I stood beside Shep's grave until the sun went down.

---

That night, I heard Bast in his room at the back of the inn. Talking. Very softly. To someone who was not there.

I did not hear what he said. I did not try to. But I heard the tone of it. Low and urgent and fierce. The tone of someone making a plan.

I did not know, then, what that plan was. I did not know for a long time.

I know now.
