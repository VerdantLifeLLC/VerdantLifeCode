# Beneath

The passage opened out onto a ledge, high up in the wall of a great round chamber, and from that ledge I looked down and saw the door.

The chamber was enormous. Bigger than the Eolian. Bigger than the Masters' Hall at the University. It was a vast round space carved out of the living rock of the ridge, with a domed ceiling so high it was lost in shadow and walls that curved down and down to a floor of smooth grey stone, polished like glass. The walls were covered in carvings. Knots, thousands of them, running in long spiraling lines from the floor to the ceiling. The names of the keepers, I thought. All of them, all the way back, to the first.

There was a stair on the far side of the chamber, wide and shallow, winding down from an archway high in the wall. That must be the stair from the house above, I thought. The one Krin had gone down as a child, on a dare, with her friend Ellie.

And at the bottom of the chamber, set into the wall directly opposite the stair, was the door.

It was grey stone. Smooth and plain and utterly featureless. It had no handle. It had no hinges. It had no lock. It was taller than three men and wider than a wagon, and it was perfectly, impossibly flat, like a sheet of still water turned on its side.

It was exactly the same as the door beneath the Archives. Exactly the same as the door I had dreamed of, all those months ago, in my narrow bed at Anker's. And it was different. It was breathing.

I could see it. The whole face of the door was moving, very slightly. Swelling out and sinking back. Swelling out and sinking back. In, and out. Like the side of a sleeping animal.

The silver light was coming from it. From a thin bright line down its center, running from top to bottom. A crack. Hair-fine. Barely visible. But it was there.

---

There were people on the floor of the chamber.

Soldiers, first. Fifty or sixty of them, in the black and silver of the house of Jakis, standing in a ring around the edges of the floor with torches in their hands. They were very still. They were very pale. Several of them were shaking. I could see the torchlight trembling on their faces.

Then, in the center of the floor, facing the door, a smaller group.

Ambrose was there. He was standing in front of the door, a few paces from it, in his black clothes, with his silver circlet on his head and the box on its chain around his neck. He was very still. His face was white. His hands were clenched at his sides.

Meluan was there. She was kneeling on the stone floor beside Ambrose, with her hands bound in front of her and her head bowed. Her pale grey robe was stained with dirt. Her dark hair hung loose over her face. A soldier stood behind her with a hand on her shoulder.

Cinder was there. He was standing a little apart, to one side, with his ice-pale sword sheathed at his hip and his hands folded behind his back. He was looking at the door. He was smiling.

And the others were there. The rest of the Seven. I saw Usnea, pale and grey, standing near the stair. Stercus, dark and stocky, near the wall. Pale Alenta. Cyphus, with blue fire flickering along his arms. Grey Mardou, a smudge of mist at the edge of the torchlight.

And Bredon.

He was sitting on a stool at the edge of the floor, a little way from the door. There was a small table in front of him, with a tak board on it, the stones laid out in their bowls. He was not looking at the door. He was not looking at Ambrose, or Meluan, or the Seven. He was looking at the board. As if he were in the middle of a game.

And beside him, sitting on the floor with her harp in her lap, was Denna.

She was singing.

---

I lay on the ledge high above the chamber, with Bast on one side of me and Vashet on the other, and I listened to her sing.

It was the Lanre song. The false one. The one she had sung at the Eolian, the night it burned. But it was different, now. Softer. Slower. It was not a song for an audience. It was a song for the door.

She was singing it to the door. And the door was listening.

I could see it. The breathing of the door had changed. It was keeping time with her song. Swelling out on the long notes, sinking back on the short. In, and out. In time with her voice. As if the door were singing along.

And the crack down its center was widening. Very slowly. So slowly I could barely see it. But it was. With every verse she sang, the silver line grew a little brighter. A little wider.

"She's opening it," Bast breathed. "Reshi, she's opening it. With the song."

"No," I said. "She's weakening it. The curse. The song weakens the curse, and the curse is part of what holds the door. It's all one thing. Lanre's curse and the Thief's prison. They were made together, by the same man, on the same night." I watched the silver line. "Every time she sings the false story, the whole binding gets a little weaker."

"Then stop her!"

"I can't. Not from here." I looked at Denna. At her bowed head, and her fingers moving on the harp strings, and her pale face in the silver light. "And she's not doing it because she wants to."

I could see her wrist. The one with the knot. It was glowing. Dull red, like an ember. Brighter every time she faltered, every time her voice wavered. And every time it glowed, she flinched, and her voice steadied, and she sang on.

Bredon was pulling the cord.

---

"What do we do?" whispered Vashet.

I looked down at the chamber. At the soldiers with their torches, and the Seven, and Ambrose, and Meluan, and Bredon at his tak board. At Denna, singing. At the door, breathing.

"We wait," I said.

"For what?"

"For him to make his move." I watched Bredon. He had picked up a white stone from the bowl and was turning it over in his fingers, the way he always did when he was thinking. "He knows I'm coming. He's been waiting for me. He's not going to open the door without me, not if he can help it. The lady's blood can crack it. Denna's song can weaken it. But only a namer of the line can open it all the way." I paused. "He's waiting for me to show myself."

"Then don't."

"I have to. Eventually. That's the whole point." I looked at Vashet. "But not yet. Not until I'm ready. Not until I know what he's going to do."

Vashet looked at me for a long moment. Then her hands moved. *Patience*, they said. And then, *good*.

---

We waited.

Denna sang. Her song went on and on, verse after verse. She sang Lanre's glory at Drossen Tor, and his death, and his return. She sang his love for Lyra. She sang the betrayal of his friends, and the jealousy of Selitos, and the terrible injustice of the curse.

And the door breathed. And the crack grew.

At last, she came to the end. The last verse. The one where Lanre, wronged and cursed and alone, stands among the ruins of the world his enemies have made, and weeps for all that was taken from him.

Her voice broke on the last line. She stopped. She sat with her fingers still on the harp strings, and her head bowed, and her shoulders shaking.

The chamber was silent. Utterly silent. Even the breathing of the door had stopped.

Then Bredon set down his white stone on the tak board. It made a small, sharp click in the silence.

"Well," he said. "That's done."

He looked up. Not at the door. Not at Ambrose. Up. At the ledge. At me.

"You can come down now, Kvothe," he said pleasantly. "I know you're there. I've known for some time. You breathe very loudly, for someone who studied with the Adem."

---

Beside me, Vashet's hand closed on my arm. I felt her fingers tighten. *Don't*, they said.

I did not listen. I stood up.

I stood on the ledge, high above the chamber, in the silver light of the cracked door, and every face on the floor below turned up to look at me.

The soldiers. The Seven. Ambrose, with his white face and his clenched hands. Meluan, lifting her head, her dark eyes wide in her thin face. Cinder, smiling. Bredon, at his tak board, with his mild kind face turned up toward me like a flower toward the sun.

And Denna.

She had lifted her head. She was looking at me. Her face was wet with tears. Her eyes were very dark.

She did not say anything. But her lips moved. Silently. One word.

*No.*

---

"Come down," said Bredon. "Please. We've all been waiting for you. It's very rude to keep people waiting."

I looked down at him. At his board, with its stones laid out in their pattern.

"Let the lady go," I said. My voice rang out in the great round chamber, strange and hollow. "Let her go, and I'll come down."

"Oh, I don't think so." Bredon smiled. "I think you'll come down anyway. Because you want to know. You've always wanted to know. It's your great weakness, your hunger for knowing." He gestured at the door. "And there it is. The last door. The one that's been waiting for you since before you were born." He looked up at me. "Don't you want to see what's behind it?"

"No."

"Liar." He said it gently, fondly, the way a grandfather says it to a child caught with his hand in the cake tin. "You've been lying to yourself for so long, Kvothe. Telling yourself you only want to close it. Only want to save your aunt. Only want to keep the world safe." He shook his head. "But I know you. I've watched you. I've played with you. And I know that underneath all of that, underneath everything, there's a little boy who just wants to know what's on the other side."

I did not answer. I stood on the ledge and looked at him.

"Come down," said Bredon softly. "Come down, and play one last game with me. And then, whatever happens, at least you'll know."

---

I looked at Vashet. She was still lying on the ledge beside me, with her hand on my arm and her face showing nothing. But her eyes were very steady.

*Choose*, they said. *And bear what comes after.*

I looked at Bast. He was lying on my other side, with his hand in his boot, on the handle of the iron knife. His face was white. His deep blue eyes were very wide.

*I'll stay*, they said. *Whatever you are. However much less.*

I looked down at the chamber. At Meluan, kneeling on the stone with her hands bound. At Denna, sitting with her harp, her lips still shaping the word *no*.

At the door, breathing.

And then I went down.

---

There was a stair cut into the wall below the ledge. Narrow and steep and very old, spiraling down along the curve of the chamber wall. I went down it slowly, one step at a time, with my hand on the cold carved stone of the wall and my eyes on the floor below.

Behind me, I heard the others moving. The Adem, spreading out along the ledge. Sim and Wil and Devi and Fela, crouching low. Dagon and his soldiers, drawing their swords. Bast, very close behind me, on the stair.

I did not look back. I kept going down.

The soldiers on the floor did not move to stop me. They stood in their ring with their torches, pale and shaking, and watched me come. The Seven did not move either. They stood where they were and watched me with their strange cold eyes.

Only Cinder moved. He took a step toward the foot of the stair, his hand on the hilt of his ice-pale sword.

"Ferule," said Bredon mildly, without looking up from his board. "Leave him. He's coming. Let him come."

Cinder stopped. He looked at Bredon. Something moved in his black eyes. Then he stepped back, and folded his hands behind his back again, and smiled.

I reached the bottom of the stair. I stepped out onto the smooth grey floor of the chamber. It was very cold. Colder than the passage. Colder than the Stormwal. The cold came up through the soles of my boots and into my bones.

I walked across the floor toward the door.

---

I walked past the soldiers. Past the Seven. Past Cinder, with his black eyes and his smile. Past Meluan, kneeling on the stone, who looked up at me with her thin white face and whispered my name.

I walked past Denna.

She did not look up this time. She sat with her harp in her lap and her head bowed, and her hands were shaking on the strings. But as I passed her, very softly, so softly that only I could hear, she spoke.

"I have a cord," she whispered. "In my sleeve. Ready. When you need it."

I did not stop. I did not look at her. I kept walking.

I stopped in front of the door.

It was even bigger up close. Taller than three men. Wider than a wagon. Smooth and grey and perfectly flat, like a sheet of still water turned on its side. The silver crack ran down its center from top to bottom, glowing faintly. And the whole face of it was breathing. Swelling out and sinking back. In, and out.

I could feel it. The breath of it. Cold and slow and deep, on my face, on my hands. Like standing in front of a great sleeping beast.

And I could hear it. Not the breathing. The other thing. The voice. Very faint. Very far away. Coming from the other side of the stone.

Saying my name.

My true name. The one the Sleeper had spoken, and the voice beneath the Archives, and the box on Ambrose's chest. The name that was the shape of me.

*Come in*, it said. *Come in, child of my child. Come home.*

I stood in front of the door and listened to it call me.

And behind me, at his little table, Bredon picked up a black stone and held it out.

"Your move," he said.
