# Nell's Story

Nell decided, about a span after I came to Hallow's Company, that I needed to be told who I was.

"Everybody else knows," she said. "It isn't fair that you don't."

She was sitting on the tailboard of the third wagon beside me, swinging her legs. It was evening. The troupe was camped in a meadow beside a river, and the fires were lit, and Mirra was cooking, and Brin was tuning his fiddle, flat as always. Lark was asleep in a basket at Mirra's feet.

"Denna told me," I said. "Who I am."

"Denna told you your *name*." Nell looked at me with great scorn. She was eight, and very certain about most things. "That's not the same. A name's just a word. You need the *story*."

I did not know what to say to that. So I did not say anything.

"I'll tell it," said Nell. "I know all of it. Mirra told me some, and Tessit told me some, and Brin told me some, but Brin gets it wrong. And I heard some in the villages. They tell it everywhere, now." She folded her hands in her lap, very primly, the way Mirra did before she began a long story. "Are you listening?"

"Yes."

"Good." She took a deep breath. "Once upon a time—"

---

I will not tell you the whole of Nell's story. It was very long, and it wandered a great deal, and there were several places where she stopped and backed up and started over because she had left something out. There were also a great many dragons. I do not know where the dragons came from. I have never met a dragon.

But I will tell you some of it. Because it was the first time I heard my own story told, after the grey came down. And because Nell told it better than anyone has, before or since. Including me.

---

"Once upon a time," said Nell, "there was a boy with red hair, whose mother and father were Ruh. And they were the best troupe on all the roads, and everyone loved them. And the boy could sing better than anyone, even when he was little. Even better than Brin."

"Most people sing better than Brin," I said.

Nell looked at me sharply. "Don't interrupt. It's rude." She went on. "And one day, the boy's family was killed. By the Chandrian. Who are very bad. They came in the night, and they killed everyone, and the boy was the only one left." She paused. "That's the sad part. There's always a sad part at the beginning. Mirra says that's so you know it's a true story."

"Is it?"

"Is it what?"

"A true story."

Nell considered this carefully.

"Most of it," she said at last. "Not the dragons."

---

She told me about Tarbean. About a boy alone in a great city, starving, sleeping on rooftops. She told it very seriously, with her small face grave, and I could tell she had been told it by someone who had known. Mirra, I thought. Or Tessit. Someone who had been there, or near it.

She told me about the University. About a boy of fifteen who talked his way in, and called the wind, and made a master very angry, and was whipped, and did not bleed. That was where the Bloodless came from, she told me. Because he did not bleed.

She told me about the Eolian, and the silver pipes. And about a girl with dark hair.

"Denna," said Nell. "Our Denna. She was in it even then. At the beginning." She looked at me. "Did you know that?"

"No."

"Well, she was. And he loved her. The red-haired boy. From the very first minute." Nell sighed deeply, with the romantic gravity of the very young. "And she loved him back. But they could never be together. Because of a curse." She frowned. "Or a patron. Something bad. The stories don't agree."

---

She told me about the Maer. About Vintas, and a box with no lid, and a poisoner. About bandits in the Eld, which she told with great relish and a great many sound effects. About the Fae, and a lady made of moonlight, and a cloak made of shadows.

She told me about the Adem, and a sword, and a hand-talk you could say secrets in.

And then she told me about the Eolian again. The second time. The song.

"He sang it to bring them," she said. Her voice had gone quiet. "The Chandrian. The ones who killed his family. He sang the true song about them, so they'd have to come. And they came. And they burned the Eolian down. And a big man with a beard died." She was quiet for a moment. "And the girl who knew stone held it up. All night. With her hands."

I did not say anything. Something was moving in me, very faintly. Like a fish in dark water, far down.

"And after that," said Nell, "he went into the Fae again. All the way to the mountains at the top of the world. And he found a man with one eye, who was older than anyone. And the man told him how to close the door."

"What door?"

Nell looked at me as if I were very slow.

"*The* door," she said. "The one the Lackless family keeps. With no lock and no key and no handle. That's in the rhyme." She recited it, singsong, the way children do:

*"Seven things stand before*
*The entrance to the Lackless door—"*

She stopped.

"I'm not allowed to say the rest," she said. "Mirra says. Not anymore. Not with Lark here." She glanced toward the basket at Mirra's feet. "Mirra says Lark should never hear it. Not ever. Not even as a song."

"Why?"

Nell looked at me for a long moment.

"You know why," she said softly. "You're the one who said."

---

I did not know why. I did not know anything. But I looked at the basket, where the baby with dark hair slept at Mirra's feet, and I felt the cord on my wrist hum, very faintly. The way it had hummed when I first held her.

"Go on," I said. "With the story."

Nell went on.

She told me about a king's camp, and a fire in the horse lines, and a baby carried out in the dark. She told me about the Lackless house, on the ridge above Levinshir. About a cellar stair and a door that was not a door. About a king who came to open it.

"And the red-haired boy killed him," said Nell. "The king. With a sword. Right in front of the door. Because the king was going to open it, and let the bad things out. And the red-haired boy said no."

"And then?"

"And then the door opened anyway." Nell's voice was very small. "A little. Just a crack. And something came through. Something very bad. The worst thing in the world." She hugged her knees. "And the red-haired boy had to close it. And the only way to close it was to give up his name. His real one. The deep one." She looked at me. "So he did."

The fire crackled. Across the camp, Brin had stopped tuning his fiddle. Mirra had stopped stirring the pot. I did not know when they had started listening. But they were listening now.

"And the girl with dark hair caught it," said Nell. "His name. When he gave it up. She caught it in a cord, and she tied it, so it wouldn't be lost. And she kept it safe." She looked at the cord on my wrist. "That's it. Isn't it? That's his name."

I looked down at the cord.

"Yes," I said.

---

Nell was quiet for a long time. She swung her legs over the tailboard. She looked at the river, going by in the dusk.

"That's the end," she said at last. "That's all the story there is. Except the end part, which isn't finished."

"What's the end part?"

"The part where he gets it back." Nell looked at me. "His name. That's how the story has to end. Every story like that ends the same way. The hero loses something, and goes on a long journey, and then he gets it back." She frowned. "But nobody knows how that part goes. Because it hasn't happened yet."

I looked at her. At her small serious face, in the firelight.

"What if it doesn't?" I said. "What if he never gets it back?"

Nell looked horrified.

"Then it's a bad story," she said. "And nobody would tell it." She shook her head firmly. "No. He gets it back. He has to. Otherwise, what was the point of all the rest?"

---

She slid down off the tailboard. She stood in front of me, in the grass, with her hands on her hips.

"You should remember it," she said. "The story. So you can tell it to yourself. When you're sad. When you don't know who you are." She considered me. "I'll tell it to you again tomorrow. And every day. Until you know it by heart."

"You don't have to."

"I know I don't *have* to." Nell looked at me with great patience, as if I were very young. "That's why it counts."

And she went off across the camp to help Mirra with the supper, and left me sitting on the tailboard in the dusk, with the story she had told me sitting in my chest like a stone.

---

She did tell it again. Every night, for the rest of that summer and all through the winter that followed, in the valley in the western hills. Sometimes it was longer, and sometimes it was shorter. Sometimes there were more dragons, and sometimes fewer. Once, memorably, the red-haired boy married the lady made of moonlight, and Mirra had to take Nell aside and have a long talk with her.

But she told it. Every night. And I listened. And by the time the spring came, and Bast and I left Hallow's Company with the cart and the chest and the mule named Patience, I knew it by heart.

I did not remember it. I want to be clear about that. It was not my story, the way it had been before. It was Nell's story. A story about a red-haired boy, that a little girl had told me. It could have been about anyone.

But I knew it. Every word. And all through the grey years behind the bar, when I did not know who I was, I could close my eyes and hear her voice. Small and serious and very certain.

*He gets it back. He has to. Otherwise, what was the point of all the rest?*

"She was right," said Chronicler quietly. "Wasn't she?"

The innkeeper did not answer at once. He was looking at the pages stacked at Chronicler's elbow. Three days of pages. Hundreds of them.

"Nell is sixteen now," he said. "Mirra sent word, once, by a tinker. Nell's got the troupe's best voice, she said. Better than Mirra's own. Better than Brin's." He smiled faintly. "Everyone's is better than Brin's."

"Will you see her? When this is done?"

"I hope so." The innkeeper looked at the fire. "I'd like to tell her how it ends. The end part. The part that wasn't finished." He was quiet for a moment. "She'll want to know if there were dragons."

"Were there?"

The innkeeper looked at him. And the corner of his mouth moved.

"Not yet," he said.
