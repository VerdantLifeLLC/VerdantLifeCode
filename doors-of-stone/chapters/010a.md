# The Countess's Library

Threpe kept his word. He always did. It was one of the things that made him so rare among the nobility of Imre, who generally regarded their word as a kind of loose currency, to be spent freely and redeemed never.

Six days after our conversation at the Eolian, a note came to Anker's, written in Threpe's large looping hand on paper so thick it was nearly card. *My dear boy*, it said. *Tomorrow at noon. Wear something without holes in it. We are calling on the Countess Laverne.*

I did not know who the Countess Laverne was. I asked Sim, who knew everyone in Imre worth knowing, mostly because his father was a duke and he had grown up having to be polite to them.

"Laverne?" He looked up from his book with an expression of horror. "Oh, Kvothe. Oh, no."

"What?"

"She's a hundred years old. She's deaf in one ear and mad in the other. She has seventeen cats and she dresses them in little velvet coats." He shuddered. "My mother made me go to tea with her once when I was nine. She pinched my cheek so hard it bruised."

"Threpe says she has a library."

"Oh, she has a library." Sim closed his book. "Everyone knows about the Laverne library. Her husband collected books. Old ones. He spent his whole fortune on them, and then he died, and she's kept them ever since, and she won't let anyone see them." He looked at me. "Nobody gets into that library, Kvothe. Nobody. Masters from the University have been trying for forty years."

"Threpe's getting me in."

Sim stared at me. Then he shook his head slowly.

"Of course he is," he said. "Of course he is."

---

The Countess Laverne lived in a tall narrow house on the richest street in Imre, a street of tall narrow houses built of pale stone with tall narrow windows and tiny gardens behind wrought-iron fences. Hers was the tallest and narrowest of them all, and the oldest, and the most in need of paint. Ivy grew over most of its front. Several of the windows were shuttered. A cat sat on the doorstep, wearing a small velvet coat of deep burgundy, and watched us come up the walk with an expression of profound contempt.

Threpe was wearing a coat of such a brilliant shade of green that it seemed to buzz. He knocked on the door with his cane.

"Now," he said, while we waited. "The Countess is a very dear old friend. Very dear. But she's a little—eccentric. You mustn't be alarmed by anything she says. Or does. Or by the cats."

"Sim said she pinches cheeks."

"Only children's." Threpe patted my arm. "You'll be fine. Just be charming. You're very good at being charming, when you want to be."

The door opened. A very old manservant looked out at us, bent nearly double, with a face like a walnut. He looked at Threpe. He looked at me. He looked at Threpe again.

"Count Threpe," he said, in a voice like a creaking hinge. "Her ladyship is expecting you." He looked at me again. "Both of you."

---

The Countess Laverne received us in a sitting room on the second floor, a room so crowded with furniture and ornaments and cats that it was difficult to see where one ended and the next began. There were cats on the chairs and cats on the tables and cats on the mantelpiece and cats on the windowsills. There were cats in baskets and cats in boxes and one very large grey cat asleep in a copper pot on the hearth. Every one of them was wearing a small velvet coat.

The Countess was sitting in a chair by the window. She was very small and very old, much older than Lady Tamsin in Renere, whom I would not meet for another year. Her face was a mass of wrinkles. Her hair was white and thin and piled on top of her head in an elaborate arrangement held in place by what appeared to be several hundred pins. She wore a dress of black silk that had been in fashion sixty years before.

And she had a cat in her lap. A small white one, in a coat of blue velvet.

"Threpe," she said. Her voice was surprisingly strong. "You're late."

"I'm exactly on time, my lady."

"That's late, for you. You're always early." She peered at me with bright black eyes. "Who's this?"

"This is Kvothe, my lady. The musician I told you about."

"The Ruh." The Countess looked at me for a long time. "The one who plays at the Eolian. Who won his pipes at fifteen."

"Yes, my lady."

"Come here."

I went. I stood in front of her chair. She looked up at me with her bright black eyes, and then she reached up and took hold of my chin with her small bony fingers and turned my face this way and that, the way you might inspect a horse.

"Hm," she said. "Hm. Yes." She let go. "You look like your mother."

---

I went very still.

"My lady?"

"Your mother. The Ruh singer. Laurian." The Countess stroked the white cat in her lap. "She played here once. In this house. Twenty years ago. Maybe more. My husband was still alive. He heard her sing in the market and he brought her home, the whole troupe of them, and they played in the great hall downstairs for a span." She smiled. It made her face crack into a thousand new wrinkles. "She had the loveliest voice. And the loveliest manners. My husband said she held a teacup like a duchess."

I did not say anything. I could not.

"She was interested in the books," said the Countess. "Very interested. My husband let her into the library. He let her read there, every afternoon, while the others rehearsed. He said she was the only person who had ever asked him the right questions about them." She looked at me. "Threpe tells me you're interested in the books too."

"Yes, my lady."

"About Lanre."

"Yes."

The Countess looked at me for a long time with her bright black eyes.

"That's what she was interested in too," she said softly. "Lanre. And the white city. And a family called Lackless." She stroked the white cat. "My husband thought it was very odd. A Ruh singer, wanting to read about an old Vintish family." She smiled faintly. "But then, he thought everything was odd. That's why I married him."

---

The library was on the top floor of the house, under the roof.

It was not a large room. I had expected something grand, a vast hall of shelves like the Tomes in the Archives. But it was a small room, long and narrow, with a sloping ceiling and a single round window at the far end. The walls were lined with shelves from floor to ceiling. The shelves were full of books.

And they were old. I could smell it the moment the walnut-faced manservant opened the door. That smell of old books, dust and leather and ink and time. Some of them were bound in leather, cracked and faded. Some of them were bound in wood. Some of them were not bound at all, only stacks of loose pages tied with string. There were scrolls in pigeonholes and cords with knots in them hanging from pegs.

"Forty years my husband collected," said the Countess. She had come up the stairs with us, very slowly, leaning on the manservant's arm and Threpe's. She stood in the doorway now, looking at the shelves. "Forty years. Every penny we had. I used to be so angry with him." She was quiet for a moment. "And now they're all I have left of him. So I keep them. And I don't let anyone touch them."

She looked at me.

"But you may," she said. "For your mother's sake. She was the only one of all of them who ever really read them."

---

I spent the whole of that afternoon in the Countess Laverne's library, and every afternoon for a span after.

It was an extraordinary collection. The Count had not been a scholar; he had been a collector, which is a different thing. He had bought whatever he could find, whatever was old and rare and strange, without much regard for whether it was important. So the shelves were full of the most bewildering mixture. Books of hours and books of spells. Herbals and bestiaries. Histories of kingdoms that no longer existed, written by men whose names no one remembered. Collections of old songs, and old recipes, and old riddles.

And some of it was about Lanre.

Not much. A few volumes. A few scrolls. A cord of Yllish knots, very old and very faded, that I could barely read. But it was more than I had found in the Archives. And it had not been cut.

That was the thing I noticed first. None of it had been cut. The chapters were whole. The pages were numbered in order. Nothing was missing.

Whoever had been going through the Archives with a knife had never been in the Countess Laverne's library.

---

I found the ballad on the fourth day.

It was in a small book bound in green leather, very worn, stuffed at the back of a shelf behind a row of herbals. It had no title. Inside, it was a collection of songs, written out by hand in a script so old and so faded that I could barely read it. Some of them I knew. Most of them I did not.

And one of them was about Lyra.

Not about Lanre. About Lyra. His wife. The one who had called him back from death at Drossen Tor with her voice speaking his name.

It was a short song. Only a few verses. It told of Lyra standing on the battlefield among the dead, with her husband's body in her arms, and calling his name. Not shouting it. Singing it. The song said that she sang his name as if it were the most beautiful word in the world, and that death itself could not bear to hear it sung so, and let him go.

And then there was a verse I had never heard in any version of the story.

It told of Lyra, later. After. Standing in a garden in a white city, with her husband beside her. Asking him if he remembered what it had been like. Being dead.

And Lanre said: *I remember nothing. Only a great quiet, and your voice at the end of it.*

And Lyra said: *Then I will always sing to you. So that if you ever go back to that quiet, you will hear me, and come home.*

---

I sat in the Countess Laverne's library with the green book in my lap and read that verse over and over until the light through the round window began to fail.

I did not know why it moved me so much. It was only a song. A few lines, in an old book, by someone whose name was long forgotten.

But I thought about my mother, sitting in this same room twenty years ago, reading these same books. Asking the Count the right questions. I thought about the song my father had been writing, the one about Lanre, the one he never finished. I thought about my mother's voice, singing harmony beside him by the fire.

I wondered if she had read this verse. I wondered if she had put it into my father's song.

I wondered if that was why they came.

---

On the last afternoon, the Countess came up to the library herself. She came very slowly, on the manservant's arm, with a cat in a purple velvet coat trailing behind her. She stood in the doorway and watched me for a while, sitting at the small table under the round window with the green book open in front of me.

"You found something," she said.

"Yes, my lady."

"Show me."

I showed her. She came and sat in the chair across from me and put on a pair of spectacles that hung around her neck on a chain, and she read the verse about Lyra. She read it twice. Then she took off her spectacles and looked at me.

"Your mother found that too," she said.

I stared at her.

"She read it to my husband," said the Countess. "Aloud. In this room. I was sitting just there." She pointed at a chair by the door, now occupied by a fat orange cat in a green coat. "She read it, and then she closed the book, and she sat very still. And my husband asked her what was wrong." The Countess was quiet for a moment. "And she said, *Nothing. It's only that I've heard this before. My father used to sing it to me when I was small. And he told me never to sing it to anyone. Ever.*"

"Why?"

The Countess looked at me with her bright black eyes.

"She didn't say," she said. "She only said her father told her it was dangerous. That it was an old song, from the keeping, and it wasn't meant for other ears." She paused. "My husband asked her what *the keeping* meant. And she laughed and changed the subject. And the next day, the troupe left. Very suddenly. Before dawn. They didn't say goodbye."

---

I sat very still.

*From the keeping.* I did not know, then, what it meant. I would not know for months. Not until I stood in a Chamber of Watches beneath the Archives and read a book with a burning tower on its cover.

But I knew it meant something. I knew it the way you know, sometimes, that a key will fit a lock before you have tried it.

"Thank you, my lady," I said.

The Countess looked at me for a long moment. Then she reached across the table and put her small bony hand on mine.

"Be careful, boy," she said. "Your mother was frightened, that last night. I saw it. She was frightened the way people are frightened when they've found something they shouldn't have." She squeezed my hand. "I'd hate for you to find the same thing."

"I'll be careful."

"No, you won't." She smiled, and her face cracked into a thousand wrinkles. "You're her son. You'll be exactly as careful as she was." She let go of my hand. "Keep the book."

I stared at her. "My lady—"

"Keep it. I've no use for it. I can't read that old script anyway." She stood up, slowly, with the manservant's help. "And I think she'd want you to have it."

---

I kept it. I have it still. It has traveled with me through everything since, wrapped in oilcloth at the bottom of one pack or another, and it is the only book from those years I never lost.

I put the verse about Lyra into *Tariniel*, when I wrote it. Near the end. After Lanre's grief, before the fall of the white city. It is a very short verse. Only a few lines. Most people who hear the song do not even notice it.

But Haliax noticed. I know he did. I saw the shadow shift when I sang it, in the Eolian, with the candles burning blue.

*Then I will always sing to you. So that if you ever go back to that quiet, you will hear me, and come home.*

I think, sometimes, that it was the cruelest thing I have ever done. To sing that verse to him. To remind him of that promise, and that voice, and the garden in the white city, ten thousand years gone.

And I think, sometimes, that it was the kindest.

I have never been able to decide.
