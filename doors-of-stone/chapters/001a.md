# Anker's

I came back to Anker's late that first night, after the Eolian, with my fingers aching and my ears still ringing with applause. The common room was nearly empty. Anker was behind the bar, wiping it down with a rag that had been white once, a very long time ago, and he looked up when I came in and jerked his head at a stool.

"Sit," he said. "You look like a man who needs a drink he doesn't have to pay for."

I sat.

The common room was the same as it had always been. Low and dim and warm, with a fire in the hearth and a smell of beer and woodsmoke and the stew that was always, always simmering in the pot. A few students I did not know were sitting at a table by the window, bent over their books. An old man I did not know was asleep in the corner.

Anker drew me a beer without asking and set it on the bar in front of me. Then he leaned on the bar and looked at me.

"So," he said. "Vintas."

"Vintas."

"And the Maer. The big one. Alveron." He shook his head slowly. "Everyone's been talking. That you went off to Severen and did something for the Maer, something big, and now you're his man. That you fought bandits in the Eld. That you went into the Fae and came out again." He looked at me sidelong. "That you learned to fight from the Adem, and they cut off your finger for it."

I held up my hands, all ten fingers. Anker squinted at them.

"Hm," he said. "Well. That one was probably Sim."

"Probably."

---

We talked for a while. Or Anker talked, and I listened, and drank my beer, and let the warm dim familiar room settle around me like an old coat. He told me who had come and who had gone, at the University. Which students had failed their examinations and gone home in disgrace, and which had passed and gone home in triumph. Which masters were feuding with which. He told me that the Eolian had a new fiddle player, a woman from Yll, who was very good, and that Stanchion was in love with her, and did not know it yet, and everyone else did.

He told me that Ambrose's father had come to visit, in the spring, and there had been a terrible row in the Golden Pony, and a window had been broken.

He told me about a fire in the Fishery, over the summer, that had nearly burned down Kilvin's workshop, and how Kilvin had put it out himself, with his bare hands, and a bucket of sand, and a great deal of Cealdish swearing.

And then, when he had run out of news, and my beer was nearly gone, he leaned closer across the bar and lowered his voice.

"And there's something else," he said. "I don't know if it's anything. But I thought you'd want to know."

---

"There was a man," said Anker. "Last autumn. Just after you left. Just after the term started. He came in one evening, late, and sat at the bar, just where you're sitting now. And he asked about you."

I set down my beer.

"Asked what?"

"Where you were. When you'd be back. Whether you still had your room." Anker frowned. "He was very polite about it. Very pleasant. Asked like he was an old friend. Said he'd met you on the road, and wanted to look you up." He shook his head slowly. "But I didn't like him. I don't know why. There wasn't anything wrong with him, that I could see. But I didn't like him."

"What did he look like?"

Anker thought about it.

"That's the thing," he said. "I can't rightly remember. I've tried. I've been trying all summer, on and off, whenever I thought of it." He rubbed his beard. "He was a man. Not young. Not old. Nicely dressed, but not flashy. Had a good voice. Very smooth. Like a priest, or a lawyer." He frowned harder. "And he had a mark on his hand. Here." He touched the back of his own right hand. "A sort of pale mark. Like a scar, or a burn. I noticed it when he paid."

"What did you tell him?"

"Nothing." Anker looked at me. "Told him you'd gone away and I didn't know where and didn't know when you'd be back. Which was true." He paused. "He smiled at that. Paid for his drink. Left a jot on the bar. Too much. And went away." He was quiet for a moment. "And the next morning, there was frost on the step."

I did not say anything.

"In autumn," said Anker. "Early autumn. Warm night. There was frost on the step, like a ring. Just where he'd been standing when he went out." He shook his head. "I scraped it off with my broom. It was gone by noon. I told myself it was nothing. A cold spot. A trick of the weather." He looked at me. "But I thought you'd want to know."

---

I sat very still at the bar. The fire crackled. The students by the window laughed at something in their books.

A man with a mark on his hand. Frost on the step, in a warm autumn.

I did not know, then, who it had been. There were too many possibilities, and not enough facts, and I did not want to think about any of them. I told myself that it was probably nothing. A creditor of Ambrose's, perhaps. A lawyer from the Jakis estate. Some stranger with a grudge. I told myself that frost is only frost, and a mark on a hand is only a mark.

I did not believe myself. But I told myself anyway.

"Thank you, Anker," I said.

"Don't thank me." He took my empty mug and refilled it. "Just don't bring any trouble into my place. I've only just had the roof fixed."

---

I found out later who it was. Not for a long time. Not until a hall in Imre full of candles burning blue, and seven figures standing among them, and one of them, pale and cold, with a pale mark on the back of his right hand where the scar had been left by something older than the world.

Usnea, perhaps. Or Grey Mardou. I have never been sure which. The seven do not always look the same, and they do not always look like themselves. They wear their signs lightly, when they want to walk among us.

But one of them had come to Anker's. Had sat at the bar where I was sitting. Had asked about me. Months before I sang the song that brought them to the Eolian.

They had been watching me, all that time. Long before I knew they were there. Long before I thought I was the one hunting them.

---

I did not sleep well, that night, in my narrow room under the eaves. I lay on the bed in the dark and listened to the tavern settle around me. The creak of the floorboards. The tick of the cooling hearth. The small scratching of mice in the walls.

And once, very late, a sound on the step outside. Like footsteps. Stopping. And then going away.

In the morning, I went out and looked.

There was nothing on the step. No frost. No mark. Only Anker's broom, leaning against the doorframe, and the grey autumn light, and the street beyond, going about its business.

I stood there for a long time anyway.

---

"Did you ever find out?" said Chronicler. "Which one it was?"

The innkeeper was quiet for a moment.

"No," he said. "I asked Haliax, once. In a manner of speaking." He looked at the fire. "He didn't answer. They don't, usually." He was silent for a long moment. "But I think it doesn't matter which one. I think that's the point. They're all the same, in the end. The same patience. The same cold." He looked at the window, at the frost creeping across the shutters, very slowly, from the edges in. "They came to see me, once, at the very beginning. Before I knew anything. And they left a ring of frost on the step, so I'd know they had been there, when I was ready to know."

"And now?"

The innkeeper looked at the frost for a long time.

"Now I'm ready," he said.
