# The Ledger

Devi came to see me on the third day in Imre, in the morning, when the others were at the University. She came up the stairs of the Grey Man with a large leather-bound book under one arm and a pot of tea in the other hand, and she kicked the door of my room open without knocking, and set both down on the table by the window.

"Sit," she said.

I sat. She poured two cups of tea. She pushed one across the table to me. Then she sat down across from me and opened the book.

It was a ledger. I could see that much. Columns of names and figures, in a small, neat, very sharp hand. Pages and pages of them. She turned the pages slowly, running one finger down the columns, until she came to a page near the back. Then she turned the book around and pushed it across the table, beside my tea.

"That's you," she said.

---

I looked at it. There was a name at the top of the page. *Kvothe.* And beneath it, a long column of entries. Dates and amounts. Some in ink, and some in pencil, and some crossed out and written over.

I did not understand it. I did not understand much, then. I looked at the name at the top of the page and it meant nothing to me. A word. A sound.

"You don't remember," said Devi. It was not a question. She was watching my face.

"No."

"No. Sim said you wouldn't." She picked up her tea and looked at me over the rim of the cup with her sharp blue eyes. They looked very strange, under her bare brow. Very wide. Very surprised. As if she had just heard something she could not quite believe. "I'm going to tell you anyway. Because somebody should. And because I've been carrying this ledger around for two years, and I'm tired of it."

---

She told me.

She told me about a boy of fifteen who came up her stairs in the rain with a lute on his back and nothing in his purse, and borrowed four talents at a rate that would have made a Cealdish banker weep. She told me about the loans after that. For tuition, and lute strings, and a new shirt, and a hundred other things. How he always paid back. Late, sometimes. With a great deal of wheedling and charm. But always.

She told me about a night in her rooms, above the butcher's shop, when he had come to her bleeding and burned, and she had stitched him up and not asked any questions. She told me about a page. A page from a book in the Archives, that she had stolen for him, at great risk, the day after the Nighting. And how he had tried to give it back to her, in the hills of Vintas, the night before a raid on a king's camp. And how she would not take it.

She told me about the raid. About what she had made, in a borrowed tent, out of powders and salts and a great deal of stolen lamp oil. About the moment it went off, in the horse lines of the king's army, and lit up the whole valley like noon.

"That's where they went," she said. She touched her bare brow with one finger. "My eyebrows. I was too close when it went up. Singed them right off. They never grew back." She shrugged. "The physicians say they might. Someday. I've stopped caring."

And she told me about a hanging. About a dead man in a cold cellar, and two days of alchemy she would not describe, and standing in a crowd at the foot of a gallows in the great square of Renere, holding a binding with her whole mind while a dead man with red hair walked up the steps.

"That," she said, "was the hardest thing I have ever done. And I have done some very hard things."

---

I listened. I did not feel anything. I sat at the table by the window with my tea going cold in front of me and I listened to this small fierce woman with no eyebrows tell me about a boy I did not remember, and the things she had done for him. And I looked at the ledger, with its long column of figures.

"It's a lot," I said at last.

"It's a great deal." Devi tapped the page. "Four talents, at the start. Then more. Then the stitching, which I charge for. Then the page, which I stole for you at considerable risk to myself, which I also charge for." Her finger moved down the column. "Then the raid. Materials, labor, and one pair of eyebrows. Then the hanging." She stopped. "I've never been able to work out what to charge for the hanging. I've tried. I keep changing the figure." She looked at the crossed-out numbers. "There isn't a number for that. Not really."

"How much?" I said. "All of it?"

She told me.

It was a very large number. I did not have it. I did not have anything. Everything I owned was in a cart in the yard of the Grey Man, and most of it was going into a box.

"I can't pay it," I said.

"No," said Devi. "I didn't think you could."

---

She looked at me for a long time across the table. The morning light came in through the window and fell across her face. She looked tired, I thought. Tired and fierce and very young, under the bare brow.

"Do you know what I said to you?" she said. "After the hanging? When you were in the wagon with the Ruh, and I came to see you before I went back to Imre?"

"No."

"I said you owed me. So much. More than you could pay in a hundred years." She looked down at the ledger. "I said it because I was angry. And frightened. And I hadn't slept in a span, and I kept seeing that dead man walking up the steps with his hood on." She was quiet for a moment. "And because I wanted you to feel it. The weight of it. I wanted you to know what it had cost."

"I'm sorry," I said. I did not know why I said it. It seemed like what she wanted.

"No you're not." Devi's voice was not unkind. "You can't be. You don't remember enough to be sorry." She picked up her tea, and drank it, and set the cup down. "That's what I realized. On the road back from Renere. You were sitting in the back of that wagon, grey as a stone, and I'd just told you that you owed me more than you could ever pay. And you looked at me and said *all right*. Like I'd told you it might rain." She shook her head. "And I thought: what's the point? What's the point of a debt, if the person who owes it doesn't know he owes it?"

---

She reached out and took the ledger back. She looked at the page for a long moment. At the name at the top. At the long column of figures, and the crossed-out numbers at the bottom.

Then she took hold of the page, very carefully, near the spine. And she tore it out.

I watched her. I did not understand.

She folded the page in half. Then in half again. She held it for a moment in her small ink-stained hands. Then she reached across the table and tucked it into the front of my shirt, against my chest.

"There," she said. "It's yours now. Put it in your box, with the rest. With your name and your lute and whatever else." She sat back. "When you get it all back—when you're you again—you can take it out and look at it. And you'll know what you owe."

"And then I'll pay it?"

"No." Devi smiled. It was a strange smile, under her bare brow. Crooked and fierce and very sad. "Then you'll know you *don't* have to. Because it's not in my book anymore. It's in yours." She closed the ledger. "I don't hold debts I can't collect. It's bad business. And I'm very good at business."

---

She stood up. She picked up the ledger and the teapot. At the door, she stopped, and looked back at me.

"One more thing," she said. "When you're you again. Come and see me. Not to pay. Just to see me." She was quiet for a moment. "I'd like to hear you say thank you. When you actually mean it. When you actually know what for."

"I will," I said.

"You won't remember saying that."

"No."

"Well." Devi shrugged. "I'll remember. That'll have to do."

And she went down the stairs, and I heard the door of the Grey Man bang shut behind her, and her quick sharp footsteps going away up the street.

---

The folded page went into the chest. I put it there myself, the night they made the chest in the Fishery, when the others were laying their things inside. Devi watched from across the room with her arms folded. She did not say anything. She only nodded, once, when I laid it beside Sim's poem.

It was still there, seven years later. When the chest opened. When I opened it.

I read it that afternoon, sitting by the fire in the common room of the Waystone, after Sim's poem, with Bast asleep in his chair and the whole town of Newarre still talking about me in low voices in the square outside.

I read every line. Every figure. Every crossed-out number at the bottom.

And I laughed, and then I wept, and then I laughed again. Because at the very bottom of the page, beneath all the crossed-out numbers, in a different ink, very small, she had written one more line. She must have written it that morning, at the table by the window, before she tore the page out. While I sat across from her and watched and did not understand.

"Have you written to her?" said Chronicler softly. "Since?"

"Not yet." The innkeeper looked at the fire. "I'm going to go and see her. Instead. When this is done." He almost smiled. "She asked me to say thank you. When I knew what for." He was quiet for a moment. "I think I'll need to say it in person. It's too big for a letter."

"Will she have eyebrows?"

The innkeeper looked at him. And then, for a moment, something moved at the corner of his mouth.

"I hope not," he said. "I don't think I'd recognize her."
