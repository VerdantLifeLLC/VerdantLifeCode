# Interlude: Blue Flame

Every candle in the Waystone Inn was burning blue.

The fire in the hearth. The candles on the tables. The lantern hanging from its hook by the kitchen door. All of them, all at once, burning a deep, cold, steady blue, the blue of a winter sky at dusk, the blue of a bruise.

The frost on the shutters crept inward. It crept across the floor, white and feathery, from the walls toward the center of the room. It crept up the legs of the tables and the chairs. It crept up the bar, across the dark wood, over the bottles on their shelves. The bottles cracked, one by one, with small sharp sounds like breaking bones, as the liquid inside them froze.

Chronicler's breath came out of him in great white plumes. His hands were so cold he could not feel his pen. He looked down and saw that the ink on the page in front of him had frozen, in the middle of a line. The last word he had written was half a word, and the rest of it was a smear of black ice.

Bast was on his feet. He had moved so fast that his chair had gone over behind him. He stood between the table and the door with his hands at his sides, and his eyes had gone black. Not dark. Black. Like holes cut in the world.

The innkeeper had not moved. He sat at the table, very still, with his hands folded in front of him, and looked at the door.

"Bast," he said. "Sit down."

"No."

"Bast."

"*No*." Bast did not turn around. His voice was strange. Deeper. Older. "Not this time, Reshi. Not this time. I let you walk out that door at the Lackless house alone. I let you go into the Tower of Thorns alone. I'm not letting you face him alone."

"You can't fight him."

"I know." Bast's voice was very steady. "I'm going to anyway."

---

Someone knocked on the door.

Three times. Slow and even. Very polite. The knock of a guest, arriving for dinner.

Nobody moved.

The knock came again. Three times. Slow and even.

Then a voice. Soft and reasonable and utterly without warmth. The voice of a man asking you to pass the salt.

"I know you're there," it said. "I can hear you. All of you." A pause. "May I come in?"

The innkeeper looked at the door for a long moment.

Then he said, very quietly, "The door is barred."

"I know." The voice sounded amused. "I'm asking to be polite. I was taught manners, once, a very long time ago. Before." A pause. "I could come in anyway. You know I could. The bar is iron, but the door is only wood, and wood is very cold tonight. Very brittle." Another pause. "But I'd rather you let me in. It's so much more civilized."

"And if I don't?"

The voice was silent for a moment.

"Then I'll come in anyway," it said. "And I'll be less polite about it. And the scribe will probably die. And the princeling. And then, afterward, I'll go down the road to the little stone church, where half the town is hiding, and I'll be even less polite there." A pause. "You know me, singer. You know what I do when I'm not polite."

The innkeeper closed his eyes.

"Bast," he said. "Lift the bar."

"*Reshi—*"

"Lift the bar, Bast. Please."

---

Bast did not move for a long time. He stood between the table and the door with his black eyes fixed on the frosted wood, and his hands clenched at his sides, and he did not move.

Then, slowly, he turned his head and looked at the innkeeper.

The innkeeper looked back at him. His green eyes were very calm.

"Trust me," he said.

Bast looked at him for a long moment. Then he went to the door. He put his hands on the heavy iron bar, and flinched, and snatched them back. The iron burned him. Even through the frost. Even through the cold.

He put his hands on it again. He did not flinch this time. He lifted the bar out of its brackets, with his face twisted in pain and the skin of his palms smoking, and set it down against the wall.

The door swung open on its own.

---

He was standing on the porch, in the dark, with the frost spreading out around his feet.

He was tall and pale and very still, with a face like a knife. His hair was the color of frost. His eyes were black. Not dark. Black. Pure and depthless, like holes cut in the world.

And there was a scar on his face.

It ran from the corner of his left eye to the corner of his mouth. A long pale ridge of tissue, puckered and twisted, like the scar of a burn. It pulled at his face when he smiled, so that the smile was lopsided. Crooked. Wrong.

He smiled now.

"Hello, singer," said Cinder.

---

He came in. He did not walk. He drifted, the way a leaf drifts, the way smoke drifts. He came through the doorway and into the common room of the Waystone Inn, and the frost went ahead of him, spreading across the floor like a carpet unrolled for a king.

He stopped a few paces inside the door. He looked around the room. At the bar, and the frozen bottles, and the tables with their chairs upside down on some of them. At the fire in the great stone hearth, burning blue. At the sword hanging above it, on two iron pegs, over a plaque with a single word burned into it.

He looked at the sword for a long time.

"*Folly*," he said. He laughed softly. "Oh, that's very good. Did you name it yourself?"

The innkeeper did not answer.

Cinder turned. He looked at Chronicler, sitting frozen at the table by the hearth with his pen in his hand and his breath smoking in the air.

"The scribe," he said. "Hello. You've been writing, I see. Three days of writing." He tilted his head. "Three days of a name coming back. I heard every word. From the woods, beyond the fields. I've been listening since the first morning." He smiled his crooked smile. "It was very beautiful. You have a lovely hand, you know. Very neat. Very small."

Chronicler could not speak. He could not move. He sat at the table with his frozen pen and stared at the man with the scarred face, and every part of him was screaming.

Cinder turned to Bast.

Bast was standing by the open door, where he had set down the bar. His hands were burned and smoking. His eyes were black. His face was white and still and utterly without fear.

"The princeling," said Cinder softly. "The little Fae prince. The one who stepped in front of five Sithe arrows and survived." He looked at Bast's burned hands. "You lifted the iron. For him. How sweet."

Bast did not say anything. But his lips pulled back from his teeth. And his teeth were not the teeth of a young man.

Cinder laughed.

"Don't," he said. "Please. I've killed Fae older than your father's court. I'd hate to have to kill you. You're very pretty."

---

Then, at last, he turned to the innkeeper.

The innkeeper had not moved. He was still sitting at the table, with his hands folded in front of him, looking at Cinder with his calm green eyes.

Cinder looked at him for a long time. His black eyes moved slowly over the innkeeper's face. His red hair, true red, the color of a flame. The bruises on his jaw. The scar on his cheek. His hands, folded on the table. His left wrist, with its thin pale line around it like a bracelet.

"Kote," said Cinder. "That's what they call you, isn't it? In this little town. Kote the innkeeper."

"Yes."

"Kote." Cinder tasted the word. "Disaster. In the old Siaru." He smiled his crooked smile. "Who gave it to you? The little girl under the University? The one who names things?"

The innkeeper did not answer.

"It was clever," said Cinder. "I'll give her that. A name so small it couldn't call anything. A name for something that happened, not something that is." He shook his head slowly. "I searched for you for years, you know. Years. After the cellar. After the old man went through the door." He touched the scar on his face. "After *she* did this to me." His voice went very soft. "I searched and searched. And I couldn't find you. Because there was nothing to find. Only a little name. A disaster. Hiding behind a stone."

He glanced toward the window, toward the crossroads, where the waystone stood in the dark.

"That was clever too," he said. "The stone. I walked past this town three times, in the last seven years. Three times. And I never heard a thing." He looked back at the innkeeper. "Until three days ago. When you started talking."

"I know," said the innkeeper.

"You knew I'd hear."

"Yes."

"And you did it anyway."

"Yes."

Cinder looked at him for a long moment. Something moved in his black eyes.

"Why?" he said.

---

The innkeeper was quiet for a long time.

"Because I was tired," he said at last. "Of being small. Of waiting. Of polishing the bar." He looked at Cinder. "Because people are dying. Because the door is cracked, and things are coming through, and every year it gets worse. Because a boy came to my door tonight and told me about eleven cows frozen standing up in a field." He paused. "Because a man named Shep is buried in the churchyard behind the little stone church, and he laughed at his own jokes, and I liked him."

Cinder's crooked smile widened.

"You're going to fight me," he said. "Aren't you? You're going to try. With your name locked up in a box upstairs, and nothing inside you but a little disaster." He shook his head. "Oh, singer. You haven't changed at all."

"No," said the innkeeper. "I don't suppose I have."

---

Cinder drew his sword.

It came out of its sheath with no sound at all. A long pale blade, the color of ice. The air around it went white with frost. The candles on the tables flickered and guttered, blue and cold.

"I'm not going to kill you," he said. "You know that. I can't. The lord still forbids it." He looked at the blade in his hand. "But I can hurt you. I can hurt you very much. And I can hurt them." He glanced at Chronicler. At Bast. "And I can take you. To the door. With the name, and the box, and everything else in that chest upstairs."

"The chest won't open," said the innkeeper. "Not for you. Not for anyone but me. And I can't open it either. Not as Kote."

"I know." Cinder smiled. "That's why I'm going to take the whole chest. And you with it. And when we get to the door, you'll open it. Because you'll have to. Because I'll make you." His black eyes glittered. "It's amazing, what a man will do, when you start taking away the people he loves. One at a time."

He turned toward Chronicler.

"I'll start with the scribe," he said.

---

Bast moved.

He moved faster than Chronicler had ever seen anything move. Faster than a cat, faster than a snake, faster than thought. One moment he was standing by the door, and the next he was in front of Cinder, between the pale sword and Chronicler, with his burned hands raised and his black eyes blazing and his lips drawn back from teeth that were long and sharp and very white.

He struck.

Cinder was faster.

He did not even seem to move. He was simply somewhere else, a pace to the left, and the pale sword was moving, and Bast was falling. Chronicler heard the sound it made. A small, sharp, wet sound. Like a knife going into fruit.

Bast hit the floor. He lay on his side on the frost-covered boards, with his hand pressed to his side, and blood coming out between his fingers. Red blood, very bright against the white frost.

He did not cry out. He looked up at Cinder with his black eyes, and his teeth bared, and he tried to get up.

Cinder put his boot on Bast's chest and pushed him back down.

"Stay," he said, as you would say it to a dog. "Stay down, little prince. I'll get to you later."

---

The innkeeper was on his feet.

He had not moved from the table in all of this. But he was on his feet now, and his chair was lying on its side behind him, and his hands were not folded anymore. They were clenched at his sides.

And his face had changed.

Chronicler saw it. He would remember it for the rest of his life. The innkeeper's face, calm and mild and bruised, changing. The calm going out of it, like a tide going out. And something else coming in. Something that had been buried a very long time, under years of polishing and pouring and waiting.

It was not Kote's face. Kote had never looked like that.

It was anger. Pure and clean and cold. Anger like a blade. Anger like the Heart of Stone.

"Get your boot off him," said the innkeeper.

His voice had changed too. It was deeper. Harder. It filled the common room like water filling a cup.

Cinder looked at him. His crooked smile flickered.

"Or what?" he said. "What will you do, Kote? You have nothing. No name. No power. No sword, even; it's hanging on the wall." He pressed down with his boot. Bast made a small sound. "What will you do?"

The innkeeper looked at him for a long moment.

Then he turned his back on Cinder, and walked to the stairs.

---

Cinder watched him go. His black eyes narrowed.

"Where are you going?" he said.

The innkeeper did not answer. He climbed the stairs. One step at a time. Steadily. Without hurry.

"The chest won't open," Cinder called after him. "You know it won't. You can't open it. Not as you are."

The innkeeper stopped on the landing. He turned and looked down at Cinder, standing in the middle of the frost-covered common room with his boot on Bast's chest and his pale sword in his hand.

"I know," he said.

And then he looked at Chronicler. Sitting frozen at the table by the hearth, with his pen in his hand and his breath smoking in the air and the pages of three days of writing spread out in front of him.

"Scribe," said the innkeeper quietly. "Bring the story."
