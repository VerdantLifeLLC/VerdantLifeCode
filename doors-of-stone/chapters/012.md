# The Cellarage

Winter came down on the University that year like a lid on a pot. The snow that had fallen the morning Ambrose left did not melt. More came after it, and more after that, until the courtyards were white and the eaves were hung with icicles as long as my arm, and the river froze along its edges into shelves of grey ice that groaned and cracked in the night.

I spent most of that winter underground.

Not all of it. I still went to classes, and to the Fishery, and to the Eolian twice a span to play. I still ate with Sim and Wil at Anker's and argued with Fela about stone and read in the Archives until Wil threw me out at closing. But more and more, as the days grew short and the nights grew long and bitter, I found myself going down into the Underthing.

It was warmer there. That was part of it. The deep tunnels held their heat the way a banked fire does, and even on the coldest nights, when the wind howled across the rooftops like a pack of wolves, the air below was still and mild. Auri had made the Underthing her home for years, and I began to understand why. It was quiet. It was private. It was a world unto itself, with its own geography and its own weather and its own strange, patient logic.

And it was full of stone.

I brought Elodin's stone with me whenever I went down. I would sit in one of Auri's rooms, in Mantle or the Twelve or the long echoing hall she called Vaults, and I would take the stone out of my pocket and set it on the floor in front of me, and I would listen.

I did not hear it. Not yet. But I was learning, slowly, what Fela had meant about waiting. About wanting. About the difference between listening and hunger.

It was not easy. I have always been a hungry person. I have always wanted things, and wanted them now, and gone after them with everything I had. I did not know how to sit still and want nothing. I did not know how to let the world be what it was without reaching out to grab it.

But the Underthing was teaching me. Slowly, patiently, the way stone teaches everything.

---

Auri did not seem to mind my presence. If anything, she seemed pleased by it. She would come and sit with me sometimes while I listened, humming softly to herself or arranging small things in patterns on the floor. Pebbles. Bits of glass. Leaves she had gathered in the autumn and kept pressed between the pages of an old book. She never asked what I was doing. She seemed to understand, in her strange way, that it was not a thing that could be explained.

Other times she would take me places. She would appear at my elbow with no warning and take my hand and say, "Come. There's something you should see." And she would lead me through the tunnels, down passages I had never known existed, through doors that seemed to open only for her, to show me a thing she had found.

A room where the water dripping from the ceiling had built up, over centuries, into a great pale column of stone, smooth and gleaming as bone.

A long narrow gallery where the walls were covered in writing, faded almost to nothing, in a script I did not know.

A tiny round chamber, no bigger than a closet, where the acoustics were so perfect that a whisper spoken at one side could be heard as clearly as a shout at the other. Auri stood at one side and I stood at the other, and she whispered my name, and it came to me as clear and sweet as a bell.

"This is Hush," she said, when I had come around to her side. "It's where the Underthing keeps its secrets."

"What secrets?"

She looked at me with her enormous eyes. "If I told you," she said, "they wouldn't be secrets anymore."

---

It was in the second month of winter that she first took me to the Cellarage.

I had come down late, after a long night at the Eolian. I was tired and my fingers ached from playing, and I wanted nothing more than to sit somewhere quiet and warm and let the stone be a stone. But when I came down through the grate behind the Mains, Auri was waiting for me at the bottom, and there was something in her face I had never seen before.

It was worry.

"Kvothe," she said. "Come. Quickly."

"What's wrong?"

"Nothing's wrong." She took my hand and began to pull me along the tunnel. "Not yet. But something's awake. And you should see it before it goes back to sleep."

She led me deep. Deeper than I had ever been. Down through the Twelve and along the passage she called Downway, past the place where the tunnels stopped being brick and started being stone. Past the steep narrow slope she had shown me once before, the one she said was too loud. We went down it now, and I felt the stone in my pocket grow heavy, heavier than it had ever been.

At the bottom, there was a door.

It was not a large door. It was old and grey and made of wood so dark with age it was almost black, bound with bands of iron that had rusted to the color of dried blood. It stood half open. Beyond it, there was darkness.

"This is the Cellarage," Auri whispered. "It's very old. Older than anything else down here. It was here before the Underthing was the Underthing."

"What is it?"

She did not answer. She let go of my hand and stepped through the door into the darkness. After a moment, I followed.

---

I held up my sympathy lamp and turned it slowly, letting the red light play across the room.

It was a large room, much larger than I had expected. Its ceiling was low and vaulted, supported by thick round pillars of rough grey stone. The floor was stone too, uneven and worn smooth in places, as if by the passage of many feet over many years. The air was cold and dry and smelled of dust and something else, something faint and sweet, like old flowers pressed between the pages of a book.

And the room was full of things.

Chests. Barrels. Crates. Long wooden boxes that looked disturbingly like coffins. Heaps of cloth, rotted almost to nothing. Racks of weapons, rusted and broken. Shelves of pottery, cracked and dusty. Piles of books, so old their bindings had crumbled away, leaving only stacks of loose pages brown and brittle as autumn leaves.

It looked like a storehouse. Or a treasury. Or a tomb.

"What is all this?" I breathed.

"Things people forgot," Auri said. She was standing a little way ahead of me, very still, her pale hair floating around her head like a cloud. "Things they put away and never came back for. Some of them are very sad. Some of them are angry." She turned to look at me. "You mustn't touch anything. They don't like to be touched."

"I won't."

She nodded and turned back. She began to walk slowly between the heaps and piles, and I followed her, holding my lamp high. The red light fell across rusted helmets and cracked shields, across moldering tapestries and heaps of tarnished coins. I saw a harp with no strings. A child's wooden horse, its paint long since faded. A crown of blackened silver set with dull stones, lying on its side in the dust as if someone had dropped it and walked away.

And then, at the far end of the room, I saw the bed.

It was a great bed, carved of black stone. It stood on a low dais at the center of the far wall, surrounded by a ring of pillars. Its posts were carved with twisting vines and leaves, so delicate and so real that I half expected them to move in the draft. Its canopy had long since rotted away, leaving only a few tattered strips of cloth hanging from the frame like cobwebs.

And on the bed, there was a man.

He lay on his back with his hands folded on his chest. He was tall and very thin, and he was dressed in the remains of what had once been fine clothes, a long robe of some dark rich fabric embroidered with thread that had once been gold. A crown sat on his head, a simple circlet of pale metal that gleamed faintly in the red light of my lamp. His face was gaunt and grey, the skin stretched tight over the bones. His eyes were closed.

He was, quite obviously, dead. He had been dead for a very long time.

And he was breathing.

---

I stood very still. I did not breathe. I watched the dead man on the bed, and I watched his chest, and I waited.

It rose. Very slowly. So slowly I would not have seen it if I had not been watching for it. It rose, and paused, and fell.

A long time passed. I counted my own heartbeats. Twenty. Thirty. Forty.

It rose again.

"Auri," I whispered. "What is that?"

"That's the Sleeper," she whispered back. "He's been sleeping for a very long time. Longer than anyone. He's sleeping so deep that he's almost dead. But not quite." She was quiet for a moment. "Sometimes he dreams. And when he dreams, he wakes up a little. Just a little. And then he goes back to sleep."

"Is he awake now?"

"A little." She took a step back, toward me. "That's why I brought you. He's been waking more lately. Since the autumn. Since you came back." She looked at me, and her eyes were very wide. "I think he knows you're here."

A chill went through me. "Why would he know that?"

"I don't know." She shook her head. "But he's been dreaming about you. I can tell. The whole Cellarage has been dreaming about you."

I looked at the man on the bed. At his gaunt grey face, his folded hands, his pale crown. I looked at the things piled around him, the weapons and the treasures and the forgotten, broken things. And I understood, suddenly, what this place was.

It was a barrow. A tomb for a king. The kind of place where the old kings of the north had been buried with their treasures around them, deep in the earth, to sleep until the end of the world.

Except that this king was not sleeping the way the dead sleep. He was sleeping the way the living do. And he was dreaming.

"Auri," I said very quietly. "I think we should go."

"Yes," she said. "I think so too."

We turned and began to walk back toward the door, slowly, carefully, the way you walk past a sleeping dog. The red light of my lamp swung across the heaped treasures. My footsteps sounded very loud on the stone.

We were halfway to the door when I heard it.

It was a sound like breath. Long and slow and deep, like wind moving through a cave. It came from behind us, from the bed on its dais. And beneath the sound of breath, so faint I could barely hear it, there was something else.

A voice. A single word, spoken so slowly it was almost not a word at all. A long deep syllable, like the grinding of stone on stone.

I did not know the word. But I knew, the way you sometimes know things, that it was a name.

And I knew, with a certainty that turned my bones to water, that it was mine.

---

We ran.

I am not ashamed to say it. I ran as fast as I have ever run, out of the Cellarage and up the steep narrow slope and back through the tunnels, Auri's hand in mine, her bare feet slapping on the stone beside me. We did not stop until we reached the Twelve, and even then, I stood in the center of the great round room with my heart hammering and my lamp shaking in my hand, listening for footsteps behind us.

There were none. The Underthing was silent.

Auri was breathing hard beside me. She was trembling. I put my arm around her, and she leaned against me, her head against my chest, the way a frightened child leans against its mother.

"I'm sorry," she whispered. "I shouldn't have taken you there. I shouldn't have. It wasn't proper."

"It's all right, Auri."

"It isn't all right." She pulled away and looked up at me, and her eyes were full of tears. "He knows you now. He's seen you. And now he'll dream about you, and when he dreams, he wakes." She shook her head. "It isn't all right at all."

I did not know what to say. I held her until she stopped trembling. Then I took her back to Mantle and sat with her until she fell asleep, curled up on her little bed of blankets with her hands tucked under her chin.

And then I sat alone in the dark, with the stone in my pocket, and I thought about what I had seen.

A king in a barrow, sleeping. Dreaming. Waking.

Speaking my name.

I did not know who he was. I did not know how he had come to be there, or how long he had slept, or what he wanted. I did not know how he knew my name, or why he had spoken it.

But I remembered something Skarpi had said, years ago, in a tavern in Tarbean. Something about the old days, before the Aturan Empire, before the Creation War. When there had been kings in the world who were more than kings. Who had been namers, and shapers, and things that were not quite human.

When they died, Skarpi had said, some of them did not die all the way.

I sat in the dark and listened to the silence of the Underthing, and I did not sleep at all that night.
