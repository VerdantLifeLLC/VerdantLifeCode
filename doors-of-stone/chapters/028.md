# The Mill Race

The Mill Race was an inn at the bottom of the cliff, in the part of the low city where the river bent close against the rock and the great wheels of the grain mills turned day and night with a sound like slow thunder. It was a respectable place, clean and quiet, the kind of inn where merchants' wives stayed while their husbands did business in the high city. It had a small walled courtyard with a pear tree in it, and a common room with whitewashed walls and a floor of scrubbed flagstones, and a landlady named Orla who looked at me when I came in as if I were something her cat had brought her.

"The singer," I said. "Dark hair. I was told she was staying here."

Orla looked me up and down. Her eyes lingered on my sword, and my lute case, and my face.

"And who might you be?"

"A friend."

"She's had a lot of friends asking after her." Orla folded her arms. "Most of them didn't carry swords."

"I'm a musician. The sword is for the road."

"Hm." She did not look convinced. But after a moment she jerked her head toward the courtyard. "She's out back. Under the pear tree. Been sitting there all morning with her harp, not playing it. If you upset her, I'll have you thrown in the river."

"I'll try not to upset her."

"Try harder than that," said Orla, and went back to her kitchen.

---

Denna was sitting on a bench under the pear tree with her harp in her lap. She was not playing it. She was looking at it, her fingers resting on the strings, her head bowed. The pear tree was in blossom, and the petals had fallen around her like snow, in her hair and on her shoulders and on the strings of the harp.

She did not look up as I came across the courtyard. But I saw her fingers tighten on the strings, and I knew she had heard me.

"Kvothe," she said.

"Denna."

"I heard you'd been expelled." She still did not look up. "I heard it three days ago, from a traveler who'd come down from Imre. He said you'd broken into the Archives and tried to steal something and the masters threw you out." Now she looked up. Her eyes were very dark. "Is it true?"

"Mostly. I didn't try to steal anything."

"But you broke in."

"I went somewhere I shouldn't have."

She looked at me for a long moment. Then she laughed, a small tired sound. "Of course you did," she said. "Of course." She moved her harp aside and patted the bench beside her. "Sit. Tell me."

I sat. The pear blossoms fell around us, white and soft and faintly sweet.

I did not tell her. Not about the stairs, or the Chamber of Watches, or the door. I told her only that I had gone looking for something in the Archives, something about my family, and that I had found it, and that it had cost me everything.

"Your family," she said. "I thought you didn't have any family. I thought they were all dead."

"They are. Mostly." I hesitated. "But I found out who my mother was. Where she came from. Before she met my father."

"Who was she?"

I looked at her. I thought about telling her. I wanted to. I wanted to tell her everything, the way I had told Meluan. I wanted to lay it all out in front of her, the blue book and the black mark and the box under my pillow, and let her see it, and let her tell me what it meant.

But I thought about the Deller farm. About the frozen mud, and a little girl in a well.

"Someone important," I said. "Once. A long time ago. It doesn't matter now."

She looked at me for a long moment. She knew I was holding something back. She always knew. But she did not press. That was one of the things that made Denna so dangerous to me: she never pressed. She simply waited, with those dark eyes, until you gave her what she wanted of your own free will.

"All right," she said softly. "Keep your secrets. We all have them."

---

We sat under the pear tree for a long time. She played a little, eventually. Not a song, just a few idle chords, the way a musician will play when she is thinking. I listened. The mill wheels turned in the distance with their slow thunder. The pear blossoms fell.

"Why are you in Severen?" I asked at last.

She glanced at me. "Work."

"For your patron?"

"Yes."

"The song?"

"Yes." She plucked a string. It rang out, clear and sweet, and faded. "It's nearly finished. There's just a little more I need. A few more sources. Old ones." She looked down at her harp. "He's sent me all over, these last few months. Up into the Commonwealth, down into Atur, across to the Small Kingdoms. Anywhere there's an old family with an old story. I go and I listen and I write it down."

"Old families," I said slowly. "With old stories. Like the Dellers?"

Her fingers went still on the strings.

She did not look at me. She sat very still under the pear tree, with the blossoms falling around her, and her face went white. Not all at once. Slowly, the way milk spreads in water. First her lips, then her cheeks, then her whole face, until she looked like a statue carved of ivory.

"How do you know that name?" she whispered.

"I was there," I said. "The night after you left."

She turned to look at me. Her eyes were enormous.

"What do you mean?"

I told her. I told her about the blue candle, and the smoke on the wind, and the burning house. I told her about the frozen mud, and the five shapes lying in it. I told her about the well, and the little girl in the bucket, and the scrap of knotted cloth.

I did not tell her what Nell had said about the pretty lady. I did not have to. I saw it land in her face, the whole weight of it, the moment I said *the night after you left*.

When I was done, she sat there under the pear tree with her hands pressed flat against her harp strings, so hard that the strings cut into her fingers. She did not seem to notice. She was staring at nothing.

"No," she said. "No. That's not—they were fine. When I left them, they were fine. Mistress Deller gave me cider. The little girl showed me her kitten. They were *fine*."

"I know."

"It was an accident. A fire. Fires happen. A lamp tipped over, or—"

"Denna." I took her hand. It was ice cold. "The mud was frozen. In spring. There was frost on their eyelashes. The fire burned blue." I held her eyes. "It wasn't an accident. You know what it was. You've been writing a song about them for three years."

She pulled her hand away. She stood up so suddenly that her harp fell off the bench and struck the flagstones with a discordant jangle of strings. She did not pick it up. She stood there with her arms wrapped around herself, shaking.

"You're wrong," she said. "You're wrong. They're a story. The Chandrian are a story. They're not *real*."

"They're real. I've seen them. They killed my parents."

She stared at me.

"They killed my parents," I said again. My voice was very steady. "And everyone in my troupe. Because my parents were writing a song about Lanre. A true one. And the Chandrian don't like true songs about Lanre." I paused. "They killed the Dellers because the Dellers had a cloth that told the truth about something. Something old. And someone sent you to read it first, so they'd know exactly what was on it before they burned it."

"No." She shook her head, again and again. "No. No. He wouldn't. He wouldn't do that. He's not—he's *kind* to me, Kvothe. He's the only one who's ever been kind to me. He's taught me so much. He's—"

She stopped. Her hand had gone to her wrist, to the place where I had seen the bruise, all those months ago on the bluff above the river at Imre. She pressed it there, hard, as if something under the skin hurt.

"He's kind to me," she said again. But her voice had changed.

---

I stood up. I picked up her harp from the flagstones and looked it over. One of the strings had snapped, and there was a small crack in the soundboard near the base. I set it carefully on the bench.

"Denna," I said. "Who is he?"

She shook her head. She was crying now, silently, the tears running down her white face.

"I can't," she whispered. "I promised. I swore it. If I tell, he'll know, and he'll leave, and—"

"And what? You'll be alone? You'll be poor?" I stepped toward her. "Denna, people are *dying*. Children are dying. That little girl lost her whole family, and she's six years old, and the last kind thing anyone did for her was you giving her mother a silver penny to look at a cloth." My voice cracked. "Please. Tell me who he is."

She looked at me. And for a moment, just a moment, I thought she would tell me. I saw it in her face. I saw her mouth open, and her breath catch, and the name rise up behind her lips.

Then something changed. Something closed.

"I can't," she said. "Kvothe, I *can't*. You don't understand. It's not just a promise. It's not just words." She held up her hand. On her wrist, just below the place where the bruise had been, there was a thin cord tied in a knot. I had never noticed it before. It was so fine and so dark it was almost invisible against her skin. "He tied it," she whispered. "When he first took me on. He said it was a promise. A binding. He said if I ever told anyone his name, or anything about him, the knot would know."

I stared at the cord. At the knot. It was small and complex, looped and twisted in a pattern I could not read. But I could feel it, even from where I stood. The faint tightening of the air. The sense of something reaching out and catching hold.

It held.

"What happens?" I asked quietly. "If you tell?"

She looked down at the knot on her wrist.

"I don't know," she said. "He never said. He just smiled." She shivered. "I've never wanted to find out."

---

We stood in the courtyard of the Mill Race, under the pear tree, with the blossoms falling around us like snow, and we did not speak for a long time.

At last she bent down and picked up her harp. She held it against her chest. Her fingers found the broken string and touched it, gently, the way you might touch a wound.

"I need to think," she said. "Please. I need to be alone. I need to think about this."

"Denna—"

"Please, Kvothe." She looked at me. Her eyes were red and swollen, but they were clear. "I'm not running away. I promise. I'm not going anywhere. I just need to think." She paused. "Come back tomorrow. In the evening. I'll be here."

I looked at her for a long moment. I wanted to stay. I wanted to take her by the hand and lead her away from Severen, away from Vintas, away from her patron and his knots and his songs and his errands to old families with old stories. I wanted to take her somewhere safe, somewhere no one could find her.

But I did not know anywhere like that. I never had.

"All right," I said. "Tomorrow evening."

She nodded. She turned and walked toward the inn, clutching her broken harp. At the door she stopped and looked back at me.

"Kvothe," she said. "The little girl. Nell. Is she all right?"

"She's with a troupe. Edema Ruh. Good people. They'll take care of her."

Denna closed her eyes. "Good," she whispered. "Good."

She went inside. The door closed behind her.

I stood alone under the pear tree for a long time. Then I turned and walked out of the courtyard and into the street.

---

I did not go back up to the high city. Not right away.

Instead, I found a tavern across the street from the Mill Race, a dim smoky place called the Wheel and Wedge, full of millers and dockhands and river men. I bought a mug of beer I did not drink and took a table by the window, where I could see the door of the Mill Race through the dirty glass.

And I waited.

I am not proud of this. I want you to know that. I had told Denna I would come back tomorrow evening, and I meant it. I did not intend to break my word. But I had seen her face when I said *the Dellers*. I had seen the knot on her wrist. And I knew, the way you sometimes know things, that she would not stay in her room and think.

She would go to him.

She would go to him and ask him if it was true. Because that is what you do, when someone you trust has been accused of something terrible. You go to them. You look them in the eye. You ask.

And if she went to him, I would follow. And I would see his face.

It was dusk when she came out. She had changed into a dark cloak with a deep hood, and she walked quickly, with her head down, the way people walk when they do not want to be noticed. She turned left out of the courtyard and went up the street toward the cliff road.

I left my beer on the table and followed her.

---

She went up. Up the long steep road cut into the face of the cliff, past the teams of oxen hauling carts, past the beggars and the pilgrims and the late-working porters. I followed a long way behind her, keeping to the shadows, keeping other people between us. She did not look back.

At the top of the cliff road, she turned right, into the high city. She walked through the wide clean streets, past the great houses behind their high walls, past gardens and fountains and statues of long-dead Maers. The lamplighters were out, and the streets were filling with soft golden light.

She walked for a long time. She seemed to know exactly where she was going. She turned down one street and then another, through a small square with a fountain, down a narrow lane between two high garden walls.

At the end of the lane, there was a door in the wall. A plain wooden door, painted green, with no handle on the outside.

Denna stopped in front of it. She stood there for a moment, very still. Then she raised her hand and knocked. Three times, then twice, then once.

The door opened.

I was at the other end of the lane, pressed into the shadow of a doorway. I could not see who opened it. The light from inside was too bright, and the door was angled away from me. I saw only a hand, holding the door. An old hand, spotted and gentle.

Denna went inside. The door closed behind her.

I stood in the shadows at the end of the lane, and I did not move for a long time.

I knew that lane. I had walked down it before. The year before, in my first months in Severen, when I had been new at court and did not know anyone. Someone had shown it to me. Someone had walked with me down this lane, on a warm summer evening, and told me that at the end of it, behind the wall, there was a private garden, very old and very beautiful, that belonged to an old friend of his.

He had said he sometimes played tak there, when he wanted to be alone.
