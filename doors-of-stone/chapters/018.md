# Valaritas

The Nighting fell on a cold clear night at the end of winter, when the snow had begun to soften and grey at the edges and the icicles dripped all day long in the thin sunshine. It was a festival of lights, the Nighting, an old Aturan custom that had outlived the empire that invented it. On the longest night of late winter, people hung lanterns in their windows and set candles on their doorsteps and walked through the streets with torches, singing, to remind the dark that it would not last forever.

Imre was ablaze with it. From the top of the stone bridge, looking across the river, the whole town seemed to be on fire. Lanterns hung from every eave. Bonfires burned in every square. The sound of music and laughter drifted across the water, and the smell of roasting meat and spiced wine.

The University was dark.

Most of the students had gone across the river to the feast. The few who remained had shut themselves in their rooms, or gathered in small quiet groups at Anker's or the Mews. The courtyards were empty. The great grey buildings stood silent and shadowed against the stars.

And the Archives stood darkest of all. A great windowless block of stone, black against the sky, like a hole cut in the night.

---

We met at the grate behind the Mains an hour after sundown. Devi came wrapped in a dark cloak with a heavy satchel over her shoulder, her pale hair tucked up beneath a knitted cap. Fela came in a plain grey dress and sturdy boots, her face pale and set. I had my sympathy lamp, and a coil of rope, and a small bag of tools, and Elodin's stone in my pocket.

And I had Caesura.

I had not meant to bring it. I had not worn my sword since I came back from Ademre; it did not seem right, somehow, to carry it through the halls of the University. But when I had stood in my room that evening, gathering my things, I had found myself reaching for it. I had strapped it on without thinking, the way you might put on a coat before going out into the cold.

Devi looked at it and raised an eyebrow. "Expecting trouble?"

"I don't know what I'm expecting."

"That," she said, "is the most sensible thing you've said in two spans." She looked at Fela. "Ready?"

Fela nodded. She did not speak. She had not spoken much all day.

We went down.

---

I led the way through the Underthing. I knew the path well by now. Down through the Twelve, along the passage Auri called Downway, past the steep narrow slope that led to the Cellarage. I did not look down that slope as we passed it. I did not want to know if the silver light was burning at the bottom.

At last we came to the fall of rubble that hid the entrance to the air shaft. I pulled away the stones I had loosened on my earlier visits, and we squeezed through one by one. Devi had to take off her satchel and push it ahead of her. Fela went through without a word, her face white in the red light of my lamp.

Then we began to climb.

It was a long climb, and a hard one. The rungs were rusted and treacherous. The walls were slick with damp. I went first, testing each rung before I put my weight on it, calling down warnings to the others when I found one that was loose. Devi came next, grunting with effort, cursing under her breath in a steady low stream that would have made a sailor blush. Fela came last. She did not curse. She did not speak at all. But she kept up.

At the top, I pried open the grate. It came away easily in my hands, the old iron crumbling with rust. I passed it down to Devi, who passed it down to Fela, who set it carefully on a ledge inside the shaft. Then I climbed through.

I stood on the great stair beneath the Four-plate door.

---

It was even larger than I had thought. Looking at it through the grate, I had seen only a small part of it. Now, standing on it, I could see the whole. The stairwell was a vast round shaft cut down into the living rock, easily fifty feet across. The stairs wound around its edge in a great spiral, down and down and down, into a darkness so deep it swallowed the light of my lamp.

Above us, the Four-plate door was a great round slab of grey stone set into the ceiling. It was even larger from this side. The four copper plates were dull and green with age. Around its edge, carved into the stone, was a ring of writing in the same strange script I had seen in the blue book.

*Valaritas*, it said. I did not know how I knew. I could not read the script. But I knew.

Devi climbed through the grate behind me and stood looking up at the door. Her face was very strange. It was the face of a woman who has wanted something for a very long time, and has finally seen it, and does not know whether to laugh or weep.

"There it is," she said softly.

Fela climbed through last. She did not look at the door. She stood very still at the edge of the stair with her hands pressed flat against the wall, and her eyes were closed.

"It's loud here," she whispered. "So loud."

"The stone?"

"Everything." She opened her eyes. "The stone. And what's under it." She shivered. "It knows we're here."

---

We went down.

The stairs were wide and shallow and very old. Our footsteps echoed in the vast round shaft, strange and hollow. Devi walked beside me, her satchel clutched against her chest. Fela walked behind us, one hand trailing along the wall.

One flight. Two. The darkness closed in around us, thick and heavy. The air grew colder. My lamp seemed to shrink, its red light pressed in on all sides by the dark.

Three flights.

At the bottom of the third flight, the stairs ended in a landing. A wide flat space of smooth grey stone, and beyond it, an archway. And beyond the archway, a small square room.

It was exactly as Devi had described it. Stone walls, stone floor, stone ceiling. Empty. Perfectly, utterly empty. There was nothing in it at all.

And yet, standing at the edge of the archway, I could feel it. A kind of tension in the air. A watchfulness. The way a room feels when someone is standing in the dark corner, holding their breath.

"That's it," Devi said quietly. "That's the second seal."

Fela stepped forward. She stood at the threshold of the archway and looked into the small square room for a long time.

Then she knelt down and laid her palms flat on the floor.

I held my breath.

For a long time, nothing happened. Fela knelt with her head bowed and her hands on the stone, and she did not move. Her lips were moving, silently. I could not hear what she was saying.

Then I felt it. A hum. Deep and low, rising up out of the floor through the soles of my feet. The same hum I had felt in the Twelve, when Fela had spoken to the stone and it had answered.

It grew louder. The dust on the walls began to tremble. The air grew thick.

And then, all at once, it stopped.

Fela sat back on her heels. Her face was white and wet with tears. She was breathing hard, as if she had been running.

"It's all right," she whispered. "It said it won't tell. For tonight."

"What did you say to it?" I asked.

She looked up at me. Her eyes were very dark.

"I said please," she said.

---

We walked through the small square room. It did not ring. It did not do anything at all. It was only a room, stone walls and stone floor and stone ceiling, and on the far side there was another archway, and beyond that, more stairs.

We went down.

The stairs were narrower now. Steeper. They did not spiral anymore. They went straight down, through a tunnel cut into the rock, so narrow we had to walk single file. The ceiling pressed down low over our heads. The walls were rough and unfinished, marked with the scars of tools.

And there was writing on them.

I noticed it first as a kind of texture, a roughness in the stone at the edge of my lamp's light. Then I held the lamp closer and saw that it was letters. Carved into the walls, row after row of them, in the same strange script as the door above. They covered every surface, floor to ceiling, as far as the light would reach.

"What does it say?" Devi breathed.

"I don't know." I ran my fingers over the letters. They were very old. Worn smooth in places, as if by many hands. "I can't read it."

"I can," said Fela.

We both turned to look at her. She was standing a few steps above us, looking at the wall, her face strange in the red light.

"Not read it," she said. "Not exactly. But I can hear it. The stone remembers what it was carved to say." She laid her hand flat on the letters. "It's names," she said softly. "It's all names. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Every one of them carved by a different hand."

"Whose names?"

She closed her eyes. She listened.

"The ones who kept the door," she said. "The ones who came down here, over the years, to make sure it stayed shut. They carved their names so the stone would remember them." She opened her eyes and looked at me. "They were Amyr, Kvothe. All of them. Every name on these walls. They were Amyr."

---

We went down. The stairs went on and on, steeper and narrower, the walls covered with names. Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. Men and women who had come down these stairs over the centuries, in the dark, to keep watch over a door.

I thought about Lorren. About what he had told me, on the road by the river. *There were rumors, when I was a boy, that some of them had come here. That they had taken refuge in the Archives, in the old stone rooms beneath the Stacks.*

He had been right. They had been here. Not hiding. Guarding.

And then, at last, the stairs ended.

We came out into a room. A great round room, like the stairwell above but smaller, its walls rising up into darkness. The floor was smooth grey stone, polished like glass, and in the center of the floor, there was a circle of writing, and in the center of the circle there was a table of stone, and on the table there was a book.

And on the far side of the room, set into the wall, there was a door.

It was grey stone. It had no handle. It had no hinges. It had no lock.

It was exactly the door I had dreamed of.

---

None of us spoke. We stood at the bottom of the stairs, the three of us, and looked at the door, and the silence was so deep it rang in my ears.

Then, very faintly, I heard it.

Breathing.

Slow and deep and patient. Coming from the other side of the door. The breath of something very large, sleeping very deeply. The breath of something that had been sleeping for a very long time.

Devi's hand found mine in the dark. Her fingers were cold and shaking. Fela stood on my other side, very still, her eyes fixed on the door, her lips pressed together so tightly they had gone white.

"Don't go near it," Fela whispered. "Kvothe. Whatever you do. Don't go near it."

"I won't."

"Promise me."

"I promise."

I did not go near it. I stood at the bottom of the stairs with Devi's hand in mine and Fela at my side, and I looked at the door.

And from the other side of the door, very faintly, something whispered my name.
