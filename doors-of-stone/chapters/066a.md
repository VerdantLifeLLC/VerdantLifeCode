# The Fall of Severen

*The Maer told me this himself, by the fire at the mouth of his cave, on the second night after I found him in the hills. He told it slowly, the way a man tells something he has been carrying for a long time and has not yet found a place to set down. I have tried to set it down here as he told it, in his own words, as near as I can remember them.*

---

They came in the spring, *said the Maer*, with the rains.

Ten thousand of them. I watched them come from the top of the cliff, from the window of my study, where you and I used to sit and talk. A great black river of men and horses and wagons, winding up the valley from the north, with the black and silver banner at its head. It took them three days to arrive. I watched the whole time. I did not sleep.

I had four thousand. Less. Three and a half. My own household guard, and the levies of the lords who owed me service and had not yet decided to change sides. The city watch. Some volunteers from the low city, men who had heard that the Jakis boy meant to hang every man in Severen who had ever worn my colors, and had decided they would rather die on the walls.

Three and a half thousand, and the walls of Severen. And the cliff.

I thought it would be enough. God help me, I thought it would be enough. Severen has never fallen. Not in four hundred years. The high city sits on the cliff like a crown on a king's head, and there is only one road up, and a hundred men can hold that road against ten thousand for as long as they have food and arrows.

I did not reckon on the old man.

---

The first month was nothing. They camped in the valley below the cliff, around the low city, and they shot arrows at the walls, and we shot arrows back. They tried the cliff road twice. Both times, we threw them down it. They lost six hundred men on that road in a single afternoon, and we lost nine.

My captains were jubilant. They said the siege would break in a season. They said the Jakis boy would go home with his tail between his legs, and the lords who had followed him would come crawling back to me, begging forgiveness.

I did not believe them. I had seen the old man.

He was in their camp. I could see him from my window, through my glass. Every morning, he came out of a tent near the king's pavilion and sat on a stool in the sun, with a board on a little table in front of him. And he played tak. By himself. For hours. Every morning. Moving the stones, one by one, very slowly.

I knew him. Of course I knew him. He had played tak with me at my own table for eleven years. He had given me a book of poems on my fifteenth birthday. He had taught my cousin, and my cousin's son.

And now he sat in the enemy's camp, below my walls, playing tak by himself in the sun. And every so often, he would look up at the palace on the cliff, at my window, and smile. As if he could see me watching.

I think he could.

---

In the second month, the low city burned.

Not by accident. Not by fire arrows, though they had plenty of those. It began in the night, in a dozen places at once. Warehouses by the river. The grain mills. The cooper's yard. The old church of Tehlu, with its wooden steeple. All at once, in the dead of night, in a dozen places no enemy should have been able to reach.

We found out later how it was done. There was a man in the low city. A baker. His name was Tolly. He had been baking bread for my household for sixteen years. He had a wife and four children and a shop on the square below the cliff road, and every morning at dawn he sent up three dozen loaves to the palace kitchens.

He had sympathy. A little. Not much. Enough. He had learned it as a boy, from a hedge-wizard in his village, and he had used it all his life to keep his ovens hot without wasting wood. And one day, the spring before, an old man had come into his shop to buy a loaf of bread. A kind old man with a walking stick, who bought his bread every morning for a month and talked to him about the weather and his children and the price of flour. And at the end of the month, the old man had given him a small knotted cord. For luck, he said. For his children.

Tolly wore it around his neck. And on the night the low city burned, he went out into the dark and set the fires, one by one, with the sympathy he had learned as a boy. He did not know why. He told us afterward, when we caught him, weeping. He did not know why he had done it. He only knew that the cord around his neck had been warm, all that night, and that he had felt it pulling, and that he could not stop.

I hanged him. From the walls. In front of everyone.

I have not forgiven myself for it. He did not know. I knew he did not know. But the city was burning, and people were dying, and they needed someone to hang.

I have thought about Tolly every day since. He is the first thing I will do penance for, when I find a way.

---

The low city burned for three days. Half the people who lived there died. The rest came up the cliff road to the high city, carrying what they could, and we let them in, because what else could we do. And the high city filled up with frightened people and sick people and hungry people, and the food began to run short.

And the old man sat on his stool in the sun, every morning, and played tak by himself.

---

In the third month, the walls began to fall.

Not all at once. Not in a great assault, with ladders and battering rams, the way the old stories tell it. One stone at a time.

It started on the south wall. A single stone, near the base, low down, where no one would notice. It cracked. For no reason. We found it one morning, split clean through, as if someone had struck it with a hammer.

The next morning, another stone. And the next. And the next. Always on the south wall. Always low down. Always in the night, when no one was watching.

My arcanist—I had a new one, after Caudicus, a young man from the University named Pell, very clever, very frightened—my arcanist said it was sympathy. Very strong. Very patient. Someone was binding the stones to something else, something far away, and breaking the something else, one piece at a time, so that the stones broke too.

"Who could do that?" I asked him. "Who could reach so far, and hold so long?"

He did not know. But I did. I looked out my window, the next morning, and saw the old man on his stool in the sun, playing tak by himself. And I saw that there was a pile of stones beside him, on the ground. Small grey stones, like river pebbles.

As I watched, he picked one up. He held it in his hand for a moment. Then he laid it on the tak board, very carefully, in the pattern.

And he closed his hand around it, and squeezed.

That night, another stone cracked in my south wall.

---

It took him forty-one days. Forty-one stones. One every night. Slowly, patiently, the way stone does everything. The way the sea wears away a cliff.

On the forty-second morning, the south wall of Severen came down.

It did not fall. It sagged. It slumped, the way a drunk man slumps against a wall, and then slid, slowly, stone by stone, into the ditch at its foot. By noon there was a gap in the wall of Severen fifty feet wide. And through the gap, ten thousand men.

We fought them in the streets. For three days. Street by street, house by house. My captains died. My soldiers died. Corrin died on the south wall, in the first hour, holding the breach with twenty men while I tried to get the women and children out through the north gate.

I did not get them all out. I got some.

---

On the third day, I went for Meluan.

She was in the palace, in her rooms, in the green room where you told her about her sister. She was very near her time. She could not run. She could barely walk. I had left her there with four guards and Stapes and a physician, because I thought the palace would hold longest, and she would be safest there.

I fought my way up the cliff road. I do not remember much of it. I remember the smoke, and the noise, and a great many men in black and silver. I remember the gate of the palace, standing open. I remember the courtyard, full of bodies. I remember the stairs.

I remember reaching the door of her rooms, and finding it open, and the four guards dead in the corridor outside, and Stapes lying on the floor inside with blood on his head, unconscious but alive. And the physician dead in a chair. And the bed empty.

And on the pillow, where her head had been, a tak stone. White. Set very carefully in the center of the pillow, the way you would set a stone on a board.

I stood in that room for a long time. I do not know how long. Stapes woke, eventually, and found me there, and took me by the arm, and led me down the back stair and out the postern gate and into the hills. I do not remember that either. I only remember the white stone on the pillow.

I have it still. Here.

---

*He reached into his shirt and drew it out. It hung on a leather thong around his neck. A small smooth white stone, the kind you play tak with. He held it in his palm and looked at it in the firelight for a long time.*

*"He wanted me to know," he said at last. "That it was him. That it was a move. That I had lost." He closed his hand around the stone. "He always did like to be sure his opponent understood the position."*

*I did not say anything. I did not know what to say.*

*The Maer looked at me across the fire. His hollow face was very still.*

*"When you go to the door," he said, "will he be there?"*

*"Yes."*

*"Will you kill him?"*

*I thought about it. About the garden in Severen, and my sword at his throat, and a thin line of blood running down into his collar.*

*"I don't know," I said honestly. "I tried once. I couldn't."*

*The Maer looked at me for a long moment. Then he took the leather thong from around his neck, with the white stone on it, and held it out to me across the fire.*

*"Take this," he said. "And if he is there, give it back to him. From me." His voice was very quiet. "Tell him it's my move."*

---

I took it. I wore it, under my shirt, beside Denna's knot, all the way to the Lackless house.

I never gave it back. Bredon went through the door before I had the chance.

But I did not throw it away, either. I gave it to the Penitent King, in the end, the morning I left Renere in the back of a cart, under a canvas cover. Stapes carried it to him.

Stapes wrote me, afterward, that the king wore it every day of his life. On the leather thong, around his neck, under the plain grey robes. And that sometimes, late at night, in the palace, he would take it out and hold it in his palm and look at it for a long time.

Stapes said he never knew why. He said the king never told him.

I think I know. I think it was a reminder. That you can lose everything, one stone at a time, while you are busy watching the center of the board.

And that the game is never over. Not really. Not while you still have a move.
