# Ambrose

I saw Ambrose one last time before he left the University for good. I did not know it would be the last time, not then. If I had, I might have done things differently. Or I might not. I have thought about it a great deal, over the years, and I have never been able to decide.

It was a cold morning near the end of the term. The first snow had fallen overnight, a thin white dusting that turned the courtyard into a clean sheet of paper and made the whole University look new and strange. I was crossing toward the Archives with my satchel over my shoulder and my breath steaming in the air, and I was thinking about my father's song, as I did most mornings now. I was not paying attention.

That was my mistake. I have always been at my worst when I am not paying attention.

"Well, well," said a voice. "If it isn't the little Ruh."

I stopped. I turned.

Ambrose was standing on the steps of the Mains, looking down at me. He was dressed for travel, in a long coat of dark grey wool trimmed with fur, and a pair of riding boots polished to a mirror shine. There was a sword at his hip, a real one, with a jeweled hilt and a scabbard of tooled leather. His hair was carefully arranged. His rings glittered in the cold light.

He was not alone. Two of his usual hangers-on stood a few steps below him, looking bored and cold. And behind him, near the door of the Mains, stood a third man, tall and white-haired, leaning on a walking stick of dark polished wood with a silver head.

I knew him. It took me a moment, because he was so utterly out of place, the way a friend's face is hard to recognize when you meet it in a city where you did not expect it. But I knew him.

It was Bredon.

I had spent long evenings with Bredon in Severen, at the Maer's court, sitting across a tak board from him while the candles burned down. He had taught me the game. He had taught me, or tried to teach me, a great deal more than the game: how to move through a court full of knives without being cut, how to smile at a man you despised, how to lose a small thing gracefully in order to win a large one. He had been kind to me when very few people at that court had been kind. I had liked him. I had liked him a great deal.

And here he was on the steps of the Mains, in the snow, standing behind Ambrose Jakis like a shadow.

He saw me recognize him. His eyebrows rose, and he smiled, and lifted his stick an inch off the ground in a small, courtly greeting, the way he used to at the start of a game.

"Ambrose," I said. I did not look away from Bredon. "I heard you were leaving."

"You heard right." Ambrose came down the steps toward me, slowly, his boots crunching in the snow. "My father has sent for me. There are matters at court that require my attention."

"I'm sure there are."

He stopped a few feet away from me. He was smiling. Ambrose was almost always smiling, but this was a different smile than usual. It was not the sneer he usually wore when he looked at me. It was something smoother, more confident. It was the smile of a man who knows something you do not.

"You've heard the news, I suppose," he said. "About my cousins."

"I heard two of them died."

"Three," Ambrose said. "As of last span. My uncle Lorin caught a chill on a hunting trip and never recovered. Very sad. He was a great favorite of the king's." He did not look sad. "That puts me seventh in line, now. Seventh." He savored the word. "Do you know how many people in the world can say that, Ruh? Seventh in line for the throne of Vintas?"

"Seven, I'd imagine."

His smile flickered. Then it came back, broader than before. "Very clever. You always were clever. Too clever by half, my father says. He says that's what gets your kind into trouble." He took a step closer. "He says that one day someone's going to teach you a lesson about knowing your place."

"And is that you?"

"Me?" He laughed. "No. Not me. I'm leaving, remember? I have more important things to do than waste my time on a gutter rat with a lute." He leaned in, close enough that I could smell the wine on his breath, and the perfume he wore, something heavy and sweet. "But I'll tell you something, Ruh. As a parting gift. You think you're special. You think because you've got a little talent and a little money and a patron who likes your songs, you're something more than you are. But you're not. You're nothing. You're a thief and a liar and the son of thieves and liars, and one day, very soon, the world is going to remember that."

I looked at him. I did not say anything.

There was a time when those words would have made me angry. When I would have lashed out at him, with my fists or my wits or something worse. I had done it before. I had broken his arm once, in a manner of speaking, and set his rooms on fire, and done a dozen other things that I was not proud of and would do again in a heartbeat.

But I had spent months among the Adem. I had learned, from Vashet and Tempi and Shehyn, what it meant to be still. To let the anger rise and pass, like wind over water. To choose the moment of action rather than let it be chosen for me.

And so I looked at Ambrose, and I let the anger rise, and I let it pass.

"Safe travels, Ambrose," I said.

He stared at me. For a moment, just a moment, I saw something flicker in his eyes. Confusion, maybe. Or disappointment. He had wanted a fight. He had wanted me to lash out, so he could leave the University with one last victory over me, one last story to tell his friends at court.

I had not given it to him.

"You'll regret this," he said. His voice was low and ugly. "When I'm king. You'll regret all of it."

"You're seventh in line, Ambrose. You're not going to be king."

He smiled. And that smile, that one, was the worst of all. Because it was not a boast. It was not a threat. It was a secret.

"We'll see," he said.

He turned and walked away across the courtyard toward the gates, where a carriage was waiting. His hangers-on scurried after him. Bredon followed more slowly, his stick tapping on the frozen stones.

As he passed me, he paused.

"Kvothe," he said warmly. "What a pleasant surprise. I had heard you'd come back to your books."

"Bredon." I did not know what else to say. "You're—traveling with him?"

"With young Jakis? For a little way." He glanced toward the carriage, and his expression was the gently rueful one of an uncle who has been saddled with an unruly nephew. "His father and I are old acquaintances. The Baron asked me to see the boy safely home, and I had business in Renere in any case. An old man takes his company where he finds it." He looked back at me, and his eyes were bright and interested. "You look well. Older. Something has happened to you."

"A great deal has happened to me."

"Good. That is what youth is for." He smiled. "You must come and play with me again, when you are next in Severen. I have missed our games. No one at court has the nerve to lose properly."

"I'd like that."

"I know you would." He looked at me for a moment longer, that mild, patient, measuring look, and something in it made me want to step back. "You have an interesting face, you know. I noticed it the first time we met. It reminds me of someone I knew long ago."

"Who?"

He only smiled. "Nobody you would know," he said. "Good day to you, young man."

He walked on toward the carriage. I watched him go. He did not look back.

---

I stood in the courtyard long after the carriage had rattled away through the gates and down the road toward the bridge. The snow had begun to fall again, soft and silent, settling on my hair and shoulders. Students hurried past me on their way to class, their heads bowed against the cold. None of them looked at me.

I was thinking about Bredon.

I told myself it meant nothing. Bredon knew everyone in Vintas worth knowing, and half of those who were not. It was perfectly natural that he should know Baron Jakis. It was perfectly natural that an old man with business in the capital should share a carriage with a young one. Bredon had always seemed to drift through the world like that, easily, at the edges of things, turning up at the elbows of important people with a joke and a tak board.

And yet I stood in the snow and felt the wrongness of it like a stone in my boot. Because for the first time since I had known him, I had looked at Bredon and seen, instead of my kindly old teacher, a man standing behind Ambrose Jakis. And he had looked entirely at home there.

It would be many months before I saw him again. And when I did, I would remember this moment, standing in the falling snow, watching him walk away. I would remember how he had looked at me, and what he had said.

*It reminds me of someone I knew long ago.*

I would remember, and I would understand. And I would wish, more than I had ever wished for almost anything, that I had followed him to his carriage that morning and asked him, plainly, what he was doing there.

---

That evening I told Sim and Wil about the encounter. We were sitting at Anker's, sharing a bottle of cheap wine and a plate of bread and cheese, and I told them what Ambrose had said, and about Bredon.

"Seventh in line," Sim said, shaking his head. "That's absurd. Three deaths in a year and a half. Nobody's that unlucky."

"Nobody's that *lucky*," Wil said. "From Ambrose's perspective."

"You think it's murder," Sim said.

Wil shrugged. "I think it's Vintas."

"It's not Ambrose," I said. They both looked at me. "Doing it himself, I mean. He doesn't have the patience. Or the skill. Ambrose is a bully. He likes to hurt people he can see. Poisoning a man on the other side of the country and waiting a month for him to die isn't his style."

"His father, then," Sim said.

"Maybe." I thought about Bredon, and his stick, and the patient, measuring way he had looked at me. "Or someone else."

"Who?"

I did not answer. I did not know. But I remembered the smile Ambrose had given me, the secret one, and I felt a chill that had nothing to do with the snow outside.

*We'll see,* he had said.

"There's something else," Wil said slowly. He had been quiet for a while, turning his cup around and around on the table. "I heard something today, at the Archives. From one of the scrivs." He hesitated. "It's probably nothing."

"Tell us anyway."

"The king," Wil said. "Roderic. He's ill."

Sim and I both stared at him.

"How ill?" I asked.

"The scriv didn't know. Just that he took to his bed a span ago, and hasn't been seen at court since. His physicians say it's a winter fever." Wil shrugged. "Could be nothing. Kings get sick like anyone else."

"Could be," I said.

We sat in silence for a long time after that. The fire crackled in the hearth. Outside, the snow kept falling, soft and white and silent, covering everything.

I thought about the Maer, and his long illness, and the physician who had been slowly poisoning him for gold. I thought about the Vintish court, with its smiling knives and its poisoned cups. I thought about Ambrose, seventh in line, riding away through the snow in his fur-trimmed coat with a secret smile on his face.

And I thought about Bredon, my old tak teacher, with his kind grandfather's eyes, and the way he had looked at me as if I were a piece on a board.

I did not know it then, but the game had already begun. The pieces were already moving.

And I was already losing.
