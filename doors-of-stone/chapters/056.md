# Felurian

She found me on the third day of the Moon's Road.

I had been following the silver string through the Deep Wood, a forest so thick and dark that even the twilight could barely reach the ground. The trees there were not silver, like the ones near the greystone. They were black. Great black trunks rising up into a darkness so deep I could not see where they ended, with bark like old iron and roots like the coils of great snakes. The air was still and heavy and smelled of earth and rot and something else, something sharp and cold, like the smell of a knife.

The string led me along a narrow path between the black trees, winding and twisting. It never stopped. It never slackened. It floated in the air ahead of me, silver and faintly glowing, pointing always north.

I had not seen anyone since the Hollow Market. I had not heard anything, either, except my own footsteps and my own breath and, sometimes, far off in the dark between the trees, something that might have been singing.

And then, on the third day, I came around a bend in the path and she was there.

---

She was sitting on a root of one of the great black trees, with her legs crossed and her chin in her hand, as if she had been waiting for me. She was naked, as she always was, and the twilight fell on her skin and made it glow, the way the moon makes water glow. Her hair was long and dark and fell around her like a cloak. Her eyes were the color of evening.

I stopped. I stood very still in the middle of the path, with the silver string floating ahead of me and my heart hammering in my chest.

I had forgotten. I had told myself that I remembered, that I knew what she was like. But I had forgotten. The mind cannot hold it. It is like trying to remember the exact color of a sunset, or the exact sound of a song you heard once in a dream. The shape stays. The substance fades.

She looked at me. And the substance came back all at once, like a wave.

"Kvothe," said Felurian.

Her voice was soft and sweet and full of music. It went into me like wine. My knees went weak. I put my hand against the nearest black tree to steady myself.

"Felurian," I said.

"You came back." She tilted her head. "I did not think you would. Mortals never come back."

"I'm not staying."

"No." She smiled. It was a small smile, and a little sad. "No, I did not think you would. You have that look. The going-somewhere look." She uncrossed her legs and stood up, in one smooth motion, like water rising. "Where are you going, my Kvothe?"

"North. To the Stormwal."

"On the Moon's Road." She looked at the silver string floating in the air. "Past the Grove."

"Yes."

Her smile faded. She came toward me along the path, slowly, her bare feet making no sound on the black earth. She stopped in front of me, close enough to touch. She did not touch me.

"You have been to the Grove before," she said. "You spoke to it."

"Yes."

"I told you not to go near it. I told you what it was."

"I didn't mean to. I didn't know."

"I know." She reached up and touched my face, very lightly, with the tips of her fingers. Her fingers were warm. "I know you did not. It was not your fault. It called to you, and you were young and curious, and you went." She looked into my eyes. "And now it has its hooks in you. I can see them. Little silver hooks, all through you. Pulling."

I did not say anything.

"It wants you to come back," said Felurian softly. "It has been waiting. Ever since you left. It has been pulling on its hooks, a little at a time, bringing you back to it." Her fingers moved to my hair. "And now you are here. On the Moon's Road. About to walk right past its door."

"I have to."

"Why?"

I told her. Not all of it. But enough. The door, and the line, and Selitos in the mountains. The war, and Meluan, and the box.

She listened with her head tilted and her fingers in my hair. When I was done, she was quiet for a long time.

"The Thief," she said at last. "The one who stole the moon."

"Yes."

"I remember him." Her voice was very far away. "I was young, then. Younger than anything in your world is now. I remember the war. I remember what he did to the sky." She looked up, at the endless twilight above the black trees. "This place. The twilight. Did you know he made it? To hold her. The moon. He made a place beyond the world, and he pulled her into it, and he tried to make her stay." She was quiet for a moment. "And we have been living in it ever since. All of us. In the house he built for a woman who did not love him."

I stared at her.

"The Fae," I said slowly. "The whole Fae. He made it."

"He made it. And then they put him behind the doors, and he could not come back to it." She smiled faintly. "It is very sad, when you think about it. He built a whole world for her, and he never got to live in it."

---

She walked with me for a while, along the Moon's Road, through the black trees.

She did not ask me to stay. That surprised me. I had been afraid, since I first saw her, that she would try to keep me. That she would use her magic on me, the terrible sweet magic that had held me in her glade for so long the first time, and that I would not be able to resist. I had been holding Fela's stone in my pocket the whole time, clutching it so hard my fingers ached, repeating its long slow *no* over and over in my head.

But she did not try. She walked beside me with her hand on my arm, and she talked about the old days, and the war, and the Thief. She told me things about the Fae that I had never heard, and that I have never told anyone. And every so often, she looked at me sidelong with her evening eyes, and smiled, and did not say anything.

At last we came to a place where the black trees opened out into a small clearing. There was a spring in the center of it, bubbling up clear and cold from between two stones. Felurian stopped beside it.

"This is as far as I go," she said. "Beyond here, the road belongs to the Sithe. They do not like me very much." She smiled. "They do not like anyone very much."

"Thank you," I said. "For walking with me."

"I did not walk with you for thanks." She looked at me. "I walked with you to give you something."

"What?"

She reached out and took my left hand. My cold hand, the one Cinder had held. She lifted it, very gently, and looked at the white print of his fingers on my wrist.

"He hurt you," she said.

"Yes."

"The cold one. The one with black eyes. I know him." Her voice was very quiet. "He was beautiful once. Before. He used to sing." She looked up at me. "Did you know that? He had the most beautiful voice. He sang at the Thief's court, before the war. People came from all over the world to hear him."

I did not say anything. I did not know what to say.

"Now he is cold," said Felurian. "All the way through. And what he touches, he makes cold." She held my hand in both of hers. "I cannot take it away. It is too deep. It is part of you now." She paused. "But I can give you something to hold against it."

She bent her head. And she kissed my wrist, very softly, right in the center of the white print of Cinder's hand.

I felt it. For the first time since the Eolian, I felt something in my left hand. Not much. Just a faint warmth. Like the memory of summer. Like the last ember in a fire that has almost gone out.

It spread. Slowly. Up my wrist and into my palm and along my fingers. Not enough to take away the cold. But enough to push it back, a little. Enough to make it bearable.

I flexed my fingers. They moved. Stiffly, slowly. But they moved, and I could feel them.

"There," said Felurian. She let go of my hand. "That will help. When you play. When you hold your sword." She smiled. "When you hold someone you love."

"Felurian—"

"Hush." She laid her finger on my lips. "Do not thank me. I did not do it for thanks either." She looked at me for a long time, with her evening eyes. "I did it because you sang to me, once. In my glade. You sang me a song about myself, and it was the truest thing anyone has ever said to me in ten thousand years." She took her finger away. "I am only paying a debt."

---

She told me about the Sithe before she left.

"They will know you," she said. "The moment you step onto their road. They will know you have spoken to the Tree. They can smell it on you, the way hounds smell blood." She looked at me gravely. "They will try to kill you."

"I know."

"You cannot fight them. Not even with your Adem sword. They are the finest warriors in the twilight. They have been guarding the Tree since before your world had a name."

"Then how do I get past?"

Felurian looked at me for a long moment.

"You do not get past them," she said. "You go *through* them. You let them see you. You let them come. And when they come—" She hesitated. "When they come, you must tell them the truth. All of it. Why you are there, and where you are going, and what you mean to do."

"And they'll let me go?"

"No. Probably not." She smiled faintly. "But the Sithe are not cruel. They are only careful. If you tell them the truth, and it is a good truth, they may listen. They may decide that you are more dangerous dead than alive." She paused. "Or they may shoot you anyway. It is hard to say, with the Sithe."

"That's not very reassuring."

"No." She laughed softly. "No, it is not. But it is the truth. And you like the truth, my Kvothe. Do you not?"

I looked at her for a long time.

"Yes," I said. "I do."

She reached up and touched my face one last time.

"Then go," she said. "Go and tell it. And if you live, come back someday and sing me another song." Her smile was very sad. "I would like that very much."

She stepped back. And then, between one breath and the next, she was gone. The clearing was empty. The spring bubbled softly between its stones.

I stood there alone for a long time.

Then I looked down at my left hand. I flexed the fingers. They moved. I could feel them. Warm, faintly, at the center of the cold.

I pulled the shaed close around me, and I followed the silver string out of the clearing and into the Sithe's country.
