# The Nightingale

I did not see Denna during those months in the Tower of Thorns. I did not know, then, where she was, or what they had done with her, or whether she was alive at all. I did not know enough to wonder. I only knew that sometimes, in the grey, I would reach for something beside me that was not there, and my hand would close on empty air, and the cord on my wrist would hum.

I learned the rest later. Much later. From Lady Tamsin, and from Bast, and from Denna herself, on a night I will tell you about in its place. I will tell it to you now, the way it was told to me, because it belongs here, in the middle of the grey. It is one of the few bright things in it.

---

They did not put her in a cell. The duke was too clever for that. The king's nightingale, the most famous singer in Vintas, the girl whose voice had made the lords of three kingdoms weep—you do not throw such a person into a dungeon. People would talk. People would ask questions.

So they gave her rooms. A pleasant suite on the second floor of the palace, overlooking the gardens, with a canopied bed and a marble hearth and a harp in the corner. They gave her a maid, and fine dresses, and good food. They treated her as an honored guest.

And they locked the door.

The duke came to see her on the second day. He sat in a chair by her hearth, heavy and grey in his mourning black, and he looked at her for a long time.

"You were close to my son," he said.

"I sang for him."

"You were more than a singer to him. He told me. He said you were the only one who understood him." The duke's heavy face was very still. "He loved you, I think. In his way."

Denna did not answer.

"I need you to do something for me," said the duke. "For him. For his memory."

"What?"

"There will be a trial. For the Ruh. The one who killed him." The duke's voice was quiet. "The lords will want to hear what happened in that cellar. They'll want witnesses. And there are very few of them left who aren't dead or fled." He looked at her. "You were there. You saw it all. You'll testify."

"I'll tell them the truth," said Denna.

"You'll tell them," said the duke, "that the Ruh murdered my son. In cold blood. Without cause. That my son was standing at that door trying to protect the kingdom from some monstrous thing the Ruh meant to let loose. And that the Ruh struck him down from behind, like the coward he is."

Denna looked at him for a long time.

"No," she said.

The duke's face did not change.

"No?"

"That isn't what happened." Her voice was steady. "Your son had a knife at a woman's throat. He was trying to open the door, not guard it. He was going to kill her. Kvothe stopped him." She paused. "I'm sorry for your loss, your grace. I truly am. But I won't lie for you."

The duke stood up. He looked down at her.

"You will," he said quietly. "Or you'll hang beside him."

He left. The door locked behind him.

---

He came back the next day. And the next. And the next. Every day for a span. Sometimes he was gentle, and talked about his son, and wept. Sometimes he was cold, and talked about the gallows, and the crows. Sometimes he brought gifts: jewels, silks, a golden harp. Sometimes he brought guards, who stood in the doorway with their hands on their swords.

Denna gave him the same answer every day.

*No.*

"I've spent my whole life lying," she told me, long afterward. "About who I was. Where I came from. What I wanted. I changed my name every time I changed towns. I said whatever people wanted to hear. I was so good at it." She was quiet for a moment. "And then I stood in that cellar and watched you pull your own name out of your chest and hand it to me. And I thought, I'm never going to lie again. Not about the important things. Not ever."

"Even to save your life?"

She looked at me.

"Especially then," she said.

---

At the end of the span, the duke tried something else.

He had a feast. A great feast, in the great hall of the palace, for all the lords who had stayed loyal to him. Hundreds of them, with their wives and their sons and their retainers. A feast of mourning, he called it, for his son, the murdered king. There would be food and wine and speeches, and at the end of it, there would be music.

And the king's nightingale would sing.

He sent her a dress for it. Black silk, very fine, embroidered with silver thread. He sent her a message with it, written in his own heavy hand.

*You will sing the song. Your song. The one about Lanre, the wronged hero. My son loved it. You will sing it in his memory. And when you are done, you will stand before the lords and tell them how he died.*

*You will tell them my version.*

*Or I will have the Ruh hanged in the morning, without a trial, and you will watch.*

---

She came to the feast in the black silk dress. She walked into the great hall with her harp in her arms and her head held high, and every lord and lady in the room turned to look at her. She was very beautiful. She was always very beautiful. But Lady Tamsin, who was there, told me that she had never seen her look like she looked that night.

"Like a sword," said Lady Tamsin. "Like someone had taken all the soft parts out of her, and left only the edge."

The duke sat at the high table, in a great carved chair draped in black. He watched her walk the length of the hall. He watched her sit on the stool that had been placed for her before the high table. He watched her set her harp in her lap and lay her fingers on the strings.

"Sing," he said. "For my son."

Denna looked up at him.

"I will," she said. "But not the song you want."

---

She sang *Tariniel*.

Not my version. Not exactly. She had never heard all of mine; she had been in the balcony of the Eolian that night, listening, but she had heard it only once, and much of it she had not understood. She had heard pieces. Fragments. The white city, and the fountains. Lyra's name, in a voice that did not lie. Selitos weeping among the ruins.

And the child. The boy under the slab of white marble, asking to be a singer.

She took those pieces. And she took the Yllish cords she had studied for years, the old ones, the ones Bredon had given her to read so that she could write his false song. She had read them all. She knew the old story better than almost anyone alive, because she had spent years learning it so that she could twist it.

And she untwisted it.

She stood in the great hall of the palace of Renere, before the duke and all his lords, and she sang the true song. Her version. Pieced together from my fragments and her cords and her own understanding. It was not the same as mine. It was different, in a hundred small ways. But it was true.

"It was the most beautiful thing I have ever heard," Lady Tamsin told me. "And I heard yours, you know. At the Eolian. I was in the second balcony." She shook her head. "Hers was different. Yours was a bell ringing. Hers was a knife. It cut. It went right through you and left you bleeding."

The hall was silent while she sang. Utterly silent. Not a cough, not a whisper, not a clink of a cup. Hundreds of lords and ladies sat frozen in their seats, and listened.

The duke did not stop her. Lady Tamsin said he tried. He half rose from his carved chair, with his face going red and then white, and opened his mouth to shout. But he did not. He stood there, half risen, with his mouth open. And then, slowly, he sat back down.

And he listened too.

---

She sang all of it. The white city, and Lanre, and Lyra. The Thief's whisper in the dark. The fall of Myr Tariniel. The curse, and Selitos weeping, and the child in the fountain.

And then she sang something that was not in my version. Something that was hers alone.

She sang the door.

She sang a door of grey stone, beneath an old house on a ridge. She sang a young king, standing before it, with a box around his neck and a knife in his hand. A young king who had been told all his life that he was special, and had believed it, and had been used because of it. Who had been taught to love an old man with a walking stick, and to trust him, and to do what he said. Who had stood before that door with a woman's blood on his hands, and been so afraid, and so proud, and so lost, that he did not know what he was doing anymore.

She sang him with love. That was what Lady Tamsin said, afterward. She sang Ambrose Jakis with love. Not the love of a woman for a man. The love of a singer for a story. For a sad story, about a boy who was broken by someone older and cleverer and colder than he could ever understand.

And then she sang a red-haired man, stepping forward with a sword. And the young king falling. And his last words.

*He said I was special.*

The duke was weeping. Openly, at the high table, in front of all his lords. His heavy grey face was wet, and his shoulders were shaking, and he did not hide it.

And Denna sang the last verse. A red-haired man, standing at the open door, with the key in his hand. Pulling his own name out of his chest, strand by strand, and letting it go. So that the door would close. So that the thing behind it could never come out.

So that no one else would have to die.

---

When she finished, no one moved.

The hall was silent. Hundreds of people sat in their seats with their faces wet and their hands still, and no one moved, and no one spoke. The candles burned. The fire crackled in the great hearth.

Denna stood up. She set down her harp. She looked at the duke, weeping at his high table.

"That's what happened, your grace," she said. Her voice was very quiet, but it carried to every corner of the hall. "That's how your son died. That's the truth." She paused. "I'm sorry. I'm so sorry. But I won't tell you a lie."

The duke looked at her for a long time. His face was grey and wet and ruined.

Then he stood up. He walked down from the high table, slowly, heavily, the way an old man walks. He walked the length of the hall, through the silent crowd of lords and ladies, to where Denna stood beside her harp.

He stopped in front of her.

Lady Tamsin said that everyone in the hall held their breath. That they all thought he would strike her. Or call the guards. Or draw his sword and kill her where she stood.

He did not.

He looked down at her for a long time. And then he said, very quietly, so quietly that only the people nearest could hear:

"He loved that song. Your other one. The false one. He used to ask you to sing it every night." He was silent for a moment. "I think he knew it wasn't true. I think that's why he loved it. Because it made a monster into a hero. And he wanted so badly to believe that could happen."

Denna did not answer.

The duke looked at her a moment longer. Then he turned and walked out of the hall. Alone. Without a word to anyone.

He did not come back to the feast. He did not come back to Denna's rooms either, the next day, or the day after. He did not threaten her again.

But he did not let her go.

---

"Why did you do it?" I asked her, long afterward. "You knew he could have killed you. He could have killed both of us."

We were sitting together by a fire, somewhere far from Renere, on a night I will tell you about when I come to it. She was holding my hand. My left hand, with the cord around its wrist.

"Because it was true," she said simply.

"That's not a reason."

"It's the only reason." She looked into the fire. "I spent three years writing a lie. The most beautiful lie I could make. And people wept at it. Lords and ladies and kings. They wept, and they believed it, and every time they did, the world got a little bit worse." She was quiet for a moment. "I owed the truth something. After that. I owed it a song."

"And the part about Ambrose? Singing him with love?"

Denna was quiet for a long time.

"He wasn't a monster," she said at last. "He was cruel, and vain, and stupid, and he did terrible things. But he wasn't a monster. He was a boy who was used. Like me. Like you." She looked at me. "Bredon used all of us. He found the broken places in us and put his fingers in them and pulled. And some of us broke worse than others." She squeezed my hand. "I could have been Ambrose. If I'd been born to a duke instead of a ditch. If Bredon had found me later, when I was prouder, instead of earlier, when I was desperate. I could have been standing at that door with a knife."

"But you weren't."

"No." She looked at me. "Because of you. Because you sang. Because you loosened the knot. Because you gave me a choice." Her eyes were wet. "He never had anyone to do that for him. No one ever gave Ambrose a choice. So I thought—someone should sing him. At least once. The way he really was. Not a hero. Not a monster. Just a boy who got lost."

I did not say anything. I held her hand, and looked into the fire.

"It didn't matter, in the end," she said. "The song. The duke didn't let me go. The trial still happened. You still hanged." She smiled faintly. "Well. Sort of."

"It mattered," I said.

"How?"

I thought about it.

"Because you sang it true," I said at last. "And somewhere, a long way off, a one-eyed man in a round tower heard it. And for one night, he didn't have to be the only one who remembered."
