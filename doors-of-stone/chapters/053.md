# A Letter in Knots

Denna's first letter came eleven days after the fire.

It did not come by post. It did not come by messenger. It came by way of a small, filthy, freckled boy of about eight, who knocked on the door of Merra's house in the early morning and asked for "the red-haired man what sings." Threpe's guards tried to send him away. He kicked one of them in the shin and bit the other one's hand and shouted my name at the top of his lungs until I came down the stairs to see what the commotion was.

"You're him," the boy said, looking up at me. He had a gap where his front teeth should have been. "The one what sings."

"I'm him."

"Lady gave me this." He held out a grubby fist. "Said give it only to you. Said you'd give me a penny."

I did not have a penny. I had nothing. Threpe's guard, rubbing his bitten hand, sighed and fished one out of his own pocket and gave it to the boy. The boy snatched it, and in the same motion pushed something into my hand, and was gone down the street before any of us could say a word.

I looked down at what he had given me.

It was a cord. A thin dark cord, about as long as my forearm, braided from three strands of what I realized, after a moment, was human hair. Dark hair. And it was knotted.

---

I took it up to my room and sat on the bed with it and did not move for a long time.

It was not like the Dellers' cloth, or the cord Denna had worn in the Grey Man. It was simpler. The knots were smaller and plainer, spaced out along the cord at even intervals, like words on a page. She had tied it for me to read. Using only the knots she had taught me, and a few that were obvious from their shape. She had tied it slowly and carefully, so that I would not mistake any of them.

And she had tied it in her own hair.

I sat on the bed and read it. It took me most of the morning. Some of the knots I had to puzzle over for a long time, turning the cord this way and that in the light from the window. But I read it. All of it.

*Siege*, it said. *Severen. Nine days. Walls hold.*

*King. Tent. Box. Guarded always.*

*Old man. Waiting. For you. He says you will come to Severen. To save your family. He says that is the trap.*

*Not Severen. Lackless house. Near Levinshir. The door is there. Under.*

*When Severen falls, they take the lady. Her blood. And then they wait for you.*

*Do not come to Severen.*

*Do not come to the door.*

*Find another way.*

And then, at the very end, after a gap, one more knot. Small and tight and very neat, with the loops doubling back on themselves in that particular way.

It was the knot that said *forgive*.

---

I sat on the bed with the cord in my hands for a long time after I had finished reading it.

*When Severen falls, they take the lady. Her blood.*

Meluan. My aunt. Nine months with child by now, or near enough. Trapped in a city under siege, behind walls that held, for now.

*He says you will come to Severen. To save your family. He says that is the trap.*

Of course it was. Of course. Bredon knew me. He had known me since the first day I sat down across a tak board from him. He knew that I would hear about the siege, and about Meluan, and that I would come running. That I would throw myself at Ambrose's army, alone if I had to, with my one good arm and my borrowed sword, to try to save her.

And when I did, he would be waiting.

*Find another way.*

I sat on the bed and thought about that. I thought about it for a long time.

What other way was there? I could not fight an army. I could not break a siege. I could not walk into Ambrose's camp and steal the box out of his tent. I could not do any of the things that needed doing.

And even if I could—even if I saved Meluan, and got the box back, and somehow kept them both safe—what then? The door would still be there. Beneath the Lackless house, waiting. Bredon would still be there, patient as stone, playing his long slow game. Haliax would still be there, wanting to die. The Thief would still be there, behind his door, breathing in the dark.

It would never end. Not as long as the door could be opened. Not as long as there was a key, and a line, and someone who could speak the name.

*A true lock needs a true key*, Skarpi had said. *So that it can be checked, and tended, and held.*

But what if the lock did not need to be tended? What if there were a way to close the door so completely that no key could ever open it again? Not the line, not the box, not anyone?

Selitos had made the door. Selitos had made the key, and bound the line to it. If anyone knew how to unmake that binding—how to close the door for good—it was Selitos.

And Skarpi had said, in the broken lighthouse, in the middle of his story, that Selitos still watched. In the ruins of Myr Tariniel. In the mountains.

I sat up very straight.

---

"You want to go *where*?" said Devi.

We were sitting in her rooms above the butcher's shop, all of us. Devi and Sim and Wil and Fela, who was well enough now to walk, though she still coughed sometimes and tired easily. Threpe had come too, in a coat of subdued grey that was, for him, practically mourning dress. And Elodin, who had not been invited but had come anyway, and was sitting on the windowsill eating one of Devi's apples.

"The Stormwal," I said. "The mountains in the north. There's a ruin there, very old. The ruins of Myr Tariniel."

"That's a story," said Sim. "Myr Tariniel. It's a place in a story."

"So was the Chandrian. Until they walked into the Eolian."

Sim opened his mouth. Then he closed it again.

"Why?" said Wil. His hands were still bandaged, and he held them carefully in his lap. "Why there? Why now?"

"Because there's someone there who knows how to close the door. For good. So no one can ever open it again." I looked around at them. "And if I can learn how, then it doesn't matter what Bredon does. It doesn't matter if Ambrose has the box, or Meluan, or anything else. If the door can't be opened, their whole game falls apart."

"Who?" said Fela quietly. "Who's there?"

I hesitated.

"Selitos," I said.

There was a long silence.

"Selitos," said Threpe. "*The* Selitos? The one-eyed man from the stories? The one who founded the Amyr?"

"Yes."

"He'd be—" Threpe did some quick arithmetic on his fingers and gave up. "He'd be thousands of years old."

"Yes."

"He'd be dead."

"Skarpi's thousands of years old," I said. "And he's not dead."

Threpe opened his mouth. Then he closed it again, the way Sim had.

---

"The Stormwal is a very long way away," said Elodin, from the windowsill. It was the first thing he had said all evening. "Two months, by road. Three, if the weather turns. And there's a war between here and there, and a king who wants you dead, and a thousand royals on your head." He took a bite of apple. "By the time you get there, Severen will have fallen. And your aunt will be in Ambrose's hands. And the old man will have moved on to the next part of his game."

"I know."

"So you can't go by road."

"No."

Elodin looked at me for a long moment. His dark eyes were very sharp.

"You're thinking of another way," he said. "A faster way."

"Yes."

"Through the Fae."

The room went very quiet.

"The *Fae*?" said Sim. His voice cracked. "Kvothe, are you mad? You barely got out of there the last time!"

"I got out. That's what matters." I looked at Elodin. "The Fae doesn't follow the same rules as the mortal world. Distances are different. Time is different. A journey that takes months here might take days there. If I can find the right roads—"

"And if you can't? If you get lost? If you wander into the wrong grove, or eat the wrong fruit, or speak to the wrong person?" Elodin's voice was very calm. "You could come out in a hundred years. Or never come out at all."

"I know."

"You don't know. You think you know. You went into the Fae once, and you met Felurian, and you came out with your mind and your life and a very nice cloak. You think that makes you an expert." He set down his apple. "It doesn't. It makes you lucky. And luck runs out."

I looked at him. I did not say anything.

"There are things in the Fae," Elodin said quietly, "that make the Chandrian look like children playing in a garden. Old things. Hungry things. Things that have been waiting a very long time for a mortal with a namer's gift and the blood of the Thief to come wandering into their woods." He looked at me. "You'd be walking into the lion's den with a steak tied around your neck."

"Then what should I do?" I said. "Sit here? Wait? Let Severen fall and Meluan be taken and the door be opened?" I looked around at them. "I don't have any other choice. There isn't time for anything else."

Elodin looked at me for a long time. Then he sighed.

"No," he said. "No, I suppose there isn't." He picked up his apple again. "When?"

"The full moon. Nine days from now."

"And how will you get in?"

"The same way I did before. There's a glade in the Eld. A pool, with a greystone beside it. That's where I went through last time."

Elodin nodded slowly. "And once you're in? How will you find your way?"

I hesitated. "I'll figure it out."

"You'll figure it out." Elodin laughed, a short humorless sound. "Of course you will." He shook his head. "Well. At least you'll have something to write about in your memoirs. Assuming you live to write them."

---

They argued for a long time after that. Sim wanted to come with me. Wil said Sim would be more hindrance than help and Sim was offended. Devi said she would come, and I said no, and she said I did not get to decide that, and I said I did, actually, and we shouted at each other for a while. Fela did not say anything. She sat quietly in the corner, with her hands in her lap, and listened.

In the end, I won. I usually do, when I argue. It is one of my less attractive qualities.

"I go alone," I said. "It's the only way. The Fae is dangerous enough for one mortal. For five, it would be a slaughter." I looked around at them. "And I need you here. All of you. I need you to help Threpe keep Imre from recognizing Ambrose. I need you to find out everything you can about what's happening in Vintas. I need you to be ready, when I come back."

"*If* you come back," said Devi.

"When," I said.

She looked at me for a long moment. Then she looked away.

"When," she said quietly.

---

Later, when the others had gone, Fela stayed behind.

She sat in the corner of Devi's room while Devi cleaned up the teacups, and she looked at me with her dark serious eyes. When Devi went out to the landing to shake out the tablecloth, Fela got up and came and sat beside me.

"The door," she said quietly. "Under the Lackless house. It's made of stone."

"Yes."

"Like the one under the Archives."

"Yes. They're the same door. Or faces of the same door. Seven of them."

She was quiet for a moment. Then she reached into her pocket and took something out and pressed it into my hand.

It was a stone. A small flat grey stone, about the size of a coin, worn smooth by water. There was a single mark scratched into it. A line, very straight, with a small curve at one end.

"I made it," Fela said. "After the fire. When I was sick. I couldn't get out of bed, so I lay there and held this stone and listened to it. For days." She looked at me. "It's a piece of the Eolian. From the foundation. One of the stones I held."

I looked down at it.

"I asked it to remember," she said. "What it did. How it held. How it said no, when the fire wanted it to say yes." She paused. "And I think it does. I think if you hold it, it'll tell you. It'll remind you."

"Remind me of what?"

"That stone can hold." Fela's voice was very quiet. "Even when everything else is burning. Even when it's alone in the dark. It can hold." She closed my fingers around the stone. "And so can you."

I looked at her. At her pale face and her singed hair and her dark serious eyes.

"Thank you, Fela."

She smiled faintly. "Just come back. That's all. Come back and give it back to me. I want it back."

"I'll give it back."

"Promise."

I thought about all the promises I had made. All the ones I had broken. All the ones I would break.

"I promise," I said.

---

That night, I went down to the river.

Not the docks. Further down, past the last of the warehouses, where the bank grew wild and overgrown and there was an old stone culvert that emptied into the water. It was a drain, from the old days, when Imre had been smaller and the University had been younger. It ran back under the town, under the river, into the dark.

I knelt by the mouth of the culvert and whistled. Three notes, rising.

For a long time, nothing happened. Then I heard a patter of bare feet on wet stone, far back in the dark. And a small pale face appeared at the mouth of the drain, looking out at me with enormous eyes.

"Kvothe," Auri whispered.

"Hello, Auri."

"You're on the wrong side of the river." She looked at me reproachfully. "You're not supposed to be on the wrong side. You're supposed to be on the right side. With me."

"I can't come to the right side anymore. They won't let me."

"I know. I heard." She crept out of the culvert and sat on the stones beside me, her knees drawn up to her chin. "The Underthing told me. The stone was very upset." She looked at me. "You woke the deep door."

"I didn't mean to."

"I know you didn't. But you did." She was quiet for a moment. "It's been dreaming ever since. Very loudly. The whole Underthing can hear it." She shivered. "It's dreaming about you."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry. Be careful." She looked at me with her enormous eyes. "You're going away again. Aren't you? I can tell. You have your going-away face on."

"Yes."

"Far?"

"Very far."

"To the other door? The one with the family?"

I stared at her. "How do you know about that?"

"The stone told me." She said it simply, as if it were obvious. "All the doors talk to each other. They're all the same door, really. Just in different places." She tilted her head. "The one here is very worried about the one there. It says the one there is very tired. It says someone's been knocking."

I did not say anything for a long time.

"Auri," I said at last. "If I don't come back—"

"Don't say that." She put her small cold hand over my mouth. "Don't say that. It's not a proper thing to say."

I took her hand away gently.

"If I don't come back," I said again, "I want you to know that you were my friend. My first friend, here. And one of my best." I swallowed. "I want you to know that."

She looked at me for a long time. Her enormous eyes were wet in the moonlight.

"I know that," she said. "I've always known that." She leaned against my shoulder, very lightly, the way a bird might land on a branch. "You're my friend too. My only friend who brings presents."

I laughed. It came out broken.

"I didn't bring you a present tonight," I said.

"Yes you did." She tilted her head up to look at me. "You came to say goodbye. That's the best present. Most people don't."

We sat there by the river for a long time, in the moonlight, with the water rushing past and the lights of Imre glittering on the far bank. She did not say anything else, and neither did I.

When she went back into the culvert, a little before dawn, she stopped at the mouth of it and turned around.

"Kvothe," she said. "When you get to the other door. If it speaks to you. Don't answer."

"I won't."

"Promise?"

"I promise."

She looked at me for a long moment. Then she smiled, a small sad radiant smile.

"You always promise," she said. "That's what I love about you. And that's what worries me."

Then she was gone, back into the dark, and I was alone by the river with the dawn coming up grey behind me.
