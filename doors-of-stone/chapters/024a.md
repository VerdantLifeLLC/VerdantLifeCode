# A Toll on the Southern Road

Somewhere south of the Commonwealth border, on the road to Vintas, I was robbed by the politest highwaymen in the Four Corners.

I was traveling alone that day. Tessit's troupe had stopped for two nights in a village to play a wedding, and I had gone on ahead, restless, meaning to let them catch me up at the next town. I had my lute and my sword and a pack with a change of clothes and a little food, and a purse with what was left of the Maer's money. It was more than I liked to carry on a lonely road.

It was late afternoon. The road ran through a stretch of woods, old beech and oak, with the light coming green and gold through the leaves. I was walking slowly, humming to myself, thinking about Denna. And about her patron, and the Maer, and what I had come south to find. And about my own foolishness, which was considerable, and which I was trying hard not to think about.

And then a man stepped out of the trees in front of me, and bowed.

---

He was tall and thin, with a long mournful face and a drooping grey mustache, and he was dressed like a gentleman who had fallen on hard times: a velvet coat, very worn, with patches at the elbows, and a hat with a bedraggled feather in it. He had a sword at his hip, which he did not draw. He swept off his hat and bowed very low, the feather brushing the dirt of the road.

"Good afternoon, young sir," he said. "I hope I find you well."

I stopped. I looked at him. I looked at the trees on either side of the road, where I could hear, now that I was listening, the small sounds of other people moving. Three of them. Perhaps four.

"Well enough," I said. "And yourself?"

"Oh, middling, middling." He replaced his hat. "The damp gets into my joints, this time of year. And business has been slow." He smiled apologetically. "Which brings me, I'm afraid, to the unpleasant part of our acquaintance."

"You're going to rob me."

"I'm going to ask you," he said, "to pay a toll. For the use of the road. It's a very fine road, you'll have noticed. We maintain it ourselves. We fill in the potholes and clear the fallen branches and keep the wolves at bay." He spread his hands. "It's only fair that those who enjoy its benefits should contribute to its upkeep."

"And if I don't?"

He looked pained.

"Then my associates," he said, "who are less courteous than I am, and considerably less patient, will be obliged to collect it on my behalf. And they are not gentle collectors." He sighed. "I do apologize. It's a very crude system. But we are very crude people, in the main."

---

Four men came out of the trees. Two on either side. They were not dressed like gentlemen. They were dressed like what they were: hungry, hard-faced men with clubs and knives, who looked at me and my lute and my sword with flat, measuring eyes.

I could have fought them. I want to be clear about that. I had done the Ketan every morning since Haert, on rooftops and riverbanks and in the empty dueling rooms of the Mains. I had Caesura at my hip. Four men with clubs and one with a sword he did not seem inclined to draw: I could have fought them, and I might have won.

But I did not want to. I was tired and sad and I had come to the end of something, at the University, and I did not want to begin whatever came next by killing four hungry men on a road in the woods. And there was something about the tall mournful man with the drooping mustache that I liked.

So I did what the Ruh do, when there is trouble on the road and fighting is not the answer.

I told a story.

---

"How much is the toll?" I said.

The tall man looked relieved. "A modest sum. For a young man of your evident quality—" He looked at my lute case, and at the weight of the purse on my belt. "Shall we say, the contents of your purse?"

"That seems steep."

"The road is very well maintained."

"It is," I agreed. "I noticed that. Very few potholes. And no wolves at all." I looked at him. "I'll tell you what. I'll make you a wager."

His eyebrows rose. "A wager?"

"I'm a musician," I said. "A storyteller. Edema Ruh." I saw the four hard-faced men glance at each other. "I'll tell you a story. Right here. Right now. And if, at the end of it, you can tell me how it ends, then I'll pay your toll twice over. My purse, and my lute besides."

"And if I can't?"

"Then I pass for free," I said. "And you let the next traveler pass for free too. Whoever it is. Whatever they're carrying."

The tall man looked at me for a long moment. Then he looked at his four associates. One of them, a broad man with a broken nose, shook his head sharply.

"Now, now, Bram," said the tall man mildly. "Let's not be hasty. It's been a very dull span." He turned back to me, and there was a light in his mournful eyes that had not been there before. "Very well, young sir. A story. But I warn you: I've heard a great many."

"Not this one," I said.

---

I sat down on a fallen log beside the road. After a moment, the tall man sat down across from me, on a stump. The four hard men stood around us, uneasily, with their clubs.

And I told them a story about a highwayman.

I did not plan it. It came to me as I began, the way the best stories do. I told them about a man who had been a gentleman once, in a city far away. Who had lost his fortune and his name through no fault of his own, through the malice of a powerful lord. Who had gone to the woods, with nothing but his sword and his good manners, and gathered around him other men who had also lost everything. And who had set up a toll on the road, and collected it courteously from those who could afford to pay, and let those who could not afford it pass, and used the money to feed his men and the poor villages along the road.

"A very noble highwayman," said the tall man dryly.

"Very," I agreed. "And one day, a young man came down the road. A musician. A storyteller. And the highwayman stopped him, and asked for his toll. And the young man said—"

I stopped.

The tall man waited. The four hard men waited. The wind moved in the beech leaves overhead.

"Well?" said the tall man. "What did he say?"

"That's the wager," I said. "You tell me."

---

He sat on his stump for a long time. His face was thoughtful. The four hard men looked at each other. The broad one with the broken nose, Bram, scowled.

"He paid," said Bram at last. "The musician. What else? He paid and went on his way."

"Is that the ending?" I said to the tall man. "Is that your answer?"

The tall man did not answer. He was looking at me very carefully, with his long mournful face.

"No," he said slowly. "No, I don't think it is." He stroked his drooping mustache. "I think the young man told the highwayman a story. About a highwayman. A noble one, who had lost everything, and took tolls from the rich to feed the poor." He paused. "And I think the highwayman listened to the story, and recognized himself in it. Or rather—" He looked at me. "Recognized the man he had meant to be. When he first came to the woods. Before the hunger and the cold and the years made him into something else."

The four hard men had gone very quiet.

"And I think," said the tall man, very softly, "that the highwayman was ashamed. And let the young man pass. Not because he lost a wager. But because he did not want to be the kind of man who would rob a storyteller for telling him the truth."

---

I did not say anything for a long moment. Neither did anyone else.

"That's a very good ending," I said at last.

"Is it the right one?"

"I don't know," I said honestly. "I didn't know how it ended when I started. I only knew how it began." I looked at him. "You finished it. So I suppose it's yours."

The tall man looked at me for a long time. Then he laughed. It was a surprisingly warm laugh, deep and rueful.

"Edema Ruh," he said. "I should have known better." He stood up and swept off his bedraggled hat and bowed again, very low. "You win, young sir. Or I lose. I'm not sure which. Either way, you pass for free." He glanced at Bram. "And so does the next traveler. Whoever it is. Whatever they're carrying."

Bram opened his mouth to protest. The tall man looked at him. Bram closed his mouth.

---

I stood up. I shouldered my pack and my lute. I nodded to the four hard men, who did not nod back, and to the tall man, who did.

"May I ask your name?" I said.

He hesitated. Then he smiled, his long mournful face creasing.

"I used to be called Lord Ferrin Vell," he said. "Of the Vells of Tinuë. Once upon a time." He shrugged. "Now I'm just Ferrin. The toll-keeper of Beech Wood." He looked at me. "And you?"

"Kvothe."

His eyebrows went up. "*The* Kvothe? From the University? The one they tell stories about?"

"Some of them are true."

"Hm." He looked at me with new interest. "Well. I'll have a story of my own to tell, then. The day I was bested on my own road by Kvothe the Arcane, with nothing but a tale about myself." He smiled. "I shall dine out on it for years. If I ever dine out again."

---

I walked on down the road. The trees closed in behind me. After a while, the sound of the highwaymen's voices faded.

I found out, much later, what had happened to them. It was Stapes who told me, in the Maer's palace, in passing, as if it were a thing of no importance.

A band of highwaymen had been operating in Beech Wood for years, he said, on the southern road. Their leader was a disgraced nobleman. They had been a terrible nuisance. But that summer, something strange had happened. They had stopped taking tolls from anyone who looked poor. They had begun leaving food on the doorsteps of the poorest farms along the road, at night, without a word. And in the autumn, when a patrol of the Maer's soldiers went into the woods to clear them out, they found the camp empty, and a note nailed to a tree.

*Gone to be what we meant to be,* the note said. *Thank the storyteller.*

Nobody knew what it meant, Stapes said. It had become a kind of joke, among the Maer's guards.

I did not tell him. I only smiled, and drank my wine, and thought of a tall mournful man on a stump in a beech wood, finishing my story for me.

It is the only time in my life I ever let someone else write the ending. It is also, I think, one of the few endings I have never regretted.
