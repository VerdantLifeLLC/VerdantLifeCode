# The Dates

I spent the next nine days in the Archives with Kilvin's ledger.

Not the whole ledger. Kilvin would not let it out of the Fishery; he said it was the only copy, and that twenty years of careful observation should not be carried across a courtyard in the pocket of a student who had once set fire to his own cloak in the Artificery. So I copied out the dates. Every day the candle had burned blue, in twenty years. There were a hundred and thirty-one of them.

I wrote them on a long strip of paper, in a column, in my smallest hand. And then I took the strip of paper into the Archives, and I began to look.

---

Wil helped. He did not want to. He said that he had a great deal of work of his own, and that my projects had a way of becoming everyone's projects, and that he still had not forgiven me for the business with the lamp in the Tomes. But I showed him the strip of paper, and told him what it was, and he looked at it for a long time with his dark Cealdish eyes.

"A candle that burns blue when the Chandrian are abroad," he said.

"Yes."

"And you want to find out what they were doing. On each of these days."

"Yes."

Wil looked at the column of dates. A hundred and thirty-one of them.

"This is going to take months," he said.

"I know."

He sighed. "What do you need?"

---

What I needed was news. Records. Anything that noted, on a particular day, in a particular place, that something strange had happened.

The Archives are full of such things, if you know where to look. Not in the Tomes, where the scholars read, but down in the Stacks, in the dusty forgotten corners where the University keeps everything it has ever been given and never thrown away. Broadsheets from Imre and Anilin and Tarbean, stacked in bundles tied with string. Letters from the University's correspondents, scholars and physicians and priests all over the Four Corners, reporting on the weather and the harvest and the local curiosities. Tax rolls. Court records. The ledgers of a hundred merchant houses, bequeathed to the Archives by heirs who did not want them.

And constables' reports. Boxes and boxes of them. The University had an arrangement, going back two hundred years, with the magistrates of the Commonwealth: copies of any report concerning *unexplained phenomena* were to be sent to the Archives for study. Most of the reports were nonsense. Lights in the marshes. Two-headed calves. A woman in Hallowfell who claimed her cat had spoken to her in Tema.

But not all of them.

---

It was slow work. We went through the dates one at a time. Wil took the broadsheets and the correspondents' letters; I took the constables' reports. We worked every evening, after his shift at the desk, and every morning before my classes, down in a forgotten corner of the Stacks by the light of a single sympathy lamp.

And slowly, date by date, a pattern began to emerge.

*Fourteen years ago, in the spring*: a wedding party in the hills south of Anilin. Thirty-one guests, all dead, in the barn where the feast had been laid. The constable's report noted that the candles on the tables had been found burned down to nothing, and that the wax had pooled and hardened *in a strange blue color*.

*Nine years ago, in the autumn*: a Tehlin monastery near the Small Kingdoms. Eleven monks, dead in the chapel. The report was brief and frightened and did not say how they had died. It said that the iron on the chapel door had rusted through overnight, *as if a hundred years had passed in a single night*.

*Seven years ago, at midwinter*: a cartwright's family outside Tinuë. A father, a mother, four children. Found in their beds, with frost on their eyelashes, in a house where the fire was still burning in the grate.

*Six years ago, late in the summer*: a troupe of Edema Ruh in the woods near the border of the Commonwealth.

I did not need a report for that one. I stopped, when I came to that date on the strip of paper. I sat for a long time in the dim red light of the Stacks with my finger on it. Wil did not say anything. He knew.

After a while, I moved my finger to the next date.

---

Not every date had a massacre. That was the strange thing. That was the thing that kept me awake, those nine nights, lying in my narrow bed at Anker's and staring at the ceiling.

Some of the dates had nothing at all. Nothing in the broadsheets, nothing in the letters, nothing in the constables' reports. The candle had burned blue, and the world had gone on, and no one had noticed anything.

"Maybe they were traveling," said Wil, when I told him. "Maybe the candle burns blue whenever they're—out. In the world. Not just when they're killing."

"Maybe."

"Or maybe they were doing something no one noticed," said Wil. "Something quiet. Something that didn't leave any bodies." He looked at me. "That's worse, I think."

I thought so too.

And some of the dates had things that were not massacres, but were strange in other ways. A bridge in Atur that collapsed on a still day, with no flood and no storm, killing no one. A library in Modeg that burned, with every book in it, and not a single person hurt. A famous singer in Vintas who lost her voice overnight and never sang again.

A scholar in Yll who had been writing a history of the Creation War, and who was found dead at his desk, with his last page burned to ash in his hand.

A tinker on the north road, near Hallowfell, who had been telling a story in an inn about seven strangers in the woods, and who disappeared the next morning, leaving his donkey and his cart behind.

---

"They're hunting stories," I said to Wil, on the ninth night.

We were sitting in our corner of the Stacks, surrounded by boxes and bundles and the dry sweet smell of old paper. The strip of dates lay on the table between us, marked now with small notes in my hand and Wil's.

"Stories," said Wil.

"Songs. Histories. Anything that tells about them. About what they are." I touched the strip. "My parents were writing a song. The scholar in Yll was writing a history. The tinker was telling a tale in an inn. The library in Modeg—" I stopped. "I'd wager it had something. Some book. Some record. Something true."

"And the bridge?"

"I don't know. Maybe something was carved on it. Maybe somebody sang a song on it once." I shook my head. "I don't know. But I'd wager it's the same. Every time. Someone, somewhere, starts to tell the truth about them. And they come."

Wil was quiet for a long time.

"Then you'd better be careful," he said at last. "Hadn't you."

"I'm always careful."

Wil looked at me with his dark calm eyes. He did not say anything. He did not have to.

---

There was one more date. I have left it for last, because I found it last, and because it frightened me more than any of the others.

It was at the very bottom of the strip. The most recent. Kilvin had noted it only a few months before, while I was in Vintas. A single day in late spring. The candle had burned blue from dawn until noon, and then gone back to yellow.

I searched everything for that date. Every broadsheet, every letter, every report. There was nothing. No massacre. No fire. No bridge or library or singer. Nothing at all.

Until, on the last night, going back through the Archives' own records to see if I had missed anything, I found it in the most unlikely place of all. The admissions register of the Archives. The daily list of everyone who had come in through the great doors, with their names and their business, kept by the scriv at the desk.

On that day, in late spring, while I was far away in Severen, one visitor had signed the register. A gentleman scholar from the south. With a letter of introduction from a lord in Renere.

His name was written in a small, neat, slanting hand.

*Brand.*

---

I did not know, then, what it meant. I want to be clear about that. I did not know, then, that I had seen that hand before, or would see it again, in six old ledgers in a cold corner of the Stacks. I did not know about the old man in the Maer's garden. I did not know anything.

I only knew that on the day a quiet gentleman with a letter from Renere came into the Archives, Kilvin's candle had burned blue from dawn until noon.

And I knew, from the register, what time he had left.

Noon.

---

I showed Wil. He looked at the register for a long time. Then he looked at the strip of dates. Then he looked at me.

"Kvothe," he said slowly. "The candle's supposed to burn blue when the Chandrian are doing something. Anywhere in the world."

"Yes."

"Then why would it burn blue for a man reading books in the Archives?"

I did not answer. I did not have an answer. Not then.

I folded the strip of dates, very carefully, and put it in my pocket. I put the register back on its shelf. And I went back to Anker's, and lay in my narrow bed, and stared at the ceiling, and did not sleep at all.

---

"Did you ever find out?" said Chronicler. "Why it burned for him?"

The innkeeper was quiet for a long moment.

"Yes," he said. "Eventually. It's in the story. You'll see." He looked at his hands. "But I'll tell you now what I didn't know then. Kilvin's candle doesn't only burn for the seven. It burns for anyone who is doing their work. Their master's work." He paused. "It burned for him because he was in the Archives looking for something the Chandrian wanted found. He was working for them. Or with them. Or they were working for him." He shook his head. "I've never been sure which. I'm not sure he's sure."

"And the other dates? The ones with nothing?"

The innkeeper looked at the fire.

"Those are the ones that frighten me," he said. "Even now. A hundred and thirty-one days in twenty years. And for forty of them, I never found anything at all." He was quiet. "Forty times, something was done in the world that served the Chandrian. And no one noticed. No one died. No one wrote it down." He looked at Chronicler. "That's the real danger. Not the fires. The quiet. The things that happen that no one ever notices, until the day they look up and see the shape the stones have made."
