# Interlude: Hands

The innkeeper stopped speaking. His hands were flat on the table in front of him, and Chronicler saw that they were pressed down so hard that the tips of the fingers had gone white.

Nobody said anything. The fire crackled in the grate. Somewhere outside, a dog was barking, far off, a thin lonely sound that came and went on the wind.

Bast was the first to move. He stood up from his backward chair, very slowly, the way a cat stands when it has seen something in the grass. His face was pale. His eyes were very dark, darker than Chronicler had ever seen them, dark as deep water.

"She took it," he said. His voice was very quiet. "She took the key and gave it to *him*."

"She didn't give it to him," said the innkeeper. "Not then. Not willingly."

"It doesn't matter. She took it." Bast's hands were shaking. "Reshi, you never told me. You never told me any of this. I thought—I always thought it was the other way. I thought he took it from you. I thought—"

"I know what you thought."

"You let me think it!"

"Yes."

"*Why?*"

The innkeeper looked up at him. His face was very tired.

"Because you would have hated her," he said. "And I didn't want you to hate her."

Bast stared at him. For a long moment he did not speak. Then he made a sound, low in his throat, a sound that was not quite human. It was the sound a fox makes in the night, the sound of something wild and hurt and furious. He turned away from the table and went to the window and stood there with his back to them, his arms wrapped around himself, his whole body rigid.

"I do hate her," he said, without turning around. "I've always hated her. You know that. Even before. Even when I thought—" He stopped. "She's always been the thing that hurt you. Every time. Every single time."

"She didn't mean to."

"*It doesn't matter what she meant!*"

The words cracked through the common room like a whip. The bottles behind the bar rang faintly. The fire in the hearth leapt up and then sank low, as if it had flinched.

Chronicler sat very still. He had seen Bast angry before, in the last two days. He had seen him threatening and sly and dangerous. But he had not seen this. This was something else. This was something old and cold and very far from human, looking out through a young man's eyes.

The innkeeper did not flinch. He sat at the table with his hands flat on the wood and looked at Bast's back.

"Bast," he said quietly. "Come and sit down."

Bast did not move.

"Bast."

For a long moment, nothing happened. Then, slowly, the rigid line of Bast's shoulders softened. He turned from the window. His face was wet. He came back to the table and sat down in his backward chair and put his arms on the back of it and put his face in his arms.

The innkeeper reached out and laid one hand on the back of Bast's dark head. He did not say anything. He simply left it there, the way you might lay a hand on a frightened animal.

After a while, Bast's shoulders stopped shaking.

---

Chronicler looked down at his page. He had stopped writing somewhere in the middle of the last part of the story, and he had not noticed. The last line on the page was half a word, trailing off into a smear of ink where his pen had slipped.

He looked at it for a long moment. Then he looked at his own hands.

They were shaking.

He did not know when that had started. He did not know why. He only knew that as he had listened to the innkeeper's voice, telling about the empty bed and the missing box and the knot on the table, something in him had gone cold and still, the way the air goes cold and still before a storm.

He had recorded a great many stories. Some of them had been sad. Some of them had been terrible. He had written down accounts of battles and massacres, of plagues and famines, of men who had done things so cruel that he had had to stop and go outside and walk around the block before he could continue.

But he had never felt like this. He had never felt as if the story he was writing was reaching out of the page and taking hold of him.

He thought about the knot. *A story that is told many times and changes in the telling.* He thought about what the innkeeper had said, that morning, about the old writing. The kind that did not remember things but made them.

He looked at his page, with its tiny crabbed cipher, its lines and loops and marks.

He wondered, for the first time in his life, what exactly he was doing.

---

"Why did you tell it?" he asked. His voice came out rough, and he cleared his throat. "That part. About that night. You didn't have to. You could have said she took the box and left and that would have been enough."

The innkeeper was quiet for a moment. His hand was still resting on Bast's head.

"Because it's true," he said. "And because I promised you the true story. All of it. Not just the parts that make me look clever, or brave, or wise." He smiled faintly. "There aren't many of those, as it turns out. Most of the story is me being a fool."

"But that part—"

"That part more than any." The innkeeper looked down at the table. "You have to understand. Everything that happened afterward, everything I did, every choice I made. It all came back to that night. To waking up and finding her gone and the box gone and the knot on the table." He was silent for a moment. "If you don't know about that night, you can't understand the rest. You'll think I did what I did for glory, or revenge, or because I'm a monster. And some of that's true. But it isn't the whole of it."

"What's the whole of it?"

The innkeeper looked up at him. His green eyes were very dark.

"I loved her," he said simply. "That's all. That's the whole of it. I loved her, and she was bound, and I couldn't cut the cord. And everything I did afterward, every terrible thing, I did because I was trying to find a way to cut it."

Bast lifted his head from his arms. His eyes were red. He looked at the innkeeper for a long moment, and something passed between them that Chronicler did not understand.

"Did you?" Bast said quietly. "Find a way?"

The innkeeper did not answer. He took his hand from Bast's head and folded both hands on the table in front of him and looked at them.

They were steady now. Perfectly steady. But Chronicler, watching them, saw something he had not noticed before. On the inside of the innkeeper's left wrist, just below the heel of the hand, there was a thin pale line. A scar. Very fine, very old, running all the way around the wrist like a bracelet.

It was the kind of scar that a cord leaves, if it is tied very tight, and left for a very long time.

---

"It's getting on toward evening," the innkeeper said at last. He glanced at the window. The light had turned golden and slanting, and the shadows of the trees stretched long across the fields. "The regulars will be in soon. I'll need to start supper."

"I'll do it," said Bast.

The innkeeper looked at him.

"I'll do it," Bast said again. He stood up. His face was still pale, but his voice was steady. "You keep talking. I'll do supper. I know how. I've watched you a hundred times."

"You'll burn the stew."

"I won't burn the stew."

"You always burn the stew."

"I won't *this* time." Bast's mouth twisted into something that was almost a smile. "Trust me, Reshi."

The innkeeper looked at him for a long moment. Then he nodded.

"All right," he said. "Go on. And don't forget the thyme."

Bast went into the kitchen. They heard him moving about in there, banging pots, rather more quietly than he had that morning.

The innkeeper turned back to Chronicler.

"You've stopped writing," he said.

Chronicler looked down. He was still holding his pen, but it had dried in his hand. He dipped it in the inkwell.

"I'm sorry," he said. "I'm ready."

"No, you're not. Your hands are shaking." The innkeeper leaned forward and looked at him, and there was something in his face that was almost kind. "It's all right. It gets to everyone, eventually. The story. I've seen it happen before." He paused. "Do you want to stop for a while?"

Chronicler looked at him. He thought about it.

"No," he said. "No. I want to hear what happened next."

The innkeeper smiled. It was a small, crooked, rueful smile.

"That," he said, "is exactly how it gets you."

He leaned back in his chair and looked out the window, at the long golden light on the fields, and the dark line of the trees, and the thin grey smoke still leaning above them.

"I went back to the Maer," said Kvothe. "And I told him I had lost his wife's inheritance. And he did the one thing I never expected."

"What?"

"He forgave me." The innkeeper's voice was very quiet. "And then he gave me a job. And the job was to go to Renere, to the king's court, and find out who was killing the heirs to the throne of Vintas." He paused. "Which is how I came to meet Ambrose Jakis again. And how I came, eventually, to kill him."

Chronicler's pen stopped.

The innkeeper looked at him, and his face was perfectly calm.

"Write it down," he said. "I'm not going to say it twice."
