# The Smith's Prentice

Aaron came to the Waystone the night after we buried Shep. Very late. After the last of the regulars had gone home, after I had banked the fire and wiped down the bar and put the chairs up on the tables. I was standing in the dark common room with a rag in my hand, not doing anything, when I heard the knock.

It was not a loud knock. Three small taps, very hesitant, the way a child knocks when he is not sure he should.

I opened the door. He was standing on the step in the cold, with his shoulders hunched and his hands shoved deep in the pockets of his coat. He was seventeen that year, almost eighteen. Tall and broad already, with a smith's arms and a boy's face. His face was grey in the starlight.

"I'm sorry," he said. "I know it's late. I saw the light."

There was no light. I had put out all the lamps. But I did not say so.

"Come in," I said.

---

I lit a single lamp and set it on the bar. I poured him a cup of cider and set it in front of him. He did not drink it. He sat on the stool with his big hands wrapped around the cup and stared at it.

I waited.

"I can't sleep," he said at last. "Every time I close my eyes, I see it. The—the thing. Coming out of him. Out of the split." His hands tightened on the cup. "And I hear the sound it made. When I hit it. That wet sound." He swallowed. "I keep thinking about the sound."

"I know," I said.

"Do you?" He looked up at me. His eyes were red. "Do you really? Because you looked—" He stopped. "You didn't look like you'd ever seen anything like it before. But you didn't look surprised, either. You looked like—" He struggled for the words. "Like a man who'd seen worse."

I did not say anything. I polished a glass.

"Have you?" said Aaron. "Seen worse?"

I set down the glass.

"Yes," I said.

---

He did not ask what. I was grateful for that. He only nodded slowly, as if I had confirmed something he had already known, and looked back down at his cup.

"Does it stop?" he said. "The seeing it? When you close your eyes?"

I thought about lying to him. It would have been kind, in a small way. It would have let him sleep.

But he was seventeen, and he had killed a thing out of the dark with a bar of iron to save a man who had not been able to save himself. He had earned the truth.

"No," I said. "Not all the way. It gets smaller. It gets further away. After a while, you'll go days without thinking of it. Then weeks. Then months." I looked at him. "But it doesn't stop. Not all the way. Some nights, years from now, you'll close your eyes and it'll be there. Just as clear as tonight."

Aaron was quiet for a long time.

"That's terrible," he said.

"Yes."

"How do you live with it?"

I looked at the sword over the bar. At the dark shape of it on the wall, just visible in the lamplight. At the plaque beneath it.

"You find something to do with your hands," I said. "And you do it. Every day. Over and over. Until the doing is louder than the seeing."

Aaron looked at me. Then he looked down at his hands. Big and scarred, with burns across the knuckles from the forge. A smith's hands.

"I've got something to do with my hands," he said slowly.

"Yes. You have."

---

He drank his cider then. All of it, in one long pull, the way a thirsty man drinks water. When he set down the cup, some of the grey had gone out of his face.

"Baxter says I did right," he said. "Hitting it. He says iron's the only thing for those. He says his grandfather told him, and his grandfather's grandfather. Cold iron, for the things out of the dark." He turned the empty cup in his hands. "I didn't believe him. When he told me, years ago. I thought it was just old stories. Smith stories. Things smiths tell each other to feel important."

"And now?"

"Now I don't know what to believe." He looked at me. "Master Kote. What was it?"

I did not answer at once.

"Something that shouldn't have been here," I said at last. "Something that came from a long way away. Through a door that should have stayed closed."

"What door?"

"An old one."

Aaron looked at me for a long moment. He was not a stupid boy. I think he knew I was not telling him everything. I think he knew I could not.

"Are there more?" he said. "Like it?"

"Yes."

"Will they come here?"

I was quiet for a long time.

"Maybe," I said. "I hope not. But maybe."

---

He sat with that for a while. I refilled his cup. He did not drink it.

"Then I'll make more iron," he said at last.

I looked at him.

"Bars," said Aaron. "Like the one I used. Plain cold iron, no fancy work, just good and heavy. Enough for every house in Newarre." He was speaking slowly, working it out as he went. "Baxter will let me use the forge after hours, if I pay for the coal. I've got some saved. And I'll make them, and I'll give them out. One for every house. To keep by the door." He looked at me. "So the next time one of those things comes, whoever it comes to won't be standing there with nothing in their hands."

I did not say anything for a long moment. I was thinking of a boy in the Fishery, years ago, with burned hands and a head full of plans, trying to build something that would warn people. A candle. A lamp. A stone you could carry in your pocket. *Something that could warn people, so maybe nobody else would have to—*

"That's a good plan," I said. My voice sounded strange. "That's a very good plan, Aaron."

---

He made them. Over the next three months, in the evenings, after Baxter had gone home, working alone in the forge by the light of the coals. Plain heavy bars of cold iron, the length of a man's forearm, with a rough grip wrapped in leather at one end. He made fifty-three of them. One for every house in Newarre and every farm within an hour's walk.

He brought them round himself, on a handcart. He did not explain much. He only knocked on each door and held one out and said, *For by the door. In case.* And most people took them, a little puzzled, a little embarrassed, and set them in a corner and forgot about them.

But not everyone forgot. Shep's widow kept hers on the table beside her bed. Old Cob hung his over his mantel, beside a faded painting of his mother. Jake gave his to his son Tam, who was six that year, and Tam carried it everywhere for a month, dragging it through the mud, until his mother took it away and put it on a high shelf.

Aaron brought the last one to the Waystone. He set it on the bar in front of me.

"For you," he said. "Though I expect you've got your own."

I had. Several. In the kitchen, and under the bar, and upstairs beside my bed. I had never told anyone.

"Thank you," I said. And I took it, and put it beneath the bar, right under the till, where my hand could find it in the dark.

It is still there. I have never had to use it. But I put my hand on it, sometimes, late at night, when the wind is loud and the fire is low. Just to know it is there.

---

Aaron did not have nightmares after that. Or if he did, he did not tell me. He came to the Waystone most evenings, with the other regulars, and drank his one cup of cider, and listened to Old Cob's stories and Graham's complaints about his barn, and went home. He grew broader. His voice deepened. He started talking about the army; there was a war on, in the south, the way there is always a war on somewhere, and the recruiters had been through Baedn-Bryt.

"I'm good with iron," he said to me one night. "They need smiths. For the horses, and the armor. It's good pay." He looked at me. "What do you think, Master Kote?"

I polished a glass. I thought of the Field of Three Rivers. Of the grey dawn and the bodies in the mud and a young man about his age with his face turned up to the sky.

"I think you should stay," I said. "I think Newarre needs its smith."

Aaron looked at me for a long moment.

"You'd know," he said quietly. "Wouldn't you. About wars."

I did not answer. I set down the glass.

He did not go. Not that year. I do not know if it was because of what I said. I do not know if I had any right to say it.

But he stayed, and he kept making iron, and on the nights when the wind was loud, I sometimes saw a light in the forge window across the square, late, long after everyone else had gone to bed. The glow of the coals. And the steady ringing of a hammer.

Something to do with his hands. Until the doing was louder than the seeing.
