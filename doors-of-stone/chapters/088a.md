# The Farmer's Daughter

We stopped for a night at a farm in the Commonwealth hills, three days out from Imre, because of the rain.

It had been raining since dawn. Not a storm; nothing so dramatic. Just a steady, grey, relentless rain that soaked through cloaks and boots and blankets and skin, and turned the road to mud, and the mud to a thick brown soup that sucked at the cart wheels. By afternoon, the horse was stumbling. Bast was swearing in a language I did not know. And Denna, walking beside the cart with her hood pulled low, was shivering so hard I could hear her teeth.

I was not shivering. I did not feel the cold. That was one of the things about the grey: you did not feel much of anything. I sat in the back of the cart with my knees drawn up and the rain running off my hair, and I looked at the wet hills going by, and I did not feel anything at all.

The farm was at the bottom of a long slope, beside a stream. A low stone house with a thatched roof, and a barn, and a yard full of mud and chickens. There was smoke coming from the chimney. Denna looked at it for a long time.

"We'll ask," she said.

---

The farmer was a big, stooped, silent man named Horrin, with a face like a potato and hands like shovels. He looked at us for a long time from his doorway: at Denna, soaked and shivering, and Bast, with his too-bright eyes, and me, sitting in the back of the cart like a sack of grain.

"What's wrong with him?" he said.

"He was hurt," said Denna. "In the war. In his head."

Horrin looked at me for a while longer. Then he nodded slowly.

"My brother came back like that," he said. "From Three Rivers. Didn't talk for a year." He stepped back from the door. "Barn's dry. There's hay. You can have supper with us, if you want. It's only soup."

---

It was only soup. Barley and onions and a little salt pork, in a big iron pot over the fire. We sat around the table in the single room of the stone house, the three of us and Horrin and his wife, a thin quick woman named Bet, and their four children.

The children stared at us. Of course they did. Strangers were rare, that far out in the hills. The youngest two, a pair of twin boys about five, stared at Bast, who made faces at them when their mother was not looking until they giggled into their soup. The oldest, a lanky boy of twelve, stared at Denna, and went red whenever she looked at him.

And the girl stared at me.

She was about nine. Small and thin, with a pointed face and straight brown hair and very large grey eyes. She sat across the table from me and did not eat her soup. She just looked at me, very steadily, the whole meal, with an expression I could not read.

When the meal was over and Bet was clearing the bowls, the girl got down from her stool and came around the table and stood in front of me.

"You're a musician," she said.

I did not answer. I did not know how.

"Wren," said her mother sharply. "Leave the man be."

"But he is," said the girl. She pointed at my hands, resting on the table. "Look at his fingers. They've got hard bits on the ends. Like Uncle Fen's. Uncle Fen played the fiddle." She looked at me. "Before he went to the war. After, he didn't anymore."

The room was very quiet.

---

"He did play," said Denna softly. "He used to play the lute. Better than anyone in the world."

Wren looked at her. Then back at me.

"Why doesn't he now?"

Denna did not answer for a long moment.

"He forgot how," she said at last.

Wren frowned. She looked at my hands again, lying on the table. At the calluses on the tips of my fingers.

"His hands didn't forget," she said. "You can see. They remember."

Nobody said anything. Horrin looked at his daughter. Bet stood by the washbasin with a bowl in her hands, very still. Bast had stopped making faces at the twins.

"Have you got a lute?" said Wren. "In your cart?"

"Yes," said Denna.

"Can I see it?"

Denna looked at me. I did not look back. I was looking at the table.

"All right," said Denna.

---

Bast went out to the cart and brought it in. Not my own lute. That was wrapped in oilcloth at the bottom of the cart, and I would not touch it, and I would not let anyone else touch it either. This was an old lute Denna had bought in a market town the week before, cheap and battered, with a crack in the soundboard patched with glue. She had bought it, she told me later, because she thought I might want it. I had not touched it.

Bast set it on the table in front of me. I looked at it.

Wren looked at me.

"Go on," she said.

I did not move.

"Wren," said her father. His deep voice was gentle. "Uncle Fen couldn't either. Remember? When you gave him his fiddle. He tried, and he couldn't, and he cried." He put his big hand on her shoulder. "Some things don't come back."

Wren did not look at him. She was looking at me. With her large grey eyes.

And then she did something none of us expected. She reached out, very carefully, and picked up my right hand from the table. Her hand was small and warm and a little sticky from the soup. She lifted my hand and put it on the lute, on the strings, over the soundhole. And then she picked up my left hand and put it on the neck, with my fingers resting on the frets.

"There," she said. "Now they know where they are."

---

I do not know what happened then. I have tried to remember, and I cannot. I remember the lute under my hands. The smooth worn wood of the neck, and the strings, cold and taut, under my fingertips. I remember the fire crackling, and the rain on the thatch, and Wren standing in front of me with her serious pointed face.

And I remember that my hands moved.

They played the way they had played by the fire, weeks before, when Denna put my own lute in my lap. Clean chords. Correct chords. Every note in its place, and nothing in any of them. A painting of a fire, hung on a wall.

Denna looked away. I saw her do it.

But Wren did not look away. She stood in front of me with her serious pointed face, and she listened, and she frowned. And then she did something no one had done, by the fire, weeks before. She began to hum.

She did not hum well. She was nine, and tone-deaf, and she did not know the chords I was playing. She hummed an old nursery tune instead, a farm song about a goose that would not lay, flat and wandering and nothing like what my hands were doing. But she hummed it right at me, standing between my knees with her small hands on the edge of the table. As if she were trying to show my hands where to go.

And my hands followed her.

Not well. They stumbled, trying to find her wandering tune. They missed. They found it. They lost it again. It was not clean anymore. It was not correct. It was clumsy and slow and a little out of tune, because the lute was cheap and I had not tuned it.

And for one chord—only one—in the middle of a farm song about a stubborn goose, it was not nothing.

It was a minor chord. A sad one. It came out of the cracked soundboard soft and hesitant in the quiet room, and there was something in it. Not much. A little warmth, at the very bottom. Like the last coal in a banked fire.

Wren stopped humming. She smiled.

"See?" she said. "They remember."

---

I did not feel anything. I want to be honest about that. I did not feel joy, or grief, or relief. I sat at the table in the farmhouse with the lute in my hands and I watched my fingers moving on the strings as if they were someone else's fingers. As if I were watching a stranger play, from very far away, through a grey fog.

But Denna was crying. I saw that. She was sitting beside me at the table with her hands pressed to her mouth, and tears were running down her face, and she did not make a sound.

And Bast was staring at my hands with an expression of such naked hope that it was painful to look at.

And Horrin had put his arm around his wife, and she was crying too, a little. For her husband's brother, I think. Who had not been able to do this.

And Wren just stood there, with her serious grey eyes, and watched my hands. And when they stopped, at last, and lay still on the strings, she nodded, very solemnly, as if something had been settled.

"You should play every day," she said. "So they don't forget again. Uncle Fen didn't. That was his mistake."

---

We left in the morning. The rain had stopped. The hills were green and wet and shining in the early light.

Wren came out to the cart as Bast was hitching the horse. She was carrying something wrapped in a scrap of cloth. She held it up to me, where I sat in the back of the cart.

"It's Uncle Fen's," she said. "His fiddle bow. He broke the fiddle, after. When he couldn't play. But he didn't break the bow." She pushed it into my hands. "I kept it. I thought someone might need it."

I looked down at it. A fiddle bow, old and worn, with the horsehair gone grey and slack. It was no use to me. I did not play the fiddle.

"Thank you," said Denna, beside me. Her voice was thick. "Thank you, Wren."

"It's not for you," said Wren. "It's for him." She looked at me very seriously. "For when you forget. You can hold it, and remember that somebody else couldn't. And you can."

---

I kept the bow. I do not know why. It went into the cart, and then into my pack, and then, when we came to Newarre, into a drawer upstairs at the Waystone, where it lay for seven years with a few other small things I could not explain keeping.

I did not play every day. I did not play at all, after that night. Not for seven years. Wren would have been disappointed in me.

But my hands did not forget. She was right about that. In all those grey years, behind the bar, polishing the same glasses over and over, my fingers never lost their calluses. They moved on their own sometimes, late at night, on the wood of the bar. Chords. Sad and slow. Going nowhere.

Remembering where they were.

---

"You still have it?" said Chronicler. "The bow?"

"Upstairs." The innkeeper looked at his hands. "In the drawer. With Kilvin's candle." He was quiet for a moment. "I took it out, the night Shep died. After Aaron. After I tried to name the thing and there was nothing there." He turned his hands over, palm up, and looked at the calluses on his fingertips. "I sat on my bed and held it all night. And I thought about a little girl in a farmhouse, who said somebody else couldn't. And you can."

"Did it help?"

The innkeeper did not answer for a long time.

"I'm here," he said at last. "Telling you this. With my hands on the table." He almost smiled. "I think that's what she'd call helping."
