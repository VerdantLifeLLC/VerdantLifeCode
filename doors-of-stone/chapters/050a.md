# Interlude: What Bast Remembered

"Tell him about the first year," said the innkeeper.

He had gone behind the bar to draw a jug of cider for the table. He said it over his shoulder, without turning around. Bast, in his backward chair, went very still.

"Reshi?"

"The first year. Here. At the Waystone." The innkeeper came back to the table with the jug and three cups. "I don't remember it well. I was very grey, that year. I remember working. I remember polishing things. I don't remember much else." He poured. "You remember it better than I do. Tell him."

Bast looked at him for a long moment. Then he looked at Chronicler.

"You want to hear it?" he said. "From me?"

Chronicler hesitated. He glanced at the innkeeper, who had sat down and wrapped his hands around his cup and was looking at the fire.

"Yes," said Chronicler. "If you'll tell it."

---

Bast was quiet for a long time. When he spoke, his voice was different. Quieter. Less sly.

"He didn't talk," he said. "That's the first thing. For the first three months, he didn't say anything that wasn't necessary. *Pass the hammer. The roof's leaking. Supper's ready.* That's all." He looked at the innkeeper. "You used to talk all the time, Reshi. From the moment you woke up to the moment you slept. Stories and songs and arguments and jokes. You talked in your sleep, some nights. And then you just—stopped."

The innkeeper did not say anything. He looked at the fire.

"He worked," said Bast. "All day. Every day. From before dawn until long after dark. He fixed the roof. He fixed the chimney. He scrubbed every inch of this place, twice. He never stopped. Not once. I'd come down in the morning and he'd already be at it, and I'd go to bed at night and he'd still be at it." Bast looked down at his hands. "I thought, at first, he was trying to make the inn nice. Then I realized he wasn't. He was trying not to stop. Because if he stopped, he'd have to sit still. And if he sat still, he'd feel it."

"Feel what?" said Chronicler softly.

"The hole," said Bast. "Where his name used to be."

---

"I tried everything," said Bast. "That first year. To bring him back. I played him songs; I'm terrible at playing, but I tried. I told him stories about the things he'd done. I brought him things. Flowers, and good wine, and once a lute I bought off a traveling bard for three talents." He laughed softly, without humor. "He thanked me very politely and put it in the cupboard under the stairs. It's still there."

The innkeeper's hands tightened, very slightly, on his cup.

"I tried being cruel, too," said Bast. "When being kind didn't work. I shouted at him. I told him he was a coward, hiding in an inn in the middle of nowhere. I told him Denna would be ashamed of him." He looked at the innkeeper. "I'm sorry, Reshi. I said terrible things. You just looked at me. Like you didn't understand what I was saying. Like I was shouting at a wall."

"I didn't understand," said the innkeeper quietly. "I remember now. I didn't understand any of it."

"I know." Bast's voice cracked. "I know. That was the worst part."

---

"And then," said Bast, "at the end of the first winter, something happened."

He looked at the innkeeper. The innkeeper looked back at him. Something passed between them.

"Tell him," said the innkeeper.

Bast was quiet for a moment.

"There was a storm," he said. "A bad one. The worst anyone in Newarre could remember. Snow for three days, and wind, and cold so hard the trees cracked in the night. Nobody could get to the inn. We were alone here, the two of us, with the snow up to the windows."

"On the third night, the wind was very loud. Howling around the inn like a pack of wolves. I couldn't sleep. I came down to the common room, and he was sitting by the fire, the way he did most nights. Not doing anything. Just sitting. Looking at the fire." Bast was quiet. "And I sat down with him. And we sat there for a long time. With the wind howling."

"And then?"

"And then the wind said something," said Bast.

Chronicler looked at him.

"Not in words," said Bast. "The wind doesn't talk in words. But I'm Fae, and I can hear things mortals can't. And I heard it. The wind, howling around the inn, all night. And in the middle of the howling, it said something." He looked at the innkeeper. "It said his name."

The common room was very still.

"His real name," said Bast. "The one in the chest. The wind knew it. It had known it since he was fifteen, since the day he called it at the University. And it had been looking for him. For a year. All over the world. And it found him, here, in a snowstorm. And it said his name."

---

"And I looked at him," said Bast. "Sitting by the fire. And he'd gone very still. Stiller than usual. And he turned his head. Toward the window. Toward the wind." Bast's voice was very quiet. "And he said, *Hello.*"

Chronicler did not breathe.

"Just that," said Bast. "*Hello.* To the wind. Very softly. The way you say hello to someone you haven't seen in a long time, and have almost forgotten, but not quite." He looked down at his hands. "And the wind stopped. All at once. Right in the middle of the storm. Like someone had shut a door. And the snow stopped falling. And the whole world went quiet."

"What happened?"

"Nothing." Bast looked up. "Nothing happened. He sat there for a moment, looking at the window. And then he looked back at the fire. And his face was just the same as before. Grey. Empty. And he didn't say anything else. And after a while, the wind started up again. Not as loud. Just an ordinary wind. And in the morning the storm had passed, and the sun was out, and he got up and went to work, the way he always did."

"Did he remember?"

"No." Bast shook his head. "I asked him, the next day. He didn't know what I was talking about." He looked at the innkeeper. "Do you remember now, Reshi?"

The innkeeper was quiet for a long time.

"Yes," he said at last. "I remember now. The story brought it back." He looked at the fire. "It was the only time, in seven years. The only time anything reached me." He was quiet for a moment. "The wind came all that way. Looking. And I said hello. And then I let it go."

"Why?"

The innkeeper looked at Bast.

"Because if I'd answered it," he said quietly, "really answered it, the way I used to, it would have been loud. And the waystone couldn't have drowned it out. And something would have heard." He was quiet for a moment. "So I said hello. And then I told it to go away. Very gently. The way you'd tell a dog to go home."

"You told it to go away?"

"Not in words." The innkeeper smiled faintly. "The wind doesn't talk in words either. But it understood." He looked at the window. "It's been very polite, ever since. It comes by, sometimes. On stormy nights. It howls around the inn for a while, and then it goes away. It never says my name again." He paused. "I think it's been waiting too. Like the rest of us."

---

Nobody said anything for a long time.

Then Bast wiped his eyes with the back of his hand.

"That was the night I knew," he said. "That he was still in there. Somewhere. Under all of it. That he wasn't gone, just—put away." He looked at Chronicler. "That was the night I started planning."

"Planning what?"

Bast looked at the innkeeper. The innkeeper looked back at him.

"Later," said Bast. "I'll tell you later. It's not time yet."

The innkeeper was quiet for a long moment. Then he nodded, very slightly.

"Later," he agreed.

He picked up his cup. He looked at Chronicler.

"Where was I?" he said.
