# Imre Again

Being expelled from the University does not mean being banished from Imre. That is a distinction that matters a great deal to students, and not at all to anyone else.

The University and Imre stand on opposite banks of the Omethi, connected by the great stone bridge. They are two towns, with two sets of laws and two sets of magistrates. The masters of the University can expel a student from the University's grounds, bar him from the Archives and the Fishery and the Medica, strike his name from the rolls of the Arcanum. But they cannot stop him from walking across the bridge and buying a pie in Imre. They cannot stop him from sitting in a tavern in Imre, or sleeping in an inn in Imre, or playing his lute in the Eolian.

I had known that when I left. I had thought about it on the road south, and in Severen, and in Renere. I had told myself that it did not matter, because I would not be coming back.

But I came back. Of course I did.

---

I walked off the *Wind's Daughter* at the docks on the Imre side of the river on a warm grey morning in late summer. Hessa Tern stood at the top of the gangplank and watched me go. She did not say goodbye. But as I reached the bottom, she called after me.

"Pretty boy!"

I turned.

"If you ever need a ship," she said, "you come find the *Daughter*. You hear me? You come find me. Doesn't matter where you're going or who's chasing you. You come find me."

I looked up at her. At her fist of a face, and her weathered hands on the rail, and the ugly old ship behind her with its patched sails.

"I will," I said.

She nodded once, sharply, and turned away. And I walked up from the docks into the streets of Imre, and I did not look back.

---

The first person I saw was Devi.

It was not planned. I did not go looking for her. I was walking up the street toward the Eolian, with my lute on my back and my pack over my shoulder, thinking about nothing in particular, and I came around a corner and there she was, standing outside an apothecary's shop with a basket on her arm.

She saw me. She stopped. Her mouth fell open.

Then she dropped the basket and ran at me and threw her arms around my neck so hard that I staggered backward and nearly fell.

"You *idiot*," she said into my shoulder. "You stupid, stupid *idiot*. Do you know what they're saying about you? Do you have any idea?"

"Some idea."

"They're saying you killed the king of Vintas!" She pulled back and glared at me. Her eyes were wet behind her spectacles. "They're saying you sang him to death! They're saying you're a demon and a witch and a murderer and there's a bounty on your head of five hundred royals! *Five hundred!*"

"That much?"

"Don't you *dare* be flattered." She hit me on the arm, hard. "Don't you dare."

"I'm not flattered."

"You are. I can see it. You're preening." She hit me again. Then she threw her arms around me again, and held on, and I felt her shaking. "I thought you were dead," she whispered. "When we heard. We all thought you were dead."

I held her. I did not know what else to do.

"I'm not dead," I said. "Not yet."

---

She took me to her rooms above the butcher's shop. She made me climb the treacherous stairs, and then she made me sit down in a chair, and then she made me drink a cup of something hot and bitter that she said would put the color back in my face. Then she sat down across from me and folded her arms and said, "Tell me."

I told her. Some of it. Enough.

When I was done, she was quiet for a long time.

"You're going to sing it," she said. "The true song. At the Eolian."

"Yes."

"And the Chandrian are going to come."

"Yes."

"And you're going to keep singing, no matter what happens, until the song is done."

"Yes."

She looked at me for a long moment. Then she took off her spectacles and rubbed her eyes.

"You know," she said, "when I first met you, I thought you were the most interesting person I'd ever lent money to. I thought you'd do something spectacular someday, and I'd get to watch." She put her spectacles back on. "I didn't think it'd be *this* spectacular."

"Neither did I."

"When?"

"I don't know yet. Soon. I need to finish the song. And I need to talk to Stanchion, and Deoch. And I need—" I hesitated. "I need help, Devi."

She raised an eyebrow. "From me?"

"From everyone."

She looked at me for a long moment. Then she smiled her spider's smile.

"Well," she said. "I did tell you you'd need something from me before the year was out."

---

Sim and Wil came that evening. Devi sent for them, by some means I never discovered, and they came up the treacherous stairs at dusk, and when they saw me, they stopped in the doorway and stared.

Sim recovered first. He came across the room and grabbed me by the shoulders and looked at me, and his face did something complicated. Then he pulled me into a hug so tight I could not breathe.

"You're alive," he said. "You're alive, you're alive, you're *alive*."

"I'm alive."

"We heard—"

"I know what you heard."

"Is it true? Did you—"

"No," I said. "I didn't kill the king. I tried to save him."

Sim pulled back and looked at me. His eyes were very bright. "I knew it," he said. "I *knew* it. I told everyone. I told Wil, I told Fela, I told everyone who'd listen. I said, Kvothe would never do that. Never. Not in a thousand years."

"Thank you, Sim."

Wil had come into the room behind him. He stood by the door with his hands in his pockets and his dark face unreadable. When Sim finally let go of me, Wil came forward and looked at me for a long moment.

"You look terrible," he said.

"I've had a difficult summer."

"Hm." He was quiet for a moment. Then he held out his hand. When I took it, he pulled me into a brief hard embrace, the way he had the day I left, and then stepped back again. "You still owe me three drinks."

I laughed. It came out of me before I could stop it, a sudden startled laugh, and it felt strange in my throat, as if I had forgotten how.

"I'll pay you back," I said. "Someday."

"You'd better." Wil looked at Devi. "Well? What's the plan?"

Devi looked at me. I looked at Sim and Wil, standing there in her cluttered room, their faces full of worry and loyalty and something I did not have a name for. The kind of look that says, *whatever it is, we're with you*.

I thought about what Skarpi had told me. *They'll kill everyone around you. Everyone you love.*

I thought about telling them to go. To leave. To get as far away from me as they could, and never come back.

And then I thought about the Eolian, full of people. About the Chandrian coming. About needing friends who knew what was coming, and were ready to act.

"Sit down," I said. "All of you. I'm going to tell you a story. And then I'm going to ask you to do something very dangerous."

---

I told them.

I told them about Skarpi, and the true song, and the Singers. I told them about Denna's song, and the curse on Lanre, and what would happen if it broke. I did not tell them about the door, or the line, or what I was. That was too much. That was a burden I did not want to put on them.

But I told them enough. And when I was done, I told them what I needed.

"I'm going to sing the song at the Eolian," I said. "Soon. As soon as it's finished. And when I sing it, the Chandrian will come. I don't know how many of them. I don't know what they'll do. But they'll come, and they'll want to stop me."

"Then don't sing it," said Sim. His face was white. "Kvothe, if you know they're coming—"

"I have to sing it. If I don't, the other song wins. And then worse things happen."

"What worse things?"

"Things I can't explain. Not right now." I looked at him. "Please, Sim. I need you to trust me."

Sim looked at me for a long time. Then he swallowed and nodded.

"What do you need?" said Wil.

"I need the Eolian to be ready," I said. "I need people who can get everyone out, fast, when things go wrong. I need the doors to be open, all of them. I need water, lots of water, in case of fire." I paused. "And I need someone who can do sympathy. Good sympathy. Fast. Someone who can hold a binding while everything around them is falling apart."

"I can do that," said Devi.

"So can I," said Wil.

"And me," said Sim, though his voice was shaking.

"And one more thing." I looked at Devi. "Fela. I need Fela."

Devi raised an eyebrow. "Why Fela?"

"Because she knows stone," I said. "And the Eolian is made of stone."

---

Fela came the next day.

She came up Devi's treacherous stairs in the afternoon, in a plain grey dress with her dark hair braided, and when she saw me, she stopped in the doorway, the way Sim and Wil had. But she did not run at me or throw her arms around me. She only stood there, looking at me, with her hand pressed flat against the doorframe.

"You came back," she said.

"I came back."

"Did you need it? The stone eye?"

I reached into my pocket and took it out. The little carved eye, smooth and cool, saying its long slow no. I held it up.

"Not yet," I said. "But I think I will."

She looked at it for a long moment. Then she came into the room and sat down across from me.

"Tell me," she said.

I told her. When I was done, she was silent for a long time. Her hands were folded in her lap, and she was looking down at them.

"You want me to hold the Eolian," she said at last. "If they come. If they try to bring it down. You want me to ask the stone to stand."

"Yes."

"I don't know if I can. It's a very big building. And stone doesn't like to be asked for things." She looked up at me. "And if there's fire—real fire, the kind they bring—stone cracks. It can't help it. It's not saying no to fire. It's saying no to change. And fire changes everything."

"I know. I'm asking anyway."

She looked at me with her dark serious eyes.

"You're asking a lot," she said.

"I know."

"You're asking us to risk our lives."

"I know."

"For a song."

"For the true song," I said. "For every song that's ever been sung true. For everyone who died so it could be sung." I looked at her. "Fela, I know it's too much. I know I shouldn't ask. But I don't have anyone else."

She was quiet for a long time. Then she reached out and took the stone eye from my hand, and held it, and closed her fingers around it.

"All right," she said. "I'll hold it. As long as I can." She gave the eye back to me. "But you'd better sing well, Kvothe. You'd better sing better than you've ever sung in your life."

"I will."

"Promise me."

"I promise."

She smiled faintly.

"You always say that," she said. "And then you do something mad instead."

"This time I'll do both."

She laughed. It was a small, surprised laugh, and it made her look younger.

"That," she said, "I believe."

---

I stayed with Devi. She had a small room at the back of her apartment, barely more than a closet, with a narrow cot and a window that looked out over the rooftops of Imre. She gave it to me without asking and without charging, which was so unlike Devi that I almost worried she was ill.

"Don't read anything into it," she said, when I tried to thank her. "It's an investment. If you survive this, you'll be famous. And famous people pay their debts." She smiled her spider's smile. "Eventually."

I spent the next nine days finishing the song.

I worked on it day and night, in Devi's little back room, with the window open and the sounds of Imre drifting in. I worked on it at the table in Devi's main room while she worked at her alchemical bench and pretended not to listen. I worked on it on the roof, at night, under the stars. I worked on it until my fingers bled and my voice cracked and my eyes burned from lack of sleep.

Threpe came, the third day. Devi had sent for him too; she seemed to know everyone. He burst into her rooms in a coat of such a violent shade of purple that it made my eyes water, and he threw his arms around me and wept and laughed and said he had known, he had always known, that I would come back and do something magnificent. He brought me paper, and ink, and a bottle of very good wine, and a stack of old books and scrolls from the private libraries of his friends, full of fragments of old songs and old stories that I had never seen before.

"It's everything I could find," he said. "Everything about Lanre, and Selitos, and the fall of the white city. Some of it's in languages I can't read. Some of it's probably nonsense. But it's all yours." He looked at me with shining eyes. "Is it true? Are you really going to sing the true story? At the Eolian?"

"Yes."

"When?"

"Soon."

He clapped his hands. "Oh, I'll be there. I'll be in the front row. I'll bring everyone. Everyone who matters." His face fell slightly. "Is it true there's a bounty on your head?"

"Yes."

"Five hundred royals?"

"So I'm told."

"Hm." Threpe looked thoughtful. "Well. I suppose we'll have to make sure no one collects it." He smiled brightly. "I'll bring my guards too."

---

On the ninth night, I finished.

It was very late, well past midnight. I was sitting on Devi's roof, under the stars, with my lute in my lap and a sheaf of paper beside me, weighted down with a loose slate. I played the last verse. I sang the last line. And then I sat there in the silence, with my hands resting on the strings, and I knew it was done.

It was not perfect. No song ever is. There were places where the words did not quite fit the melody, and places where the melody did not quite carry the words. There were pieces of the story I had left out, because they were too long or too strange or too terrible to put into a song. There were pieces I had changed, a little, to make them fit.

But it was true. At the bottom, where the truth lives, it was true. It was the fall of Myr Tariniel, and the betrayal at Drossen Tor, and Lyra, and Lanre's grief, and Selitos weeping among the ruins. It was the Thief and his stolen moon and the war that followed. It was all of it, the whole of it, as true as I could make it.

I called it "Tariniel." After the white city. After the children in the fountains.

I sat on the roof for a long time after I finished, looking up at the stars. They were very bright, and very far away, and very cold.

Somewhere out there, Denna was singing her song. In some great hall in Vintas, before some lord and his court, with her harp in her lap and a knot on her wrist. Singing a beautiful lie about a man who was wronged.

And soon, very soon, I would sing mine. And we would see which one was stronger.

I did not light Kilvin's candle that night. I did not want to know what color it would burn.
