# Haven

Elodin came for me on the fourth day after the fire.

I was still in Merra's house behind the ruins of the Eolian. I had not left it since that night. Threpe's men stood guard at the door, day and night, and a crowd of people still gathered in the street outside, though fewer each day. Some of them left gifts on the step. Some of them simply stood and looked at the house, and at the blackened walls of the Eolian across the street, and went away again.

Fela was awake by then, and sitting up, though she was very weak. Wil sat with her every day, his bandaged hands resting in his lap. They did not talk much. They did not seem to need to.

Sim came every morning with bread and news. The news was always bad. The war in Vintas was spreading. Ambrose's army had crossed the river at Tiller's Ford and was marching south through the green hills toward Severen. Towns were burning. Refugees were streaming north into the Commonwealth. The council of Imre was arguing about whether to recognize Ambrose as king, and the argument was very loud and very long and seemed likely to go on forever.

And the bounty on my head had doubled.

"A thousand royals," Sim said, on the third morning. His face was white. "Ambrose raised it himself. There are posters all over Imre. They've got a drawing of you." He hesitated. "It's not a very good drawing. They made your nose too big."

"That's a relief."

"Kvothe, this isn't funny."

"I know." I looked out the window at the blackened walls. "But if I don't laugh, Sim, I'll do something else. And I'd rather laugh."

---

Elodin came in the afternoon. He did not knock. He simply appeared in the doorway of my room, the way he always did, leaning against the frame with his arms folded.

"You look terrible," he said.

"Everyone keeps telling me that."

"Everyone keeps being right." He came into the room and looked at my arm. I had it resting on a pillow; it still would not move properly from the elbow down. The white hand-shaped mark on my wrist had not faded at all. "Let me see."

I held it out. He took it in both of his and turned it over, very gently. He looked at the mark for a long time. He pressed his thumb against the white skin. I did not feel it.

"Cinder," he said.

"Yes."

He was quiet for a long moment. Then he let go.

"It'll heal," he said. "Mostly. It'll take time. Months. Maybe a year. You'll get the feeling back, a little at a time." He looked at me. "But it'll always be cold. That hand. Always. Even in summer. Even by a fire. That's what his touch does. It leaves a little of the winter behind."

I looked down at my wrist. At the pale print of a hand on my skin.

"Can I still play?"

Elodin looked at me for a long time.

"I don't know," he said. "Ask me in a year."

---

He did not stay long. He looked in on Fela, and spoke to her quietly for a while, and she wept a little and he held her hand. He looked at Wil's hands and shook his head and said something in a language I did not know that made Wil's face go very still.

Then he came back to my room and stood in the doorway again.

"Get your cloak," he said. "We're going for a walk."

"Threpe's men won't let me leave."

"Threpe's men will do what I tell them." He smiled faintly. "I can be very persuasive, when I want to be."

"Where are we going?"

He looked at me for a long moment. Something moved in his dark eyes.

"You asked me a question," he said. "On the roof. Before the song. You asked me what happened to me." He paused. "I said I'd tell you after, if you were still yourself." He looked at me. "Are you still yourself?"

I thought about it. I thought about lying on the stage in the dark, with the fire roaring above me and my name gone. I thought about the knot around my neck.

"Mostly," I said.

"Good enough." He turned away. "Get your cloak."

---

Threpe's men did let me leave. I do not know what Elodin said to them. He spoke to the captain for a moment at the door, very quietly, and the captain's face went pale, and then he stepped aside and did not look at either of us as we passed.

Elodin led me across Imre and over the stone bridge, and at the top of the arch he stopped, as I always did, and looked out over the river. The water was low and brown in the late summer heat. The trees along the banks were dusty green.

"You can't go onto the University grounds," he said. "You know that. You've been expelled."

"I know."

"But you're with me. And I'm a master. And masters can bring guests." He smiled. "It's a very old rule. Most of the other masters have forgotten it exists."

He led me down the far side of the bridge and onto the grounds of the University. It was strange, walking there again. Everything was the same, and everything was different. The same grey buildings. The same courtyards. The same students hurrying to class with their books under their arms. But they stopped when they saw me. They stared. Some of them whispered. Some of them backed away.

I pulled my hood up over my hair. It did not help much.

Elodin did not seem to notice. He walked across the courtyard with his hands in his pockets, whistling, as if he were taking a stroll on a sunny afternoon. He led me past the Mains, and the Archives, and the Fishery. He led me to the edge of the University grounds, where a high wall of old grey stone ran along the northern boundary.

There was a gate in the wall. An iron gate, very old and very heavy, with bars as thick as my wrist. Beyond it, I could see a long low building of grey stone, with small barred windows and a slate roof.

I knew what it was. Everyone at the University knew.

It was the Rookery. Not the one where Elodin had his office, but the other one. The old one. The one they called Haven.

It was where the University kept its madmen.

---

"I lived here," Elodin said. "For three years."

We were standing at the gate, looking through the bars. The building beyond was quiet. There was a small garden in front of it, neatly kept, with gravel paths and flowerbeds full of late summer roses. A man in a grey robe was walking slowly along one of the paths, his head bowed, his lips moving. He did not look up.

"I know," I said.

"Do you know why?"

"Everyone says you went mad."

"Everyone is right." Elodin looked through the bars at the man in the grey robe. "But do you know why I went mad?"

"No."

He was quiet for a long time.

"I was younger than you are now," he said at last. "The youngest student ever admitted to the University. Did you know that? Fourteen years old. They called me a prodigy. They said I was the most gifted namer in a hundred years." He smiled faintly. "I believed them. Why wouldn't I? I could hear names the way other people hear music. Wind, fire, water, stone. They came to me as easily as breathing. By the time I was fifteen, I was a Re'lar. By sixteen, an El'the. By seventeen, I had learned more names than any living master."

"What happened?"

"I got curious." He shrugged. "Like you. I wanted to know everything. Every name. Every secret. And I found out that there was a kind of naming that no one at the University would teach. That no one even talked about. A kind of naming so dangerous that the masters had forbidden it, centuries ago, and written it out of the books." He looked at me. "Can you guess what it was?"

I thought about it. I thought about the knots, and the king's hair, and what Elodin had said on the roof. *Some of them forgot who they were.*

"Changing names," I said slowly. "Not just learning them. Changing them."

Elodin nodded. "Changing them. Taking a thing's true name and making it into something else. Something new." He looked through the bars. "Shaping, the old stories called it. The thing the Thief did, and the shapers who followed him. The thing that started the war."

"You tried it."

"I tried it." His voice was very quiet. "I was seventeen, and brilliant, and stupid, and I thought I could do anything. And I thought, what's the harm? I'll start small. I'll change something small. Something no one will miss." He was silent for a moment. "So I started with myself."

---

I stared at him.

"You changed your own name."

"I tried to." He leaned his forehead against the bars of the gate. "I didn't like my name. I thought it was too small. Too ordinary. I thought I could make it bigger. Better. Wiser. Stronger." He laughed softly. "I thought I could make myself into something more than I was."

"What happened?"

He did not answer for a long time. When he did, his voice was very far away.

"I broke," he said. "Not all at once. Slowly. A little at a time. The name I was trying to make didn't fit. It was too big, or the wrong shape, or something. It pushed at me from the inside, like a foot in a shoe that's too small. And the old name, my real name, didn't want to go. It held on. It fought." He closed his eyes. "They tore me in half, the two of them. The old name and the new. They pulled me apart between them. And for a long time, I didn't know which one I was. Or if I was either. Or anything at all."

"Three years," I said.

"Three years in there." He opened his eyes and looked through the bars at the long grey building. "Three years not knowing my own name. Not knowing what I was. Talking to walls. Screaming at night. Eating what they gave me and sleeping where they put me and understanding nothing." He was quiet for a moment. "They thought I'd never come out. The masters. They'd given up on me. Written me off. Another brilliant boy who flew too close to the sun."

"But you did come out."

"I did." He smiled, a small, strange, broken smile. "Do you want to know how?"

"Yes."

"I stopped trying." He looked at me. "I stopped trying to be the new name. And I stopped trying to be the old one. I stopped trying to be anything at all. I just—let go. Of both of them. And sat in the dark, with nothing. No name, no self, nothing." He was quiet for a moment. "And after a long time, something came back. Not the old name. Not the new one. Something else. Something in between. Something that had grown, on its own, in the empty space where the others had been."

"Your name now."

"My name now." He nodded. "It's not the name I was born with. It's not the name I tried to make. It's the name I *became*, when I stopped trying to be anything." He smiled. "It's a strange name. Crooked. A little broken. It doesn't fit quite right, and it never will. But it's mine."

---

We stood at the gate for a long time without speaking. The man in the grey robe had reached the end of the garden path and turned around and was walking back, slowly, his lips still moving. A bird was singing somewhere in the roses.

"Why are you telling me this?" I asked at last.

Elodin did not answer right away. He was looking through the bars at the man in the grey robe.

"Because I saw you," he said. "On the stage. At the end of the song." He turned to look at me. "I was there, you know. In the crowd. At the back, by the south door. I saw everything."

"You were there?"

"Of course I was there. Did you think I'd miss it?" He smiled faintly. Then his smile faded. "I saw the song go through you. I saw it fill you up. And I saw your name go out of you, at the end. Just for a moment." He looked at me. "I saw you lying on the stage, empty. And I thought, *that's it. He's gone. Just like I was.*"

"But I came back."

"You came back." He looked at the knot on its thread, where it showed at the neck of my shirt. "You held onto something small. Like I told you." He paused. "But next time you might not."

"Next time?"

"There'll be a next time. You know there will." He turned back to the gate. "You're a namer, Kvothe. A true one. The first I've seen in a very long time. And you're going to do things with names that no one has done in a thousand years. Big things. Dangerous things." He was quiet for a moment. "And one day, you're going to come to a place where the only way forward is to do what I did. To change a name. Maybe your own."

I did not say anything. I did not know what to say.

"When that day comes," said Elodin, "I want you to remember this. This gate. That building. That man, walking in the garden, talking to himself." He nodded at the man in the grey robe. "His name is Tamlin. He was a student here, thirty years ago. Brilliant. Like me. Like you. He tried to change the name of the moon."

I stared at the man. At his bowed head and his moving lips.

"He's been in there for thirty years," said Elodin quietly. "He'll be in there until he dies. He doesn't remember his own name. He doesn't remember anything. He just walks in the garden and talks to the moon." He looked at me. "That's what it costs, Kvothe. When it goes wrong. That's what it costs."

---

He walked me back to Merra's house as the sun was setting. At the door, he stopped and looked at me for a long time.

"One more thing," he said. "About names."

"Yes?"

"A name can be put down." He said it slowly, carefully, as if choosing each word. "Not changed. Not broken. Put down. Set aside. The way you set down a heavy pack at the end of a long road." He paused. "It's very rare. Very difficult. I've only heard of it being done a handful of times, in all the old books. But it can be done."

"How?"

"You need someone to hold it for you." Elodin looked at me. "You can't just drop it. It has to go somewhere. It has to be kept. By someone you trust, in something that will hold." He was quiet for a moment. "And while it's put down, you're—less. Not empty, like Tamlin. Not broken, like I was. Just less. A shadow of what you were. You can't name. You can't do much of anything, really. You're just a person, walking around, without the thing that makes you *you*."

"Why would anyone do that?"

"To hide," said Elodin. "Or to protect someone. Or to keep a name safe from someone who wants to use it." He looked at me. "Or to stop being something you don't want to be anymore. Just for a while. Until you're ready to pick it up again."

I looked at him for a long time.

"Why are you telling me this?" I asked again.

Elodin smiled. It was a very strange smile. Sad and fond and a little bitter.

"Because I have a feeling," he said, "that someday you're going to need to know."

He turned and walked away down the street, with his hands in his pockets, whistling. He did not look back.

I stood in the doorway of Merra's house, in the red light of the setting sun, and I watched him go.

*A name can be put down*, he had said. *You need someone to hold it for you.*

I touched the knot around my neck. The one Denna had given me at the Grey Man. *Road. Promise. A story told many times, and changes in the telling.*

And I did not know, then, what I was thinking. I did not know that I had just been told the shape of my own ending.

But some part of me did. Some deep, quiet part, down at the bottom, where the truth lives.

And it began, very quietly, to make a plan.
