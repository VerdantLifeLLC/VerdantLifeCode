# Kvothe

The sword did not land.

Chronicler saw it. He would tell it afterward, many times, to many people, and none of them would believe him. But he saw it. The pale sword coming down, fast as a falling star, toward the innkeeper's bowed neck. And then stopping.

Not stopped. Not blocked. Stopping. In the air, a hand's breadth above the innkeeper's red hair. As if it had struck something. Some invisible thing. Some wall.

Cinder's face twisted. He pressed down on the sword with both hands. It did not move.

"*No*," he said. His soft reasonable voice was not soft anymore. "*No. He said I could not kill you. He never said I could not stop you.*"

The innkeeper did not look up. He was kneeling in front of the open chest with the knotted cord in his hands. His head was bowed over it. His lips were moving.

"It's not me," he said quietly. "It's not my doing. It's the name." He did not look up. "It's been locked away for seven years. It's very angry. And it knows you." He was quiet for a moment. "It remembers your hand on my wrist. At the Eolian. In the burning camp. It remembers Tempi. It remembers Stanchion." He looked up, at last, at the pale sword hanging in the air above him. "It's not going to let you touch me again."

---

He looked down at the cord in his hands.

His fingers were moving on it. Slowly. Carefully. Finding the knots, one by one. Looping and twisting and undoing.

"She taught me," he said, very softly, as if he were talking to himself. "In the Grey Man. The first time. I forgot, for a long time. But the story brought it back." His fingers found a knot and loosened it. "This one says *road*. And this one says *promise*. And this one—" His fingers stopped. "This one says my name."

He began to untie it.

---

Chronicler would try, afterward, to describe what happened next. He would try in taverns, and in the great halls of lords, and in the quiet of his own rooms, late at night, with a pen in his hand and a blank page in front of him. He would try for the rest of his life.

He never succeeded. There were no words. There are no words.

But he saw it. He was sitting on the narrow bed in the innkeeper's room, with the pages of the story scattered around his feet, and he saw it.

The cord came undone.

Knot by knot. Loop by loop. The innkeeper's fingers moved through it, quickly now, surely, the way a musician's fingers move through a song he has known all his life. And as each knot came undone, something came out of it.

Light.

A thread of silver light. Very thin. Very bright. It came out of the loosened knots and hung in the frozen air above the chest, trembling, shining, getting longer and longer as each knot came undone. It was the most beautiful thing Chronicler had ever seen. It was like moonlight on water. It was like the last note of a song. It was like a name, spoken aloud for the first time in a very long time.

And it was moving toward the innkeeper.

Slowly. Gently. The way water moves toward the sea. The silver thread drifted through the cold air toward the kneeling man, toward his chest, toward the place over his heart. And where it touched him, it went in.

---

The innkeeper gasped.

It was not a sound of pain. Or not only pain. It was the sound a man makes when he comes up out of deep water, after holding his breath for a very long time. It was the sound of the first breath.

His back arched. His head went back. His hands, still full of the knotted cord, clenched. And the silver thread of his name poured into him, faster now, a river of light, going into his chest and filling him the way water fills a cup.

And the color came back.

Chronicler saw it. He saw it come back into the room. Into the world. It had been grey, all of it, since Cinder came in. Grey frost, and grey light, and grey faces. He had not noticed. He had thought it was only the blue candles, the cold. But it was not. It was the innkeeper. The emptiness in him had been so large that it had pressed on everything around it, and made it grey.

And now it was filling. And the color was coming back.

The red of the innkeeper's hair. Red, true red, the color of a flame that has been burning for a long time and means to burn for a long while yet. The green of his eyes. The dark reddish black of the roah wood. The silver of the thread, pouring into him. The gold of the lute's ivory pegs, catching the light.

And the fire.

---

It came up out of him like a wave.

Chronicler felt it. Even sitting on the bed, a pace away, he felt it. Heat. Real heat. The first real heat he had felt since the frost came. It came off the innkeeper the way cold came off Cinder, in waves, like heat off a forge.

The frost on the floor around him began to melt.

It went from white to grey to clear, and then it was water, spreading out across the boards in a widening circle. The frost on the walls began to drip. The frost on the shutters. The ice on the washstand cracked and slid.

And the pale sword, hanging in the air above the innkeeper's head, began to smoke.

Cinder cried out. He let go of it. He stepped back, fast, with his hands held up in front of him, and Chronicler saw that the palms of his hands were red. Blistered. Burned.

The pale sword fell. It struck the floor beside the chest with a sound like breaking glass. Where it landed, the water hissed.

---

The last knot came undone.

The last of the silver thread poured out of it and into the innkeeper's chest. And the cord in his hands was only a cord now. A length of dark braided hair. Empty.

He knelt very still in front of the open chest, with his head bowed and the empty cord in his hands.

The room was silent. Utterly silent. The frost was gone. The water was dripping. The blue light of Cinder's cold was fading, flickering, guttering, like a candle in a draft.

Then the innkeeper lifted his head.

And Chronicler looked at him, and saw a stranger.

---

It was the same face. The same red hair and green eyes, the same bruises on the jaw and the same scar on the cheek. Nothing about it had changed.

And everything had changed.

It was like the difference between a painting of a fire and a fire. The same shape. The same colors. But one of them was only paint on a wall, and the other was burning.

He was burning.

Not with flame. Not with anything Chronicler could see. But he was burning, from the inside, the way a lantern burns. His green eyes were bright. So bright they were almost hard to look at. His face was alive. Every line of it was alive, every shadow, every muscle. He looked, Chronicler thought, like a man who has been asleep for seven years and has just woken up, and is not yet sure whether the world he has woken into is real.

He looked down at his hands. At the empty cord in them. At the thin pale scar around his left wrist, like a bracelet.

He flexed his fingers. Slowly. One at a time.

Then he looked up. At Chronicler. At the room. At the frost melting on the walls and the water dripping from the shutters and the blue light fading.

At Cinder, standing by the door with his burned hands held up in front of him and his black eyes wide.

"Oh," said the man who had been Kote.

His voice was different too. Not the flat, toneless voice of the innkeeper. Not the warm, rich, storytelling voice of the last three days. Something else. Something deeper. Something that filled the room the way the silver thread had filled him.

"Oh," he said again, softly. "*There* I am."

---

He stood up.

He did it slowly. Carefully. The way a man stands up who has been kneeling for a long time, and is not sure his legs will hold him. He stood in front of the open chest, with the empty cord in one hand, and he looked around the small room. At the narrow bed, and the washstand, and the window. At the frost melting on the shutters. At Chronicler, sitting frozen on the bed with the pages of the story scattered around his feet.

He looked at Chronicler for a long moment.

"Thank you," he said.

Chronicler could not speak. He stared up at the man standing in front of him, and he could not say a single word.

The man who had been Kote smiled. It was a small smile. Crooked. A little sad. But it was real. It went all the way up to his eyes, and his eyes were burning.

"Three days," he said. "Three days of listening. Three days of writing it down. Every word." He looked down at the scattered pages. "You didn't just record it. You kept it. You held it, while I found my way back to it." He looked at Chronicler. "That's what a name needs. Someone to hold it."

He looked down at the empty cord in his hand. Then he folded it very carefully, and put it in the inside pocket of his shirt, over his heart. Beside something else. Something small, on a thread, that had been there a very long time.

Then he turned to face Cinder.

---

Cinder had not moved. He was standing by the door with his burned hands held up in front of him. His black eyes were wide. His crooked scarred face was very still.

The two of them looked at each other across the small room for a long time.

"Kvothe," said Cinder at last. Softly.

"Ferule," said Kvothe.

Cinder flinched.

It was a small movement. Barely a movement at all. A tightening of the shoulders. A narrowing of the eyes. But Chronicler saw it. And so, he thought, did Kvothe.

"You know my name," said Cinder.

"I know your name." Kvothe's voice was very quiet. "I heard it first from a trapper in the Medica, with your hand frozen into his arm. And then I heard it spoken three times more. By Haliax, in the Eolian. By Haliax, on a ridge above a burning camp. By Haliax, in the chamber beneath the Lackless house. And then by me, telling the story, where it went back into me with everything else." He took a step forward. "I didn't know what it meant, when I heard it. I didn't know it was your true name. I thought it was only what he called you." Another step. "But I know now."

Cinder took a step back. His back was against the door frame.

"You're the key again," he said. His voice had lost its softness. It was high and thin and cracked. "You know that. Don't you? You've got your name back. The whole line is in you again. The door can hear you. The Thief can hear you." His crooked smile flickered. "He'll come. The lord. He'll come for you himself, now. You'll never be safe again. Not anywhere. Not ever."

"I know."

"You were safe. For seven years. Hiding behind your little stone, with your little name." Cinder's voice rose. "And you threw it away. For what? For a scribe? For a Fae princeling bleeding on your floor? For a town full of farmers who don't even know your name?"

Kvothe looked at him for a long moment.

"Yes," he said.

---

Downstairs, very faintly, someone moaned.

Kvothe's face changed. Just for a moment. Something went across it, quick and fierce and cold.

"Bast," he said.

He looked at Cinder. And his green eyes were not burning anymore. They were something else. Something colder. Something that Chronicler had seen once before, on the first night, when the innkeeper looked at the sword above the hearth.

It was the Heart of Stone.

"Get out of my way," said Kvothe.

Cinder stood in the doorway with his back against the frame. His black eyes were very wide. His burned hands were raised in front of him.

"*Make me*," he whispered.

Kvothe looked at him.

Then he opened his mouth, and spoke Cinder's name.
