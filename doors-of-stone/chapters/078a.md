# Interlude: What Bast Saw

"I don't remember it," said the innkeeper. "That's the trouble. The moment itself. I remember the door, and the light, and Denna's hands. And then the grey." He looked down at the table. "But the moment between—the moment it went—I don't have. It's the one thing that didn't come back."

Chronicler waited, his pen lifted.

"Bast was there," said the innkeeper. He did not look up. "He saw it. He's never told me." He was quiet for a moment. "I've never asked."

The common room was very still. Bast, in his backward chair, had gone rigid.

"Reshi," he said. "Don't."

"I think you should tell it," said the innkeeper gently. "I think it belongs in the book. And I think I'd like to hear it." He looked up, at last, at Bast. "I think I'm ready."

---

Bast did not answer for a long time. He sat with his arms folded across the back of his chair and his chin resting on them, and he looked at the fire, and his face was a stranger's face. Old, and tired, and very sad.

"You want me to tell the scribe," he said at last. "Or you?"

"Both."

Bast closed his eyes.

"All right," he said.

---

"We came down the stair behind the Adem," said Bast. "Me and Sim and Wil and Fela and Devi. We were late. We'd been fighting in the passage above, with the soldiers the king left to guard it, and by the time we got down, it was already happening."

He opened his eyes. He did not look at either of them. He looked at the fire.

"The door was open," he said. "Just a crack. A thin line of light down the middle of it, silver-white, so bright it hurt. And things were coming through. You told that part. The scrael, and the faceless thing, and the rest. The Adem were fighting them. Vashet and Tempi and the others, back to back, in a ring around the door. There was blood on the floor. There was a dead king on the floor, with a box on a chain around his neck. There was a woman in black and silver kneeling beside him, with her hands over her face." He swallowed. "And there was you."

The innkeeper did not move.

"You were standing in front of the door," said Bast. "With your back to us. Right in front of the crack. So close the light was on your face. And your hands were up. Both of them. Flat. Not touching it. Just an inch away, the way you hold your hands up to a fire." He was quiet for a moment. "And you were talking to it."

---

"I couldn't hear what you were saying," said Bast. "Nobody could. The noise was too loud. The things coming through, and the Adem fighting, and the soldiers screaming on the stair. And the door. The door was making a sound. Like a wind. Like a very big wind, a long way off, blowing through a very small hole." He shivered. "But you were talking. I could see your lips moving. Calm. Steady. The way you talk to a horse that's frightened."

"The way Auri talked to it," said the innkeeper softly. "In the snow."

Bast looked at him.

"I don't know," he said. "Maybe. I didn't know about Auri, then." He looked back at the fire. "And the thing on the other side—the Thief—it was talking back. Not with words. With wanting. I could feel it, Reshi. Even from the stair. Even with Fela's stone in my pocket. It wanted you to come in. It wanted it so much. It was pulling. Like a river pulls. Like the moon pulls the sea." His voice cracked. "And you were leaning. Just a little. Toward the crack."

---

"Fela saw it," said Bast. "She was beside me. She saw you leaning. And she screamed your name. Your real one. The one you'd been using. *Kvothe.*" He closed his eyes. "And you turned your head. Just a little. And looked back at us. Over your shoulder."

He was quiet for a long time.

"You smiled," he said.

The innkeeper's hands tightened on the table.

"You looked at us," said Bast. "At Fela and Sim and Wil and Devi and me. On the stair. Covered in blood and ichor and dust. And you smiled. Not a big smile. Just a small one. The kind you give someone when you're about to do something and you want them to know it's all right." His voice was very quiet. "I'd seen that smile before. On the ridge above the king's camp. On the ice in the Stormwal. Every time you were about to do something terrible and brave and stupid. I knew that smile."

"And then?" said Chronicler. His voice was hoarse.

"And then you turned back to the door," said Bast. "And you put your hands on it."

---

The fire cracked. Nobody moved.

"Flat," said Bast. "Both hands. On either side of the crack. And you *pulled*." He pressed his own hands flat against the table, as if he could feel it. "Not with your arms. With something else. I don't know what. And something started coming out of the stone. A thread. A thread of light, silver, out of the door. Out of the place where your family was braided into it." He swallowed. "And the door screamed. I'd never heard stone scream. And you screamed too."

The innkeeper's hands tightened on the table.

"It was your name," said Bast. "Coming out. Strand by strand. I knew it the moment I saw it. You can't be Fae and not know a name when it's pulled out into the air in front of you." His voice was very quiet. "It was the most beautiful thing I ever saw, Reshi. In all my life. In the mortal world or the twilight. Every song you ever sang. Every joke you ever told me. The way you laugh. The way you hold a lute. The way you look at Denna when you think nobody's watching. All of it. At once. Hanging in the air in front of that door like a thread of moonlight."

"And the door closed," said Chronicler.

"The door started closing," said Bast. "The moment the last strand came out. Grinding. Like a millstone. But the wind from the crack—the Thief—it was pulling at the thread. At your name. Pulling it toward the crack, the way it had been pulling you. And you couldn't hold it. You were on your knees. Your hands were empty." He was quiet. "And the old man was there. Bredon. At the edge of the doorway. With his stick. Watching. Smiling." Bast's lip curled. "And just before the door closed, he stepped through it. Into the nothing. Like a man stepping through a door into his own house."

He was quiet for a long moment.

"I was looking at you," he said. "Not at him. You were still kneeling there. And the light was going out of you. Out of your face, and your hair, and your eyes. Draining out, like water out of a basin. Your hair went dark. Did you know that? Just for a moment. Red to brown to almost black. Like a coal going out. And then it came back red again, but—dull. Like old rust." He swallowed. "And your eyes—"

He stopped.

"Tell me," said the innkeeper quietly.

"Your eyes went grey," said Bast. "Not the color. The *look*. They were green, and bright, and full of you. And then they were just eyes. Looking at a door. Like a stranger's. Like nobody was behind them at all."

---

"And then Denna," said Bast. "She came from somewhere. I don't know where. She'd been in the chamber all along, I think, behind the Adem. And she ran to you. Right past the scrael, right past everything. And she had a cord in her hands. A new one. Thin and dark. Braided out of her own hair. And she was tying."

He was quiet for a moment.

"She was tying so fast," he said. "I've never seen hands move that fast. Not the Adem. Not anyone. Knot after knot after knot. And your name—that thread of light, hanging in the air, with the wind pulling at it—she caught it. With the cord. She wrapped it around, and around, and around, and tied it. Right at the very last second. Right before the wind took it." His voice shook. "And the door closed. All the way. With a sound like the world ending. And you fell down."

"And she caught me."

"She caught you." Bast looked at him. "She caught you, and she held you, and she tied the cord around your wrist. And she was saying something, over and over, very fast, in a language I didn't know. And then she stopped, and put her forehead against yours, and just knelt there. On the floor. Holding you." He was quiet. "She was crying. Denna was crying. I'd never seen her cry before. I didn't know she could."

---

Nobody said anything for a long time.

The innkeeper sat with his eyes closed and his hands flat on the table. Chronicler did not write. He sat with his pen in his hand and his page in front of him and did not write a single word, because it did not seem right, and because his hand was shaking too much.

At last the innkeeper opened his eyes.

"Why didn't you ever tell me?" he said.

Bast looked at him.

"Because it was the worst thing I ever saw," he said simply. "Worse than my mother leaving. Worse than the Cthaeh. Worse than anything." He looked down at his hands. "I watched you go out, Reshi. Like a candle. I watched the most beautiful thing in the world go into a door and not come out. And I thought, every day, for seven years, that I'd never hear it again." His voice was very small. "How was I supposed to tell you that? When you couldn't even remember what it was you'd lost?"

The innkeeper looked at him for a long time.

Then he reached across the table and took Bast's hand. And held it.

"You'll hear it again," he said quietly. "Soon. I promise."

Bast looked at their hands, together on the table. Then up at the innkeeper's face. His deep blue eyes were very bright.

"You always promise," he said. His voice cracked. "That's what I love about you. And that's what frightens me."

"I know," said the innkeeper. "Denna used to say the same thing."

He let go of Bast's hand. He looked at Chronicler.

"Write it down," he said. "All of it. Just the way he told it." He picked up his cup. "And then we'll go on."
