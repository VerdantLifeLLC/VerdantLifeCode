# The Ledgers of the Scrivs

Wil came to me with the ledgers a span after Auri woke.

I was in the Fishery, at my bench, not working. I had been not working a great deal, since the Cellarage. I would sit at my bench with a half-finished project in front of me and a tool in my hand, and I would look at it, and I would think about a king on a bed of black stone, breathing, and a voice like stone grinding on stone saying *key*. And the afternoon would go by, and the project would not get finished, and Kilvin would come by and look at me and grunt in a worried way and go back to his office.

Wil did not grunt. Wil came and stood beside my bench with a stack of old leather-bound books under one arm and his dark face very serious, and he said, "Come with me."

"Where?"

"Somewhere private." He glanced around the Fishery. At the students at their benches, and the apprentices at the forges, and Manet grinding something in his mortar by the kilns. "Not here."

---

We went to Anker's. To my room under the eaves. Wil closed the door behind us and set the stack of books on my narrow table and sat down on my bed and looked at me.

"You asked me," he said, "a long time ago. About the cut books. The ones with the pages missing. Rebound, and renumbered."

"Lorren's looking into it."

"Lorren has been looking into it for two months. Lorren has found eleven more books and written a very long report and locked it in his desk and told no one what's in it." Wil folded his hands. "I got tired of waiting."

He tapped the stack of books on my table.

"These are the scrivs' ledgers," he said. "The old ones. The records of who took what out of the Archives, and when, and who brought it back. Every book that leaves the Stacks for the Tomes, every book that goes to a master's office, every book that gets sent to the bindery for repair. The scrivs write it all down. They've been writing it all down since before there was a University."

I looked at the books. They were very old. Their leather covers were cracked and faded, and the pages, where they showed at the edges, were brown and brittle.

"Where did you get them?"

"From the basement of the Archives. There's a room down there, full of them. Hundreds of years of ledgers, stacked up to the ceiling. Nobody ever looks at them. Nobody even remembers they're there, except the old scrivs, and most of the old scrivs are dead." Wil's face did not change. "I've been going down there every night for a month. After my shift. Reading."

"Reading ledgers."

"Reading ledgers." He opened the top one. "Looking for the cut books. Looking for when they went to the bindery, and who sent them, and who brought them back."

---

He showed me.

It was slow work, the way he had done it. The ledgers were written in a dozen different hands over hundreds of years, some neat and some crabbed and some nearly illegible, in inks that had faded to brown or grey or nothing. They used abbreviations that had changed a dozen times. They recorded books by their shelfmarks rather than their titles, and the shelfmarks had changed too, whenever the Archives had been reorganized, which had happened, as far as Wil could tell, about every forty years.

But Wil was Cealdish, and patient, and very good at columns of numbers. He had made a list of the cut books, all twelve of the ones we knew about. He had traced their shelfmarks back through the reorganizations. And then he had gone looking for them in the ledgers.

"Here," he said. He turned the brittle pages of the oldest ledger, very carefully, and laid his finger on a line near the bottom. "Look."

I looked. It was a single line, in faded brown ink, in a small neat hand.

*St. VII. xii. 44. To bindery. Loose leaves. Returned. —B.*

"That's one of ours," said Wil. "The book you showed Lorren. The history of the Aturan provinces, with the chapter on the Seven replaced. Its shelfmark, before the reorganization, was Stacks seven, twelve, forty-four." He tapped the line. "It went to the bindery for loose leaves, and came back. And whoever took it signed for it."

"B."

"B." Wil turned the page. And another. And another. He laid his finger on another line.

*St. II. iv. 108. To bindery. Spine cracked. Returned. —B.*

"That's another one." Another page. "And another." Another. "And another."

He had found them all. Every one of the twelve. And every one of them had gone to the bindery, at one time or another, and come back. And every time, the scriv who signed for it had signed the same way.

*—B.*

---

"When?" I said. My voice sounded strange.

Wil looked at me.

"That's the thing," he said quietly. "That's why I brought them to you."

He laid the ledgers out on my narrow table, side by side, all six of them. He opened each one to a page he had marked with a slip of paper.

"This one," he said, touching the first, "is from two hundred and forty years ago. This one is from a hundred and ninety. This one, a hundred and thirty. This one, ninety. This one, sixty. This one, twenty-two." He looked at me. "The same initial. The same hand. I've compared them. The loops of the B, the way the stroke starts. It's the same hand, Kvothe. Over two hundred and forty years."

I stared at the ledgers.

"A family," I said. "Scrivs. Father to son. Handwriting passed down."

"Maybe." Wil's face did not change. "I thought of that. So I went looking for the scrivs' rolls. The lists of who worked the desk, every term. They're in the basement too." He took a folded paper out of his shirt and unfolded it. "Two hundred and forty years ago, there was a scriv named Brede. A hundred and ninety years ago, a scriv named Bran. A hundred and thirty years ago, Bredwyn. Ninety years ago, Brennan. Sixty years ago, Brand." He looked at me. "Twenty-two years ago, a man named Beredon worked the desk for one term. Just one. And then he left."

"Different names."

"Different names." Wil folded the paper. "Each one worked for a term. Maybe two. Never longer. And then left. And then, forty or fifty years later, another one came. With a name beginning with B. And the same handwriting." He was quiet for a moment. "And every time one of them worked the desk, some of the books about the Seven went to the bindery. And came back with pages missing."

---

I did not say anything for a long time.

I sat on the edge of my bed and looked at the ledgers spread out on my table. At the faded brown lines, and the small neat hand, and the single initial at the end of each one.

*—B.*

I did not think of Bredon. I want to be honest about that, since I have been honest about everything else. I sat there with the evidence in front of me, and I did not think of a kind old man with a walking stick who had taught me to play tak in the Maer's court.

I thought about the Chandrian. About Cinder, who did not age. About Haliax, who could not die. I thought about a man who could come to the University every forty or fifty years, under a different name, and work the desk in the Archives for a term, and take the books he wanted to the bindery, and come back with pages missing. A man who did not age. A man who had been doing this for two hundred and forty years, and perhaps much longer.

I thought it must be one of the Seven. Or a servant of theirs. Some creature in human shape.

I did not think it might be a man. Just a man. Kept alive past his time by some bargain, some promise, some dark thing he had answered in the night.

I did not think it might be someone I knew.

---

"What do we do?" said Wil.

I looked at him. At his dark patient face, and the ledgers spread out on my table.

"We take it to Lorren," I said.

"Lorren already knows about the books."

"Lorren doesn't know about this." I touched the ledgers. "This is different. This is a person. A name, or a set of names. A hand we can recognize." I looked at Wil. "If he comes back—when he comes back—we'll know him. By his handwriting. By his name."

Wil was quiet for a long time.

"Kvothe," he said at last. "Twenty-two years ago. That was the last one. Beredon. One term." He looked at me. "The pattern is forty or fifty years. Which means he's not due back for another twenty years, at least."

"So?"

"So he's not coming back to the Archives. Not for a long time." Wil's voice was very quiet. "Which means he's somewhere else. Doing something else. Right now." He paused. "And we don't know where."

---

We took it to Lorren anyway.

He listened to Wil explain, sitting behind his desk in his long narrow office, with his long pale hands folded and his face showing nothing. He looked at the ledgers. He looked at Wil's list of names. He looked at the handwriting, comparing the six lines one after another, for a very long time.

Then he closed the ledgers, one by one, and stacked them neatly on his desk.

"Thank you, E'lir Wilem," he said. "This is very thorough work. Very thorough indeed." He looked at Wil with his calm colorless eyes. "You will not speak of it to anyone. Is that understood?"

"Yes, Master Lorren."

"And you, Re'lar Kvothe."

"Yes, Master Lorren."

Lorren looked at us both for a long moment. Then he said something I have never forgotten.

"I knew Beredon," he said.

Wil and I both went very still.

"Twenty-two years ago," said Lorren. "I was a young master then. New to the chair. He came to me and asked for a place at the desk, and I gave it to him. He was very good. Very quiet. Very careful with the books." He looked down at the ledgers. "He was an old man, even then. Kind. Well-spoken. He used to play tak with me in the evenings, in this office, after the Archives closed." Lorren's long pale hands were very still on the desk. "He always won."

"What happened to him?" I said.

"He left. At the end of the term. He said he had been offered a position at court, in Vintas, as tutor to a nobleman's son." Lorren was quiet for a moment. "I was sorry to see him go. He was very good company."

"Do you remember what he looked like?"

Lorren looked at me for a long time. His face showed nothing at all.

"Tall," he said at last. "White hair. A pleasant face. He walked with a stick." He paused. "A stick of dark wood. With a silver head."

---

Wil and I walked back across the courtyard in the grey afternoon without speaking.

It was cold. The snow was old and grey and packed hard on the flagstones. Students hurried past us with their heads down. The Archives stood behind us, windowless and grey, holding its secrets.

"A stick," said Wil at last. "With a silver head."

"Yes."

"Plenty of old men carry sticks."

"Yes."

"Plenty of them have silver heads."

"Yes."

Wil stopped walking. He turned and looked at me.

"Do you know him?" he said. "Kvothe. Do you know an old man with white hair and a stick with a silver head, who plays tak and always wins?"

I stood in the cold courtyard with the grey snow under my feet and the grey sky over my head, and I thought about the steps of the Mains, in the first snow of the winter. Ambrose in his fur-trimmed coat. And behind him, near the door, an old man leaning on a stick. Raising it an inch off the ground in a small courtly greeting, the way he used to at the start of a game.

*You have an interesting face. It reminds me of someone I knew long ago.*

"I don't know," I said.

It was a lie. Wil knew it was a lie. I saw it in his face.

But he did not press. That was one of the things I loved about Wil. He knew when to stop asking.

He only looked at me for a long moment. Then he nodded, very slowly, and turned, and walked on across the courtyard toward the Archives, where his shift was starting.

I stood alone in the cold for a long time.

And I told myself it meant nothing. That there were a thousand old men with white hair and walking sticks. That Bredon had been at the Maer's court for eleven years, and at the court before that, and had never been a scriv in his life. That it was a coincidence. That it was nothing.

I have always been a very good liar, when the person I am lying to is myself.

It would be months before I stopped. And by then, of course, it was far too late.
