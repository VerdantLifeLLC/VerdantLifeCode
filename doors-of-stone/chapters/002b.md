# Rhetoric and Logic

I should not have taken Hemme's class. Everyone told me so. Sim told me, and Wil told me, and Fela told me, and Manet, who had been at the University for thirty years and had seen every kind of foolishness a student could commit, looked at my schedule on the first day of term and laughed until he had to sit down.

"Rhetoric and Logic," he said, wiping his eyes. "With Hemme. You." He shook his head. "Boy, you've got a death wish."

"It's required," I said. "For advancement to El'the. Every El'the has to have sat a term of Rhetoric. Herma put it in my admissions paper."

"Herma did that on purpose," said Manet. "You know that, don't you? He's got a sense of humor, Herma. Not much of one. But it's there." He handed me back my schedule. "Keep your head down. Keep your mouth shut. Don't correct him. Whatever he says, however wrong it is, don't correct him." He looked at me with his wild grey eyebrows drawn together. "You can do that, can't you? For one term?"

"Of course I can," I said.

Manet looked at me for a long time. Then he started laughing again.

---

The first class went well. I want that on the record. I sat in the back row of the lecture hall in the Mains, among forty other students, with my head down and my mouth shut, and I took notes, and I did not correct anyone about anything.

Hemme lectured for two hours on the structure of the formal syllogism. He was not a good lecturer. He had a heavy, droning voice, and a habit of repeating the last word of every sentence, as if to make sure it had arrived. *The major premise establishes the general case. The general case.* He paced back and forth at the front of the room with his hands clasped behind his back, and his round red face shone with sweat under the lamps.

He did not look at me once. I had made sure of that. I had come in late and sat behind a very tall student from Modeg, and I had kept my hair tucked under a cap.

He knew I was there. Of course he knew. Everyone knew. But he did not look at me, and I did not look at him, and we got through the first class like two cats in a very small room, carefully not noticing each other.

---

The second class did not go well.

It was not my fault. I want that on the record, too. Hemme was lecturing on fallacies. Common errors in reasoning. The appeal to authority, the false dilemma, the slippery slope. It was actually interesting, or would have been, in someone else's hands. And in the middle of it, he stopped pacing, and turned, and looked straight at the back row.

"Let us take an example," he said. "An example. Suppose a student—a hypothetical student—argues that because he has been admitted to the Arcanum, he must therefore be competent. Competent." His small eyes glittered. "What is the fallacy?"

Forty heads turned to look at me.

I kept my head down. I kept my mouth shut. I thought of Manet, laughing until he had to sit down.

"No one?" said Hemme. "No one at all? Then let me offer another example. Another." He began to pace again, slowly. "Suppose that same student—still hypothetical—argues that because he has written a song, which some people have sung in taverns, he must therefore be a person of consequence. Consequence." He smiled. "What is the fallacy there?"

A few students laughed nervously. The tall Modegan in front of me slid a little lower in his seat, as if to get out of the line of fire.

"And suppose," said Hemme, stopping directly in front of the back row, "that this same hypothetical student had been absent from the University for nearly a year, on business of his own, and returned expecting to be welcomed back as if nothing had happened. As if nothing. What fallacy would that be, do you think? Mister—" He looked down at his roll. "Kvothe?"

---

I looked up. I could not help it. I had been doing so well.

"I'm not sure, Master Hemme," I said. "I don't think it's a fallacy at all."

"No?"

"No. A fallacy is an error in reasoning. An argument that seems valid, but isn't." I kept my voice very polite. "But you haven't described an argument. You've described a student. And a student isn't an argument. He's just a student." I paused. "Unless you're suggesting he's a straw man. In which case, the fallacy would be yours."

There was a silence in the lecture hall. The kind of silence that makes your ears ring.

Hemme's face went from red to purple. I watched it happen. It was like watching a sunset, if the sunset wanted to kill you.

"A straw man," he said. "Straw man." His voice was very quiet. "You're accusing me of the straw man fallacy. In my own class."

"Only hypothetically, Master Hemme."

Somebody in the third row made a sound like a strangled goose. Somebody else put their face in their hands.

---

Hemme did not throw me out. I had expected him to. I think everyone had. Instead, he stood in front of the back row for a long moment, breathing heavily through his nose, and then he smiled. It was a very unpleasant smile.

"Very well," he said. "Very well. Since Mister Kvothe is so fond of hypotheticals, let us have a debate. A debate. Next class. Mister Kvothe will argue a proposition of my choosing, before the whole class, and the class will judge whether he has argued it well." His smile widened. "If he loses, he will write a formal apology to me, on the subject of fallacies, to be read aloud at the start of every class for the rest of the term. The rest of the term."

"And if I win?"

Hemme looked at me as if I had said something in a foreign language.

"If you win," he said, "you may sit in the back row and correct me as much as you like. As much as you like." He turned and walked back to the front of the room. "The proposition is this: *That the Arcanum should have the power to expel any student, for any reason, without appeal.*" He looked back at me. "You will argue for it."

---

"He did that on purpose," said Sim that night at Anker's. "You know he did that on purpose."

"I know."

"He wants you to argue for your own expulsion. In front of everyone. And then when it happens—because it will happen, Kvothe, you know it will, sooner or later—everyone will remember that you stood up in Rhetoric and argued it was fair." Sim put his head in his hands. "It's diabolical. I didn't know Hemme had it in him."

"He's cleverer than he looks," said Wil. "Not much. But a little."

"What are you going to do?" said Fela.

I drank my beer. I thought about it.

"I'm going to argue for it," I said. "What else can I do?"

---

The lecture hall was packed for the next class. Word had spread. There were students standing along the walls and sitting in the aisles, and a few I did not recognize, who had clearly come from other classes just to watch. Sim and Wil and Fela were in the front row. Manet was at the back, by the door, with his arms folded and his wild grey eyebrows drawn down.

Hemme sat in a chair at the side of the stage with his hands folded over his belly and a look of deep contentment on his round red face.

"Mister Kvothe," he said. "The floor is yours. Yours."

I stood up at the front of the room. I looked out at the crowd. And I began.

---

I argued for it. I argued very well. I want that on the record most of all.

I said that the Arcanum was a community of scholars, bound together by trust. I said that the masters were chosen for their wisdom, and their judgment, and their long experience of the world. I said that there were dangers at the University that ordinary students could not understand: dangerous knowledge, dangerous materials, dangerous names. And I said that a community that held such dangers must have the power to protect itself. Swiftly. Absolutely. Without the delays and distractions of appeal.

"Consider," I said, "a student who has learned something he should not. Something dangerous. A name, perhaps, that he cannot control. Or a binding that could burn down half of Imre. Should the masters have to wait? Should they have to convene hearings and summon witnesses and argue about procedure, while the danger grows?" I shook my head. "No. They must be able to act. At once. On their own judgment. Because their judgment is the only thing standing between the University and disaster."

I saw Hemme nodding, very slightly, in his chair. Smug. Satisfied.

"And the masters are wise," I said. "All of them. Every one. We know this, because they are masters. And we know they are masters because they are wise." I paused. "Which is, of course, a circular argument. But it's a very comfortable circle. Very warm. Very safe."

A few people laughed. Hemme's nod stopped.

---

"So yes," I said. "The Arcanum should have the power to expel any student, for any reason, without appeal. Because the masters are wise. And because they would never abuse such a power." I looked at Hemme. "Would they, Master Hemme?"

Hemme did not answer. His face was going purple again.

"They would never," I said, "expel a student out of spite. Or jealousy. Or a personal grudge. They would never let their own feelings cloud their judgment. They would never use their power to punish someone who had embarrassed them, or contradicted them, or asked them a question they couldn't answer." I spread my hands. "That would be unthinkable. That would be a terrible abuse. And since the masters are wise, it could never happen." I smiled. "So there is no need for appeal. None at all. I rest my case."

---

There was a silence. And then someone in the back started clapping. Slowly. And then someone else. And then the whole lecture hall was clapping and stamping and cheering, and Sim was standing on his chair, and Wil was shaking his head with his hand over his eyes, and Fela was laughing so hard she had to hold on to the bench in front of her.

And Manet, at the back of the room, by the door, was not laughing. He was looking at Hemme, sitting purple and silent in his chair. And his face was very grave.

Hemme stood up. The noise died.

"The class will judge," he said. His voice was thick. "As agreed. As agreed. Those who believe Mister Kvothe has argued the proposition well?"

Every hand in the room went up.

Hemme looked at them for a long time. Then he looked at me.

"Very well," he said. "Very well. You may sit in the back row, Mister Kvothe. And correct me as much as you like." He turned and walked out of the lecture hall, and the door banged shut behind him.

---

"That," said Manet afterward, in the courtyard, "was the stupidest thing I have ever seen a student do. And I once saw a Re'lar try to call the name of iron while standing in a smithy."

"I won," I said.

"You won the debate." Manet looked at me with his wild grey eyebrows drawn together. "And you made a master look like a fool in front of fifty students. Again. And you did it by arguing that masters would never abuse their power to punish someone who'd embarrassed them." He shook his head slowly. "Boy. Think about it. What do you think he's going to do?"

I did not answer. I had not thought about it. I had been too busy winning.

"You made an argument," said Manet quietly, "that's going to come true. And you're going to be standing right in the middle of it when it does."

---

He was right, of course. Manet was always right about those things. I did not listen to him. I never did.

But I remembered it, a few months later. In a hearing room in Hollows, with the masters sitting on their dais and Hemme at the end of it, his round red face twisted in satisfaction. With Kilvin arguing for me, and Arwyl, and Mandrag. And the vote going five to three.

Hemme did not say anything, when the vote was taken. He did not need to. He only looked down at me from the dais, and smiled, very slightly.

And I thought: *that would be a terrible abuse. And since the masters are wise, it could never happen.*

It was a very comfortable circle. Very warm. Very safe.

Right up until it closed.
