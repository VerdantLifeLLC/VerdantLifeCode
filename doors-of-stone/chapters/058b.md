# Letters in the Dust

On the third night after the Sithe let us go, I found out that Bast could not read.

We were camped in a hollow beside the Moon's Road, among silver grass that whispered when the wind moved through it. I had taken out the little book I carried everywhere, the one I had bought in Imre for a single jot, with its cheap paper and its cracked binding, and I was writing in it by the light of a small fire. Notes, mostly. The things I had learned from the Sithe. The shape of the road. A verse that had come to me, walking, about the color of the twilight.

Bast was lying on his back in the grass on the other side of the fire, with his hands behind his head, watching the sky. After a while he rolled over and propped his chin on his fists and watched me instead.

"What are you doing?" he said.

"Writing."

"I know that. What are you writing?"

I held the book up so that he could see the page. He looked at it for a long moment. His face did something strange. It went very still, and then very bright, and then very carefully careless.

"Oh," he said. "That. Yes. Very nice."

I lowered the book.

"Bast," I said. "What does it say?"

He looked at me. Then at the book. Then at the fire.

"Words," he said. "Mortal words. About—things."

"Which things?"

He did not answer. He rolled back over onto his back and looked at the sky again.

"I can't," he said, after a while. Very quietly. "Read. Mortal letters. I never learned."

---

I did not say anything for a moment. I was surprised. I should not have been. There was no reason a prince of the Twilight Court should know how to read the letters of a world he had never been to. But Bast knew so many things. He knew the names of every flower in the Fae, and which ones would kill you and which ones would only make you wish you were dead. He knew the songs of his father's court, and the dances, and the courtesies, and the insults. He knew how to glamour his face and his voice and the shape of his shadow. He knew a hundred languages, most of which had not been spoken in the mortal world for a thousand years.

But he could not read my little book.

"We don't write things down," said Bast, to the sky. "In the twilight. Not like that. Not in letters. We remember. Or we sing it. Or we weave it into something, or carve it into a tree. But we don't make little marks on paper." He was quiet for a moment. "My mother tried to teach me. Once. When I was very small. She had a book. A mortal book. She'd brought it with her, when she came to the twilight. She used to read it to me."

"What was it?"

"I don't know." His voice was very small. "I never found out. She stopped, before I learned. And then she left. And she took the book with her."

---

I closed my own book. I put it down on the grass. I looked at Bast, lying on his back in the silver grass with his face turned up to the sky, and I thought about a boy in Tarbean, twelve years old, who had known how to read, and had no books.

I thought about an old arcanist named Abenthy, sitting in the back of a wagon on the road, with a boy of eleven beside him and a slate between them. Teaching him his letters. Not because he had to. Because he could.

"Bast," I said. "Would you like to learn?"

He did not move. He did not look at me.

"It's a mortal thing," he said. "Why would I want to?"

"Because your mother wanted you to."

He was quiet for a very long time.

"Yes," he said at last. Very softly. "I would."

---

I did not have a slate. I did not have chalk, or ink to spare, or paper I could afford to waste. But I had the Fae. And in the Fae, the dust of the Moon's Road is very fine and very silver, and it holds a mark the way wet sand holds a footprint.

I smoothed a patch of it with my palm, beside the fire. I took a twig. And I drew a letter.

"That's *A*," I said. "It's the first one. It makes a sound like *ah*. Like when you open your mouth to sing."

Bast looked at it. He sat up and crossed his legs and leaned forward, very close, the way a cat leans toward something it has not yet decided whether to pounce on.

"It looks like a tent," he said.

"It does look like a tent."

"Why does a tent say *ah*?"

I opened my mouth. Then I closed it. I had never thought about it. No one had ever asked me that. Not Abenthy, not the masters at the University, not anyone.

"I don't know," I said. "It just does."

Bast looked at the letter for a long time.

"Mortals are very strange," he said.

---

It took him longer than it had taken me. I will not pretend otherwise. Bast is very quick in a great many ways, but he had no patience for things that did not make sense, and he found a great many things about mortal letters that did not make sense. Why there were two shapes for every letter, a large one and a small one. Why *c* could sound like *k* sometimes and *s* other times and why no one had just made up a new letter for one of them. Why *e* at the end of a word was sometimes silent, and what it was doing there if it was not going to say anything.

"It's lurking," he said darkly. "It's just sitting there. Lurking. Waiting."

"It changes the vowel before it."

"Then why doesn't it just *say so*?"

But he learned. Every night, on the Moon's Road, and then in the Stormwal, in caves and hollows and the lee of great boulders, while the wind howled outside. I would draw letters in the dust, or on the snow, or on the frost of a cave wall with my fingernail, and Bast would copy them, frowning, with his tongue caught between his teeth. And then words. And then short sentences, which he read out loud, slowly, sounding them out, with his finger moving under each letter.

*The cat sat.*

*The road is long.*

*Bast is very clever.*

"Is that true?" he said suspiciously, when he had sounded that one out. "Or are you just saying it?"

"It's written down," I said. "So it must be true."

He looked at me for a long moment. Then he laughed, delighted, and threw a handful of snow at me.

---

The first real thing he read was on the white city's walls.

We had come over the high pass into the bowl of Myr Tariniel, and we were walking through the ruins, among the broken columns and the drifted snow. And on the side of one of the fallen buildings, carved into the white stone, there was writing. Not in the old script of the white city, which neither of us could read. In Tema. Newer, rougher, scratched into the stone with a knife, by someone who had come here long after the city fell.

Bast stopped in front of it. He looked at it for a long time. His lips moved.

"*Here,*" he read, very slowly, sounding out each word. "*I—sat—and—wept.*" He stopped. He looked at me. "*Here I sat and wept.*" His face was very strange. "Who wrote that?"

"I don't know."

"Someone who came here. And saw it. And sat down. And—" He looked at the words. "And wrote it down. So that someone else would know." He was quiet for a moment. "So that they wouldn't be the only one."

"Yes."

Bast reached out and touched the letters, very gently, the way you touch a thing that might break.

"That's what it's for," he said slowly. "Isn't it? Writing. It's not just for remembering. It's so you're not alone."

I did not answer. I did not need to. He had worked it out himself.

---

He carried a little book after that. I gave it to him. My own little book, the cheap one from Imre, with its cracked binding. I tore out the pages I had written on and gave him the rest. And he wrote in it. Badly, at first, in big clumsy letters that wandered all over the page. Then better.

I do not know what he wrote. He never showed me. He kept the book in the inside pocket of his coat, and he took it out at night, sometimes, when he thought I was asleep, and wrote in it by the light of the fire, with his tongue between his teeth.

---

"He still has it," said the innkeeper. "The book. Did you know?"

Chronicler looked at Bast. Bast had gone very red. He was studying the grain of the table with great attention.

"He's filled it," said the innkeeper. "Years ago. He's on his fourth, now. Or his fifth. He buys them from the tinkers, when they come through." He looked at Bast with an expression Chronicler had not seen on his face before. Something very fond, and very sad. "I've never read them. I never asked. I didn't—" He stopped. "I didn't know how to ask, for a long time. I didn't know how to want to know things."

Bast did not look up.

"They're not very good," he said, to the table. "My letters are still terrible. And I can't spell. The *e*s still lurk."

"What's in them?" said Chronicler gently.

Bast was quiet for a long moment.

"Him," he said at last. "Every day. For seven years. What he said. What he did. Whether he smiled." His voice was very small. "So that if he ever came back, he'd know what he missed. And so that if he never did—" He stopped.

"So you wouldn't be alone," said the innkeeper softly.

Bast looked up. His deep blue eyes were very bright.

"You taught me that," he said. "In the snow. *Here I sat and wept.*"

The innkeeper did not say anything. He reached across the table, very slowly, and put his hand over Bast's hand. And left it there.

"May I read them?" he said. "Someday? When this is over?"

Bast looked at their hands for a long time.

"They're terrible," he said. "The spelling."

"I don't care about the spelling."

"And some of it's—" Bast swallowed. "Some of it's not very kind. The first year. When I was angry."

"I don't care about that either."

Bast looked at him for a long moment. Then he nodded. Once. Very slightly.

"Someday," he said.

And Chronicler, who had spent his whole life among other people's words, looked down at his own page and found that he could not see it clearly. He wiped his eyes with the back of his scabbed hand and dipped his pen, and went on.
