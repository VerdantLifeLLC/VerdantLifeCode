# The Drowned Bell

On the tenth night aboard the *Wind's Daughter*, the night after the pirates, Hessa Tern told me why she had become a sailor.

We were sitting in the bow, where I always sat, with my lute across my knees and the wind playing in the rigging above us. The crew were asleep, or pretending to be. The sea was calm, black and silver under a half moon. Far off to the north, the coast was a low dark line, with here and there a single light.

"See that?" said Hessa Tern. She pointed with her pipe at one of the lights. It was higher than the others, and brighter, and it did not flicker. "That's the lighthouse at Keth. Where I was born."

I looked at it. A small bright point of light, very far away.

"There's a bell," she said. "Under the water. Off Keth. You can hear it on still nights, if the tide's right. Ringing."

I looked at her. Her fist of a face was very still in the moonlight.

"A bell," I said.

"A church bell. Big one. Bronze. They say it's been there three hundred years." She puffed her pipe. "My grandmother told me about it when I was small. She said there used to be a town there, where the sea is now. A fishing town, with a church and a square and a harbor. Called Old Keth. And the people of Old Keth were greedy. They fished too much, and they didn't give anything back. Not to the sea, not to the poor, not to anyone."

"And the sea took them."

"The sea took them." Hessa Tern nodded. "One night, in a storm. The whole town slid into the water, church and square and harbor and all. And the bell went down with the church, and it's been ringing ever since. Down there. In the dark." She looked at me. "My grandmother said it rings for the drowned. So they'll know the way home."

---

I did not say anything. I sat with my lute in my lap and looked at the distant light.

"I didn't believe her," said Hessa Tern. "Of course not. I was a sensible child. I thought it was a story to frighten children away from the water." She smiled thinly. "And then, when I was eleven, my father's boat went out one evening and didn't come back."

The wind moved in the rigging. The sails creaked.

"There wasn't a storm," she said. "It was a calm night. Still. Like this one. My father went out with my two brothers to set the pots, the way he did every evening. And the boat didn't come back. They never found it. Never found any of them." She drew on her pipe. "My mother went mad. Sat on the harbor wall every night for a year, looking out at the water. And one night, I sat with her. And I heard it."

"The bell."

"The bell." Hessa Tern's voice was very quiet. "Very faint. Very far down. Ringing. Slow. Like a church on a feast day." She was silent for a long moment. "My mother heard it too. She turned to me, and she had the strangest look on her face. And she said, *They're home, Hessa. They found the way.*"

---

"She died that winter," said Hessa Tern. "Of a cough. And I went to sea the next spring, at twelve, as a ship's boy on a coaster out of Keth. Because I thought—" She stopped.

"You thought you'd find them."

"I thought I'd find the bell." She laughed, a short hard sound. "Stupid. A child's idea. I thought if I could find the bell, I could find the town, and if I could find the town, I could find my father and my brothers. I thought they'd be down there, in Old Keth, in the drowned square, sitting on the church steps, waiting for me."

"Did you look?"

"For thirty years." She knocked out her pipe on the rail. "Every time I passed Keth. I'd drop anchor off the point, where the bell is supposed to be, on still nights, and listen. And sometimes I'd hear it. And sometimes I wouldn't." She was quiet. "I sent divers down, twice. Paid them good money. They never found anything. Just sand and weed and dark."

"And now?"

"And now I'm old and ugly and I've got a ship that lists to port." She grinned, a fierce crooked grin. "And I still drop anchor off Keth, on still nights, when I'm passing. And I still listen." She looked at me. "Tomorrow night, we'll pass Keth. It'll be still. I can feel it. I'm going to drop anchor."

"Why are you telling me?"

Hessa Tern looked at me for a long moment with her small hard eyes.

"Because you called the wind," she said. "And sank three ships with it. And the crew think you're a devil, and I think you're something else, and I don't know what." She looked out at the distant light. "And because I thought, maybe, a man who can talk to the wind can talk to the sea. And ask it where the bell is."

---

I did not know what to say. I did not know how to talk to the sea. I had never tried. The wind had come to me, on the dock at Tarbean, because it was curious, and I had not asked it for anything. The sea was not the wind. The sea was old and deep and very cold, and it did not seem to me to be curious about anything.

But the next night, we dropped anchor off the point at Keth.

It was very still. Hessa Tern had been right. There was no wind at all; the sails hung slack, and the wind that had followed us all the way from Tarbean had gone quiet, the way a dog goes quiet when it lies down to sleep. The sea was flat and black and glassy, and the moon lay on it like a silver coin.

The crew stood along the rail and did not speak. They had heard the story, all of them. Every sailor on that coast had.

Hessa Tern stood in the bow with her hands on the rail and her face turned toward the water.

I sat on the deck beside her with my lute. I did not play. I closed my eyes, and I let my mind go still, the way I had in the round room at the top of the Rookery, the way I had on the rocks outside Skarpi's lighthouse.

And I listened.

---

At first there was nothing. The creak of the hull. The small lapping of the water against it. The breathing of the crew along the rail.

Then, very faint, very far down, something else.

It was not the bell. I want to be honest about that. It was not a sound. It was more like what I had heard in the stone, the patient no, only different. The sea did not say no. It did not say yes, either. It said something I did not have a word for. Something like *always*. Something like *here*. Something like *everything that falls into me is kept*.

I listened to it for a long time. And then, very softly, I began to hum.

Not to the sea. I did not know how. I hummed the only thing I could think of. A lullaby. A Ruh lullaby, the one my mother used to sing. A song for bringing children home.

And far, far below the black glassy water, something rang.

---

Everyone on the *Wind's Daughter* heard it. I know, because they told me afterward, every one of them, in hushed voices, as if they were afraid of being overheard.

It was very faint. Very deep. Very slow. A single note, like a great bronze bell struck once, far off, in a church on a feast day. It came up through the water and through the hull and through the soles of our feet, and it hung in the still air for a long, long time.

And then it rang again.

And again.

Three times. Slow. Like someone at the door, knocking.

Hessa Tern was standing at the rail with both hands gripping it so hard her knuckles were white. Her fist of a face was wet in the moonlight. She did not make a sound.

When the third note had faded, she let go of the rail. She stood very straight.

"Thank you," she said. To the water. Not to me.

And then she turned and walked aft, past the silent crew, and went down into her cabin, and closed the door.

---

We weighed anchor at dawn. The wind came back, as if it had only been waiting, and filled the sails, and we went on east along the coast.

Hessa Tern did not speak to me for two days. On the third, she came and sat beside me in the bow, the way she had the night after the pirates. She did not say anything for a long time.

"It didn't ring for me," she said at last. "Not once. In thirty years. I heard it, sometimes. But it never rang when I was listening for it." She looked at me. "It rang for you."

"It rang for them. For your father. Your brothers."

"Did it?"

"I don't know," I said honestly. "I only hummed. I don't know what it heard."

Hessa Tern was quiet for a long time.

"My mother said it rings so the drowned know the way home," she said at last. "I always thought that meant it rang for them. For the drowned. So they could find the church." She looked out at the sea. "But maybe it rings for us. The ones left on the wall. So we'll know they found it."

She did not say anything else. But she did not drop anchor off Keth again, she told me, years later, when we met by chance in a harbor town I will not name. She said she did not need to.

She said she had heard them knock.
