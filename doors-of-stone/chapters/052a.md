# One Hand

Vashet left on the ninth day. On the tenth, I tried to play.

I had not tried since the fire. I had not wanted to. I had sung at Stanchion's grave with one hand on the open strings of his cracked old lute, and that had been enough, and I had told myself that it would be enough for a while. That the hand would heal, as Elodin said, a little at a time. That I would wait.

But I am not good at waiting. You will have noticed that by now.

So on the tenth day, in my room at the top of Merra's house, with the window open and the smell of the burned Eolian still drifting in on the wind, I took Stanchion's lute out of its case and sat on the edge of my bed and tried.

---

My left hand would not do it.

It moved, now. That was something. Felurian had not kissed it yet; that would come later, in the twilight. But in the fortnight since the fire, a little feeling had crept back into it, the way feeling creeps back into a foot that has fallen asleep. Pins and needles, first. Then a dull ache. Then, very faintly, the sense of the fingers, like the memory of fingers.

But they would not bend. Not properly. I could lay them on the neck of the lute, over the frets, and I could press down, a little. But I could not make them go where I wanted. They were stiff and slow and clumsy, like the fingers of an old man with the joint-ache. They slid off the strings. They muted notes they should have stopped and stopped notes they should have let ring.

I tried a simple chord. The first chord any child learns. It came out as a dull buzzing mess.

I tried again. The same.

I tried a hundred times. Every chord I knew. Every song. The ones I had played since I was three years old on my father's knee, the ones that were in my hands more deeply than they were in my head. My hands knew the shapes. My right hand, the good one, knew them perfectly. It struck the strings clean and bright, the way it always had.

My left hand did not know anything. It had forgotten. Or rather, it knew, but it could not do. Like a man who knows every word of a language but has lost his tongue.

---

I sat on the edge of the bed with the lute in my lap for a long time after I stopped trying.

I did not weep. I want to be honest about that, since I have been honest about everything else. I have wept a great deal in this story, and I am not ashamed of it. But I did not weep then.

I only sat there. And I felt something I had not felt since I was twelve years old, sitting in an alley in Tarbean with the pieces of my father's lute in my lap.

It was not grief. It was worse than grief. It was the feeling of a door closing. Of something that had always been there, always been mine, that no one could take from me, being taken.

*You'll get the feeling back*, Elodin had said. *A little at a time. But it'll always be cold. That hand. Always.*

*Can I still play?*

*I don't know. Ask me in a year.*

I did not want to wait a year. I did not know how.

---

Sim found me there that evening. He came up the stairs with a basket of bread and cheese from the market and a bottle of cheap wine, whistling, and stopped in the doorway when he saw me.

He looked at the lute in my lap. At my face. At my left hand, lying on the strings, stiff and white.

He set down the basket. He did not say anything. He came and sat on the bed beside me and looked at the lute too.

"It won't work," I said at last.

"Your hand?"

"Yes."

Sim was quiet for a long time.

"Elodin said it would get better," he said.

"Elodin said to ask him in a year."

"That's not no."

"It's not yes either."

Sim looked at me with his thin earnest face. His eyes were very bright. He did not have a patch yet. That would come later too, after the Lackless house.

"Play something," he said. "Anyway. With one hand. The way you did at the grave."

"That's not playing. That's plucking open strings like a child."

"It made three hundred people cry," said Sim. "On a hill. In the rain." He looked at me. "I don't care if it's playing. I want to hear it."

---

I did not play for him that night. I put the lute back in its case and closed the lid and we ate the bread and cheese and drank the terrible wine and talked about other things.

But I lay awake afterward, in the dark, with my cold hand lying on my chest, and I thought about what he had said.

*I don't care if it's playing.*

And I thought about the Adem. About Vashet, in the small cramped room, making me do the Ketan over and over with one arm hanging useless at my side. About how, somewhere in the middle of that morning, I had stopped fighting my arm. Stopped trying to make it do what it could no longer do. And found that my body could move around it. That there was a Ketan for one arm, as well as two.

Not the same. It would never be the same.

But something.

---

I got up. I lit a candle. I took the lute out of its case again.

I did not try to play chords. I did not try to make my left hand do anything at all. I let it rest on the neck of the lute, lightly, not pressing. I let it be what it was.

And I began to play with my right hand alone.

Open strings, first. Then harmonics: the high ringing bell-notes you can make by touching a string very lightly at certain places along its length, without pressing it down. My left hand could do that. It did not need to bend. It only needed to touch, lightly, and lift away. Like laying a finger on a sleeping child's cheek.

It was not playing. Not the way I had played before. There were no chords. No fast runs. No melody in the ordinary sense.

But there were notes. Bright ringing notes, and deep open ones, and the strange floating bell-notes of the harmonics, like stars coming out one by one in a darkening sky. I could make patterns with them. Slow patterns. Spare ones. Music with a great deal of silence in it.

It reminded me of the Adem songs. The ones I had played at the Eolian my first night back, which are not really songs at all but more like patterns of breath and silence.

It was not the same.

But it was something.

---

I played until the candle burned out. Then I played in the dark.

And near dawn, very tired, not thinking, I found that my left hand had moved. Not much. Only a little. It had pressed down, very slightly, on a single string, at a single fret. Just for a moment. Just enough to change one note.

It was a clumsy note. Buzzing and dull. But it was a note that I had made with my left hand, on purpose, for the first time since the Eolian burned.

I sat very still in the grey dawn light with the lute in my lap.

Then I did it again.

---

It took nine months. I want you to know that, because the stories never say it. The stories say that Kvothe was wounded by Cinder and healed by Felurian's kiss, and that is true, in its way. Felurian's kiss gave me the warmth. It gave me the feeling back. But it did not give me the playing.

That, I did myself. With one hand, and then with one hand and a little of the other. With harmonics and open strings and slow spare patterns full of silence. With one clumsy note, and then two, and then a chord, very simple, very slow. Every night. On the road and in the twilight and in the Maer's camp in the hills. On the far side of the Stormwal, in a cave, with scrael clicking outside, playing a lullaby for a sleeping Fae prince with fingers that bled.

Nine months.

And when I sang *Tariniel* again, the second time, on the day I became Kvothe again after seven years of being Kote, in the common room of the Waystone Inn at dawn, I played it with both hands. And the people who heard it said it was the most beautiful thing they had ever heard.

But I will tell you a secret. The most beautiful thing I ever played was not that. It was not *Tariniel* at the Eolian, either, or any of the songs I played for Felurian, or the lullaby I sang to the Sleeper in the dark.

It was one clumsy buzzing note, in a small room at the top of a widow's house in Imre, at dawn, with the smell of smoke still in the air.

I have never played a better one.
