# The Tree's Voice

The Moon's Road runs north through the twilight for a very long way, and for most of that way, it runs within earshot of the Grove.

I did not know that when I bought the road from the acorn-colored mapmaker in the Hollow Market. I had thought, from the way the mapmaker spoke, that the road passed near the Grove at one point, the way a road passes near a town, and then went on its way. I had thought that once I was past the birch wood and the Sithe, I would be past the danger.

I was wrong.

The road ran north. The Grove lay to the east. And for three long days after we left the Sithe, the road curved slowly around the Grove in a great wide arc, like the rim of a wheel around its hub, never coming closer, never going further away.

And for three days, the Cthaeh talked to us.

---

It began on the first morning, as we set out from the hollow where we had slept. I heard it as a whisper at the very edge of hearing, off to the right, beyond the grey-green hills. Not words. Not yet. Just a voice. Soft and sweet and a little sad, like someone humming to themselves in another room.

Bast heard it too. I saw him stop, and turn his head, and his face go pale.

"Don't listen," I said.

"I'm not."

"You are. I can see it. Don't."

"How am I supposed to not listen?" His voice was tight. "It's right there. I can hear it. I can't just not hear it."

"You can." I took Fela's stone out of my pocket and held it up. "Hold this. Listen to it instead. It's saying no. Say it with it. Over and over. *No. No. No.* Until you can't hear anything else."

He looked at the stone. Then at me. Then, slowly, he took it.

He held it in both hands. He closed his eyes. His lips moved.

After a while, some of the color came back into his face.

"It's very stubborn," he said, without opening his eyes. "This stone."

"That's why it works."

"Where did you get it?"

"A friend gave it to me. She knows stone. She held up a building with it once, while it burned."

Bast opened his eyes and looked at me. "Your friends are very strange."

"You have no idea."

---

We walked. The voice followed. It grew louder, slowly, as the morning wore on. Not closer. Louder. As if whoever was humming in the other room had noticed us listening and had begun to hum a little more clearly, for our benefit.

By midday, I could make out words.

*Kvothe*, it said. *Little Kvothe. You came back.*

I did not answer. I walked. I held my own hand pressed flat against my chest, over the knot that hung there on its thread. *Road. Promise. A story told many times.*

*I knew you would*, said the Cthaeh. *I have been waiting. It has been so long, and so dull. No one comes to see me anymore. The Sithe are very strict.*

I walked.

*You have a friend now. A Fae princeling. With hooves.* A soft laugh, like honey poured over broken glass. *How sweet. How very like you. You always did collect strays.*

Beside me, Bast's hands tightened around Fela's stone.

*Shall I tell him about his mother?* said the Cthaeh. *He wants to know so badly. He came all this way. It would be cruel not to tell him.*

"Don't listen," I said to Bast. My voice was very quiet. "Whatever it says. Don't listen."

"I'm not," said Bast. His face was white. "I'm not listening. I'm not."

*She left because of him*, said the Cthaeh softly. *Did you know that, little prince? Because of you. She looked at you, when you were small, and she saw—*

I began to sing.

---

It was not a song I had planned. It came up out of me the way songs sometimes do, all at once, without asking. It was "Tinker Tanner." The silliest song I know. The song every child in the Four Corners learns before they can walk. A song with a thousand verses, most of them rude, about a tinker who travels the roads and gets into trouble.

I sang it loudly. As loudly as I could. I sang it the way you sing in a tavern at the end of a long night, when you are too drunk to care how you sound.

Bast stared at me.

"Sing," I said, between verses. "Sing with me."

"I don't know it."

"Everyone knows it."

"I'm *Fae*."

"Then learn it." I launched into the next verse, about the tinker and the miller's daughter and what happened in the barn. "Come on. It's easy. The chorus is the same every time."

Bast looked at me for a long moment. Then, hesitantly, he began to sing along with the chorus.

He had a good voice. Clear and light and a little wild. He did not know the words, so he sang nonsense syllables. But he sang.

And the Cthaeh's voice faded. Not gone. It was still there, underneath, a whisper at the edge of hearing. But it was hard to hear a whisper over a tinker's song sung at the top of two voices.

We sang all afternoon.

---

We made camp that night in the lee of a great grey boulder, in a hollow of grey-green grass. We were both hoarse. My throat felt like I had swallowed sand. Bast had sung himself nearly voiceless; he sat against the boulder with Fela's stone in his lap and his hooves drawn up, looking exhausted.

But the Cthaeh was still there. Quieter, now that we were not walking. But there. A soft voice in the dark, off to the east, humming to itself.

*You cannot sing all night*, it said. *You will have to sleep eventually. And when you sleep, you will dream. And in dreams, I can say anything I like.*

I did not answer. I took out my lute.

My left hand was still stiff. Still cold, though not as cold as it had been, before Felurian's kiss. I could move the fingers now, a little. Enough to make chords, if they were simple. Enough to play.

I played. Softly, this time. Not "Tinker Tanner." Something else. A lullaby. The one my mother used to sing to me when I was very small and could not sleep. I had not played it in years. I had not let myself.

I played it now. For Bast. For me. Against the dark.

Bast listened with his eyes closed. After a while, his head drooped against the boulder, and his breathing slowed, and he slept.

I kept playing.

*How long can you keep this up?* said the Cthaeh. *A night? Two? Three? Your fingers are already bleeding. That cold hand of yours won't last.*

I kept playing.

*You know I'm right*, said the Cthaeh. *You always know. That's the delicious thing about you, little key. You always know, and you always do it anyway.*

I kept playing.

*Shall I tell you something? Something true? For free, since you've been such good company?*

I kept playing. But I felt my fingers slow, just slightly.

*When you set down your name*, said the Cthaeh, very softly, *who will you trust to hold it?*

---

My fingers stopped.

I did not mean them to. They simply stopped, on their own, in the middle of a chord. The last note hung in the air for a moment, and faded.

I sat very still in the dark. My heart was pounding.

*There*, said the Cthaeh. Its voice was very gentle. *There. You've been thinking about it, haven't you? Ever since the madman at the gate. Ever since he told you. A name can be put down. You need someone to hold it for you.* A soft laugh. *You've been thinking about it every night. Who would you trust? Who would you give it to?*

I did not answer. I could not.

*Your friends? The poet? The Cealdish boy with the burned hands? The moneylender who takes blood as collateral?* The Cthaeh's voice was amused. *The stone girl? She'd hold it. She'd hold it forever. She's very good at holding things.* A pause. *Or the princeling, perhaps. Sleeping so sweetly there against his rock. He owes you a life. He'd hold it. He'd hold it very carefully. He'd hold it for years and years and years, and never give it back, because he'd be so afraid of what you'd do with it.*

Beside me, Bast stirred in his sleep. He did not wake.

*Or*, said the Cthaeh, and its voice went very soft, very sweet, *the girl.*

I closed my eyes.

*The girl with the knots. The one who ties names. She could do it, you know. She's the only one who could. The only one in all the world who knows how to tie a name into a cord so that it holds.* The Cthaeh laughed softly. *And you love her. And she loves you. And she's bound, body and soul, to the man who wants you most.*

I sat in the dark with my eyes closed and my lute in my lap and I did not move.

*Who will you trust, little key?* said the Cthaeh. *When the time comes? Who will you give your name to?*

*And what will they do with it?*

---

I did not sleep that night. I sat against the boulder with my lute in my lap and Bast asleep beside me, and I listened to the Cthaeh talk.

It did not stop. It talked all night, softly, sweetly, reasonably. It told me things. Some of them I already knew. Some of them I did not. Some of them I have never repeated to anyone, and never will.

It told me about Denna. About where she had been, and what she had done, and what she would do. It told me about Bredon, and his true name, and how old he really was. It told me about Meluan, and the child she carried, and what would happen to them both.

It told me about the door.

I listened. I could not help it. I had tried not to, all day, and I had succeeded, with Bast's help and the tinker's song. But in the dark, alone, with my throat raw and my fingers bleeding and my cold hand aching, I did not have the strength.

I listened. And every word it said went into me like a hook.

---

When the grey not-dawn of the twilight came, the Cthaeh stopped talking.

It did not fade away. It simply stopped, all at once, in the middle of a sentence, as if it had been cut off. The silence afterward was enormous.

Bast woke. He sat up, rubbing his eyes, and looked at me.

"Did you sleep?" he asked.

"No."

He looked at my face. His own face changed.

"It talked to you," he said. "All night. While I slept."

"Yes."

"What did it say?"

I looked at him. At his deep blue eyes and his fine-boned face, still soft with sleep. At the stone in his lap, Fela's stone, that he had held all night without knowing it.

"Nothing I didn't already know," I said.

It was a lie. He knew it was a lie. I saw it in his face.

But he did not press. He only looked at me for a long moment, and then he held out Fela's stone.

"Here," he said. "You need it more than I do."

I took it. It was warm from his hands.

We got up. We walked. And on the third day, at last, the road turned away from the Grove, and the voice faded behind us, and was gone.

But the hooks stayed.

I could feel them. All through me. Little silver hooks, as Felurian had said, pulling. And I knew, walking north through the grey-green hills with Bast beside me and the silver string floating ahead, that they would never come out.

*Who will you trust to hold it?*

I did not know. Not then.

But I would.
