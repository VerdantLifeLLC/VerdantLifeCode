# The Night Before

We did not sleep the night before the raid on the king's camp. None of us. We sat by the fires in the Maer's hidden valley, in twos and threes, and talked, the way people talk the night before something they may not come back from.

I went from fire to fire. I did not plan it. I only found, as the night wore on, that I could not sit still. That I needed to see each of them, one more time, before the morning.

---

Wil was sitting alone at the edge of the camp, on a rock above the stream, with his scarred hands in his lap. He was looking at the stars.

I sat down beside him. He did not look at me.

"Can't sleep?" I said.

"Don't want to." He was quiet for a moment. "If I sleep, I'll dream. And I'll dream about tomorrow. And I'd rather dream about it once, when it happens, than twice."

I looked at his hands. The skin was still pink and shiny and tight, the way skin is after a bad burn. He could move the fingers, but stiffly.

"Do they hurt?" I asked.

"All the time." He said it without any particular feeling. "Arwyl says they always will. A little." He flexed them slowly. "It's all right. It reminds me."

"Of what?"

He looked at me for the first time.

"That I did something," he said. "Once. Something that mattered." He was quiet. "I'm Cealdish, Kvothe. My father is a merchant. His father was a merchant. My whole family, back as far as anyone remembers. We count things. We weigh things. We make ledgers and balance them." He looked down at his hands. "I came to the University to learn to count better. That's all. To come home and help my father count."

"And now?"

"And now I've held up a chandelier with my mind while a Chandrian burned down a music hall. And carried a girl out of a fire. And taught thirty arcanists how to break a riverbank." He laughed softly. "My father will never understand. I'm not sure I do."

"Will you go home? After?"

Wil was quiet for a long time.

"I don't think so," he said at last. "I think I'll stay. At the University. With Kilvin." He looked at his hands. "He says there's a kind of artificing that doesn't need fine fingers. Big work. Heavy work. Bridges and dams and siege engines. He says I'd be good at it." He was quiet. "He says someone should learn it, now. With the door cracked, and things coming through. Someone should know how to build walls."

---

Sim was sitting by the big fire with a sheet of paper on his knee and a pen in his hand, not writing. He looked up as I came over and smiled, a little crookedly.

"I'm trying to write a poem," he said. "For tomorrow. For after. For whoever doesn't come back." He looked at the blank page. "It's not going very well."

I sat down beside him.

"You don't have to come," I said. "Tomorrow. You're a poet, Sim. You're not a fighter. You could stay here, with the Maer. No one would think less of you."

"I would." He looked at me. "I'd think less of me." He set down the pen. "Do you know what I was thinking about, just now? Before you came?"

"What?"

"The first time I met you. In the Mews. My first year. You were so thin, and so angry, and so proud, and you didn't have two pennies to rub together. And you sat down at our table and started talking about sympathy like you'd invented it." He laughed softly. "Wil hated you. Did you know that? For the first month. He said you were insufferable."

"He was right."

"He was. You were." Sim smiled. "But you were also the most interesting person either of us had ever met. And we couldn't stop listening to you." He was quiet for a moment. "I've been listening to you ever since, Kvothe. For three years. Through all of it. The fire and the Chandrian and the door." He looked at me. "I'm not going to stop now. Not tomorrow. I want to hear how it ends."

I did not say anything. I could not.

Sim looked back down at his blank page.

"Besides," he said, with an attempt at lightness, "if I don't come, who's going to write the poem about it? Devi? She'd rhyme *Surprise* with *explode your eyes*."

I laughed. It came out broken.

"That's not a bad rhyme," I said.

"It's a terrible rhyme." Sim picked up his pen again. "Go on. Go see the others. I'm going to try this again."

He did not write the poem that night. He wrote it later. After. When he had one eye, and a black patch with a white star on it, and a great many more things to write about.

It is still a terrible poem. But I have read it more times than I can count.

---

Devi was not by any fire. I found her in the back of a wagon, with a lantern and her satchel and a great many small clay pots laid out on a blanket in front of her, sealing them with wax.

"Go away," she said, without looking up. "I'm working."

I climbed into the wagon and sat down across from her.

"I said go away."

"I heard you."

She looked up at me over her spectacles. She had no eyebrows yet; that would come on the Field of Three Rivers. Her face was pale and tired and fierce.

"What do you want?" she said.

I reached into my shirt and took out the page. The one from the Book of Watches, folded small. The one she had given me in her rooms above the butcher's shop, the day after the Nighting. I held it out to her.

"You said you wanted it back," I said. "Someday."

Devi looked at the page for a long time. She did not take it.

"That's not someday," she said. "That's tonight. Tonight's not someday. Someday is after. When it's over and we're all sitting at Anker's getting drunk and laughing about it." She looked at me. "Put it away."

"Devi—"

"*Put it away*, Kvothe." Her voice cracked. "If you give it back to me tonight, it means you don't think you're coming back. And I won't take it. I won't." She looked back down at her clay pots. "You give it back to me at Anker's. Someday. Over a drink. That's the deal."

I looked at her for a long moment. Then I folded the page again and put it back inside my shirt.

"At Anker's," I said. "Someday."

"Good." She sealed another pot. Her hands were shaking. "And you're buying."

---

I never did give it back to her. Not really. The next time that page passed between us, it went the other way. She pressed it into my hand in an upstairs room of the Grey Man in Imre, the night before Denna left, when I did not remember who she was or what the page was or why it mattered. *You still owe me for this*, she said. *Don't you forget it.*

I did forget it. For seven years. It lay folded in the bottom of a drawer behind the bar of an inn at a crossroads, and I never once took it out.

I have it in my pocket now. I found it this morning. I intend to pay what I owe.

At Anker's. Over a drink.

---

Fela was not by a fire either. She was sitting alone on the flat rock at the head of the valley, where the Maer's cave was, with her palms pressed flat against the stone and her eyes closed.

I did not interrupt her. I sat down a little way off and waited.

After a long time, she opened her eyes and looked at me.

"It's afraid," she said. "The stone. Here. All through these hills." She looked down at her hands, flat on the rock. "It knows something's coming. It can feel the door. Far off. Stirring." She was quiet for a moment. "It's been saying no for ten thousand years. And it's afraid that tomorrow, it won't be enough."

"Will it be?"

Fela looked at me with her dark serious eyes.

"I don't know," she said. "Stone doesn't know the future. It only knows now, and before. Very long before." She took her hands off the rock. "But I'll help it. Tomorrow. Whatever happens. I'll ask it to hold."

"You held the Eolian for three hours."

"I know." She looked down at her hands. "I don't know if I can do it again. It nearly killed me."

"Then don't."

"I have to." She looked at me. "It's what I'm for, Kvothe. I didn't know it, before. I thought I was a student. I thought I'd be a scriv, maybe, or a master someday, if I was lucky. I thought stone was a thing I was studying." She was quiet for a moment. "But it's not. It's a thing I *am*. The way music is a thing you are. I didn't choose it. It chose me." She smiled faintly. "And tomorrow, it needs me."

I did not say anything for a long time.

"Sim loves you," I said at last. "You know that."

"I know." Her smile softened. "I love him too. He writes terrible poems."

"He knows."

"I like them." She looked out at the dark valley, at the scattered fires, at the small hunched shape by the biggest one, bent over a sheet of paper. "They're the only poems anyone's ever written for me. And every one of them is true, even when the rhymes are awful." She was quiet. "Keep him safe tomorrow, Kvothe. If you can. He'll try to be brave. He's no good at it."

"He's better than he thinks."

"I know." She looked at me. "That's what frightens me."

---

I did not go to Bast's fire. Bast came to mine.

I had built a small one of my own, at last, near the Maer's cave, and I was sitting beside it with my lute across my knees and my cold hand resting on the strings, not playing. It was very late. The other fires had burned down to embers. The camp was quiet.

He sat down beside me without a word. He held out his hands to the fire.

"You've been to all of them," he said. "I watched you. Wil, and Sim, and Devi, and Fela."

"Yes."

"Saying goodbye."

"Not goodbye."

"Yes it was." He looked at me. "You're not stupid, Reshi. You know what's at the door. You know what you're going to do there." He looked back at the fire. "You were saying goodbye."

I did not answer.

Bast was quiet for a long time.

"You didn't come to mine," he said at last.

"You came to mine."

"That's not the same."

"No." I looked at him. "It's not. I didn't come to yours because I'm not saying goodbye to you."

He went very still.

"You said you'd stay," I said. "Whatever I am, afterward. However much less. You said you'd stay until I'm me again." I looked back at the fire. "I'm holding you to it. So there's nothing to say goodbye for."

Bast did not say anything for a long time. When I looked at him, his face was wet in the firelight, and he was not wiping it.

"That's not fair," he said. His voice was thick. "You can't just—you can't just say something like that, the night before, and expect me to—" He stopped. He pressed his hands against his eyes.

I began to play. Softly. With my stiff cold fingers, clumsily, very slowly. The lullaby my mother used to sing. The one I had played for him in the grey-green hills, while the Cthaeh whispered.

After a while, he took his hands away from his eyes. He leaned his head against my shoulder, the way a child does. And he listened.

We sat by the fire until the sky began to grey in the east. And then we got up, and went to find the others, and walked down out of the hills toward the king's camp.

I have never been able to decide whether I said goodbye to him that night or not.

I think, perhaps, that neither of us has ever stopped saying it.
