# A Matter of Tallow

The duel was Fenton's idea. I want that on record.

He found me in the Fishery on a grey afternoon late in the autumn, when I was at my bench working on a heat-funnel for Kilvin and thinking about stone. He came and stood beside my bench with his arms folded and a smile on his face that I had seen before, on other faces, usually shortly before someone got hit.

"Kvothe," he said.

"Fenton."

"I hear you've been learning stone. With Elodin."

"Trying to."

"And not doing very well, I hear." His smile widened. "Three spans, and you can't hear a pebble."

I did not look up from my work. "Who told you that?"

"Everyone knows. It's all over the Arcanum." He leaned against my bench. "The great Kvothe, who called the wind at fifteen. Can't hear a rock."

I set down my tools. I looked at him.

Fenton was a Re'lar, like me. He was a year older than me and had been in the Arcanum a year longer, and he had always resented the fact that I had passed him. He was good at sympathy. Very good. He had beaten me in a duel once, my first year, in front of half the Arcanum, and he had never let me forget it.

He was also one of Ambrose's friends. Not one of the close ones, the ones who followed him about like hounds. But close enough to be seen with him, and to laugh at his jokes, and to pick fights on his behalf when Ambrose could not be bothered to pick them himself.

"What do you want, Fenton?"

"A rematch," said Fenton. "Sympathy. In the Mains, in the old dueling room. Tomorrow at sixth bell." He smiled. "Unless you're afraid."

---

I should have said no. I know that now. I knew it then, a little. I had more important things to worry about than Fenton's pride. There was the candle, and the stone, and the cut pages in the Archives that no one could explain. There was Denna, and her knots. There was the strange cold feeling I had been carrying since I heard about the Chandrian in the Eld.

But I was seventeen. And he had said *afraid*.

"Sixth bell," I said.

---

Sympathy duels are an old tradition at the University. Older than most of the masters, older than the Arcanum itself. They are not forbidden, exactly, but they are not encouraged either. The masters prefer to pretend they do not happen. Which means they happen a great deal, in empty rooms after dark, with an audience of students who have bet more than they can afford on the outcome.

The rules are simple. Each duelist has a candle. Each candle is identical, poured from the same batch of tallow, with a lock of the duelist's hair worked into the wax so that the candle is bound to him, sympathetically. The duelists sit at opposite ends of a table. The candles are lit.

The object is to put out your opponent's candle while keeping your own lit.

That is all. It sounds very simple. It is not. Because every trick you use against your opponent's candle, he can use against yours. Every binding you make, he can break. Every drop of heat you steal from his flame, he can steal back. It is a battle of wills, of concentration, of the ability to split your mind in two and hold two bindings at once while your opponent is trying to break both of them.

I had lost to Fenton, my first year, because he had been better at holding two bindings. I was very good at sympathy, but I was young, and I tired easily, and he had simply outlasted me.

I was not young anymore. Not in the ways that mattered.

---

The old dueling room was in the basement of the Mains, a long low room with a stone floor and a vaulted ceiling and a single long table down the middle. By sixth bell, it was packed. Word had spread, the way word always spreads at the University. The great Kvothe, returned from his mysterious travels, against Fenton, who had beaten him once before. Students were crammed along the walls three deep, and standing on benches, and sitting on each other's shoulders. Money was changing hands. A great deal of money.

Sim was there, looking anxious. Wil was there, looking bored, which meant he had bet heavily on me and was very nervous. Fela was there, standing near the door, with her arms folded and her face serious.

Fenton was already at the table when I came in. He was sitting very straight, with his hands folded, and he did not look at me.

I sat down at the other end.

The candles were brought. Two identical white tallow candles, each about the length of a hand, set in brass holders. A Re'lar I did not know, one of the ones who organized these things, took a lock of hair from each of us with a small pair of scissors and worked it into the soft wax at the base of each candle while we watched. Mine went into one candle. Fenton's into the other.

Then the candles were set in front of us, and lit.

---

For the first few minutes, nothing happened.

That is how it always goes. Each duelist feels out the other. Tests his defenses. Makes small probing bindings, the way a swordsman makes small probing thrusts, to see how his opponent responds. Fenton's first probe came almost at once: a quick thin binding, reaching across the table toward my candle, trying to link its flame to the cold stone of the floor.

I felt it coming. I broke it without effort. I sent a probe of my own back toward his candle, and he broke it just as easily.

We went back and forth like that for a while. Small bindings, quickly made and quickly broken. The crowd along the walls was very quiet. They knew this part. They were waiting for the real fight to start.

Then Fenton made his move.

It was a very good move. I will give him that. He made three bindings at once. One reaching for my candle's flame, trying to link it to the stone floor. One reaching for my candle's wax, trying to link it to a bucket of water by the door. And one reaching for me.

Not for my candle. For me.

I felt it settle on me like a cold hand on the back of my neck. A binding between me and the lock of my hair in the candle's wax. And through that, between me and the stone floor. He was trying to link *me* to the cold. To draw the heat out of my body and into the stone, so that I would be too cold to concentrate.

It was a clever trick. It was also, strictly speaking, against the rules. You were not supposed to bind your opponent directly, only his candle. But the rules of sympathy duels were never very strictly enforced.

I felt the cold begin to seep into me. I felt my fingers go numb. I felt my concentration start to waver.

And I thought about stone.

---

I had been listening to stone for three spans. Sitting with Elodin's pebble in the round room at the top of the Rookery, in my room under the eaves, in the Underthing with Auri. I had not heard it. Not yet. But I had learned something, in all that listening.

I had learned what it felt like to be patient.

Fenton was pulling the heat out of me, into the stone floor. And the stone was taking it. Cold, patient, uncaring. It did not want my heat. It did not want anything. It simply took what was given, because that was what stone did.

I stopped fighting.

I let the cold come. I let it seep into my fingers and my arms and my chest. I did not break Fenton's binding. I did not try to hold my heat. I simply let it go, the way you let go of a rope when you know you cannot win a tug of war.

And as I let go, I let my mind go still. The way I had learned in the round room. Not reaching. Not wanting. Just being there, with the stone, while the cold came in.

And I felt it.

Not the stone's voice. Not its name. I was still too far from that. But I felt its patience. The long slow endless patience of something that has been itself since before the world had a name. I felt it the way you feel the warmth of the sun on your face with your eyes closed.

And in that patience, there was room. Room for me to be cold, and not care. Room for my fingers to go numb, and my breath to go white, and my concentration to slip—and not fall.

I sat at the table in the old dueling room with my body growing colder and colder, and I was as still as a stone.

---

Fenton frowned.

I saw it. I saw him feel it, across the table. His binding was working. The heat was flowing out of me, into the floor. By all rights I should have been shivering, fumbling, losing my grip on my own candle's defenses.

But I was not. I was sitting very still, with my hands folded on the table and my eyes half closed, and my candle was burning straight and steady and yellow.

He pushed harder. I felt the cold deepen. My breath came out in a plume of white. Someone in the crowd along the wall gasped.

I did not move.

And then, very slowly, I reached across the table.

Not with a binding. With the patience. With the stillness. I reached across the table toward Fenton's candle the way stone reaches toward the sea: not quickly, not forcefully, but inevitably. I did not try to snuff it. I did not try to steal its heat. I simply laid a single thin binding between its flame and the cold stone of the floor, the same binding he had been trying to lay on mine.

And I let it sit there.

Fenton felt it. He broke it at once, with a quick hard twist of his mind.

I laid it again.

He broke it again.

I laid it again.

---

We went on like that for a long time. Longer than any sympathy duel I had ever seen. I would lay the binding, and he would break it, and I would lay it again. Over and over. Patiently. Without hurry. Without force.

It was not a clever strategy. It was not a strategy at all. It was stone. It was the sea wearing away the cliff, one wave at a time. It was the root cracking the rock, one hair's breadth a year.

And Fenton was not stone.

He was tired. I could feel it. He was holding his binding on me, drawing my heat into the floor, and he was defending his own candle against my endless patient binding, and he was doing both at once, and it was costing him. His face was pale. There was sweat on his forehead, despite the cold. His hands were clenched on the table.

And I was cold, and still, and patient, and I did not care.

"Give up," he said. His voice was strained. "You're freezing. I can see your breath. You can't hold on much longer."

I did not answer. I laid the binding again.

He broke it. His hands were shaking.

I laid it again.

"*Give up!*"

I laid it again.

And this time, he did not break it.

He tried. I felt him try. I felt his mind twist, reaching for the binding, trying to tear it loose. But he was too tired. His concentration was too frayed. He had been holding two bindings for too long, fighting too hard, and he had nothing left.

The binding held.

And his candle, very slowly, went out.

---

The old dueling room was silent.

Then it exploded. Shouts and cheers and groans and curses. Money changing hands. Students pounding on the walls. Sim leaping up and down, shouting something I could not hear. Wil sitting very still on his bench with his eyes closed and an expression of profound relief on his dark face.

I did not move. I sat at the table with my hands folded and my candle burning, and I let Fenton's binding go, and I let the heat come back into me. Slowly. The way the sun comes up in winter.

Across the table, Fenton was staring at me. His face was grey. His hands were shaking.

"How?" he said. His voice was barely a whisper under the noise of the crowd. "How did you do that?"

I thought about it.

"I stopped fighting," I said.

He stared at me. Then he laughed, a short broken sound, and put his face in his hands.

---

Fela found me afterward, outside the Mains, in the cold dark courtyard. I was sitting on the steps with my cloak wrapped around me, still shivering a little, while students streamed past me and slapped me on the back and shouted congratulations.

She sat down beside me. She did not say anything for a while.

"You heard it," she said at last. "Didn't you? The stone. In there."

"No."

"You did. I felt it. When you went still." She looked at me with her dark serious eyes. "You didn't hear the name. But you heard something."

I thought about it. About the patience. The long slow endless patience, and the room it had made in me.

"I heard it being patient," I said.

Fela looked at me for a long moment. Then she smiled. It was a small smile, and a real one.

"That's the first step," she said. "That's how it starts. You hear it being patient." She stood up. "The name comes later."

"How much later?"

She looked down at me. Her smile widened, very slightly.

"Be patient," she said.

And she walked away across the dark courtyard, laughing softly, and left me sitting on the steps of the Mains in the cold.
