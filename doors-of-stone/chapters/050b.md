# What Auri Brought

Auri came on the second night after the fire.

I did not hear her. I was lying awake in the narrow room at the top of Merra's stairs, the way I lay awake every night after the fire, with my frozen left hand wrapped in linen and resting on my chest, and the smell of smoke still in my hair no matter how many times I washed it. Threpe's men were at the door downstairs. The street outside was quiet. The ruins of the Eolian were a black shape against the stars, through the small high window.

And then there was a small soft tap on the glass. And a face at the window. Pale and thin, with a cloud of hair floating around it like a dandelion's ghost.

I got up. I opened the window with my good hand. She did not come in. She crouched on the sill outside, barefoot, balanced on the narrow ledge two stories above the alley, and looked at me with her enormous eyes.

"You're cold," she said.

"A little."

"Not a little." She looked at my wrapped hand. Her face was very serious. "The cold got into you. His cold. I can see it. It's still there, hiding under the cloth. Being cruel." She tilted her head. "That kind of cold doesn't like to leave. It sulks."

---

She would not come in. She said the room had too many corners and the corners were cross with each other and she did not want to be in the middle of an argument. So I sat on the floor under the window, with my back against the wall, and she sat on the sill above me with her feet dangling over my head, and we talked.

Or she talked, mostly. Softly, in her quick light voice, the way she did. She told me that the Underthing had been very loud the night of the fire. That the pipes had all been singing at once, and the stones had been humming, and water had run in places where water never ran. She told me that she had hidden in Mantle with her blanket over her head, and sung to the walls until they stopped shaking.

"They were frightened," she said. "The stones. All of them. All the way down. Because the stones over here were frightened." She pointed across the rooftops, toward the black shape of the Eolian. "Stones talk to each other. Did you know? Under the ground. Under the river. They're all one stone, really, if you go down far enough. And when one part is frightened, all the parts are frightened."

"The Eolian's stones held," I said. "Fela held them."

"I know." Auri's face softened. "I heard her. All night. Asking them to stand. Very politely. Very firmly." She smiled. "She has lovely manners with stone. Better than yours."

"Most people have better manners than me."

"Yes," Auri agreed. "But you're getting better."

---

She was quiet for a while. She swung her feet over my head. The night wind moved across the rooftops, cold and damp, smelling of ash and river.

"The door heard," she said.

I went still.

"The one under the Archives," said Auri. "The one that isn't a door. It heard the fire. It heard the song you sang before the fire. It heard the stones being frightened." She hugged herself. "It was listening, Kvothe. All night. Harder than it's ever listened. Like a dog with its ears up."

"Did it say anything?"

"No." She shook her head. "It never says anything. It only listens. And hums." She was quiet for a moment. "But the humming changed. After. It's different now. Lower. Like someone humming a tune they've only just remembered."

I did not know what to say to that. I sat with my back against the wall and my cold hand on my chest and I felt the hair rise on my arms.

"Are you frightened?" I said.

Auri looked down at me. Her eyes were very large and very dark in the starlight.

"Yes," she said simply. "But I sing to it. Every night. The way I always do. And it goes to sleep, after a while." She almost smiled. "It likes lullabies. Everything does, really. Even doors."

---

"I brought you things," she said, after a while.

She reached into the pocket of her dress. She took out three small things and set them on the windowsill beside her, one after another, very carefully, the way she always set things down.

The first was a feather. Grey and white, barred with black, soft as smoke.

"That's from an owl," said Auri. "She lives in the chimney over the Masters' Hall. She's very wise and very rude. She let me have it. For your hand." She looked at me. "Feathers are good for cold. They remember what it's like to be warm in a nest."

The second was a small stoppered bottle of pale gold liquid.

"Honey," said Auri. "From the bees on the roof of the Mews. I asked them. They said yes, because it's for you, and you once fixed their hive when the wind knocked it over." I had not known that. I had done it absentmindedly, on my way somewhere else. "Rub it into your wrist. Not too much. Just enough. It'll remind the skin that summer is coming."

The third was a key.

---

It was a small key. Brass, and very old, with a round bow and a short plain shaft and two teeth. It was green at the edges with age.

I looked at it. I looked at Auri.

"What does it open?" I said.

"Nothing." Auri's face was very serious. "That's why it's for you. It's a key that doesn't open anything. It's been looking for its lock for a very long time, and it never found it, and it's very tired." She touched it gently with one finger. "I thought it would understand you. Because you're looking too. For a lock. For the thing that fits."

I did not say anything.

"You don't have to find it," said Auri. "That's the secret. The key knows. It's been looking for a hundred years and it never found its lock and it's still a key. It's still exactly what it's supposed to be." She pushed it toward the edge of the sill, toward me. "Even if you never find what you're looking for. You're still you. You're still exactly what you're supposed to be."

---

I took the three things. The feather and the honey and the key. I held them in my good hand in my lap, sitting on the floor under the window.

"Thank you, Auri," I said. My voice was not steady.

"You're welcome." She looked down at me, and her face changed. It went soft, and sad, and very old. Much older than she usually looked. "Kvothe. You're going away again. Aren't you?"

"How do you know?"

"You have the look." She tilted her head. "The look of someone who's going to go very far. Further than before. Somewhere I can't follow." She was quiet. "I don't like it."

"I don't either."

"Then don't go."

"I have to."

Auri looked at me for a long time. Then she nodded. Once. Very slightly. The way she nodded when something had been settled that she did not like but could not change.

"Then take the key," she said. "And when you get where you're going, and you can't find the lock, and you're very tired, hold it. And remember that it's still a key." She stood up on the sill, balanced on her bare toes, with the night behind her. "And come back. After. Come back and tell me if you found it."

"I promise."

She looked at me. And for a moment, just a moment, there was something in her face I had never seen there before. Something like a princess, looking down from a tower. Something like a girl who had once had a name that was not Auri, and a crown, and a whole kingdom waiting for her.

Then it was gone. And she was only Auri again, small and pale and barefoot on a windowsill.

"You always promise," she said. "It's very sweet."

And she turned and was gone, across the rooftops, quick and light as a moth.

---

I used the honey on my wrist. A little, every night, the way she said. The feeling came back faster than the physician said it would. Not all of it. The hand stayed cold, the way Elodin said it always would. But it came back.

I kept the feather in the case of my lute, tucked under the strings at the neck, where it rode with me into the Fae and up into the Stormwal and down into the chamber beneath the Lackless house. It was there, still, when Denna put my lute into the chest. I suppose it is still there.

And I kept the key.

I did not know, then, what it meant. I only knew that it was small and green and old, and that it opened nothing. I carried it in my pocket, through everything, and I did not think about it very often.

But when I came back to Imre, grey and nameless, a year and a season later, and Auri came to me on the roof of the Grey Man in the dark, she looked at me for a long time with her enormous eyes. And then she reached into my pocket, without asking, and took out the key. And held it up in the starlight.

"You didn't find it," she said.

"No," I said. I did not know what she meant. I did not know her.

She looked at the key. Then at me.

"But you're still a key," she said softly. "See? I told you."

And she put it back in my pocket. And then she gave me a name. A small one. For something that happened. So that I would have something to be, while I waited.

---

The innkeeper reached into his apron pocket. Chronicler thought he would take out the knot again, or the little wooden wheel, or the white stone. But he took out something else. A small brass key, green at the edges, with a round bow and two teeth.

He set it on the table.

"It still doesn't open anything," he said. "I've tried it in every lock in the Waystone. For seven years. In the doors and the cellar and the cashbox and the chest." He almost smiled. "It doesn't fit any of them."

"Then why keep it?"

The innkeeper looked at the key for a long time.

"Because she was right," he said at last. "It's still a key. Even if it never finds its lock." He picked it up and turned it over in his fingers. "And so was I. For seven years. Behind that bar. Not fitting anything. Not opening anything." He put it back in his pocket, very carefully. "But still a key."
