# Interlude: Why a Scribe

"Why do you do it?" said Bast.

It was a little after noon. The innkeeper had gone into the kitchen to see about the bread, and for the first time in two days, Chronicler found himself alone at the table by the hearth with Bast. The young man was sitting in his backward chair, chin on his folded arms, looking at Chronicler with an expression of frank curiosity.

"Do what?"

"Write things down. Other people's things." Bast tilted his head. "You've been scratching away at that paper for two days and a half. Your hand's all cramped up. I've seen you rub it when you think no one's looking." He nodded at the stack of pages. "Why?"

Chronicler set down his pen. He flexed his fingers. They ached, as they always did by the middle of a long telling, a deep dull ache in the joints that no amount of rubbing would ease.

"It's my trade," he said.

"That's not an answer. That's a way of not answering." Bast smiled. It was not an entirely pleasant smile. "Reshi taught me that one."

Chronicler looked at him for a long moment. Then, somewhat to his own surprise, he told him.

---

"My father was a scribe," he said. "In Tinuë. In the counting-house of a wool merchant. He copied ledgers. Bills of sale. Contracts. Twelve hours a day, six days a span, for thirty years." He turned his pen over in his fingers. "He had the most beautiful hand you ever saw. Every letter perfect. And he never once in his life wrote anything that mattered."

Bast said nothing.

"When I was nine, my grandmother came to live with us. My mother's mother. She was very old, and she was dying, and she knew it. And she had stories." Chronicler smiled faintly. "Oh, she had stories. About the village she grew up in, in the hills above Rannish. About the flood that took half of it when she was a girl. About a man who could talk to bees. About the winter the wolves came down, and her father went out with a pitchfork and never came back." He was quiet for a moment. "She told them to me every night. For a year. And then she died."

"And you wrote them down."

"No." Chronicler shook his head. "That's the thing. I didn't. I was nine. I thought she'd always be there. I thought I'd always remember." He set down the pen. "And a year after she died, I realized I'd forgotten half of them. And two years after, I'd forgotten most of the rest. And now—" He spread his hands. "Now I remember the man who could talk to bees. And that's all. I don't remember what he said to them. I don't remember his name. I don't remember what happened to him."

The common room was very quiet. From the kitchen came the sound of the innkeeper moving about, the clink of a pan.

"So I learned my father's trade," said Chronicler. "But I didn't use it for ledgers. I went out into the world and found old people with stories and sat with them and wrote them down. Every word. Before they were gone." He looked at the stack of pages at his elbow. "I've been doing it for twenty years. I have trunks of them. Rooms full. Stories no one will ever read, most of them. Stories about floods and wolves and men who talk to bees." He smiled. "I can't stop. Every time I meet someone old, I think, *what are you going to take with you when you go?*"

---

Bast was looking at him with a strange expression. The curiosity had gone out of it. Something else had come in.

"That's why you came," he said slowly. "Here. For him. Not because he's famous."

"I came because Skarpi told me to." Chronicler hesitated. "And because I'd heard the stories, of course. Everyone has. But—" He stopped. He looked toward the kitchen door. "When I came in that first night, and saw him behind the bar, I thought, *there's a man carrying something heavy. And no one's ever asked him to set it down.*"

Bast did not say anything for a long time.

"He's not old," he said at last.

"No." Chronicler looked at him. "But he's been waiting to die for a long time. Hasn't he? I could see that too."

Bast's face went very still.

"Yes," he said quietly. "Yes. He has."

---

The kitchen door swung open. The innkeeper came out, carrying a loaf of bread on a board, and a knife, and a crock of butter. He set them on the table and looked from one of them to the other.

"You've been talking about me," he said mildly.

"No, Reshi," said Bast.

"Yes, Reshi," said Chronicler, at the same moment.

The innkeeper looked at them both. Then, very faintly, the corner of his mouth moved.

"Well," he said, and sat down, and began to cut the bread. "I suppose that's fair. I've been talking about me for two days." He set a slice in front of Chronicler. "Eat. Your hand needs a rest. And then I'll tell you about a set of stairs, and a door, and the worst idea I ever had." He paused. "One of the worst."

Chronicler ate. It was very good bread.

And when he picked up his pen again, a little later, he found that his hand did not ache quite so much as it had. He did not know why. But he found himself thinking of an old woman in a house in Tinuë, telling stories to a boy by the fire, and he found that he was glad, very glad, to be sitting at this table, writing this down.

Before it was gone.
