# How to Be No One

Tessit made me into no one that evening, by the light of a cookfire, with a comb and a fiddle and a great deal of patience.

"Sit," he said. "There. On the crate. And don't talk."

I sat. He stood in front of me with his battered hat pushed back on his head and his lean brown face very serious, and he looked at me for a long time. Not at my face. At all of me. The way a carpenter looks at a board before he cuts it.

"Well," he said at last. "The hair's the worst of it."

"I know."

"No, you don't." He pulled a lock of it between his fingers and held it up to the firelight. "You think it's the color. Red like a fire, red like a fox. Everyone in the Four Corners has heard about the red-haired Kingkiller, or they will soon." He let it go. "But the color's easy. Your Fae friend says he'll see to the color, tomorrow, with his glamours. It's what you *do* with it that'll hang you."

"What do I do with it?"

"You toss it." Tessit sat down on the crate across from me. "When you're thinking. You push it back off your forehead with your left hand. Like this." He did it, and it was so exactly the way I did it that I felt myself flush. "And you tilt your head, just a little, when someone's talking to you, so it falls to one side. Like a bird. Like you're listening with your whole face." He shook his head. "You've done it three times since I sat down. A man who's seen you once would know you across a field by that. Dyed or not."

---

Mirra did the combing. "A glamour changes the color," she said. "It doesn't change how you wear it. And no Ruh fiddler wears his hair like a lord's poet." She combed it flat and dull and tied it back at the neck with a scrap of string, the way the working men of the troupe wore theirs, so it would hang lank over my shoulders under a hat, while I sat on the crate with my eyes shut.

"Your mother used to do something like this," she said, as she worked. "Dyed hers. Every few months. You wouldn't remember. You were very small. She'd sit on the tailboard, and your father would do it for her, with walnut hulls, and the two of them would laugh the whole time and get it all over everything." Her fingers were gentle on my scalp. "I asked her once why she bothered. Her hair wasn't red like yours. It was dark, with only a little red in it. You had to be close to see."

"What did she say?"

"She said, *Close is what I'm worried about.*" Mirra was quiet for a moment. "I didn't understand. I thought she meant jealous wives, or creditors, or some lord she'd crossed. I didn't know about the door."

I did not say anything. I sat with my eyes shut and let her work, and thought about my mother on a tailboard, laughing, with walnut dye on her hands.

---

When the combing was done, Tessit took over again.

"Stand up," he said. "Walk to the wagon and back."

I walked to the wagon and back.

Tessit sighed.

"Again," he said. "And this time, walk like you've never killed anyone."

I stopped. I looked at him.

"You walk like a fighter," said Tessit, not unkindly. "Weight forward. Feet quiet. Hands loose. Eyes going everywhere, counting the exits." He shrugged. "I don't know where you learned it. But any soldier in that camp will see it the moment you step out of the wagon. And a soldier who sees a fighter where he expected a fiddler asks questions."

"Then how should I walk?"

Tessit thought about it.

"Like Brin," he said.

---

Brin was the fiddler of Hallow's Company. He was a stout, balding, cheerful man of about fifty, who played the fiddle with great enthusiasm and very little skill, and who walked everywhere as if the ground were slightly uneven and he were slightly drunk. Which, most of the time, he was.

"Come here, Brin," said Tessit. "Walk for the boy."

Brin walked. He walked from the fire to the wagon and back, with his belly out and his shoulders slumped and his feet shuffling in the grass. He hummed as he went. He scratched his ear. He stopped halfway to look at something on the ground, lost interest, and went on.

"There," said Tessit. "Do that."

I tried. It was the hardest thing Tessit ever asked me to do.

---

I had played a great many parts in my life. I had played them on the stage, as a child, in my father's troupe: kings and beggars and heroes and villains and once, memorably, a talking goat. I had played them in the Maer's court, and the Eolian, and the Archives, and a hundred inns and taverns across the Four Corners. I had played Kvothe the Arcane and Kvothe the Bloodless and Kvothe who talked his way into the University at fifteen. I had played the hero of every story anyone had ever told about me.

But I had never played no one.

It is harder than you would think. Everything in me wanted to be seen. Everything I had ever done had been done to be seen. The songs, the stories, the clever tricks, the brave stupid gestures. I had spent my whole life standing in the center of the stage with every light on me.

And now Tessit wanted me to stand at the back. In the shadows. With my shoulders slumped and my belly out and my feet shuffling. Humming tunelessly. Scratching my ear. Being nobody anyone would ever look at twice.

"No," said Tessit, the first time. "You're *acting* like Brin. Everyone can see you acting. Stop acting."

"How do I stop acting and still act?"

Tessit looked at me for a long moment.

"You stop wanting them to watch," he said.

---

I tried again. And again. And again. For an hour, by the light of the cookfire, while the children giggled and the dogs barked and Brin sat on a crate eating stew and offering helpful comments.

And somewhere in the second hour, I found it.

I do not know how to describe it. It was not a thing I did. It was a thing I stopped doing. I stopped reaching. I stopped listening for applause. I stopped waiting, in the back of my mind, for someone to turn and look and see me. I let my shoulders go. I let my feet go. I let my face go slack and a little stupid, and my eyes go soft, and I thought about nothing. About stew. About whether it would rain. About a tune that went nowhere.

And I walked to the wagon and back.

Nobody laughed. Tessit did not say anything. He watched me, with his lean brown face very still. Then he nodded, slowly.

"There," he said quietly. "That's it. That's no one."

---

Mirra was looking at me strangely.

"What?" I said.

"Nothing." She shook her head. "Only—" She hesitated. "Your mother could do that. Just like that. I saw her do it once, in a town where there'd been trouble. Soldiers in the square, looking at every face. She went from being the most beautiful woman in the troupe to being nobody. Just like that. Like blowing out a candle." She looked at me. "I never knew how she did it. I asked her. She said, *You just stop wanting to be found.*"

I stood by the wagon in the firelight with my lank tied-back hair and my slumped shoulders, and I thought about my mother. Blowing herself out like a candle, in a square full of soldiers. Wrapping herself in a small name, and a stained face, and a trouper's life, so that the thing beneath her family's house would never hear her.

*You just stop wanting to be found.*

---

Tessit gave me the fiddle last. An old one, scarred and cheap, with a crack in the back and strings that would not hold their tune.

"Play badly," he said. "Like Brin. On purpose. Every third note flat." He handed me the bow. "It's the hardest part. I know. You'll want to play well. Your fingers will want to. Every time they reach for the right note, you'll have to stop them."

I held the fiddle. I put it under my chin. I drew the bow across the strings, and played "Tinker Tanner," and made every third note flat.

It hurt. I want you to understand that. It hurt the way it hurts to walk on a broken ankle. Every wrong note was a small betrayal of everything I was. My fingers screamed at me. My ears screamed at me. I played the whole song through, flat and scratchy and clumsy, and when I finished, I was sweating.

Brin applauded, delighted.

"Better than me," he said. "Almost."

---

"You'll do," said Tessit, when the fire was burning low. "Tomorrow night. You'll sit on the tailboard of the third wagon and play the second fiddle, badly, and you won't look at anyone, and no one will look at you." He was quiet for a moment. "Except him. The king. He might. He's seen you before."

"He's seen Kvothe before."

"Yes." Tessit looked at me. At my lank hair and my slack face and my slumped shoulders. "Let's hope that's not who he sees."

---

He saw me anyway. You know that. I have told you. The barest pause, when his eyes found me on the tailboard. He knew. He had known the moment he saw me.

But I have thought, since, that it was not my face he knew. It was not my hair, or my walk, or the way I held the fiddle. I had done all of those perfectly. Tessit said so, afterward. He said he had never seen anyone learn to be no one so fast.

I think Ambrose knew me because he wanted to. Because he had been looking for me, every day, for a year, in every face. And a man who is looking that hard for someone will find them, no matter how well they hide.

That is the other half of what my mother knew. The half she did not tell Mirra. You can stop wanting to be found. But you cannot stop someone else from wanting to find you.

---

"That's what the inn was," said Chronicler slowly. "Wasn't it? Seven years of no one."

The innkeeper looked at him.

"Kote," said Chronicler. "The walk. The face. The polishing. The way you stand behind the bar so no one looks at you twice." He looked down at his page. "Tessit taught you that. By a cookfire. With a fiddle."

The innkeeper was quiet for a long time.

"Tessit taught me how," he said at last. "The grey did the rest. I didn't have to stop wanting to be found, behind that bar. There was nothing left to want." He looked at his hands. "But yes. I suppose it was the same thing. The same part. I just played it for seven years instead of one night."

"And now?"

The innkeeper looked at the frost on the shutters. At the thin line of darkness beneath the door.

"Now," he said, "someone's looking very hard." He picked up his cup. "And I've stopped hiding."
