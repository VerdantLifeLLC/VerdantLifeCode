# Interlude: Bast's Confession

The innkeeper stood beside the table, very still, looking at the frost on the shutters.

Chronicler looked up at him. At his red hair, and his green eyes, and the bruises on his face, fading now from purple to yellow. At his hands, hanging at his sides. They were steady. They were perfectly steady.

"What do you mean?" said Chronicler. "It hasn't happened yet?"

The innkeeper did not answer. He was looking at the window. At the frost, thick and white and feathery, covering the dark wood. At the thin line of darkness at the edge of the shutters, where the night showed through.

"He's close," he said quietly. "Closer than before. I can feel him." He touched his chest, over his heart. "Here. Where it's warm again. I can feel him, the way you feel a draft from a door that's been left open."

"Cinder."

"Ferule." The innkeeper nodded. "That's his name. His true one. I know it now." He was quiet for a moment. "I didn't, before. When I was Kote. I didn't know anything. But I told you the story, and it came back. A piece at a time. And that came back with it."

He turned from the window and looked down at Chronicler.

"I know a great many things now," he said. "That I didn't know three days ago."

---

Bast had not moved. He was still kneeling on the floor beside the innkeeper's empty chair, where he had knelt when Aaron left. His head was bowed. His hands were clenched in his lap.

"Reshi," he said. His voice was very small.

The innkeeper looked down at him.

"I have to tell you something," said Bast. "Before he comes. I have to tell you, or I'll never be able to."

"I know, Bast."

"No. You don't." Bast looked up. His face was white, and wet, and his deep blue eyes were full of something terrible. "You don't know. You can't know. I never told you. I never told anyone."

The innkeeper looked at him for a long moment. Then he pulled out his chair and sat down in it, facing Bast, with his hands on his knees.

"Then tell me," he said gently.

---

Bast did not speak right away. He knelt on the floor with his hands clenched and his head bowed, and his shoulders were shaking. Chronicler sat very still at the table and did not move, did not write, did not breathe.

"The soldiers," said Bast at last. "The two soldiers. Two nights ago." He swallowed. "I sent them."

The common room was silent.

"I found them on the Rannish road," said Bast. His voice was very quiet, very flat, as if he were reciting something he had memorized. "A span before. Deserters. Drunk. Mean. I followed them for a day. I listened to them talk. They were going to rob a farm near Baedn. Kill the farmer, maybe. They talked about it like it was nothing." He was quiet for a moment. "And I thought, they're exactly what I need."

"Bast—"

"Let me finish." Bast's voice cracked. "Please. Let me finish, Reshi, or I'll never be able to say it."

The innkeeper was silent.

"I went to them," said Bast. "At their camp, that night. I glamoured myself. Made myself look like a merchant. Rich. Frightened. I told them there was an inn in Newarre, the Waystone, with an innkeeper who had gold hidden in a chest in his room. Lots of gold. I told them he was soft. A coward. Wouldn't fight back." He took a shaking breath. "I told them where to find you. I told them when."

He looked up at the innkeeper. His face was naked with grief.

"I wanted them to hurt you," he said. "Not kill you. Just hurt you. Enough to—enough to make you *angry*. Enough to make you fight back. Enough to make you remember what you were." He was weeping now. "I thought if you were in danger—real danger—your body would remember. Your hands. Your feet. Your name. I thought you'd fight them, and win, and it would wake something up in you. Like the scrael did. Like it did on the north road, that first night, when you came back with blood on your hands and color in your face." He pressed his hands against his eyes. "I thought it would *wake you up*."

"And it didn't."

"It didn't." Bast's voice was muffled. "You just stood there. You let them hit you. Over and over. You didn't raise your hands. You didn't do *anything*." He took his hands away from his face. His eyes were red and wild. "And I stood in the kitchen and watched it. And I waited. And I kept thinking, *now, now he'll fight back, now*. And you didn't. And they kept hitting you. And I couldn't—"

He stopped. He put his hands over his mouth.

"I killed them," he whispered through his fingers. "I came out of the kitchen and I killed them. Both of them. With my hands. Because I couldn't stand it anymore. Because I'd done it, I'd brought them here, and they were hurting you, and you weren't fighting back, and it was all my fault—"

He broke down completely then. He folded over on the floor, with his face in his hands and his forehead nearly touching the boards, and he wept. Great heaving sobs that shook his whole body.

---

The innkeeper did not move for a long time. He sat in his chair with his hands on his knees and looked down at Bast, weeping on the floor at his feet.

His face did not change. It was calm. Very calm. The calm of someone who has been expecting something for a long time, and is not surprised when it comes.

"I know," he said at last.

Bast went still. Then, very slowly, he lifted his head.

"What?"

"I know," said the innkeeper. "I've known since the second night. Since I sat in the kitchen with a wet cloth on my face and thought about it." He looked down at Bast. "They said something. The soldiers. While they were hitting me. One of them said, *the merchant said you were soft*. And I thought, what merchant? And then I thought about you. And how you'd been talking in your room at night, for a year. Making plans." He was quiet for a moment. "And I knew."

Bast stared up at him. His face was white and wet and utterly still.

"You knew," he whispered. "And you didn't say anything."

"No."

"*Why?*"

The innkeeper looked at him for a long moment.

"Because you did it out of love," he said simply. "The way Denna took the box out of love. The way she caught my name out of love, and tied it, and walked away out of love." He reached out and laid his hand on Bast's dark head. "You've been doing things out of love for me for a very long time, Bast. Some of them were foolish. Some of them were brave. Most of them were both."

Bast made a sound. A small, broken sound.

"The Cthaeh said," he whispered. "In the net. It said I'd keep you in a cage of my own making. And call it love."

"I know. You told me. Tonight."

"And I did." Bast's voice cracked. "I did it, Reshi. I kept you here. In this inn. In this town. Being small. Being quiet. For years. Because I was afraid. Of what you'd do if you remembered. Of what you'd become. Of what would come for you." He pressed his face against the innkeeper's knee. "And then I saw it was killing you. And I tried to break the cage. And I brought those men here to hurt you. And that was love too. Wasn't it? That was love, and it was the cage, and it was all the same thing, and I couldn't tell the difference—"

"No one can," said the innkeeper quietly. "That's what the Cthaeh knew. That's why it said it." He stroked Bast's hair. "It didn't tell you anything that wasn't true, Bast. It just made you afraid of it. And being afraid of it made you do things you wouldn't have done otherwise." He was quiet for a moment. "That's how it works. That's how it always works."

---

Bast was quiet for a long time. He knelt with his face against the innkeeper's knee, and his shoulders slowly stopped shaking.

"There's more," he said at last. His voice was muffled. "Not just the soldiers."

"I know."

"You don't. You can't." Bast lifted his head. "The scribe. Chronicler." He looked across the table at Chronicler, who had not moved, who was sitting very still with his pen motionless in his hand. "Skarpi didn't just send him. I asked Skarpi to send him."

Chronicler went very still.

"After Shep died," said Bast. "I went looking for Skarpi. I went to the crossroads where we met him, the first time. He wasn't there. So I waited. I waited for nine days, sitting on that bench outside that empty inn. And on the tenth day, he came." Bast looked at the innkeeper. "I told him everything. About the scrael. About the skin dancer. About you, getting greyer every year. Fading. I told him you were dying, Reshi. Slowly. Like my mother did, in the twilight."

The innkeeper did not say anything.

"And I asked him," said Bast, "when the scribe was coming. The one he'd told you about. The one who'd ask for the story." His voice cracked. "And he looked at me with those bright bird's eyes of his, and he said, *whenever you like, little prince. The scribe is only waiting to be sent.*"

"So you told him to send me," said Chronicler. His voice sounded strange in his own ears.

"I told him to send you." Bast looked at him. "I'm sorry. I didn't know you. I didn't know anything about you. I only knew that Skarpi said a scribe would come, and Reshi would tell him the story, and the telling would bring his name back." He looked at the innkeeper. "Skarpi told me that. At the crossroads. He said, *stories are names too. The oldest kind. Tell a man's whole story and you speak his name.*" His voice dropped. "And he said, *but be careful, little prince. A name coming back is a very loud thing. It will be heard.*"

"And you sent me anyway," said Chronicler.

"I sent you anyway." Bast looked down at the floor. "Because I couldn't stand it anymore. Watching him fade. Watching him polish the bar and pour the drinks and wait. I thought, better he's himself again and in danger, than safe and nothing." His voice was very small. "Was that wrong?"

Nobody answered.

---

The innkeeper looked down at Bast for a long time. His face was very calm. His hand was still resting on Bast's dark head.

"Get up, Bast," he said quietly.

Bast looked up at him.

"Get up," said the innkeeper again. "Please. Sit in your chair. Like you've been sitting for three days. Backward, with your arms on the back." He smiled, very faintly. "I've grown used to it. It would be strange, telling the rest of this without you in your chair."

Bast stared at him. Then, slowly, he got up off the floor. He picked up his chair from where it had fallen and set it on its feet. He turned it backward and sat in it, with his arms folded across the back, and his chin on his arms.

His face was wet and red and utterly miserable. But he was sitting in his chair.

"Now," said the innkeeper. He looked at Bast, and then at Chronicler. "Now I'm going to tell you both something. And I want you to listen."

---

"It wasn't wrong," he said.

Bast went very still.

"It wasn't wrong," the innkeeper said again. "Sending the soldiers. Sending the scribe. Keeping me here. Letting me go. None of it." He looked at Bast. "It wasn't right either. It was only a choice. You made it. And you've borne what came after." He paused. "That's the Lethani. That's all anyone can do."

"But the Cthaeh—"

"The Cthaeh told you that you'd keep me in a cage and call it love. And you did." The innkeeper's voice was very gentle. "But it didn't tell you the rest. It didn't tell you that you'd also be the one to open the cage. That you'd see it was a cage, and you'd break it, even though it terrified you. Even though it meant this." He gestured at the frost on the windows. "It didn't tell you that part, because it didn't want you to know. Because knowing it would have made you less afraid."

Bast stared at him.

"You did both, Bast," said the innkeeper. "You kept me safe, and you let me go. You built the cage and you broke it. That's what love is, sometimes. Both at once." He smiled faintly. "The Cthaeh only ever tells you half of the truth. The half that hurts."

---

Bast did not say anything. He sat in his backward chair with his chin on his arms and his wet eyes on the innkeeper's face, and he did not move.

Then, very slowly, something in his face began to change. The misery did not go away. But something else came into it. Something that had not been there in a very long time.

It looked a little like hope.

"Reshi," he said. "Are you—" He stopped. He swallowed. "Are you back? Are you yourself again?"

The innkeeper looked at him for a long moment.

"Not all the way," he said. "Not yet. Most of me is back. The story brought it back. Everything I told, I remembered. Everything I remembered, I got back." He touched his chest. "But not the name. Not the true one. That's still in the chest. Upstairs. In a cord of Denna's hair." He looked up at the ceiling. "I can feel it. For the first time in years, I can feel it. Up there. Humming." He was quiet for a moment. "Calling me."

"Then open it."

"I can't." The innkeeper smiled, a small crooked rueful smile. "That's the joke, Bast. That's the beautiful game. The chest will only open for Kvothe. And I'm not Kvothe. Not yet. Not until I have my name." He spread his hands. "And my name is in the chest."

Bast stared at him.

"So you're stuck," he said slowly. "Halfway. Almost yourself. With your name right there, upstairs, and no way to get it."

"Yes."

"And Cinder's coming."

"Yes."

"And he can hear you. Now that you're almost yourself again. He can hear you, and he's coming, and when he gets here—"

"He'll want the name," said the innkeeper quietly. "He'll want to take it to the door. And he'll want to take me with it. Because I'm the key. Or I will be, the moment I open that chest." He looked at the frost on the window. "And if he takes me, and the name, and the box with no lid—"

He did not finish. He did not need to.

---

The common room was silent.

Outside, very faintly, beyond the frost-covered shutters, something moved. A sound. Soft and slow. Like footsteps on frozen grass.

The fire in the hearth flickered. It had been burning yellow, bright and heatless, for hours. Now it flickered.

And turned blue.

The innkeeper looked at it. His face did not change.

"There he is," he said quietly.
