# Chapter Eight: A Story Tied

The Grey Man was an inn on the western edge of Imre, a tall narrow building of grey stone with a slate roof and a sign that showed a man in a grey cloak with his face in shadow. It was not a cheap inn, but it was not one of the grand ones either. It was the kind of place where respectable merchants stayed when they came to Imre on business, the kind of place where the sheets were clean and the food was plain and nobody asked questions.

Denna was sitting in the common room when I came in, at a table near the window with a pot of tea in front of her and a book in her lap. She looked up as the door opened, and when she saw me, she smiled.

It was a real smile. Not the careful one she wore for strangers. It went all the way up to her eyes.

"You came," she said.

"You knew I would."

"I hoped you would." She closed the book and set it aside. "That's not the same thing."

I sat down across from her. She poured me a cup of tea without asking. We sat in silence for a while, sipping our tea and looking out the window at the grey street. It was not an uncomfortable silence. It was the kind of silence that only happens between people who know each other very well, or who want to.

"I'm sorry," she said at last. "About yesterday."

"You don't have to be."

"I do. I was cruel. You were only trying to help." She looked down into her cup. "I'm not used to people trying to help me. I don't know what to do with it."

"You could let them."

She laughed softly. "Maybe. Someday."

We were quiet again for a while. Then I took her letter out of my pocket and unfolded it and laid it on the table between us.

"This," I said, and touched the knot in the corner. "What is it?"

She looked at the knot for a long moment. Then she looked at me. There was something careful in her face, something guarded.

"Why do you ask?"

"Because I've seen it before. Or something like it." I hesitated. "In a book. And in something else."

"What book?"

"An old one. In the Archives."

She was quiet for a long time. Then she reached up and touched her hair, the intricate braid that wound around her head like a crown. Her fingers found something there and held it.

"It's Yllish," she said. "A story knot."

"I know that much. But what does it mean?"

She hesitated. Then she reached into her hair and drew out a thin cord, dark brown, about as long as her forearm. It had been woven into her braid so cleverly that I had never noticed it. She laid it on the table beside the letter.

The cord was knotted. Dozens of knots, maybe hundreds, running along its length. Some were small and simple. Some were large and complex, looping and twisting around each other in patterns that made my eyes ache to follow. It looked, at first glance, like a child's toy. A bit of string that someone had fiddled with to pass the time.

But the longer I looked at it, the more it seemed to mean something. The way a page of writing in a foreign script can seem to mean something, even when you cannot read a word of it.

"Everyone thinks the Yllish knots are just decoration," Denna said quietly. "Pretty things, like embroidery. But they're not. They're a language. A very old one. Older than Tema, older than Aturan. Some people say it's the oldest writing in the world."

"Can you read it?"

"Some of it." She touched one of the knots with the tip of her finger. "This one means *beginning*. Or *birth*. Or *spring*. It depends on what's around it. This one means *road*. This one means *a promise*. This one—" she touched a large, complex knot near the center, "—this one means something like *a story that is told many times and changes in the telling*. There isn't a word for it in Aturan."

I stared at the cord. "Who taught you this?"

She looked up at me. For a long moment she did not answer.

"My patron," she said at last.

I felt something cold settle in my chest. "Why?"

"Because it's part of the song." She wrapped her arms around herself, the way she did when she was uncomfortable. "The song isn't just a song, Kvothe. It's—" She struggled for the words. "It's a history. A true history. Or it's meant to be. My patron says that the old stories were written down wrong. That the people who wrote them had their own reasons for telling them the way they did. And that if you want to know what really happened, you have to go back to the oldest sources. The ones that were written before anyone had a reason to lie."

"And those sources are written in knots."

"Some of them." She nodded. "He has a collection. Cords and ropes and tapestries, very old, from all over the world. Some of them are so old they crumble if you touch them. He's been teaching me to read them. So I can write the song properly. So I can tell the truth."

"The truth about Lanre."

She looked at me sharply. "Yes."

I took a deep breath. I did not want to fight with her again. I did not want to lose her again. But I could not let it go.

"Denna," I said carefully. "I've heard the story of Lanre. The one the old storytellers tell. And in that story, Lanre was a traitor. He betrayed his friends. He destroyed the city of Myr Tariniel. He killed thousands of people."

"I know that story."

"Then why—"

"Because it's not the whole story." Her voice was calm, but there was steel beneath it. "It's the story the winners told. It's the story Selitos told, after he cursed Lanre. After he blinded himself with a stone, so he'd never have to look at what he'd done." She leaned forward. "Did you know that part? That Selitos blinded himself?"

"Yes."

"Why would he do that? Why would a man put out his own eye, if he were only punishing a traitor?" Her eyes were very bright. "Maybe because he knew he was wrong. Maybe because he couldn't bear to look at the truth."

I opened my mouth to argue. Then I closed it again.

I did not know the answer. I had never thought about it. In Skarpi's story, Selitos had put out his own eye as a kind of oath. A sacrifice. A way of binding himself to his purpose, so that he would never be deceived again. That was how I had always understood it.

But I had never asked why. I had never questioned it.

"I don't know," I said at last.

Denna sat back. She looked, for a moment, almost disappointed. As if she had wanted me to argue. As if she had wanted me to have an answer.

"Neither do I," she said quietly. "That's why I'm writing the song."

---

We talked for hours. I asked her about the knots, and she told me what she could. She showed me the simple ones, the ones that stood for common words and ideas, and she showed me how they combined to make more complex meanings. She showed me how the direction of a loop could change a word from past to future, and how a double twist could make it a question instead of a statement. She showed me how some knots could only be read in relation to the knots around them, the way a single note of music means nothing until you hear the notes that come before and after.

I was fascinated. I had always loved languages, and this was a language unlike any I had ever encountered. It was not written in lines on a page, flat and still. It was written in three dimensions, in loops and twists and tensions. It had a *shape*. When you held a knotted cord in your hands, you could feel its meaning, the way you could feel the shape of a word in your mouth.

And there was something else. Something I could not quite put my finger on. As Denna showed me the knots, as I held the cord in my hands and turned it this way and that, I felt the same strange prickling I had felt when I looked into the heart of Kilvin's candle. The same sense of something almost there. Something just out of reach.

"Denna," I said. "When you tie these knots. When you write in them. Does it feel like anything?"

She looked at me for a long moment. "What do you mean?"

"I mean, when you tie a knot that means *road*, does it feel like—" I did not know how to say it. "Does it feel like more than just a knot? Does it feel like you're making something?"

Something flickered across her face. It was there and gone so fast I almost missed it. But I saw it. It was fear.

"Sometimes," she said very quietly. "Sometimes it does."

"What does it feel like?"

She looked down at the cord in my hands. "It feels," she said slowly, "like the knot is holding on to something. Like it's reaching out and catching hold of something and not letting go." She shivered. "My patron says that's how you know you've tied it right. When it holds."

"Holds what?"

She shook her head. "I don't know. He won't tell me. He says I'm not ready yet."

I looked at the cord. At the hundreds of knots running along its length, each one holding on to something, each one reaching out and not letting go.

"Denna," I said. "This song. When it's finished. What will it do?"

She looked at me. Her eyes were very dark.

"It will tell the truth," she said. "That's all. It will tell the truth about Lanre, and everyone who hears it will know what really happened." She paused. "My patron says that's the most important thing in the world. The truth. He says that a lie told long enough becomes a kind of prison. That the old story of Lanre is a prison. And that my song will set him free."

"Set who free?"

She did not answer. She reached out and took the cord from my hands and began to weave it back into her hair, her fingers moving quickly and surely.

"I should go," she said. "It's getting late."

"Denna—"

"I'll be here until the end of the span." She stood up. "Come back tomorrow. I'll teach you more, if you want."

"I want."

She smiled at me. It was the careful smile again, the one for strangers. But there was something beneath it, something sad and tired and very, very young.

"I know," she said. "That's what worries me."

---

I went back the next day, and the day after that, and every day until the end of the span. Denna taught me the knots, and I learned them faster than she expected. I have always learned quickly, and this was a language that seemed to want to be learned. By the end of the span, I could read simple cords on my own, and I could tie a few simple words.

But I never tied a knot that held. I tried. I tried every day, tying and retying, feeling for that strange sense of something reaching out and catching hold. It never came. My knots were only knots. Loops of string, twisted and tied. They meant things, but they did not *do* anything.

Denna's did. I could feel it. When she tied a knot, something changed. The air around her hands seemed to tighten, the way the air tightens before a storm. And when she was done, the knot was not just a knot. It was a thing that held.

I asked her once how she did it. She looked at me for a long moment and then shook her head.

"I don't know," she said. "It just happens. Sometimes it holds and sometimes it doesn't. I can't make it happen. I can only let it."

It sounded like something Elodin would say. It sounded like the way I had called the wind.

It sounded like naming.

---

On the last day of the span, Denna left.

She did not say goodbye. She never said goodbye. I came to the Grey Man in the morning and her table by the window was empty, and the innkeeper told me she had paid her bill and gone before dawn. She had left a note for me. It was folded small and sealed with plain wax.

Inside, there were no words. Only a single knot, tied in a short length of dark cord and pressed flat between the folds of the paper.

I held it up to the light. It was a complex knot, looped and twisted in a pattern I had never seen before. I turned it this way and that, trying to read it. It took me a long time. It was not a word I knew, but I could make out pieces of it. A knot for *road*. A knot for *promise*. And at the heart of it, holding it all together, a knot I had only seen once before, in the center of the cord she wore in her hair.

*A story that is told many times and changes in the telling.*

I did not know what it meant. I still do not know, not entirely.

But when I held it in my hand, I felt it. Just for a moment. The faint tightening of the air. The sense of something reaching out and catching hold.

It held.

I have it still. I have carried it with me every day since. Through everything that happened after, through fire and blood and darkness, through the loss of everything I had and everything I was, I have kept that little knot of cord.

It is the only thing she ever gave me that she did not later take back.
