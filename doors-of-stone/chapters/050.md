# Interlude: Supper

The door of the Waystone opened, and the regulars came in out of the dusk.

There were more of them than usual. Graham and Old Cob and Jake, of course. Aaron the smith's apprentice. Carter, back from wherever he had been, with his arm in a sling and a sour look on his face. Shep's widow, who did not usually come in, and her sister, who came in with her. A pair of farmers from the far end of the valley whose names Chronicler never learned. They came in stamping and talking and shaking the evening chill from their coats, and they filled the common room with noise and warmth and the smell of woodsmoke and wet wool.

The innkeeper rose to meet them. It was the same smooth motion Chronicler had seen at noon, and the same thing happened. Something went out of him as he stood. The man who had been sitting at the table, with his voice like dark honey and his eyes like green fire, went away, and in his place was Kote, the innkeeper, mild and tired and bruised.

"Evening," he said. "Stew's on. Bast made it."

"Bast made it?" Graham looked alarmed. "Is it safe?"

"I heard that," said Bast, coming out of the kitchen with a stack of bowls.

"You were meant to," said Graham, and grinned.

---

The stew was, as it turned out, very good. Better than good. Bast stood behind the bar with his arms folded and watched everyone eat it with an expression of fierce satisfaction, and when Old Cob grudgingly admitted that it was the best stew he'd had in a month, Bast looked so pleased that Chronicler thought he might burst.

"Thyme," Bast said. "That's the secret. I didn't forget the thyme."

"Good lad," said Kote, without looking up from the bar.

They talked, as they always talked. About the weather, and the harvest, and the war. Carter told a long story about how he had broken his arm, which involved a cart, a mule, and a woman in Baedn whose husband had come home early. Jake told a story about his cousin's wedding, which involved a pig. Old Cob told a story about something that had happened when he was a boy, which involved a great deal of detail and no discernible point.

Kote poured drinks, and laughed in the right places, and said nothing.

And then Aaron, who had been quiet all evening, said, "I heard something else. In Rannish. When I was there for the iron."

The room went quiet.

"About Kvothe?" said Jake.

Aaron flushed. "Sort of. About a song." He looked down at his cider. "The man at the inn, the traveler. He sang some of it. He said it was Kvothe's song. The one he sang in Imre, the night the Eolian burned."

Behind the bar, Kote had gone very still. His cloth had stopped moving on the wood.

At the table by the hearth, where Chronicler sat, Bast had turned his head.

"What song?" said Graham.

"It's called *Tariniel*," said Aaron. "It's about the old stories. About Lanre and the white city. The man said it's forbidden now, in Vintas. The Penitent King banned it. Anyone caught singing it gets their tongue cut out." He looked up. "But people sing it anyway. In secret. In the hills, and the villages, and the back rooms of inns. The man said it's the most beautiful song he ever heard." He hesitated. "He sang some of it. Just a little. Very quietly. And everybody in the inn stopped talking and listened. And when he was done, there was a woman at the next table crying, and she didn't even know why."

The Waystone was silent. Everyone was looking at Aaron.

"Sing it," said Old Cob.

Aaron went red to the roots of his hair. "I can't sing."

"Just a bit. Just what you remember."

"I don't remember it right. I only heard it once."

"Doesn't matter. Sing what you remember."

Aaron looked around the room. At Graham and Jake and Carter and Old Cob, all looking at him. At Shep's widow, sitting very still with her hands in her lap. At Kote, behind the bar, with his cloth motionless on the wood and his face completely blank.

Then he took a breath, and he sang.

---

It was only a few lines. His voice was rough and untrained, the voice of a young man who had never sung anything but drinking songs and hymns. He did not have the melody quite right. He stumbled over one of the words.

But it did not matter.

He sang about a white city on the last day of its life. About towers rising against a blue sky, and gardens full of flowers, and children playing in the fountains. About a woman singing in a great hall, her voice rising like a bird.

It was only a few lines. And when he finished, the Waystone was silent.

Shep's widow was weeping. She sat very still at her table with her hands folded in her lap and tears running down her face, and she did not wipe them away.

Graham cleared his throat. "Well," he said. His voice was rough. "That's a sad one."

"It's not sad," said Shep's widow softly. "It's—" She stopped. She shook her head. "I don't know what it is. But it's not sad."

No one said anything.

Behind the bar, Kote picked up his cloth and began to polish the bar again. His hands were perfectly steady. His face was perfectly calm. He did not look at anyone.

But Chronicler, watching from the table by the hearth, saw that the cloth was moving in circles over the same spot of wood, over and over, the way a man's hands move when his mind is somewhere else entirely.

---

They left late. Later than usual. They lingered at the bar and at the tables, talking in low voices, and when they finally went, they went slowly, the way people go when they do not want to leave a warm room for a cold night. Shep's widow was the last. She stopped at the door and looked back at Kote, standing behind the bar.

"Kote," she said. "That song. Do you know it?"

Kote looked at her. His face was mild and pleasant.

"I've heard of it," he said.

She looked at him for a long moment. Then she nodded slowly.

"If you ever hear it," she said, "the whole of it. Will you tell me? I'd like to hear the rest."

"If I ever hear it," said Kote, "I'll tell you."

She went out into the night. The door closed behind her.

Kote stood behind the bar for a long time. Then he set down his cloth and came back to the table by the hearth and sat down across from Chronicler.

He did not say anything. He sat with his hands folded on the table in front of him, and he looked at them.

"It's still sung," Chronicler said quietly. "Your song. After all this time."

"So it seems."

"You didn't know?"

"I knew it was banned. I didn't know it was still—" He stopped. He was quiet for a long time. "I didn't know."

Bast had come to stand beside the table. He looked down at the innkeeper, and his face was very strange. It was full of something that Chronicler could not name. Something that looked almost like hope.

"Reshi," he said softly. "Do you hear it? They're still singing it. After everything. They're still singing your song."

The innkeeper did not look up.

"It's not my song, Bast," he said. "It never was. It belongs to the white city. To the children in the fountains." He was quiet for a moment. "I was only the one who sang it."

"But they remember it. They remember *you*."

"They remember a song." The innkeeper looked up. His green eyes were very tired. "That's all. A song. Songs outlive their singers. That's what they're for."

Bast looked at him for a long moment. Then he turned away. He went to the window and stood looking out at the dark.

"It's more than that," he said, without turning around. "You know it is."

The innkeeper did not answer.

---

The fire had burned low. Chronicler got up and put another log on it, and the flames caught and crackled and rose, and the shadows in the common room danced.

When he sat back down, the innkeeper was looking at him.

"You're tired," he said.

"A little."

"We can stop. Pick it up in the morning."

"No." Chronicler picked up his pen. "No. You said three days. Today is the third day. If we stop now, we'll never finish."

The innkeeper looked at him for a long moment. Something moved in his face. Respect, perhaps. Or something like it.

"All right," he said. "But I warn you. The rest of it is long. And it's not pleasant. There's war, and death, and a great deal of foolishness. Most of it mine."

"I've recorded wars before."

"Not like this one." The innkeeper leaned back in his chair. "And there's a journey. A long one. Through places you've only read about in stories. The Fae. The mountains. The ruins of the white city." He paused. "I met a god there. Or something close enough to one that the difference doesn't matter."

Chronicler's pen stopped.

"Selitos," he said.

The innkeeper smiled. It was a small, strange, weary smile.

"We'll get there," he said. "First I have to tell you about Elodin. And a place called Haven. And what he told me there, about what it costs to lose your name." He looked at Bast's back, at the window. "And then I have to tell you about how I met Bast."

At the window, Bast went very still.

"Reshi," he said. "You don't have to tell that part."

"Yes, I do." The innkeeper's voice was gentle. "It's part of the story, Bast. You're part of the story. You always were." He turned back to Chronicler. "Get a fresh page. This is going to take a while."

Chronicler got a fresh page.

Outside, the night was very dark. There was no moon. The wind had died, and the trees at the edge of the fields stood still and black against the stars.

And beyond the trees, very faintly, where the smoke had leaned all day, a light was burning. Small and steady and far away.

It was blue.
