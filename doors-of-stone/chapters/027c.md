# One Ear

Meluan sent for me again three days later. Not through the Maer, and not through Stapes. A maid I had never seen before found me in the library, late in the afternoon, and curtsied, and said that her ladyship wished to see me, if I was not otherwise engaged, and would I follow her please, and could I be quiet on the stairs.

I followed her. Up a narrow back stair I had not known was there, and along a corridor hung with faded tapestries, to a small door at the end. The maid knocked twice, opened it, curtsied again, and was gone.

It was not the room where I had met Meluan before. That had been a formal room, a lady's receiving room, all silk and gilt and high-backed chairs. This was something else. A small room at the top of the house, under the eaves, with a sloping ceiling and a single round window. There was a narrow bed, and a washstand, and a chest at the foot of the bed. The walls were bare. The floor was bare boards, scrubbed pale.

Meluan was sitting on the bed. She was not dressed as a lady. She was wearing a plain grey wool dress, like a servant's, and her dark hair was loose down her back, and her feet were bare. She looked, I thought, about fourteen years old.

"Close the door," she said.

I closed it.

---

"This was my room," she said. "When I first came here. Before we were married. Alveron gave it to me because I asked for somewhere small. He thought it was very strange. He had forty rooms ready for me, each one grander than the last." She almost smiled. "I didn't want any of them. I wanted a room like the one I grew up in. Under the eaves. With a round window. So I could lie in bed at night and look at the stars and pretend."

"Pretend what?"

She did not answer. She reached down to the chest at the foot of the bed and opened it. It was nearly empty. Some folded linens. A small wooden box. And, lying on top of the linens, something small and grey and soft.

She lifted it out and held it in her lap.

It was a rabbit. A child's toy, made of grey felt, stuffed with something that had gone lumpy with age. It had two black buttons for eyes, one of them hanging by a thread. And it had one ear. Where the other ear should have been, there was only a small ragged hole, neatly darned shut with grey thread.

---

"Her name was Moss," said Meluan. She was looking down at the rabbit. "The rabbit. Netalia named her. She made her for me when I was four, out of an old cloak and two buttons from Father's waistcoat. She got in terrible trouble for the buttons."

I did not say anything.

"The ear came off when I was six," said Meluan. "The dog got it. I cried for two days. Netalia tried to sew a new one on, but she was terrible at sewing, and it came out crooked, and I cried harder." She touched the darned hole with one finger. "So she took it off again, and darned the hole, and told me Moss had lost her ear in a great battle with a dragon, and it was a badge of honor, and only the bravest rabbits had one ear." She smiled faintly. "I believed her. I believed everything she said."

"I believed my mother too," I said. "Everything she told me."

Meluan looked at me.

"What did she tell you?" she said. "About—about before. About where she came from."

"Nothing," I said. "She never talked about it. Not once. I asked, sometimes, when I was little. She always said she came from the road, like everyone else in the troupe. That the road was her mother and her father and her house." I was quiet for a moment. "I didn't know she had a sister. I didn't know anything."

Meluan looked down at the rabbit again.

"I was holding Moss," she said, "the night she left."

---

"I was asleep," said Meluan. "We shared a room, you know. At the house. Under the eaves, with a round window, like this one. She had the bed by the window and I had the bed by the door. And that night I went to sleep with Moss under my chin, the way I always did. And when I woke up, her bed was empty." She was quiet for a moment. "And her things were gone. Her clothes, and her comb, and the little silver mirror Grandmother gave her. And her lute. She'd been learning the lute, in secret. From a trouper who came through at midsummer. Father didn't know." She swallowed. "I didn't know either. Not until that morning. When I saw the empty place against the wall where she'd kept it hidden under a blanket."

"She didn't say goodbye."

"No." Meluan's hands tightened on the rabbit. "She didn't say anything. She just went. In the night. Like a thief. And I lay in my bed for an hour, holding Moss, looking at her empty bed. And I didn't cry. I remember that. I didn't cry at all. I just lay there and thought: *she'll come back. She's only gone for a walk. She'll come back for breakfast.*"

"But she didn't."

"She didn't." Meluan looked up at the round window, at the late afternoon light coming in, gold and slanting. "I waited for her to come back for years. Every night. I'd lie in bed with Moss and look at the stars through the window and pretend she'd come climbing in through it. With her lute. Laughing. Saying she was sorry, she'd only been gone a little while, and wasn't it a grand adventure." Her voice broke. "I did that for years. Long after Father died. Until I was twenty, and far too old for rabbits. And then I stopped. Because there was no point anymore. She wasn't coming back."

---

I did not know what to say. I sat on the floor of that small bare room, with my back against the door, and looked at my aunt on the narrow bed with the one-eared rabbit in her lap. And I thought about my mother. Climbing out of a round window, in the night, with a lute on her back. Not looking back. Because if she looked back, she would not be able to go.

I did not know, then, why she had gone. I did not know about the door, and the humming, and her name being called in the night. I did not learn that until much later, from a letter in a scorched box on a rock in the hills. I only knew that my mother had left her little sister asleep, holding a rabbit with one ear. And had never gone back.

"I'm sorry," I said. It was all I had.

"Don't be." Meluan wiped her eyes. "It's not your fault. It's not anyone's fault. She did what she did." She looked at me. "And she had you. And she was happy. You said so."

"She was."

"Then that's something." Meluan looked down at the rabbit for a long moment. Then she held it out to me.

---

"Take her," she said.

I stared at her. "My lady, I can't—"

"You can. You must." Her voice was very steady. "I've been sleeping with Moss under my pillow for twenty-three years. Every night. Even after I married. Alveron thinks it's very odd. He's never said so, but I can see him thinking it." She almost smiled. "But I'm going to have a child. In the winter. And I can't be a woman who sleeps with a one-eared rabbit under her pillow, waiting for her sister to climb in the window. Not anymore." She pushed the rabbit into my hands. "I have to stop waiting. I have to be someone's mother."

I looked down at the rabbit. It was very soft, and very worn, and it smelled faintly of lavender and dust.

"What should I do with her?" I said.

Meluan looked at me for a long time.

"I don't know," she said at last. "Take her to wherever Netalia is. Her grave. Wherever they buried her." She stopped, seeing my face. "What?"

"There's no grave," I said quietly. "There wasn't anything left. To bury. Only ash. In a clearing in the woods, at the edge of the Commonwealth." I looked at the rabbit. "I don't even know where it is anymore. I ran. I was eleven. I never went back."

Meluan closed her eyes.

"Then keep her," she said. "Keep her until you know what to do with her. You'll know. Someday." She opened her eyes. "Netalia always knew what to do with things. She'd look at something broken and know exactly how to make it into something else. A one-eared rabbit into a hero. A button into an eye." She looked at me. "You have her face, you know. Around the eyes. When you're thinking. I saw it at dinner, the first night, and I nearly dropped my glass. You'll know what to do."

---

I kept Moss. I carried her with me in the bottom of my pack, wrapped in a handkerchief, to Renere and to Tarbean and across the sea to Imre. I carried her into the Fae, and up into the Stormwal, and down again, and into the Maer's camp in the hills. I did not know what to do with her. I did not take her out and look at her. But I kept her.

And on a morning in the spring after everything, on a ridge above a valley in the western hills, when a small girl with dark hair and dark eyes, not yet two years old, toddled across the grass to me and reached out one small hand and touched my face, I knew.

I did not remember Moss. I did not remember Meluan, or the small room under the eaves, or the round window. I did not remember anything. But my hand went into my pack, the way it had gone to the chest, the way it had gone to the corner of bread. And it came out with a small grey rabbit with one ear. And it put the rabbit into her hands.

She held it. She looked at it with her dark solemn eyes. And then she put the one ear in her mouth and chewed on it, very seriously, the way very small children do.

Mirra laughed.

"She likes it," she said. "What's her name? The rabbit?"

I did not know. I did not know anything.

"Moss," I said.

---

"Does she still have it?" said Chronicler. "Lark?"

The innkeeper was quiet for a moment.

"I don't know," he said. "I hope so." He looked at the fire. "I hope she sleeps with it under her pillow. And I hope that someday, when she's old enough, someone tells her it lost its ear in a great battle with a dragon. And that only the bravest rabbits have one ear."

He picked up his cup. His hand was not quite steady.

"And I hope," he said, "that she never, ever has to wait for anyone to climb in through a window."
