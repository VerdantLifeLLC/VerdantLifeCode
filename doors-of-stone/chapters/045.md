# Preparations

Stanchion did not want me to sing it.

"Five hundred royals," he said. He was standing behind the bar of the Eolian in the grey hour before opening, with a rag in one hand and a glass in the other, and he was not polishing either one. "Five hundred royals on your head, Kvothe. Do you know how many people in Imre would sell their own mothers for five hundred royals?"

"Most of them, I'd imagine."

"All of them. Every last one." He set down the glass. "And you want me to put you on my stage. In front of everyone. With your hair shining like a bonfire under the lamps."

"Yes."

"And you want me to put up posters. All over town. Telling everyone exactly when and where you'll be."

"Yes."

He stared at me. Stanchion was a big man, broad and bearded, and when he stared at you it was like being stared at by a bear that has just been told a joke it does not find funny.

"You've gone mad," he said. "That's it. You've been out in the world too long, and it's cooked your brains."

"Probably."

"Why?" He leaned on the bar. "Why here? Why now? Why like this? If you want to sing some song, fine. Sing it. Sing it in a barn in the Commonwealth where nobody knows you. Sing it on a ship. Sing it to the fish. Why do you have to sing it here, with posters, in front of every bounty hunter from here to Atur?"

I looked at him for a long moment. Then I told him. Not all of it. But enough.

When I was done, Stanchion was quiet for a long time. He picked up the glass again and turned it over in his big hands, looking at it as if it held some answer.

"The Chandrian," he said at last.

"Yes."

"The real ones. From the stories."

"Yes."

"And you want to sing a song that'll bring them here. To my place."

"I don't *want* to. I have to." I leaned forward. "Stanchion, the Eolian is the best place I know. It's round, with doors all the way around. It's built of stone. It's got the best acoustics in the Four Corners. When a song is sung here, it's heard. Truly heard. Not just by the people in the room, but—" I stopped. I did not know how to explain it. "It goes out. Into the world. Like a bell ringing."

"And that's what you need."

"That's what the song needs."

He looked at me for a long time. Then he set the glass down very carefully on the bar.

"I've owned this place for twenty-two years," he said. "I built it up from nothing. When I bought it, it was a stable. A stable, Kvothe. Horse dung on the floor and rats in the rafters. I cleaned it out with my own two hands. I laid every stone in that stage myself." He looked out at the empty room, the tables and chairs, the balconies circling above. "It's the only thing I've ever made that mattered."

"I know."

"And you're asking me to risk it."

"Yes."

He was silent for a very long time. Then he sighed, a great heavy sigh that seemed to come up from the soles of his boots.

"Tehlu's balls," he said. "All right. All right. But I want water. Barrels of it, all around the room. And buckets. And I want every door open, and a man on every door, and I want Deoch on the front door with his big stick." He glared at me. "And if my place burns down, you're paying to rebuild it. Every stone."

"Every stone," I said.

---

Deoch did not argue. He only looked at me for a long time, with that easy handsome face of his gone very still, and then he nodded and said, "When?"

"Four days. At the dark of the moon."

He nodded again. "I'll be there." He hesitated. "Kvothe. Denna. Will she come?"

I looked at him. Deoch had known Denna longer than I had. He had loved her, once, or thought he did. I had never been sure how he felt about her now.

"I think so," I said. "I think she'll be made to."

His face tightened. "Made to."

"It's complicated."

"It's always complicated, with her." He looked away. "Is she in trouble?"

"Yes."

"The kind you can help with?"

"I don't know. I'm going to try."

Deoch was quiet for a long moment. Then he said, very softly, "Good. Somebody should."

---

The posters went up the next day. Threpe had them printed on good paper, with his own coat of arms at the top, and he had them pasted up on every wall and door and lamppost in Imre. They were very simple. They said:

*At the dark of the moon, at the Eolian, the Ruh Kvothe will sing a new song.*

*"Tariniel."*

*The true story of the fall of the white city.*

That was all. No price, no time, no mention of bounties or kings or anything else. Threpe had wanted to say more. He had wanted to say *the greatest song of the age* and *not to be missed* and a dozen other things. I had made him take them out.

"Just the truth," I told him. "That's all it needs."

By noon, everyone in Imre was talking about it. By evening, everyone at the University was talking about it too. Students crossed the bridge in droves to look at the posters and argue about them. Some said I was mad. Some said I was a murderer who should be arrested on sight. Some said I was a hero, falsely accused, come back to clear my name. Some said the song was a trap, and that the whole thing was a trick by the Maer of Severen to lure the new king of Vintas's men into Imre and kill them.

Nobody said anything about the Chandrian. Nobody knew to.

---

On the second day, Elodin came.

I was sitting on Devi's roof in the late afternoon, going over the last verses of the song for the hundredth time, when I heard a sound behind me. I turned. Elodin was sitting on the ridge of the roof a few feet away, his legs dangling, eating an apple. I had not heard him climb up. I had not heard anything.

"Hello, Kvothe," he said.

"Master Elodin."

"Not your master anymore. You've been expelled." He took a bite of apple. "Just Elodin. Or, if you prefer, that mad old bastard from across the river. That's what most people call me."

I did not say anything. I did not know what to say.

He chewed his apple for a while, looking out over the rooftops of Imre. Then he said, "I saw the posters."

"I thought you might."

"Everyone's seen them. The whole University is buzzing like a kicked hive." He glanced at me. "Lorren is very unhappy. He came to see me this morning, to ask if I knew what you were planning. He was very pale. I don't think I've ever seen Lorren pale before."

"What did you tell him?"

"That I had no idea what you were planning, which was true. And that whatever it was, it was probably very foolish, which was also true." He took another bite of apple. "Then he asked me if I thought he should do something about it. Send the constables. Have you arrested. Something."

"And?"

"And I told him that if he did, I'd set his beard on fire." Elodin smiled faintly. "He went away after that."

I stared at him. "Why?"

"Why what?"

"Why would you do that? You voted to expel me."

"I did." He looked at me. "I voted to expel you because you needed to be somewhere other than on top of that door. You still do. But you're not on top of it now. You're across the river, singing songs." He shrugged. "That's not a crime. Not even at the University."

We sat in silence for a while. The sun was going down, painting the rooftops of Imre with gold and red.

"The song," Elodin said at last. "The one on the posters. *Tariniel*. What is it?"

I looked at him for a long moment. Then I told him.

Not all of it. Not about Skarpi, or the Singers, or the line. But about the true song, and the curse, and the false song Denna was singing. I told him what I thought would happen when I sang it.

Elodin listened without interrupting. When I was done, he was quiet for a long time. He had finished his apple, and he was turning the core over and over in his fingers.

"That's very dangerous," he said at last.

"I know."

"No. You don't." He looked at me, and his dark eyes were very serious. "You think you know. You think it's dangerous because they'll come and try to kill you. But that's not the danger. That's just the risk." He tossed the apple core out over the edge of the roof. We both watched it fall. "The danger is the song itself."

"What do you mean?"

"A true song, sung truly, is a kind of naming." Elodin's voice was very quiet. "You know that. You've done it. You sang the Sleeper to sleep, and you sang the knots out of a king's hair. You've been naming with your voice, Kvothe, whether you knew it or not." He looked at me. "And you want to sing the true name of Lanre's story. The whole of it. In front of hundreds of people. With every ounce of skill and heart you have."

"Yes."

"Do you know what happens when you speak a name that large? That old? That full of power?" He shook his head. "It doesn't just go out into the world. It goes *through* you. It uses you. It burns through you like fire through dry grass." He paused. "I've seen it happen. I've seen namers speak names that were too big for them. Some of them went mad. Some of them died. Some of them—" He stopped.

"Some of them what?"

He was quiet for a long time.

"Some of them forgot who they were," he said at last. "The name was so big, and so true, that it filled them up. It pushed out everything else. Their own names, their own selves. When it was over, they were empty. Just shells, with the shape of a person and nothing inside." He looked away, out over the rooftops. "I know a little something about that."

I looked at him. At his lean face and his dark restless eyes, and the faint lines of old pain around his mouth.

"Elodin," I said. "What happened to you?"

He did not answer for a long time. Then he stood up, all at once, balancing on the ridge of the roof as if it were solid ground.

"Ask me after," he said. "If you're still yourself. I'll tell you then."

"And if I'm not?"

He looked down at me. For a moment, just a moment, his face was very gentle.

"Then it won't matter," he said. "Will it?"

He turned to go. Then he stopped and looked back.

"Hold onto something," he said. "When you sing. Something small. Something that's yours. Something that reminds you who you are." He paused. "A name is a big thing. But it's easier to hold onto a small one."

Then he walked away along the ridge of the roof, and dropped down over the edge, and was gone.

---

I sat on the roof until the sun went down. I thought about what he had said. About the song going through me. About forgetting who I was.

I took out the things I carried. I laid them on the slates in front of me, one by one, in the last of the light.

Kilvin's candle, in its wooden box.

The scrap of the Dellers' knotted cloth.

The page from the Book of Watches, folded small.

Fela's stone eye.

And Denna's knots. Both of them. The one she had left me at the Grey Man, the first one, with *road* and *promise* and *a story told many times*. And the one she had left me on the table in Severen, with *forgive* and *keep* and *safe*.

I looked at them for a long time. Then I picked up the first knot, the one from the Grey Man. The one she had given me before any of it. Before the box and the garden and the knots in the king's hair. When she was only a girl I loved, teaching me to read her secret language.

I held it in my palm. I felt it, very faintly. The tightening in the air. The sense of something reaching out and catching hold.

It held.

I tied it to a length of thread and hung it around my neck, under my shirt, against my skin. It lay there, small and light, over my heart.

*Something small. Something that's yours.*

It was not mine, exactly. But it was the closest thing I had.

---

On the third day, there was news from Vintas.

It came by way of a merchant who had just arrived in Imre from Renere, and who told it in the common room of the Grey Man to anyone who would listen. Devi heard it from a friend who had been there, and she came home and told me, standing in the doorway of my little back room with her face very white.

King Ambrose of Vintas had declared the Maer of Severen a traitor to the crown. He had stripped him of his lands and titles and ordered his arrest. He had called his banners, and the banners of every lord who owed him fealty, and he was marching south toward Severen with an army of ten thousand men.

The Maer had called his own banners in reply. He had refused to surrender. He had closed the gates of Severen and was preparing for a siege.

It was war. Civil war, in Vintas. The first in two hundred years.

"And there's more," said Devi. She hesitated. "The merchant said—he said there's a singer traveling with the king's court. A young woman. Very beautiful. She's been singing a song about Lanre in every town they pass through, and people are weeping in the streets." She looked at me. "He said the king calls her his nightingale. And that an old man with a walking stick is always at her side."

I sat very still on my narrow cot.

"Is she coming here?" I said.

"The merchant didn't know. But he said the king's court has an envoy in Imre. A lord, sent to negotiate with the Commonwealth for supplies and loans. The envoy arrived yesterday." Devi's voice was very quiet. "He brought a singer with him. For entertainment."

I closed my eyes.

Of course. Of course he had. The board was set. The stones were placed. Bredon had heard about my posters, about the song and the Eolian and the dark of the moon, and he had sent Denna here to meet me. To sing her song against mine.

*A true song and a false song, sung against each other*, Skarpi had said. *That's a battle. And whichever song is stronger will win.*

"Kvothe?" said Devi. "Are you all right?"

I opened my eyes. I looked at her.

"Tomorrow night," I said. "At the Eolian. She'll be there."

"How do you know?"

"Because he wants her to be," I said. "Because it's the move he'd make. It's the beautiful one."

Devi looked at me for a long moment.

"Then you'd better be more beautiful," she said.
