# The Beautiful Game

The small garden was not small. It was only smaller than the other gardens of the Maer's palace, which were so vast and so elaborate that you could get lost in them for an afternoon. The small garden was merely enormous. It was a walled square of green behind the western wing, with gravel paths and clipped hedges and a fountain in the center where a stone fish spat water into a stone basin. There were benches set along the paths, in the shade of old trees. And under the oldest tree, a great spreading oak whose roots had cracked the gravel of the path, there was a small stone table.

Bredon was sitting at it. There was a tak board in front of him, the stones already laid out in their bowls, black and white. His walking stick leaned against the table beside him. He was reading a book, and he did not look up as I came down the path toward him.

"Kvothe," he said, turning a page. "You're late."

"You didn't say a time."

"No. But you're late nonetheless. I've been waiting since noon." He closed the book and set it aside and looked up at me, and his face broke into a smile of such warmth and pleasure that I felt my own face answer it before I could stop myself. "Sit. Sit, my boy. It's good to see you."

I sat. He looked at me across the board, his bright old eyes moving over my face, my clothes, my sword.

"You've changed," he said. "I said so in the snow, and it's even more true in the sun. You carry yourself differently. You're harder around the edges. Something's happened to you."

"A great many things have happened to me."

"Yes. I heard." He picked up a white stone and turned it in his fingers. "Expelled. From the University. At seventeen." He shook his head slowly. "Do you know, I think that must be some kind of record."

"I'm told it is."

"Well. There are worse things to hold records in." He set the stone down on the board, at one of the corner points. An opening move. The same one he always made. "Your move."

I looked at the board. I picked up a black stone. I placed it.

We played.

---

I have tried, over the years, to explain tak to people who do not know it. It is not easy. It is a very simple game, on the surface. Two players, black and white, taking turns placing stones on a grid of lines. The object is to surround your opponent's stones and capture them, while protecting your own. That is all. A child can learn the rules in an afternoon.

But underneath the simple rules, there is a depth that goes down and down, like a well with no bottom. Every stone you place changes the shape of the board. Every move opens some possibilities and closes others. The best players do not think in terms of single moves. They think in shapes, in patterns, in long slow flows of influence that stretch across the whole board and take dozens of moves to play out.

Bredon was the best player I had ever met. He had told me once that he had been playing for sixty years, and I believed him. He saw the board the way a master musician hears a song: not as a series of notes, but as a whole, a single shape unfolding in time.

When I had first played him, the year before, he had beaten me so thoroughly and so easily that it had been almost funny. I had lost every game, dozens of them, one after another. But I had learned. I had studied. I had thought about the game on the long road to Ademre and back, laying out imaginary boards in my head and playing against imaginary opponents. And by the time I left Severen, I had been able to hold my own against him. Sometimes. For a while.

Now, sitting across the board from him in the small garden, I found that I had forgotten nothing.

We played for an hour. The board filled with stones, black and white, in patterns that shifted and flowed like water. Bredon was attacking on the eastern side, a long slow push that I could see coming but could not quite stop. I was building on the western side, a wall of black stones that I hoped would hold.

"You've been practicing," Bredon said.

"Only in my head."

"That's the best kind of practice. The board in your head is always perfect." He placed a stone. "Tell me about the University. What did they expel you for?"

I hesitated. I placed a stone.

"I went somewhere I shouldn't have."

"Where?"

"Down a set of stairs."

He laughed. It was a warm, delighted laugh, the laugh of a man who has heard something genuinely funny. "Down a set of stairs! They expelled you for going down a set of stairs?"

"They were very important stairs."

"They must have been." He placed a stone. "In the Archives, I suppose?"

I looked up. He was looking at the board, not at me. His face was mild and interested.

"Yes," I said. "In the Archives."

"Beneath the Four-plate door?"

I did not answer. I placed a stone.

Bredon looked up at me. His eyes were bright and kind. "Ah," he said. "I've touched a nerve. I'm sorry. It's none of my business, of course. I was only curious." He smiled. "Old men are always curious. It's the only vice we have left."

"How did you know about the Four-plate door?"

"Everyone knows about the Four-plate door. It's famous." He placed a stone. "I visited the University once, many years ago, when I was young. I saw it. A great grey slab of stone with four copper circles. I remember thinking that it looked like a face. A very old, very patient face, waiting for someone to come and talk to it." He shook his head. "I always wondered what was behind it."

"Nobody knows."

"Nobody? Not even you?" He looked at me with mild amusement. "You went down the stairs, after all."

I placed a stone. I did not answer.

Bredon watched me for a moment. Then he smiled, and let it go, and returned his attention to the board.

---

He won that game. He won it slowly and gracefully, in the way he always won, surrounding my western wall so gradually that I did not notice until it was too late. When he placed the last stone and my wall collapsed, I sat back and laughed, because there was nothing else to do.

"Beautiful," I said. "I never saw it coming."

"You never do. That's what makes it beautiful." He began to gather up the stones, sorting them into their bowls. "Again?"

"Again."

We played again. And again. The afternoon wore on. The shadows of the old oak stretched out across the garden. Servants came and brought us wine and little cakes and went away again. Bredon won the second game, and the third. I won the fourth, barely, by a single stone.

"Well done," he said, when I placed the winning stone. He looked genuinely pleased. "Very well done. You played that like a master."

"I got lucky."

"There's no luck in tak. Only patience." He sat back and looked at me. "Tell me. Do you remember what I told you about the beautiful game?"

I thought about it. He had told me a great many things, the year before, over the tak board. Most of them I had forgotten. But I remembered that.

"You said that winning isn't the point," I said slowly. "You said that anyone can win, if they're good enough. The point is to win beautifully. To play a game that's worth remembering."

"Yes." He nodded. "And?"

"And you said that sometimes the most beautiful game is the one you lose."

"Yes." He smiled. "Do you understand that now? Better than you did then?"

I thought about it. I thought about the Four-plate door, and the Masters' Hall, and the long lonely road south.

"I think so," I said. "I think sometimes you have to lose something to win something else. Something bigger."

"Close. Very close." He picked up a single white stone and held it up between two fingers, looking at it in the slanting afternoon light. "But it's more than that. The beautiful game isn't about sacrifice. Sacrifice is crude. Anyone can throw away a stone to gain a stone. The beautiful game is about *time*." He set the stone down on the empty board, at the very center. "It's about playing a game so long, so patient, so slow, that your opponent never realizes they're losing. They think they're winning. They win every battle. They take every stone you offer them. And then, at the very end, they look down at the board and see that every stone they took was a stone you wanted them to take. Every battle they won was a battle you arranged for them to win. And the shape of the board, the shape you've been building all along, one patient stone at a time, has closed around them like a hand."

He looked at me. His eyes were very bright.

"That's the beautiful game," he said. "Winning so slowly that no one ever knows you've won. Not even the one you've beaten. Not until it's far, far too late."

---

I sat there in the dappled shade of the old oak and looked at him, and I felt something cold go down my spine. I did not know why. He had said nothing sinister. He had said nothing I had not heard him say before, in one form or another. He was an old man talking about a game.

But there was something in his voice. Something in his eyes. A kind of hunger, very old and very patient, like the hunger of a tree for light.

"That sounds lonely," I said.

He blinked. Then he laughed, a little surprised. "Lonely?"

"To win like that. So that no one knows. Not even the one you beat." I shrugged. "It sounds lonely. What's the point of winning if no one knows?"

He looked at me for a long moment. Something moved in his face. It was there and gone so fast I almost missed it.

"You're very young," he said at last. His voice was gentle. "When you're young, you want people to know. You want them to see. You want applause." He smiled. "When you're old, you learn that applause is a very small thing. The shape of the board is what matters. Whether anyone sees it or not."

He picked up the stone from the center of the board and put it back in its bowl.

"Enough philosophy," he said. "It's getting late. The Maer dines at sundown, and he'll want you at his table." He reached for his stick. "Come back tomorrow. We'll play again."

"I'd like that."

"I know you would." He stood, leaning on his stick. He looked down at me with his kind grandfather's eyes. "It's good to have you back, Kvothe. I've missed our games. There's no one else at court who plays with any real *heart*."

He began to walk away down the gravel path. Then he stopped and turned back.

"Oh," he said. "I nearly forgot. I heard something today that might interest you. About a friend of yours."

"Which friend?"

"A young lady. A singer. Dark hair, lovely voice. I believe you know her." He smiled. "She's in Severen. Arrived a few days ago, I'm told. Staying in the low city, at an inn near the river. The Mill Race, I think it's called."

My heart stopped.

"Denna," I said.

"Is that what she's calling herself these days?" Bredon's smile widened. "I never can keep track. Well. I thought you'd want to know." He lifted his stick an inch off the ground in his small courtly salute. "Good evening, Kvothe."

He walked away. I watched him go, down the gravel path between the clipped hedges, his stick tapping on the stones. The late sun turned his white hair to gold.

I sat alone under the old oak, with the empty tak board in front of me, and I did not move for a long time.

---

I did not go down to the low city that night. I wanted to. Every part of me wanted to. But the Maer had invited me to dine with him, and you do not refuse the Maer. And besides, I needed to think.

Dinner was long and formal and very dull. The Maer sat at the head of the table, and Meluan sat at his right hand, pale and heavy with child, picking at her food and saying nothing. I sat halfway down the table between a minor lord who talked about horses and a minor lady who talked about the lord who talked about horses. I smiled and nodded and made polite conversation and did not taste a single bite.

I was thinking about Denna. About the Deller farm, and the frozen mud, and a little girl in a well saying *Dinael*.

I was thinking about Bredon, and how he had known she was in Severen, and how he had known I would want to know.

And I was thinking about Meluan, sitting at the head of the table with her hand resting on the swell of her belly, and the box that she kept under her pillow, and the look on her face when she had shown it to me, the year before. The look of a woman guarding something she did not understand, and did not want, and could not put down.

She looked up once, during the long dull dinner, and met my eyes across the table.

I do not know what she saw in my face. But she went very pale, even paler than before, and looked away quickly, and did not look at me again.

After dinner, when the guests were dispersing into the gardens and the long galleries, Stapes found me.

"Sir," he said quietly. "Her ladyship asks if you would attend her in her rooms. Now, if you please."

"Now?"

"Now, sir." His face was calm, but his eyes were not. "She was quite insistent."

I looked at him. Then I nodded.

"Lead the way," I said.
