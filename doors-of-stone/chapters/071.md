# Levinshir

I had been to Levinshir once before.

I do not know if I told you about it. It was on the road back from Ademre, with Tempi, more than a year before. We had come upon a troupe of false Edema Ruh on the road—men who wore the colors and sang the songs but were not Ruh at all, only bandits in borrowed clothes. They had stolen two girls from a farm near Levinshir, and they were keeping them in their wagons, and doing things to them that I do not like to think about.

I killed them. All of them. I burned their wagons. And I brought the girls home.

I was not proud of it, afterward. Not the killing. The girls, yes. I was proud of bringing them home. But I had killed those men in a cold fury, one by one, in the night, and I had enjoyed it. That frightened me. It still does.

The girls' names were Krin and Ellie. I had not thought about them in a long time.

I thought about them now, as we came over the last ridge and saw Levinshir below us in the valley.

---

It was a small town. Smaller than I remembered. A few dozen houses clustered around a crossroads, with a church and a mill and an inn. The same as a thousand towns across the Four Corners. Gray stone and brown thatch and green fields running away to the hills.

But it was empty.

We could see that from the ridge. No smoke from the chimneys. No people in the streets. No animals in the fields. The doors of the houses stood open. The mill wheel was still. A cart stood abandoned in the middle of the crossroads, with its traces empty.

"They've run," said Vashet. "From the army."

"Or been taken," said Wil.

We went down into the town. Carefully, slowly, with the Adem spread out ahead of us and Bast's glamour thin around us all. There was no one there. The houses were empty, their doors swinging in the wind. There was food on some of the tables, half eaten. There were clothes on the lines, flapping.

In the inn, there was a fire still smoldering in the hearth.

"A day," said Vashet, crouching beside it. "Maybe less. They left in a hurry."

I stood in the doorway of the inn and looked out at the empty crossroads. At the abandoned cart. At the hills beyond the town, rising toward a high rocky ridge in the east.

And on the ridge, faint against the grey sky, a shape. A house. A great old house, built of dark stone, with towers at its corners and a wall around it. It stood alone on the ridge like a crow on a fence post.

"There," I said.

---

We found Krin on the road out of town.

She was sitting on a stone at the side of the road, with a bundle on her lap and her face in her hands. She was older than I remembered. Taller. Her hair was longer, and she was wearing a woman's dress now, not a girl's. But I knew her.

She looked up as we came down the road. She saw the Adem first, in their red clothes, and her face went white with terror. She grabbed her bundle and started to run.

"Krin," I said.

She stopped. She turned. She looked at me.

For a long moment, she did not move. Then her face changed. It crumpled, the way a child's face crumples just before it cries.

"You," she said. "It's you."

"It's me."

She dropped the bundle. She ran at me and threw her arms around me and held on, sobbing, the way she had held on to me in the back of the false troupe's wagon, the night I burned it.

"You came back," she said into my shirt. "You came back. I always knew you would. Everyone said you were dead. Everyone said you were a murderer. But I knew. I knew you'd come back."

I held her. I did not know what else to do.

---

She told us what had happened, sitting on the stone at the side of the road, with the Adem standing guard around us and Bast crouched beside her with a waterskin.

The king's army had come through two days before. Not all of it. Only the front of it, a few hundred riders in black and silver. They had come into Levinshir and told everyone to leave. Everyone. Right away. They said the king was coming, and the king wanted the town empty. They said anyone who stayed would be hanged.

"Most people went," said Krin. "To their cousins in Hallowfell, or to the hill farms, or just away. Anywhere." She wiped her eyes. "My mam went. My da. My little brothers. They wanted me to go too."

"Why didn't you?"

She looked at me. "Because of the house," she said.

I went very still.

"The Lackless house," said Krin. "Up on the ridge. It's been empty for years. Since the old lord died. Nobody goes there. Everyone says it's cold. Everyone says it's haunted." She looked up at the ridge, at the dark shape of the house against the sky. "But I went there. Once. When I was little. On a dare. With Ellie."

"What did you find?"

Krin was quiet for a long time.

"There's a door," she said at last. "In the cellars. Way down. Past the wine and the old furniture and the rats. Down a long stair. At the bottom." She shivered. "It's made of stone. It doesn't have a handle. We went down and looked at it, and we were so scared we couldn't move." She looked at me. "And it talked to us."

"What did it say?"

"I don't know. Not really. It wasn't words." She wrapped her arms around herself. "But Ellie said it was asking us to come in. And we ran. We ran all the way home and never told anyone."

"Where's Ellie now?"

Krin's face changed. "She died," she said quietly. "Last winter. Of a fever." She looked down at her hands. "She was never the same, after the wagons. After what they did. She was always sad. And then she got sick, and she didn't want to get better."

I did not say anything. I put my hand on her shoulder.

"I stayed," said Krin, "because when the soldiers came, they were talking. About the house. About the door. They said the king was going to open it. They said the king had a key." She looked up at me with wet eyes. "And I thought, somebody should tell somebody. Somebody should know. And I didn't know who to tell. So I stayed, and I waited, and I thought maybe somebody would come." She laughed, a broken sound. "I didn't think it would be you."

---

We left Krin with two of the Adem, to take her to the hill farms where her family had gone. She did not want to go. She wanted to come with us. She said she knew the house, knew the way down, knew where the door was.

"You've told us enough," I said. "That's all we needed. You were very brave to stay."

"I'm not brave. I'm stupid."

"Those are often the same thing," said Bast, and grinned at her.

She looked at him. At his fine-boned face and his deep blue eyes. She blushed, very slightly.

"Who's he?" she whispered to me.

"A friend."

"He's very handsome."

"Don't tell him," I said. "He already knows."

She laughed. It was a small, surprised, watery laugh. Then she hugged me one more time, fiercely, and went with the Adem down the road toward the hills.

At the bend in the road, she turned and looked back.

"Kvothe," she called. "The door. Don't answer it. Whatever it says. Don't answer."

"I won't," I called back.

She looked at me for a long moment. Then she went around the bend and was gone.

---

"Everyone keeps telling you that," said Bast, as we started up the long road toward the ridge. "Don't answer the door."

"I know."

"Auri told you. And Krin. And the Sleeper, back at your University. And Selitos, sort of."

"I know."

"Why do you think that is?"

I looked up at the house on the ridge. Dark stone, against the grey sky. Towers at its corners, and a wall around it, and no light in any of its windows.

"Because," I said, "they all know I'm going to."
