# Caught

Lorren was the first one through the door.

I had known he would be. I had not known what he would look like, coming down those stairs in the golden light of a dozen lanterns, with his black robe billowing around him and his long pale face set like stone. I had seen Lorren angry before. I had seen him on the day I lit a candle in the Archives and he barred me for a year and a half. But I had never seen him like this.

He was not angry. He was afraid.

He came down the great spiral stair so fast that the men behind him had to run to keep up. There were six of them: scrivs, mostly, in their grey robes, carrying lanterns and looking terrified. And behind them, moving more slowly, the Chancellor, Herma, with his white beard and his heavy chain of office glittering on his chest.

I was standing on the landing a flight below the door, in the full light of their lanterns, with Caesura at my hip and my hands held out and empty.

Lorren stopped when he saw me. He stood on the stair above me and looked down, and for a long moment he did not speak. His face did not change. But his eyes went to the stair below me, spiraling down into darkness, and then back to my face.

"How far?" he said.

"All the way," I said.

Something moved behind his eyes. It was there and gone so fast I almost missed it. But I had been watching faces my whole life, and I knew what I had seen.

It was grief.

"Did you touch the door?" he said. His voice was very quiet.

"No."

"Did you speak to it?"

"No."

"Did it speak to you?"

I hesitated. It was only for a moment. But Lorren saw it.

He closed his eyes. He stood there on the stair, in the golden lantern light, with his eyes closed and his long pale hands hanging at his sides, and he looked, for the first time since I had known him, like an old man.

"Bind him," he said to the scrivs. "Take him up."

---

They did not bind me. Herma would not allow it. He came down the stairs past Lorren and looked at me for a long time, his old face creased with worry, and then he shook his head.

"He came up to meet us," he said. "He's not going to run. Are you, Kvothe?"

"No, sir."

"Then there's no need for ropes." He looked at Lorren. "Take his sword, if you like. But no ropes."

Lorren did not answer. One of the scrivs came forward nervously and held out his hands, and I unbuckled Caesura and gave it to him. He took it the way you might take a live snake.

Then they led me up the stairs, through the Four-plate door, and into the Archives.

I had never seen the Four-plate door open before. Nobody had, in living memory. It stood wide now, the great round slab of stone swung back on some hidden pivot, and the four copper plates on its face gleamed dully in the lantern light. As I passed through it, I felt something brush against me. A coldness. A kind of attention. As if the door were looking at me.

I did not look back.

---

They took me to Lorren's office. A long narrow room lined with books, lit by the red glow of sympathy lamps. Lorren sat behind his desk. Herma sat in a chair beside it. I stood in front of them.

The scrivs had been sent away. The door was closed. It was very quiet.

"Tell us," said Herma, "what happened."

So I told them. Not everything. I did not tell them about Devi or Fela. I did not tell them about the air shaft, or the Underthing, or Auri. I told them I had found my own way down, that I had gone alone, that I had been curious about what lay behind the Four-plate door and had found a way past it.

I told them about the stairs, and the small square room, and the names on the walls. I told them about the round room at the bottom, and the table, and the book. I told them about the door.

I did not tell them about the voice. Or what I had read in the book.

When I was done, they were both silent for a long time.

"You went alone," said Lorren at last. His voice was flat.

"Yes."

"You found your own way past the Four-plate door. Alone. Without help."

"Yes."

"And you passed the second seal. Alone. Without help." He leaned forward. "Tell me how."

I had known this question was coming. I had been dreading it. I had no good answer, because the true answer would betray Fela.

"I asked it," I said. "I asked the stone to let me pass."

Lorren looked at me for a long moment. Then he looked at Herma.

"He's lying," he said.

"Perhaps," said Herma. "Or perhaps not." He was looking at me with a strange expression. "Elodin has been teaching him stone this term. I asked."

"Nobody has passed that seal in two hundred years. Nobody. Not Elodin, not me, not anyone." Lorren's voice was very cold. "And you expect me to believe that a Re'lar with a single name to his credit, and that one called by accident, walked down those stairs and *asked* it?"

"I don't expect you to believe anything, Master Lorren," I said. "But I'm telling you what I told you."

Lorren stood up. He walked around his desk and stood in front of me, very close, looking down at me from his great height. His eyes were pale and very cold.

"Someone was with you," he said softly. "Someone who can hear stone. Someone who asked the seal to be silent, and kept it silent until something was taken. And then it rang." He paused. "What did you take, Kvothe?"

"Nothing."

"Something was taken."

"I didn't take anything. I promise you. On my name and my blood, I didn't take anything from that room."

He looked at me for a long time. I held his gaze. It was not a lie. I had not taken anything. I had been very careful about that.

At last Lorren turned away. He went back to his desk and sat down heavily.

"Search him," he said to Herma.

Herma searched me. He was gentle about it, and thorough. He found my sympathy lamp, and my tools, and Elodin's stone, and the little knot Denna had given me, which he looked at with some curiosity before handing it back. He found nothing else.

"Nothing," he said.

Lorren did not look up. "Then someone else has it," he said. "And they are out there somewhere, in the dark, with a page from the Book of Watches in their hands." He was quiet for a moment. "Do you know what you've done, Kvothe? Do you have any idea?"

"No, sir," I said. "I don't. I only know what I read."

He looked up sharply. "You read the book."

"Some of it."

"How much?"

"The front. The account. The one without a name." I swallowed. "About the war. And the Thief. And the doors."

Lorren and Herma looked at each other. Something passed between them, some silent communication I could not follow.

"And the line?" said Herma quietly. "Did you read about the line?"

"Yes."

"Then you understand," said Lorren, "why you should not have gone there."

I did not answer. I did not have to.

---

They kept me in Lorren's office all night. They did not lock me in; they did not have to. I sat in a chair by the wall and waited, and they talked in low voices by the desk, and sometimes one or the other of them went out and came back with a book or a paper or a message from somewhere else in the University.

Near dawn, Elodin came.

He came in without knocking, as he always did, and stood in the doorway looking at me. His face was very strange. It was not angry, and it was not sad. It was the face of a man who has watched someone walk toward a cliff for a very long time and has finally seen them go over the edge.

"Well," he said. "You did it."

"I didn't open it."

"No. But you went down. And you listened. And it heard you." He came into the room and sat down on the edge of Lorren's desk, ignoring Lorren's glare. "Did it speak to you?"

I hesitated. Then I nodded.

"What did it say?"

"My name."

Elodin closed his eyes. For a long moment he did not say anything. Then he opened them again and looked at Lorren.

"You know what that means," he said.

"I know what it means," said Lorren. His voice was very tired. "It means he can never go down there again. It means he can never be allowed near the Archives again. Not the Four-plate door, not the Stacks, not any of it." He looked at me. "It means he has to leave."

"Leave?" I said. "Leave the Archives?"

"Leave the University," said Herma quietly.

The world seemed to tilt beneath me. I gripped the arms of my chair.

"You can't," I said. "You can't expel me for this. I didn't take anything. I didn't touch the door. I didn't—"

"You went down the stairs," said Lorren. "That is enough. That has always been enough. It is the oldest rule of the Archives, older than the University itself. No one goes down the stairs." He looked at me with his pale, cold eyes. "Not because of what they might take. Because of what they might wake."

"And you woke it," said Elodin softly. "Or near enough. Everyone felt it, Kvothe. Every namer in the University. Every man and woman who's ever heard the stone. We all felt it wake up and turn its head. And we all felt what it turned its head toward." He looked at me. "You."

---

The formal proceeding came later. There were rules, even for this. There were forms to be followed, records to be kept. At the University, nothing ever happened without a great deal of paper.

But I knew, sitting in Lorren's office in the grey light of dawn, that it was already decided. I could see it in their faces. In Herma's worried eyes and Lorren's cold ones. In the way Elodin sat on the edge of the desk with his arms folded and his head bowed, not looking at me.

They were not angry at me. That was the worst of it. If they had been angry, I could have fought. I could have argued, and pleaded, and talked my way out of it the way I had talked my way out of so many things before.

But they were not angry. They were afraid. And you cannot argue with fear.

"Master Elodin," I said at last. "Is there anything I can do?"

He looked up at me. For a long moment he did not answer.

"Yes," he said. "You can go. Quickly. Far away. And you can try very hard to forget everything you saw down there."

"And if I can't forget?"

He smiled. It was a strange smile, sad and fond and a little bitter.

"Then you'll have to be very careful," he said, "about what you remember."
