# The Queen in the Tower

She came to see me nine days before my trial, with snow-white hair and a lock of a baby's hair folded in a square of blue silk.

I did not know her. I was sitting on my pallet in the grey, with my back against the wall, when the iron door of my cell opened. Not the grate. The door itself. It swung back on its hinges with a great groaning of iron, and the guards stood aside, and a woman came in.

She was thin. Very thin, and very pale, in a plain grey gown with a white thread down the breast, like the Penitent King's. She walked slowly, with a cane, and a small precise man in grey walked at her elbow, ready to catch her. Her face was lined, the face of a woman twenty years older than she was. Her hair was white. Not grey. White, like snow, like bone, like the frost on the inside of my cell walls in winter.

She stood just inside the door and looked at me.

I looked back. I did not know her.

"Leave us, Stapes," she said.

The small precise man hesitated. "Your Majesty—"

"Leave us." Her voice was quiet and very firm. "And close the door. He will not hurt me."

Stapes looked at me for a long moment. Then he bowed and went out, and the door groaned closed behind him, and we were alone.

---

She did not sit. There was nowhere to sit, except the pallet, and the floor. She stood in the middle of my small grey cell, leaning on her cane, and looked at me for a long time.

"You don't know me," she said.

"No."

"Meluan," she said. "I am your mother's sister. Your aunt." She was quiet for a moment. "I was in the chamber. At the door. You saved my life."

I looked at her white hair. At the thin pale line across her throat, just visible above the collar of her gown.

"I'm sorry," I said. "I don't remember."

"I know." She came a little closer. "Alveron told me. And the girl. Denna. She told me what you did. With your name." Meluan was quiet for a moment. "I didn't believe her, at first. I thought it was a story. Something to make me feel better about—" She stopped. "About everything."

"It's true."

"Yes. I can see it now." She looked at me. "There's nothing behind your eyes. Nothing at all. It's like looking into an empty house." Her voice cracked, very slightly. "Your mother had the most alive eyes of anyone I ever knew."

---

She lowered herself, slowly and painfully, onto the edge of my pallet. Not beside me. At the far end. As if she were afraid to come too close.

"I came to tell you some things," she said. "Before the trial. Before—whatever happens." She looked down at her hands. "Things about her. Your mother. I thought—" She hesitated. "Denna said you gave your name away. All of it. Everything that made you you. And I thought, perhaps, if you had nothing of your own, you might like to have something of hers."

I did not say anything. I did not know what to say.

Meluan took that for yes.

---

She told me about Netalia.

Not the Netalia of the blue book, the black mark where a name should have been. Not the Netalia of the Maer's court, the scandal, the girl who ran away with a tinker. The other one. The sister.

She told me that Netalia had been born in the spring, in a thunderstorm, and that their mother had said it was a sign she would be loud. She told me that Netalia had been loud. That she had sung before she could talk, and talked before she could walk, and never stopped doing either. That she had climbed every tree on the Lackless estate by the time she was seven, and fallen out of most of them, and broken her arm twice.

She told me that Netalia had taught her to read. When Meluan was five and Netalia was eight, in the window seat of the library, with a book of old ballads, sounding out the words one by one with her finger under each.

She told me that Netalia had been afraid of the dark. Terribly afraid. That she had slept with a candle burning every night until she was fourteen, and that when their father forbade it, saying it was a waste of good wax, she had crept into Meluan's bed instead, every night, and held her hand until she fell asleep.

"She said there was something in the house," said Meluan quietly. "Under it. Something that breathed. She said she could hear it at night, through the floor." She looked at her hands. "I never heard it. I thought she was being silly. Making up stories, the way she always did."

She was quiet for a long time.

"I understand now," she said. "After the chamber. After the door." She looked at me. "She wasn't making it up. She heard it. Every night. For fourteen years. And no one believed her."

---

"Why did she leave?" I said.

It was the first question I had asked. Meluan looked at me, startled.

"You asked," she said softly. "You haven't asked anything. Stapes said. Not in all these months."

I did not know why I had asked. It had come up out of the hole in my chest without my deciding. But now that it was said, I wanted the answer. Or something in me did.

"Why did she leave?" I said again.

Meluan was quiet for a long time.

"There was an old man," she said at last. "When we were girls. A friend of our father's. He came to the house every few months, to play tak with Father and tell stories by the fire. We loved him. Both of us. He was so kind. He brought us sweets, and books, and once a little silver bird that sang when you wound it."

I sat very still.

"He was kind to both of us," said Meluan. "But he talked to Netalia more. He sat with her in the library, and told her stories about the old days, and the old families, and the old secrets. And she asked him questions. So many questions. About the box, and the rhyme, and the thing she heard breathing under the house." Meluan's voice was very quiet. "And he answered some of them. Not all. Just enough. Just enough to make her want to know more."

"And then?"

"And then, the summer she was seventeen, he came to the house, and he sat with her in the library for a whole afternoon. With the door closed. And when she came out, she was white as a sheet." Meluan looked at me. "She wouldn't tell me what he'd said. She wouldn't tell anyone. She went to her room and locked the door and didn't come out for two days."

"And on the third day?"

"On the third day, a troupe of players came through the village below the house. Ruh. With painted wagons. And there was a red-haired man with them, who sang." Meluan closed her eyes. "And on the fourth day, she was gone."

---

I did not say anything for a long time.

"She ran from him," I said at last. "The old man. Not to my father. From the old man."

Meluan opened her eyes and looked at me.

"I think she ran from both," she said softly. "From him, and from the house, and from the thing under it. From everything she'd learned in that library." She was quiet for a moment. "And to your father. Both at once. People can run from and to at the same time, you know. I didn't understand that, when I was fourteen. I thought she was only running to him. To some tinker. And I hated her for it."

"And now?"

"And now I've stood in front of the door," said Meluan. "With my hand on it. And felt it drink me." She touched her white hair. "And I understand why anyone would run."

---

She stood up to go, after a while. Slowly, painfully, leaning on her cane. She stood in the middle of my grey cell and looked down at me.

"I brought you something," she said.

She reached into the sleeve of her gown and took out a small square of blue silk, folded very carefully. She held it out to me.

I took it. I unfolded it.

Inside, there was a lock of hair. Very fine. Very soft. Dark, with the faintest hint of a curl. Tied with a single thread of white.

"Your cousin's," said Meluan. "My daughter. The Ruh have her now. Tessit's wife cut it for me, before they left. So I would have something." She was quiet for a moment. "I have another. I wanted you to have this one."

I looked at the lock of dark hair in my palm.

And the cord on my wrist hummed.

It was very faint. Barely there. But I felt it. The way a string hums when another string nearby is plucked. As if something in the cord recognized something in the hair. As if there were a thread running between them, very thin, very old.

Meluan saw my face change. I do not know what it did. But she saw it, and her own face went very still.

"You felt that," she whispered.

"Yes."

"What was it?"

I looked down at the lock of hair.

"Family," I said. I did not know how I knew the word. But it was the right one.

Meluan made a sound. A small sound. Half a laugh and half a sob. She put her hand over her mouth.

Then she turned and went to the door, and knocked on it with her cane, and it groaned open, and Stapes was there. She leaned on his arm. She did not look back.

But at the door, she stopped.

"Her name is Lark," she said, without turning around. "Among the Ruh. They call her Lark." She was quiet for a moment. "After your mother. After the way she sang."

Then she was gone.

---

I kept the lock of hair. I kept it through the trial, and the hanging that was not mine, and the long road west in the back of a cart. I kept it through a winter with the Ruh, when I held the child it came from in my lap and watched her take her first steps.

I gave it back to her, in the end. To Lark. On a ridge above a valley in the western hills, in the spring, when she reached out one small hand and touched my face and said *again*.

I tied it into her hair myself, with the white thread, while Mirra watched. I did not know why. I only knew that it was hers, and it should go home.

Knots are very good at finding their way home.

Someone told me that once. I did not remember who. But it was true.
