# Ashes

Wil came out of the fire with Fela in his arms.

I will never forget it. I was lying on the cobblestones with my head in Devi's lap and Sim kneeling beside me, and I was watching the north door of the Eolian, which was a black rectangle full of smoke and roaring orange light. And I saw a shape come out of it. A man, bent almost double, stumbling. His clothes were smoking. His hair was smoking. He was carrying something in his arms.

He came out into the street and fell to his knees on the cobblestones. People ran to him. Someone threw a bucket of water over him and it hissed on his coat. Someone else took the thing he was carrying out of his arms and laid it down gently on the stones.

It was Fela. Her grey dress was scorched black. Her dark hair was singed. Her face was covered in soot and her eyes were closed.

But she was breathing.

I tried to get up. I could not. Devi held me down with one hand on my chest and said something sharp that I did not hear. Sim ran to Fela and knelt beside her and put his ear to her chest, and then looked up at me and nodded, and his face was wet.

Wil was kneeling on the cobblestones with his head bowed. His hands were held out in front of him, palms up, and even from where I lay I could see that they were burned. Red and raw and blistering.

He did not make a sound. He knelt there with his burned hands held out, and he looked at Fela, and his face was very still.

---

They told me later what had happened.

Wil had gone in through the north door and down the cellar stairs, through the smoke and the heat. The fire had not reached the cellar; the stone floor of the main room lay between it and the flames. But the smoke had. It was pouring down the stairs like water, thick and black and choking, and by the time Wil reached the bottom, he could not see his hand in front of his face.

He found Fela by feel. She was kneeling where she had knelt all night, with her hands pressed flat against the great stone foundations. She was unconscious. But her hands had not moved. They were still pressed against the stone, so hard that when Wil tried to pull them away, he could not.

She was holding it. Even unconscious. Even with the smoke filling her lungs and the fire roaring overhead. She was holding the stone, asking it to stand, and she would not let go.

Wil told me afterward that he had begged her. He had knelt beside her in the smoke and the dark and begged her to let go, to come away, to let the building fall. She did not answer. She did not hear him.

So he had taken hold of her wrists, and he had pulled. And the stone had been hot. Very hot. Hot as a forge, from the fire above. And when he pulled her hands away from it, he had to press his own hands against it, to brace himself.

He held them there for a long time. Long enough to pull her free. Long enough to lift her in his arms and carry her up the stairs and out through the smoke.

His hands were never the same afterward. He could still use them, mostly. He could still write, and hold a cup, and turn the pages of a book. But the skin never healed properly. It stayed tight and shiny and pink, like the skin of a newborn thing. And he could never again do fine sympathy work. The kind that needs delicate fingers, and a sensitive touch.

He never complained. Not once. When I tried to apologize to him, much later, he looked at me with his dark calm eyes and said, "She held the building up for three hours, Kvothe. Three hours. With nothing but her hands and her will." He looked down at his scarred palms. "I held a hot stone for a minute. I think I got the better end of the bargain."

---

The Eolian burned until dawn.

The roof went first, falling in with a great roar and a shower of sparks around midnight. Then the balconies, the old dry wood going up like kindling. Then the stage, and the bar, and the tables and chairs, and everything else that could burn. By the time the sun came up, grey and pale through the smoke, there was nothing left inside the walls but ash and charcoal and blackened stone.

But the walls stood.

They stood all through the night, while the fire raged inside them. They stood when the roof fell. They stood when the balconies collapsed. They stood in the grey dawn, blackened and scorched and cracked in places, but upright. A great round ring of stone, open to the sky, with smoke rising out of it like a chimney.

People came to look at it, that morning. Hundreds of them. They stood in the street and stared at it with the vague numb expressions of people who have seen something they cannot understand. They talked in low voices. They pointed at the walls.

"Should've fallen," I heard one man say. He was an old stonemason, grey-bearded and bent, with the thick hands of his trade. "Fire like that. Should've cracked 'em right through. Should've brought the whole thing down." He shook his head. "Never seen stone hold like that. Never in my life."

I did not say anything. I sat on the steps of a house across the street, wrapped in a blanket someone had given me, and I looked at the walls.

Fela was asleep inside the house behind me. A physician from the Medica had come in the night and looked at her and said that she had breathed a great deal of smoke, and that she was very weak, but that she would live. He had looked at Wil's hands, too, and wrapped them in cool wet cloths, and given him something for the pain.

And he had looked at me, and frowned, and asked what had happened to my arm.

I did not know how to answer. My left arm was still numb from the elbow down. The skin of my wrist, where Cinder had held it, was white and cold and hard, like the skin of a man who has been frozen. I could move my fingers, a little. But I could not feel them.

The physician had looked at it for a long time. Then he had said, very quietly, that he had seen frostbite before, in the northern mountains, but never like this. Never in summer. Never in the shape of a hand.

---

They brought Stanchion out a little after dawn.

Deoch carried him. He went into the smoking ruin of the Eolian alone, when the fire had died down enough, and he came out with Stanchion's body in his arms. He had wrapped it in a tablecloth he had found somewhere, a big white cloth only a little scorched at the edges. He carried it out into the street and laid it down on the cobblestones, very gently, and knelt beside it.

The crowd went quiet.

Deoch knelt there for a long time. His face was black with soot and his clothes were burned and his hands were blistered. He did not seem to notice. He knelt beside Stanchion's body and looked at the white cloth, and he did not move.

I got up. I walked across the street, slowly, with the blanket around my shoulders. I knelt beside him.

He did not look at me.

"He built it from a stable," Deoch said. His voice was hoarse. "Did you know that? A stable. Horse dung on the floor and rats in the rafters."

"He told me."

"He told everyone. He was so proud of it." Deoch laughed, a broken sound. "He laid every stone in the stage himself. He carved the balconies. He picked out every table. He hung every lamp." He shook his head. "Twenty-two years. Twenty-two years he spent on that place. It was his whole life."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't." Deoch looked at me for the first time. His eyes were red and wet. "Don't be sorry. He knew. He knew what was coming. You told him, and he knew, and he did it anyway." He looked back down at the white cloth. "He told me, last night. Before it started. He said, 'Deoch, if anything happens to me, make sure the boy keeps singing.'" His voice cracked. "He said it was the most important thing he'd ever done. Letting you sing that song. He said it was worth more than the whole building."

I did not say anything. I could not.

"You did keep singing," said Deoch. "You kept singing, the whole time. Even when she—" He stopped. "Even when she killed him. You kept singing."

"Yes."

"Good." He reached out and laid his blistered hand on the white cloth, over Stanchion's chest. "Good. That's what he wanted."

---

The constables came at noon.

I had been expecting them. I had known they would come. Five hundred royals is a great deal of money, and I had sung in front of half of Imre. Someone would have gone to them. Many someones, probably.

There were a dozen of them, in the grey and blue of the Imre watch, with truncheons at their belts and a magistrate at their head. They came marching down the street toward the ruins of the Eolian, and the crowd parted before them like water before a ship's bow.

I was sitting on the steps of the house again. I saw them coming. I did not move. I did not have the strength to run.

The magistrate stopped in front of me. He was a thin, severe man in a black robe, with a gold chain of office around his neck. He looked down at me with an expression of distaste.

"Kvothe," he said. "Called the Ruh. Called the Arcane. Called the Kingkiller." He held up a paper, heavily sealed. "I have here a warrant for your arrest, issued by the court of King Ambrose of Vintas and recognized by the council of Imre, for the murder of King Roderic. You will come with us."

I looked up at him. I did not say anything.

"Did you hear me? You will come with us. Now."

"He's not going anywhere," said a voice.

It was Threpe. He came pushing through the crowd with half a dozen men in his livery behind him, big men with swords and hard faces. His coat was purple and his face was red and he looked, for the first time since I had known him, genuinely angry.

"Count Threpe," said the magistrate stiffly. "This is a matter of law. You have no standing—"

"I have every standing. I'm a member of the council of Imre, and I have been for thirty years." Threpe planted himself between me and the constables, his small round body quivering with indignation. "And I say the council does not recognize any warrant issued by a usurper who took his throne over the bodies of four murdered heirs."

A ripple went through the crowd. The magistrate's face went white.

"That is treason," he said.

"It's the truth," said Threpe. "And everyone in this street heard the truth last night. Didn't they?" He turned to the crowd. "Didn't you? You heard him sing it. You saw what came to stop him. You saw what came." His voice rose. "Are you going to let them take him? After that? After he kept singing while the whole world was burning down around him?"

The crowd was silent. Then someone, somewhere, said, "No."

And someone else said it. And someone else. It spread through the crowd like fire through dry grass, until the whole street was saying it. *No. No. No.* Not loudly. Not angrily. Just steadily, patiently, like stone.

The magistrate looked around at them. His face had gone from white to red.

"This is a lawful warrant," he said. "Any man who interferes with it—"

"Is exercising his rights as a citizen of Imre," said Threpe. "To question the authority of a foreign king." He smiled, not very pleasantly. "Take it to the council, magistrate. We'll discuss it at the next session. In a span or two. Or three." He waved a hand. "In the meantime, the boy stays here. Under my protection."

The magistrate looked at him. Then at the crowd. Then at me.

Then he turned on his heel and marched away, and his constables marched after him.

---

The crowd did not disperse. They stayed in the street, all through the afternoon, standing around the ruins of the Eolian and talking in low voices. Some of them brought food and left it on the steps where I sat. Bread and cheese and fruit. A jug of wine. Someone left a small bunch of flowers, and I looked at them for a long time.

Threpe sat with me. He did not say much. He only sat there on the steps beside me, with his men standing guard around us, and looked at the blackened walls of the Eolian.

"Stanchion was my friend," he said at last. "For twenty years. He used to let me sit at the bar after closing and drink his best wine and talk about music until dawn." He shook his head. "I'll miss that."

"I'm sorry, Threpe."

"Stop saying that. Everyone keeps saying they're sorry. As if being sorry does anything." He was quiet for a moment. "What did you see, Kvothe? Last night? At the end? When everyone else had run, and you were alone on the stage, and there was that—that *thing*, in the middle of the room?"

I looked at him. I did not know how to answer.

"I was on the first balcony," Threpe said. "I didn't run. I couldn't. I was frozen. I saw it all." He shivered. "I saw that shadow. I heard it speak. I didn't understand most of what it said. But I heard it say that it wanted to die." He looked at me. "Is that true? Is that what it wants?"

"Yes."

"And to get what it wants—"

"It needs me to do something," I said. "Something I won't do."

Threpe looked at me for a long moment. Then he nodded slowly.

"Good," he said. "Good. Don't do it."

---

That night, Denna came.

I was in the house behind the Eolian, the one where Fela was sleeping. The owner, an old widow named Merra, had given me a room at the top of the stairs, with a narrow bed and a window that looked out over the ruins. I was lying on the bed, not sleeping, looking at the ceiling. My arm ached with a deep bone-cold ache that would not go away.

I heard the window open. I turned my head.

She was climbing in over the sill. She was wearing a dark cloak with a deep hood, and her face was hidden in shadow. But I knew her. I would have known her in the dark, in a crowd, in a dream.

She dropped down into the room and stood by the window. She pushed back her hood.

Her face was pale and drawn. Her eyes were red. She looked as though she had not slept in days.

"Kvothe," she whispered.

I sat up. "Denna."

She did not move. She stood by the window, with her hands clasped in front of her, and looked at me.

"I'm so sorry," she said. "About Stanchion. About the Eolian. About all of it." Her voice cracked. "I didn't know. I didn't know they'd come. He told me it was just a song. He told me it didn't matter. He told me—"

"It's all right."

"It's *not* all right." She was crying now. "It's not all right at all. People died. Stanchion died. And it's because of me. Because of my song. Because I sang first and made them angry and—"

"Denna." I got up. It was harder than it should have been; my legs were still weak. I crossed the room to her. "Denna, look at me."

She looked at me.

"It wasn't your song that brought them," I said. "It was mine. They came because I sang the true story. They'd have come if you'd never sung a note."

She stared at me. "But he said—"

"He lies," I said. "Not with words. With everything else. With the shape of what he tells you. You know that. You've always known it."

She looked at me for a long moment. Then she held up her wrist.

The knot was still there. But it was different. Looser. One of the loops had slipped, and hung slack, and the whole knot seemed somehow smaller. Less tight. Less sure of itself.

"It's been like this since your song," she said. "Since the last verse. I felt it let go. Just a little." She touched it with one finger, wonderingly. "I can say things now. Things I couldn't say before. Not everything. But some things." She looked up at me. "I can tell you his name. His true name. The one he had before Bredon."

My heart stopped.

"Tell me," I said.

She opened her mouth. And then she stopped. Her face twisted. Her hand went to her throat.

"I can't," she gasped. "It's still—it's still holding that. That part. I'm sorry. I'm sorry." She took a shaking breath. "But I can tell you other things. I can tell you about the box."

"Tell me."

She looked at me with red wet eyes.

"I hid it," she said. "After Severen. I took it from under your pillow, and I rode all night, and I hid it. Somewhere I thought no one would ever find it. Not him, not you, not anyone." She swallowed. "And then I went back to him. Because I had to. Because the knot—" She held up her wrist. "And he asked me. The first night. He asked me where I had been. And I tried not to tell him. I tried so hard." Her voice broke. "But the knot pulled. And I told him."

I closed my eyes.

"He has it," I said.

"Not him. He gave it to Ambrose." She was weeping openly now. "Ambrose keeps it with him. Always. In a chest in his tent, guarded day and night. He's marching south with it. To Severen." She looked at me. "To the Lackless lands."

I opened my eyes. I looked at her.

"And the knots," I said quietly. "In the king's hair. On Andrian's bridle. Did you tie them?"

She went white. Whiter than I had ever seen her.

"What knots?"

I told her. I watched her face as I told her. I watched it crumple, piece by piece, the way the Eolian's roof had crumpled into the fire. I watched her understand.

"The practice cords," she whispered. "He gave me names. Old names, he said. From the history. Kings and princes from the old stories. He said I needed to practice tying them, so I could write them into the song." She pressed her hands against her mouth. "I tied them for months. Dozens of them. Over and over. *Ending*, he said. *Put ending beside them, because they're all dead now.*" She was shaking. "I thought they were *dead*. I thought they'd been dead for *hundreds of years*."

"I know."

"I killed them."

"No. He did. He used your hands. But he did it." I took her wrists, very gently, the good one and the knotted one. "Denna. Listen to me. You didn't know."

"That doesn't matter!"

"It matters to me."

She stared at me. Tears were running down her face. She was shaking so hard I could feel it through her wrists.

"What do I do?" she whispered. "Kvothe, what do I do?"

I looked at her for a long moment. At the knot on her wrist, loosened by the true song. At her face, wet and white and terrified.

"Go back to him," I said.

She stared at me. "What?"

"Go back to him. Stay with him. Stay with Ambrose's court." I held her wrists tighter. "Be my eyes. Tell me where they go. Tell me what they do. Tell me when they go to the door." I paused. "Can you do that? With the knot the way it is now?"

She looked down at her wrist. At the loosened knot. She was quiet for a long time.

"I think so," she said at last. "Some things. Not everything. But some things." She looked up at me. "I could write them. In knots. He'd never think to look at the cords in my hair."

"Then do it."

"It's dangerous."

"I know."

"If he finds out—"

"I know." I let go of her wrists. I touched her face, very lightly, the way she had once touched my scar. "It's all dangerous, Denna. Everything. But this way, at least, we're playing our own game. Not his."

She looked at me for a long time. Then she laughed. It was a small, broken, astonished laugh, and it made her look very young.

"Our own game," she said. "Against *him*."

"Why not? He taught me to play."

She laughed again, and then she was crying again, and then she was in my arms, and I held her while she shook. We stood there in the dark room at the top of the stairs, with the ruins of the Eolian smoking outside the window, and we held each other.

And when she left, a little before dawn, climbing back out the window into the grey light, she turned back once and looked at me.

"I'll write," she said. "I promise. In knots."

"I'll read them."

She smiled. It was a real smile, the kind that went all the way up to her eyes.

"I know you will," she said. "You always do."

Then she was gone.
