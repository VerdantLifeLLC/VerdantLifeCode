# Manet

If you spend enough time in the Fishery, sooner or later you will learn everything Manet knows. It is not that he teaches it. Manet does not teach, not really. It is that he is always there, at his bench near the kilns, with his wild grey hair and his burn-scarred hands and his clothes that smell of smoke, and if you stand near him long enough, what he knows begins to soak into you the way smoke soaks into cloth.

Manet had been an E'lir for thirty years. Everyone at the University knew that. He had come to the University as a young man, before most of the current masters, and he had never left. He had never advanced. He had studied everything and mastered nothing, and he had been perfectly happy about it the whole time. He knew more about the practical side of artificing than anyone except Kilvin, and possibly more than Kilvin, though you would never have got either of them to admit it.

I went to him on the fifth day after Kilvin gave me the candle.

I had been working on it every spare moment. Sitting at my bench with the candle in its box and the ledgers open beside it, looking at the strange knotted binding I had seen in the heart of the wax. Trying to understand it. Trying to copy it.

I could not. Every attempt failed. I would melt down a little of Kilvin's ordinary beeswax, and pour it into a mold, and try to work the binding into it as it cooled. I would try with sympathy. I would try with sygaldry, scratching runes into the wax with a fine steel point. I would try with things I did not have names for, things I made up as I went along, half by instinct and half by desperation.

Nothing worked. The candles I made were only candles. They burned yellow, and they went on burning yellow, and they did not do anything else at all.

---

Manet was at his bench, grinding something in a stone mortar with a stone pestle. He did not look up as I came over.

"You've been staring at that box for five days," he said. "Kilvin's worried about you."

"Kilvin said that?"

"Kilvin grunted in a worried way." Manet ground his pestle. "I've known him long enough to translate." He tipped the contents of the mortar into a jar and looked up at me with his bright shrewd eyes. "What's in it?"

I hesitated. Then I opened the box and showed him.

He looked at the candle for a long time. He did not touch it. Then he looked at me.

"That's the old blue one," he said. "Kilvin's mystery candle. He's had it for twenty years."

"He gave it to me."

Manet's eyebrows went up. They were very expressive eyebrows, wild and grey and bristling, like two small animals living on his forehead.

"Did he now," he said. "Well. He must think a great deal of you." He looked back at the candle. "And you've been trying to make another one. Haven't you? For five days. And failing."

"How did you know?"

"Because I tried too." Manet smiled faintly. "Twenty years ago. When he first got it. I spent a whole term trying to copy it. Every method I knew, and some I invented." He shook his head. "Never got close."

---

He took me outside, into the yard behind the Fishery, where the scrap metal was piled and the slag heaps smoked and nobody ever went unless they had to. He sat down on an upturned crate and took out a pipe and filled it very slowly with tobacco from a leather pouch.

"Sit," he said.

I sat on another crate.

He lit the pipe. He smoked for a while, looking at the slag heaps.

"Do you know," he said at last, "why I never became a master?"

"People say you couldn't choose a field."

"People say a great many things. Some of them are even true." He blew out a thin stream of smoke. "I couldn't choose. That's true. But it's not the whole of it." He looked at me. "When I was about your age, I made something. Something I didn't understand. I'd been trying to make a lamp that would burn without fuel, drawing heat from the air around it. Everyone told me it couldn't be done. I did it anyway." He smoked for a moment. "It worked for eleven days. Then it drew all the heat out of my room, and the room next to it, and the room next to that, and three students nearly froze to death in their beds before Kilvin broke it with a hammer."

I stared at him.

"I didn't understand it," said Manet. "That was the problem. I'd made it, but I didn't understand it. I'd got it right by accident, by fiddling, by trying things until something worked. And when it went wrong, I didn't know why, and I couldn't stop it." He looked at the slag heaps. "After that, I decided I wasn't going to make anything I didn't understand. Ever again. And it turns out that when you make that rule, you don't make very much."

"And the candle?"

"The candle," said Manet, "wasn't made by someone who understood it either. I'd bet my beard on it."

"How do you know?"

He looked at me for a long moment with his bright shrewd eyes.

"Because nobody understands it," he said. "Not Kilvin, not me, not Lorren, not anyone. And I don't think the person who made it did either. I think they did it the way I made my cold lamp. By feel. By instinct. By reaching into something they didn't have a name for and pulling out whatever came to hand." He tapped his pipe against the crate. "Some things aren't made, Kvothe. They're *found*. Somebody finds the shape of them, in the dark, by accident, and puts it into wax or iron or stone. And then it's there, and it works, and nobody knows why."

---

"The binding in it," I said slowly. "In the wax. I can see it, a little. When I look with the Heart of Stone. It's not like sympathy. It's like—" I struggled for the word. "Knots. It's like looking at cloth and seeing it's not woven but knotted."

Manet was very still.

"Knots," he said.

"Yes."

He did not say anything for a long time. He smoked his pipe and looked at the slag heaps, and his face was very strange.

"There was a woman," he said at last. "When I was young. Not a student. A peddler. She came through Imre once, when I was a second-year, and she sold charms in the market. Little things. Cords with knots in them. She said they'd keep you safe on the road, or bring you luck, or make a girl love you." He laughed softly. "Everyone said she was a fraud. I bought one anyway. For luck."

"Did it work?"

"I don't know." Manet reached into his shirt and drew out a thread. There was something on the end of it, very small and very dark and very old. A knot. "I've worn it for forty years. I've never been killed on the road. I've never been very unlucky." He looked at it. "I've never married, either. So I can't speak to that part."

I looked at the knot on the end of his thread. It was very small. Very simple. A few loops of dark cord, twisted around each other.

But I felt it. Very faintly. The same thing I had felt in the heart of Kilvin's candle. A kind of tightening. A kind of holding.

"It's the same," I said. "Isn't it? The same kind of thing."

Manet looked at me. Then at the knot. Then he tucked it back inside his shirt.

"I don't know," he said. "Maybe. I've never known what it was. I only know that some nights, when I'm lying in bed and can't sleep, I hold it in my hand, and it feels like someone's holding my hand back." He smiled faintly. "Foolish, I know. An old man's fancy."

"It's not foolish."

"No?" He looked at me. "Well. Maybe not." He knocked out his pipe. "I'll tell you something, Kvothe, since you're the first person in forty years who's looked at that knot and seen something in it. Don't try to copy the candle. You'll fail. I did. Kilvin did. Everyone does."

"Then what should I do?"

"Find the person who made it," said Manet. "Or someone like them. Someone who knows how to find the shapes in the dark." He stood up, slowly, with a hand on his back. "And when you find them, be careful. People who can do that kind of thing—" He hesitated. "They're not always safe to be around. The peddler woman who sold me this knot. I asked about her, years later. Nobody knew where she'd gone. But there was a story, in a town in the south, about a woman who sold charms in the market and was found one morning in her wagon, frozen solid. In summer."

He looked at me.

"I've always wondered," he said, "if she found a shape in the dark that someone else didn't want found."

---

I went back to my bench. I did not try to copy the candle again.

But I did something else. I took a single hair from my own head, a long red one, and I tied it around the base of Kilvin's candle, very carefully, in a knot. The simplest knot I knew. A single loop, doubled back on itself.

I did not know why. It was not sympathy, or sygaldry, or anything I had been taught. It was only a feeling. A shape I had seen in the dark, the way Manet said.

And that night, in my room under the eaves, when I lit the candle, it burned yellow. As it always did.

But when I snuffed it, and sat in the dark, and held my hand over it, I felt something I had not felt before.

It was warm. Not the warmth of a flame just snuffed. A different warmth. The warmth of something alive.

I did not know what it meant. I did not know anything.

But I slept, that night, with my hand resting on the box, and I did not dream of doors.
